# Junior Service Designer - Psychometric Assessment Resources

## Introduction

Welcome. This guide is written for you as a junior service designer in the UK Government Digital and Data profession — someone who explains design decisions, works collaboratively, has responsibility for a service, works independently after being given direction by more senior designers, and independently identifies user issues and important needs. That combination of independence and direction shapes everything that follows.

You might be preparing for a formal assessment, thinking about your next step, or simply curious about how psychometric tests connect to the design work you do each week. All good reasons. Let us start by demystifying the assessments themselves. They are not intelligence tests, they do not measure your worth, and they are not designed to catch you out. They are standardised exercises that sample the everyday thinking a job actually uses. In your case that means analysing evidence about a service and explaining what it shows, working out whether a journey step assumes something users do not have, reading a standard and applying it correctly, interpreting analytics well enough to know where to look, and making sensible calls when a design decision has to be made before anyone senior is available.

Your role level raises the bar in three specific ways compared with a trainee, and the questions here reflect them. You analyse and synthesise evidence yourself rather than only reading someone else's findings, which means you will be asked to judge what evidence supports rather than merely to spot patterns in it. You prototype at different fidelities and iterate based on successive rounds of research, which means questions about method choice and about what an iteration can and cannot tell you. And you have responsibility for a service, which means the prioritisation and judgement items assume you will act, not just observe.

2. A numeric reasoning assessment — percentages, drop-off rates, channel comparisons, averages, ratios and journey timings, with an emphasis on interpreting figures rather than only calculating them.
3. A verbal reasoning assessment — reading service standards, accessibility guidance, policy summaries and stakeholder correspondence, then answering true/false/cannot-say and comprehension questions.

Each section follows the same shape: what the assessment measures, how it maps to the named skills in your role, twelve practice questions with full explanations, preparation tips, and common pitfalls.

To get the most from this, commit to an answer before reading the explanation. An answer you have written down teaches you something; one you merely considered does not. Read the explanations even when you were right, because the reasoning transfers further than the answer. And pay particular attention where you were confident and wrong, because that is where a habit is hiding.

One last thought before we begin. Service design is the discipline that insists on looking at the whole thing — the journey across channels, across organisations, including the weeks where nothing visible happens and the people who fall out at the edges. A great deal of what these assessments measure is that habit of zooming out, applied to a table of numbers or a paragraph of policy prose instead of a wall of sticky notes.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the reasoning your role genuinely uses, presented through practical materials rather than abstract shapes. Instead of rotating cubes, you will work with what a junior service designer actually handles: a service blueprint, a set of journey steps, a design standard, analytics, research findings, a channel comparison, an extract from a team's design principles.

The format is normally an online test of fifteen to thirty minutes covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective against a norm group at a comparable level. Many platforms are adaptive, raising difficulty after correct answers, so a test that feels hard is usually a test you are doing well on. Most employers receive an acuity report showing speed against accuracy rather than a single number, which rewards careful work and penalises reckless pace.

For a junior service designer this section is closer to your daily work than it may appear, because the core of your craft is disciplined noticing applied at the scale of a whole journey. You notice that a blueprint shows a step users described completely differently in research. You notice that the "quick" route takes three weeks once a postal wait is included. You notice that a form asks for something the service already holds. You notice that two channels have been designed by different teams to work in incompatible ways. Each of those is a small act of comparison, deduction or error detection, and together they are most of what separates a service that works from one that only looks like it does.

What rises at your level, compared with a trainee's, is the expectation that you judge as well as notice. Several questions below ask you to decide how strongly a finding can be stated, to choose between reasonable methods, or to identify which of two plausible explanations the evidence actually supports. There is still nothing to memorise — every rule and figure appears in the question — but you will need to exercise judgement rather than only apply rules.

A practical note about the options. Wrong answers are not random. They are built from specific, predictable errors: counting mentions rather than people, stopping at the first rule breach, ignoring an exception clause, comparing raw counts across unequal groups, ranking by seniority rather than consequence. Learning the shape of those errors is most of the preparation.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The cognitive dimensions map onto the skills named in your role summary:

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **Evidence-based design**, where you analyse, synthesise and clearly explain evidence relevant to users or a service, such as web analytics data.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Evidence-based design** and to **Design communication**, where you clearly explain design decisions using appropriate tools and methods. A decision explained on an overreaching claim does not survive its first challenge.
- **Error checking** maps to **Designing for everyone**, where you ensure a design meets appropriate standards such as accessibility regulations, with support.
- **Prioritisation** maps to **Iterative design**, where you apply iterative design principles and agile methodologies to your work.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Iterative design** again, where you prototype ideas at different fidelities to explore and test designs, and to **Designing for everyone**, where you work with others to deliver services meeting the needs of all users.
- **Seeing the whole journey** maps to **Designing strategically**, where you describe how your design work aligns to the wider goals and vision of your team.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Error checking a service blueprint**

Your team's blueprint standard requires every step to record four things: the user action, the frontstage service response, the backstage process, and the duration. You review four steps:

| Step | User action | Frontstage | Backstage | Duration |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Submits form | Confirmation shown | Case created | 10 min |
| 2 | Uploads evidence | Upload confirmed | — | 15 min |
| 3 | Waits | — | Case assessed | 3 weeks |
| 4 | Receives letter | Letter delivered | Letter generated | — |

How many required items are missing in total?

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Count items, not steps, because the question asks for items. Step 1 is complete. Step 2 is missing the backstage process — one. Step 3 is missing the frontstage response — two. Step 4 is missing the duration — three. So three items are missing, giving C. Option B is the trap for anyone who miscounts, and a plausible alternative reading would produce "three steps affected", which happens to be the same number here but for a different reason — worth noticing, because the two counts diverge as soon as one step has two gaps. Note also which gaps matter most: step 3 has no frontstage response at all during a three-week wait, which is a design finding rather than a documentation gap. A user waiting three weeks with nothing happening in front of them is a user who telephones, or gives up.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction from a governance rule**

Your team's rule states: "A change to a live service may be deployed only if it has been through design review, unless it is a content correction approved by the content lead." A colleague says: "The change went live yesterday and it did not go through design review." Which must be true?

- A) The rule was broken.
- B) The change was a content correction approved by the content lead.
- C) Either the change was an approved content correction, or the rule was broken.
- D) The change was risky.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A conditional rule with a stated exception, plus facts silent on the exception, yields only the disjunction. Options A and B each assume one branch. Option D introduces a judgement about risk the question does not support — an unreviewed content correction may be entirely safe. Only C holds in every possible case. This structure recurs constantly in assessments and is worth recognising instantly. It also has a workplace parallel worth carrying: the accurate and collegial move is to ask which route was used, not to assume the worst, and at your level — where you have responsibility for a service and may be the one asking — the difference between an enquiry and an accusation matters.

**Question 3 (moderate) — Pattern recognition across evidence sources**

You have three sources about the same service. Research with ten participants found that seven struggled to understand the eligibility wording. Analytics show only 3% of users leave at the eligibility page. The contact centre reports eligibility as its second-highest call topic. What is the strongest interpretation?

- A) The research is unrepresentative; the analytics show few people have a problem.
- B) The sources measure different things — the analytics measure leaving, not understanding — and the contact centre volume corroborates the research: users are proceeding past the page without understanding it, and phoning to find out.
- C) The analytics and research contradict each other and cannot be reconciled.
- D) The eligibility page should be rewritten immediately.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The apparent contradiction dissolves once you name what each source measures. Analytics record behaviour — whether someone left the page — while the research measured comprehension. A user who does not understand the criteria but continues anyway produces no abandonment signal at all, and may go on to make an application they were never eligible for. The contact centre data is the pivot: high call volume on eligibility is behavioural evidence, at scale, that people are not getting what they need from the page, and it corroborates the research using exactly the kind of data that sceptical colleagues find credible. Option A privileges scale over relevance. Option C gives up on an available reconciliation. Option D is probably the right eventual action and skips the analysis that would justify it and shape it.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Prioritisation across a sprint**

You have capacity for one substantial piece of design work this sprint:

1. A new eligibility screen goes into build in five days; nothing has been designed yet.
2. A live journey has a 35% drop-off at document upload, unchanged for months.
3. A policy team wants exploratory work on a concept for next year.
4. The team wants to revisit a screen you designed six weeks ago after minor copy changes.

Which should you prioritise?

- A) Item 2, because it affects the most users.
- B) Item 1, because it is the only one where design work can still change what gets built inside the window, while items 2 and 3 remain valuable but are not time-boxed in the same way.
- C) Item 3, because early-stage work has the highest leverage.
- D) Item 4, because unverified changes are a risk.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The deciding factor in agile prioritisation is not importance in the abstract but whether work can still change a decision. Item 1 has a hard five-day window: design done now shapes what gets built, and design done afterwards becomes a critique of something already made. Item 2 matters greatly and affects more users, but it has persisted for months and will still be there next sprint, so deferring it costs comparatively little. Item 3 has real leverage and no deadline pressure. Item 4 is weakest — minor copy changes rarely justify revisiting a screen, and the team's instinct to check is better served by a quick review than by substantial work. Option A ranks by scale rather than by timing, which is the most common error in this question type and the one that leaves teams perpetually researching problems they cannot yet act on.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Choosing a prototype fidelity**

Your team needs to test whether users understand a new eligibility concept before committing to build. You have one week. Which approach best fits?

- A) A high-fidelity interactive prototype of the full journey.
- B) A low-fidelity prototype — sketches or a simple clickable outline — focused on the eligibility explanation, because the question is about comprehension of a concept rather than about interaction detail, and low fidelity is faster to make and easier to change between sessions.
- C) A written description read aloud to participants.
- D) A live A/B test of two versions.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Match the fidelity to the question. You need to know whether people understand a concept, which does not require working interactions, real data or polished visuals — and high fidelity actively works against you here, because participants respond to the polish, comment on colours and buttons, and are more reluctant to criticise something that looks finished. Low fidelity is faster, cheaper, and crucially can be changed between sessions, which lets you test three versions of the explanation in a week rather than one. Option A spends most of the week building. Option C removes the visual context that carries much of the meaning in an eligibility explanation. Option D cannot run without something built, and would tell you which version performed better without telling you why. Your role names prototyping at different fidelities to explore and test designs, and the skill is precisely this judgement about which fidelity answers which question.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Deduction about what a finding supports**

In research with twelve participants, eight failed to notice a "save and return" link. Which conclusion is best supported?

- A) 67% of users do not notice the link.
- B) The link's discoverability is a substantial problem, evidenced across most participants, warranting design work and a follow-up check on whether the change helps.
- C) The link should be moved to the top of the page.
- D) The link is invisible.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Eight of twelve is a strong qualitative signal, strong enough to act on, and option B states it at that strength while specifying what acting looks like. Option A converts twelve participants into a population rate, which is the most common overreach in design reporting and the one most likely to be quoted back at you in a meeting you are not in. Option C prescribes a specific solution, and at your level the more useful contribution is usually to establish the problem clearly enough that the design conversation can happen properly — the fix might be position, wording, visual weight, or the user's mental model of the task. Option D overstates: participants did not notice the link, which is different from the link being invisible, and the distinction points at different causes. Note that B also builds in the follow-up check, which is what makes it iterative design rather than a one-off change.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Applied problem solving with a constraint**

A service must let users upload a document. Users are predominantly over 70 and many are not confident online. The team proposes a smartphone-only photo upload because it is simplest to build. What is the strongest analysis?

- A) The team is right; most people have smartphones.
- B) Smartphone-only upload assumes a suitable phone, data, somewhere appropriate to photograph a document, and the confidence to do it — assumptions that fail disproportionately in this user group, so the design would exclude a substantial share of the service's core users; the useful next step is to establish how many, and to identify an alternative route such as post or assisted digital.
- C) The team should add a postal route.
- D) The team should test the upload with older users first.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The strongest analysis names the mechanism and its scale rather than only the gap. A smartphone-only upload bundles four separate assumptions, and each fails more often in this population than in the general one. Because the affected group is the service's core user base rather than an edge case, the exclusion is the main case. Option B also proposes the two things that would move the conversation: quantifying the affected group, which turns a concern into something a delivery plan can weigh, and naming an alternative. Option A treats a majority statistic as sufficient, which is precisely the reasoning that produces inaccessible public services. Option C is a good solution offered without the analysis that would justify it, and unsupported solutions get overruled. Option D is a sensible research step that does not address the current design decision, which is to offer no alternative at all.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Reading analytics to focus design work**

A journey shows: 20,000 users start, 17,000 pass eligibility, 10,200 upload evidence, 9,690 submit. Where should design attention go first?

- A) The start, because 3,000 users are lost there.
- B) Evidence upload, because 6,800 users are lost between eligibility and upload — more than twice any other step.
- C) Submission, because it is closest to the outcome.
- D) All steps equally.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Calculate each loss before choosing. Start to eligibility loses 3,000. Eligibility to upload loses 17,000 − 10,200 = 6,800. Upload to submission loses only 510. The upload step is losing more than twice as many users as anywhere else, so it goes first. Expressing these as rates is often more informative still: the upload step loses 40% of those who reach it, against 15% at the first step and 5% at submission. Option A identifies a real but smaller loss. Option C mistakes proximity to the outcome for size of problem. Option D sounds even-handed and is not a plan — with finite capacity you go where the evidence points. This is a good example of quantitative data setting the agenda for qualitative work: the analytics tell you where to look, and only research will tell you why.

**Question 9 (harder) — Distinguishing a pattern from an artefact**

Six participants tested a new journey. The four who completed it easily all used a desktop computer; the two who struggled both used a phone. A colleague concludes the journey works on desktop and fails on mobile. What is the most rigorous response?

- A) Agree; the split is consistent.
- B) Note that device is a plausible explanation but rests on two phone users, and that those two may differ in other relevant ways — digital confidence, or whether they had their documents to hand — so it is a hypothesis to test rather than a finding, and the obvious next step is to recruit more phone users.
- C) Reject it; two participants show nothing.
- D) Recommend removing mobile access until the journey is fixed.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A perfect split like this is exactly the kind of thing that should prompt investigation, and two phone users is a very thin base for a conclusion. Both are true, and holding them together is what rigour looks like. The two groups may also differ in ways that travel with device: people using a phone may be applying away from home, without documents to hand, or may simply be less digitally confident, any of which could be doing the work that device appears to be doing. Option B says this and names the cheap next step. Option A promotes a promising hypothesis into an established finding. Option C dismisses a genuine signal, which is its own failure — small numbers justify caution, not blindness. Option D proposes a drastic action on very thin evidence, and would remove access from users who may have no alternative.

**Question 10 (harder) — Prioritising a genuine trade-off**

You have three weeks. You can either map the whole end-to-end journey at moderate depth, or work in detail on the two steps where analytics show the largest losses. The team is about to rebuild those two steps and has no plans to change anything else. Which is stronger?

- A) The whole-journey map, because end-to-end understanding is always more valuable.
- B) The detailed work on two steps, because that is the only surface the team can act on in this cycle, and depth there will produce something robust enough to guide a rebuild — while a map of screens nobody will change cannot influence anything now.
- C) The whole-journey map, because focusing on two steps risks losing context.
- D) Split the time evenly.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Design value is a function of what a team can act on, and here the actionable surface is two steps. Option A applies a general preference without checking it against the situation; end-to-end understanding is most valuable when a team can change the end-to-end journey, and it remains worth doing later. Option C raises a real risk with a straightforward mitigation — capturing enough context around the two steps to know what users arrive with and what happens next preserves the journey view without diluting the focus. Option D is weakest, producing a map too shallow to be useful and detail too thin to guide the rebuild, which is the characteristic failure of splitting a constrained resource down the middle. Designing so that work can be embedded into an agile workflow is part of your role, and this question is that skill in miniature.

**Question 11 (harder) — Reconciling contradictory evidence**

A satisfaction survey of 1,500 users reports 78% positive. Your twelve research sessions found substantial frustration, with nine participants describing significant difficulty. How should you reconcile these?

- A) Trust the survey; 1,500 responses outweigh twelve sessions.
- B) Trust the sessions; observed behaviour beats self-report.
- C) Examine what each measures and who answered: satisfaction surveys are usually served at the end of a journey, so people who abandoned rarely see them, and they often ask about the outcome rather than the process — so a service can be effective and punishing at once, and the gap between the two is itself a finding.
- D) Average them and report moderate satisfaction.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The instinct to declare a winner is the error. Satisfaction surveys typically reach only people who reached the end; they often ask whether the user got what they needed, which someone can answer positively about a process they found gruelling; and satisfaction is a judgement about outcome, whereas your sessions observed the experience of getting there. All of that can be true simultaneously. Option C also identifies the genuinely interesting move: the gap between "I got what I needed" and "that was hard" is worth reporting in its own right, because a service that is effective and punishing will show up as satisfactory in every headline metric while quietly costing people hours and generating contact centre calls. Options A and B pick a side on a general heuristic rather than the specifics. Option D is the worst answer — averaging incommensurable measures produces a number describing nothing.

**Question 12 (harder) — A flaw in work you have already shared**

You realise that a journey map you produced and shared a fortnight ago omits an offline step in which many users telephone the contact centre after receiving a letter. The team has planned two sprints against the map. What is the strongest response?

- A) Update the map and mention it at the next stand-up.
- B) Tell the team immediately, explain exactly what was missing, and flag which planning decisions may have been based on the incomplete picture — then update the map and, since the omitted step is a large volume of calls, propose looking at why the letter generates them.
- C) Update the map quietly.
- D) Note it in the retrospective.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Two sprints have been planned against an incomplete picture, so the correction alone is insufficient — the important element is flagging which decisions may have relied on the gap, which is the part people skip because it is the most uncomfortable. Option B also does something distinctively service-design-shaped: it recognises that the omitted step is not merely a documentation gap but a finding. A letter that reliably generates a phone call is a letter that is not doing its job, and the map's omission was hiding a real problem as well as a real step. Option A delays disclosure and treats it as housekeeping. Option C leaves the downstream consequences unexamined. Option D defers to a scheduled meeting when the information is needed now. Everyone produces incomplete artefacts; what distinguishes people is the speed and completeness of the telling.

### Preparation tips

- **Practise on your own team's artefacts.** Check a real blueprint against the standard it should meet. You exercise the tested skill and improve the artefact.
- **Ask what each source measures.** When analytics and research appear to disagree, the reconciliation is almost always in the definitions.
- **Rehearse the strength-of-claim ladder.** Observation, pattern, supported finding, measured rate — and what each licenses.
- **Match fidelity to question.** Comprehension needs low fidelity; interaction detail needs higher; rates need analytics.
- **Decide what you are counting.** People, steps, items, losses — the question always specifies one.
- **Calculate losses at every step before choosing where to look.** The biggest gap is rarely the one people talk about.
- **Read the stem's last sentence twice.** At this level items often ask something narrower than the scenario implies.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Converting participants into percentages.** "Eight of twelve participants" must never become "67% of users".
- **Declaring a winner between data sources.** A contradiction is usually a category error.
- **Treating a striking split as a finding.** Four versus two across six participants is a hypothesis.
- **Prescribing the solution instead of establishing the problem.** It is weaker and it is usually not your call yet.
- **Ranking by scale rather than by timing.** Work that cannot change a decision in the window has less value now.
- **Splitting constrained capacity down the middle.** Two half-studies inform nothing.
- **Treating a majority statistic as sufficient.** "Most people have a smartphone" excludes real people.
- **Stopping at the first rule breach.** "How many" needs a full count.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A numeric reasoning assessment measures how accurately you handle the numbers your job produces. Let us clear something up first: this is not a maths exam. There are no equations and no formulae to remember. Most platforms provide a calculator, and where they do not, the arithmetic is deliberately simple. What is measured is whether you can read a table correctly, identify which figures matter, choose the right operation, and interpret the result without overstating it.

The format is typically a timed online test of fifteen to thirty minutes, each item presenting a small table, chart or paragraph of figures followed by a multiple-choice question. Time per item is tight, often around a minute, so the real skill is locating the relevant numbers quickly and disregarding the deliberately irrelevant ones.

Your role names analysing, synthesising and clearly explaining evidence relevant to users or a service, and specifically mentions web analytics data. That makes this section directly job-relevant rather than a generic hurdle. A junior service designer reads drop-off at each step to decide where to focus, compares channel performance to spot exclusion, works out what a journey actually costs a user in elapsed time, checks whether a change made any difference, and translates all of that into something a team can act on. Getting it wrong sends a team to redesign the wrong step for a fortnight.

What rises at your level is interpretation. Several items below have a correct calculation attached to a wrong conclusion, and telling them apart is the test. A designer who can compute a percentage but cannot say whether it means anything is not much use in a prioritisation meeting; a designer who can say "that rate is measured against the wrong group, so it understates the problem" is genuinely valuable.

Four habits carry most of the load. Work out what the question asks before looking at the data. Identify the denominator before calculating anything. Estimate roughly, calculate precisely, then compare the two. And sanity-check against the world — a figure implying that most users complete a form in under three seconds is measuring something other than what it claims.

One warning about the options. Distractors are constructed from specific predictable errors: dividing by the wrong total, confusing a percentage with a percentage point, using the new value as the base in a percentage change. Finding your answer among the options is therefore not evidence that you are right. But knowing the catalogue means you can often see the trap coming.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The numerical skills tested map onto your named skills:

- **Reading analytics accurately** maps to **Evidence-based design**, where you analyse, synthesise and clearly explain evidence such as web analytics data.
- **Comparing rates between groups and channels** maps to **Designing for everyone**, where you work with others to deliver services meeting the needs of all users. A gap between groups is often the first numerical trace of exclusion.
- **Measuring whether an iteration worked** maps to **Iterative design**, where you iterate and improve a design based on successive rounds of research.
- **Elapsed journey time** maps to **Designing strategically**, where aligning to wider goals means understanding the service as users experience it, including the waiting nobody designed.
- **Explaining figures honestly** maps to **Design communication**, where you clearly explain design decisions using appropriate tools and methods.
- **Working with researchers and analysts** maps to **Evidence-based design**, where you use evidence to inform, develop and test a design idea.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Completion rate**

Of 320 users who started a journey, 240 completed it. What percentage completed?

- A) 70%
- B) 75%
- C) 80%
- D) 85%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 240 ÷ 320 = 0.75, so 75%, and the answer is B. Estimate first — 240 of 320 is three quarters, since 160 would be half. Option C comes from 240 ÷ 300 and option A from 240 ÷ 343, both the kind of mis-keyed entry that time pressure produces. Even on an item this simple it is worth noting how you would report it: "75% of users who started completed" is accurate, whereas "75% of users completed" quietly changes the denominator to everyone who might have started, including people who never reached the service at all.

**Question 2 (easy) — Drop-off measured correctly**

A step is reached by 2,400 users and passed by 1,800. What is the drop-off rate at that step?

- A) 25%
- B) 33%
- C) 75%
- D) 30%

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The drop-off is 2,400 − 1,800 = 600 users, measured against the number who reached the step: 600 ÷ 2,400 = 0.25, or 25%, giving A. Option B is 600 ÷ 1,800, which divides by the number who continued rather than the number who arrived — the single most common error in journey analytics, and one that overstates every drop-off it touches. The rule worth fixing in memory: a drop-off rate is always measured against the number who reached that step. Reporting 33% instead of 25% to your team describes the step as a third worse than it is, and if two steps are being compared with different errors, the comparison becomes meaningless.

**Question 3 (easy) — Channel comparison**

| Channel | Started | Completed |
|---|---|---|
| Online | 6,000 | 4,800 |
| Telephone | 1,200 | 1,080 |
| Post | 800 | 320 |

Which channel has the lowest completion rate, and what is it?

- A) Online, 80%
- B) Post, 40%
- C) Telephone, 90%
- D) Post, 60%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Convert each to a rate. Online is 4,800 ÷ 6,000 = 80%. Telephone is 1,080 ÷ 1,200 = 90%. Post is 320 ÷ 800 = 40%. Post is lowest at 40%, giving B. Note what the raw counts would have told you instead: online loses 1,200 users against post's 480, so by volume online looks like the bigger problem — and that matters too. But a 40% completion rate signals something badly broken in the postal route, and postal users are frequently those who cannot use the online channel, which makes their failure rate a likely inclusion problem rather than an efficiency one. Both readings are legitimate; reporting only one of them is not.

**Question 4 (easy) — Elapsed journey time**

A journey has: complete form (25 minutes), wait for reference (3 working days), post evidence (5 minutes plus 2 working days in transit), assessment (4 weeks), decision letter (2 working days). Roughly how long does the journey take from the user's point of view?

- A) About 30 minutes
- B) About 4 weeks
- C) About 5 to 6 weeks
- D) About 3 months

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Add the waits, which dominate. Three working days, plus two working days in transit, plus four weeks, plus two working days is roughly four weeks plus seven working days — call it five and a half weeks. The half hour of actual user activity is negligible. So C. Option A is the instructive trap: it counts only the time the user spends doing something, which is exactly how a service comes to be described internally as "a half-hour application" while the person experiencing it waits a month and a half. Seeing the whole elapsed journey, including the parts where nothing visible happens, is close to the definition of your discipline — and it is where most of the anxiety, the repeat contact and the abandonment actually live.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Did the iteration work?**

Before a change, 480 of 800 users completed a step. After the change, 630 of 900 completed it. Did completion improve, and by how much in percentage points?

- A) Yes, by 150 users
- B) Yes, by 10 percentage points
- C) Yes, by 16.7 percentage points
- D) No, the rate is unchanged

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Convert both to rates, because the totals differ. Before: 480 ÷ 800 = 60%. After: 630 ÷ 900 = 70%. The improvement is 10 percentage points, giving B. Option A compares raw completions across different-sized groups, which is meaningless here — more users completed partly because more users started. Option C looks like a relative calculation gone astray. This is exactly the comparison your role requires when you iterate a design based on successive rounds of research, and the trap is real: teams routinely report "150 more people completed" after a change when traffic also rose, which credits the design with an increase it did not produce.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Comparing unequal groups**

Of 500 users under 65, 425 completed online. Of 150 users over 65, 45 completed online. What is the gap in completion rates, in percentage points?

- A) 30
- B) 45
- C) 55
- D) 85

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Under-65s: 425 ÷ 500 = 85%. Over-65s: 45 ÷ 150 = 30%. The gap is 85 − 30 = 55 percentage points, giving C. Option A is the over-65 rate mistaken for the gap, and option D is the under-65 rate — both are numbers from the working rather than answers to the question, which is a distractor pattern worth recognising. A gap of this size is a serious signal and would justify immediate investigation of what in the online journey older users cannot get past. Note also that converting to rates was essential: comparing 425 completions with 45 would have been meaningless given the group sizes.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Percentage change**

Weekly completed applications rose from 350 to 434 after a redesign. What is the percentage increase?

- A) 19%
- B) 24%
- C) 84%
- D) 124%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Percentage change divides the difference by the original value. The difference is 434 − 350 = 84, and 84 ÷ 350 = 0.24, which is 24%, giving B. Option A is 84 ÷ 434, dividing by the new figure — the most common error in this question type, and one that under-reports every improvement. Option C mistakes the raw difference for a percentage. Option D expresses the new figure as a percentage of the old rather than the change. The rule: for percentage change, the denominator is always where you started. It is worth saying that to yourself before an assessment, because under pressure the new number is the one most recently in your working memory and it is the wrong one.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Working back to a base**

A colleague says 15% of users abandon at the identity step, and that this is 2,700 people a month. How many reach the identity step monthly?

- A) 3,105
- B) 4,050
- C) 18,000
- D) 40,500

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** If 15% equals 2,700, the total is 2,700 ÷ 0.15 = 18,000, giving C. Stepwise if preferred: 15% is 2,700, so 5% is 900, and 100% is 18,000. Verify by reversing — 15% of 18,000 is 2,700, which matches. Option A adds 15%, option B is 2,700 × 1.5, option D is 2,700 × 15. This calculation matters in real work because colleagues quote percentages far more readily than base numbers, and 15% of 18,000 is a very different design problem from 15% of 180. Being able to establish the base in ten seconds lets you judge whether a figure deserves the weight it is being given.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Choosing the denominator**

A journey shows 25,000 start, 20,000 pass eligibility, 12,000 upload evidence, 10,800 submit. What percentage of those who uploaded evidence went on to submit?

- A) 43%
- B) 54%
- C) 60%
- D) 90%

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** The stem names users who uploaded evidence, so the denominator is 12,000 and the part is 10,800: 10,800 ÷ 12,000 = 0.9, which is 90%, giving D. Option A uses 25,000, answering a whole-journey question nobody asked; option B uses 20,000; option C is the eligibility-to-upload rate. All four are true statements about this journey and only one answers the question, which is exactly the trap. Strategically, the funnel also tells you where to look: 8,000 lost between eligibility and upload, against 1,200 at submission. A team proposing to redesign the submission step would need a very good reason.

**Question 10 (harder) — Percentage points and relative change**

Completion rose from 25% to 40% after a change. Which statement is accurate?

- A) Completion rose 15%.
- B) Completion rose 15 percentage points, a 60% relative increase.
- C) Completion rose 60 percentage points.
- D) Completion rose 37.5%.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The arithmetic gap between two percentages is 40 − 25 = 15 percentage points. The relative increase is 15 ÷ 25 = 0.6, or 60%. Option B labels both correctly. Option A uses "15%" ambiguously, and it will generally be read as a relative change, making it wrong. Option C misapplies the percentage-point label. Option D divides by the new value. This matters beyond the test because "completion rose 60%" and "completion rose 15 percentage points" describe the identical change, and the first sounds four times more impressive. Choosing which to report, and labelling it honestly, is part of explaining a design decision — and being the person who quotes both is a cheap way to be trusted.

**Question 11 (harder) — An average that hides a bimodal pattern**

Time on a page averages 40 seconds. Your research showed participants either glancing at it for a few seconds or reading it carefully for two to three minutes. What is the most useful response?

- A) The average is wrong.
- B) The average is probably correct and uninformative: it sits between two clusters and describes neither, so the useful request is for the distribution rather than the mean — which will show whether the page has two distinct audiences behaving differently.
- C) The research participants were unrepresentative.
- D) The page needs to be shorter.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** When behaviour splits into two clusters, the mean lands in the gap between them and describes nobody. A 40-second average is entirely consistent with half the users spending five seconds and half spending two minutes, and those are two different design problems: people glancing may be skipping content they need, while people reading carefully may be struggling with it. Option B asks for the distribution, which is the single most useful request you can make of an analytics colleague, and names what it would reveal. Option A disputes a figure that is probably calculated correctly. Option C blames the research rather than reconciling the sources. Option D leaps to a solution that might harm the careful readers. Working with analysts to use evidence is a named skill in your role, and knowing what to ask them for is most of it.

**Question 12 (harder) — What a small comparison can show**

Your team wants to know whether a design change improved completion. Baseline is 65%, and a 5 percentage point improvement would be meaningful. A colleague suggests testing with 20 participants before and 20 after. What is the strongest response?

- A) Agree; 40 participants is reasonable.
- B) Explain that a 5 point difference on 20 per group is one participant and well inside ordinary variation, so the comparison cannot detect it — recommend measuring the rate with live analytics across all users, and spending the 40 sessions on understanding why users succeed or fail, which sessions do uniquely well.
- C) Increase to 40 per group.
- D) Run it and report the difference with caveats.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** With 20 per group, a five point difference is a single participant, which is indistinguishable from chance — detecting a difference that small reliably needs hundreds per group. Option B says so and, importantly, redirects rather than merely objecting: analytics can measure the rate across the whole user base at no recruitment cost, while moderated sessions are the only method that explains why the rate is what it is. That division of labour is the substantive recommendation. Option A endorses a design that cannot answer its own question. Option C moves towards adequacy without approaching it. Option D is the most damaging, because a reported difference from an underpowered comparison invites the team to act on noise, and caveats do not travel — the number does. Knowing which method answers which question, and saying so before the study runs, is a genuinely valuable contribution at your level.

### Preparation tips

- **Find the denominator first.** Underline the group the question names before touching any figures.
- **Measure drop-off against the number who arrived.** Not the number who continued.
- **Convert to rates before comparing anything.** Raw counts across unequal groups mislead every time.
- **Add up the waiting, not just the doing.** A journey's real duration is dominated by the gaps.
- **Ask for the distribution.** It is the most useful request you can make of an analytics colleague.
- **Learn the percentage point sentence until it is automatic.** It appears constantly.
- **Know roughly what samples can detect.** No formal calculation needed to see that 20 per group cannot see five points.
- **Practise on your own service's numbers.** Calculate every step's loss on a real journey; the biggest is rarely the one people discuss.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Dividing by the wrong total.** Funnel items are built around this single error.
- **Comparing raw completions before and after a change.** Traffic changes too, and credits the design with an increase it did not produce.
- **Confusing percentages with percentage points.** Both are correct labels for different things.
- **Counting only active time in a journey.** This is how "a half-hour application" comes to mean six weeks.
- **Accepting a mean without checking for clusters.** Bimodal behaviour hides entirely behind an average.
- **Endorsing an underpowered comparison.** Acting on noise is worse than acting on nothing.
- **Reporting a number from your working as if it answered the question.** Distractors are frequently built from exactly that.
- **Rounding too early.** Carry a decimal and round at the end.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately you read. It is one of the strongest predictors of performance in roles that run on documents, and service design is emphatically such a role. You read service standards, accessibility requirements, policy summaries, operational procedures, letters sent to users, contracts with suppliers, and the words users say. Reading any of those slightly wrong changes what you design.

The classic format presents a short passage of dense workplace prose, typically three to six sentences, followed by statements to classify as True, False or Cannot Say. True means the statement follows necessarily. False means the passage contradicts it. Cannot Say means the passage neither establishes nor excludes it. Alongside these come comprehension questions, main-point questions, and items asking which of four summaries is most accurate.

The governing rule is that you answer from the passage alone. Everything you know about accessibility regulations, everything your team does, everything obviously true about the world — set it aside. If the passage contradicts what you believe, the passage still governs. This feels unnatural and it is the largest single source of lost marks: candidates recognise a statement as true of the world and mark it True even though the passage never said it. That is a Cannot Say.

Cannot Say deserves particular attention because people avoid it. It feels evasive, so candidates reach for True or False to appear decisive. In practice Cannot Say is frequently correct, and choosing it confidently signals good reading. The reliable diagnostic: if you find yourself supplying a link the passage does not make, that is a Cannot Say.

Small words carry the meaning. "All" is not "most". "May" is not "must". "Should" is not "will". "Encouraged" is not "required". "Unless", "provided that", "where practicable" and "predominantly" flag conditions governing everything around them. Wrong answers are built almost entirely from swaps of exactly these terms, so a statement reading like a faithful paraphrase may still be False.

For a service designer there is a specific payoff here. A great many design opportunities are hiding in the gap between what a policy actually requires and what everyone assumes it requires. Reading the requirement precisely is how you find that gap — and this section is that skill under exam conditions.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The verbal skills tested map onto your named skills:

- **Reading precisely** maps to **Designing for everyone**, where you ensure a design meets appropriate standards such as accessibility regulations, with support.
- **Separating statement from inference** maps to **Evidence-based design**, where you analyse, synthesise and clearly explain evidence.
- **Identifying the main point** maps to **Design communication**, where you clearly explain problems and design ideas to others.
- **Handling qualified language** maps to **Designing strategically**, where you align design work to organisational goals expressed in documents full of "should" and "where practicable".
- **Reading for absence** maps to **Designing for everyone**, where noticing that a procedure never mentions people without internet access has a direct human consequence.
- **Understanding written feedback and direction** maps to **Designing together**, where constructive feedback only works if it is understood as given.

### Practice questions

*Passage A — for Questions 1 to 4*

"All new public-facing services must meet the accessibility standard before launch. Where a service cannot meet a specific requirement, the team must publish an accessibility statement setting out the gap and the plan to close it. Existing services must meet the standard within 18 months of this policy taking effect. Internal-facing tools are outside the scope of this policy, although teams are encouraged to apply the same standard."

**Question 1 (easy)** — Statement: "Internal tools must meet the accessibility standard." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The passage places internal-facing tools outside the policy's scope and says teams are *encouraged* to apply the same standard. Encouragement is not requirement, so the statement's "must" contradicts the passage and the answer is False. Note the two-part structure — an exclusion followed by a softer aspiration — which appears constantly in policy prose. Candidates who register only the second half will mark this True. It is worth being alert to this pattern in real documents too, because "teams are encouraged to" is frequently reported back as though it were an obligation, and occasionally as though it were a prohibition.

**Question 2 (easy)** — Statement: "A service that cannot meet one requirement has breached the policy." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The passage anticipates this and provides a route: publish an accessibility statement setting out the gap and the plan to close it. Failing to meet a requirement is therefore not itself a breach — the breach would be failing to publish the statement. The statement asserts a breach from the failure alone, contradicting the passage, so it is False. Well-drafted standards commonly work this way, setting an expectation and then defining what honesty looks like when it cannot be met. Reading them as absolute produces unnecessary alarm and, more damagingly, encourages teams to conceal gaps rather than declare them.

**Question 3 (moderate)** — Statement: "A service launched three years ago must now meet the standard." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** Existing services must meet the standard within 18 months of the policy taking effect — but the passage never says when the policy took effect. If it took effect three years ago, the deadline has passed; if six months ago, it has not. Because the fact you would need is absent, the answer is Cannot Say. This is a good example of an item that feels answerable until you look for the specific piece of information required. Whenever a passage sets a deadline relative to an event, check that the event's date is actually given.

**Question 4 (moderate)** — Statement: "A team that publishes an accessibility statement has met the policy's requirements." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** Publishing a statement is required where a service cannot meet a specific requirement, and the statement must set out both the gap and the plan to close it. But the passage does not tell you whether publishing alone discharges every requirement — a team might publish a statement while also failing something else entirely, and the passage says nothing about that. Nor does it say publishing is insufficient. The answer is Cannot Say. Candidates who reason that the passage "provides a route" and therefore taking the route means compliance are supplying a link the text does not make, which is precisely the habit this section penalises.

*Passage B — for Questions 5 to 8*

"The service review found that applicants using the assisted digital route completed applications more slowly than online applicants, and required an average of two contacts with the service. The review notes that assisted digital users differed from online users in several respects, including age and first language. The review did not assess the quality of the completed applications. It recommends that the service team consider whether additional guidance would reduce the number of contacts."

**Question 5 (easy)** — Statement: "Assisted digital applicants required an average of two contacts with the service." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The first sentence states this directly. The statement restates it without addition, so it is True. Answer quickly and bank the time for harder items. The only care needed is to notice that the claim is about assisted digital users specifically, not about applicants generally — a statement saying "applicants required an average of two contacts" would broaden it beyond what the passage supports and would be a Cannot Say.

**Question 6 (moderate)** — Statement: "The assisted digital route causes applications to take longer." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The passage reports an association and then explicitly flags that the two groups differed in other respects including age and first language. Either could be responsible for the difference in speed. The passage neither asserts nor excludes a causal role for the route itself, so the answer is Cannot Say. The design consequence is real: if the route's design causes the delay you fix the route, and if assisted digital users are largely people for whom English is an additional language then the fix is translation, interpretation or plainer content. Treating an association as a cause is one of the most reliable ways for a team to spend a quarter improving the wrong thing.

**Question 7 (moderate)** — Statement: "Applications submitted through the assisted digital route are of lower quality." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The passage says explicitly that the review did not assess the quality of completed applications. Declared silence establishes nothing in either direction, so the answer is Cannot Say. Candidates sometimes reason that slower completion and more contacts imply worse applications, which is exactly the inference the item is built to catch — and it may well be the reverse, since two contacts with the service might produce a better application than one unaided attempt. A review that did not look cannot be evidence about what it did not look at, and treating its silence as information is a common organisational error as well as a test error.

**Question 8 (harder)** — Statement: "The review recommends providing additional guidance." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The review recommends that the service team *consider whether* additional guidance would reduce contacts. That is a recommendation to evaluate an option, including the possibility of concluding it would not help. The statement asserts a recommendation to provide, which the review did not make, so it is False rather than Cannot Say — the passage tells you what was recommended and it was something different. This distinction pervades real review documents, and misreading it produces the familiar sequence in which a recommendation to consider becomes a commitment to deliver, which is then tracked and reported against as though someone had decided it.

*Passage C — for Questions 9 to 12*

"Teams should offer users a choice of channel wherever practicable. A digital channel must not be the only option for services used predominantly by people at risk of digital exclusion. Where a team withdraws a channel, it must assess the impact on affected users and record the assessment; the record is reviewed annually. Users who need assistance must be able to reach a person by telephone during published service hours."

**Question 9 (easy)** — Statement: "Users must be able to reach a person at any time." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The passage says users *who need assistance* must be able to reach a person by telephone *during published service hours*. The statement drops both qualifications, replacing the second with "at any time", which the passage does not support and effectively contradicts. So it is False. Two qualifiers removed is a compact demonstration of why the small words matter: a statement can be assembled entirely from the passage's own vocabulary and still say something the passage does not.

**Question 10 (moderate)** — Statement: "A team that withdraws a channel without recording an impact assessment has breached the requirements." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The passage requires that where a team withdraws a channel it must assess the impact and record the assessment. Withdrawing without recording fails that requirement directly, so the statement follows and is True. Contrast this with Question 2 in Passage A, where the passage supplied a route for handling a failure and the breach lay in not taking that route. Here the requirement is unconditional. Distinguishing a rule with an escape route from a rule without one is one of the most useful habits this section builds, and it transfers directly to reading real standards, where teams frequently assume an escape route exists because one usually does.

**Question 11 (harder)** — Statement: "A digital-only service for a general-population service breaches the policy." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The prohibition is specific: digital must not be the only option for services used *predominantly by people at risk of digital exclusion*. A general-population service is not described by that condition, so the prohibition does not obviously apply — but the passage also says teams *should* offer a choice of channel *wherever practicable*, which is a softer expectation whose breach depends on whether offering a choice was practicable, something you are not told. Because the passage neither clearly permits nor clearly prohibits, the answer is Cannot Say. This item rewards holding two provisions in mind at once and noticing that the softer one has not been discharged by the narrow scope of the harder one.

**Question 12 (harder) — Main point**

Which best captures the main point of Passage C?

- A) Digital services are unsuitable for many users.
- B) Users should be able to reach services through a channel that works for them, with digital-only ruled out where exclusion is likely, channel withdrawals assessed and recorded, and human help available during service hours.
- C) Teams must record an impact assessment when withdrawing a channel.
- D) Users must be able to speak to a person.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The main point must span the passage while adding nothing. Option A overstates a targeted restriction into a general judgement. Options C and D are each accurate about one clause and silent on the rest, making them details rather than main points — and D also drops the "during published service hours" and "who need assistance" qualifications. Option B captures all four elements and the logic joining them: channel choice is the principle, digital exclusion is the boundary case, impact assessment protects people when things change, and human contact is the backstop. Main-point items reward the option broad enough to cover everything and disciplined enough to invent nothing, and three of these four are true details promoted beyond their scope.

### Preparation tips

- **Point at the words.** Before marking anything True, identify the phrase that makes it true.
- **Trust Cannot Say.** If you are supplying a link, that is your diagnostic.
- **Circle every qualifier.** All, some, most, may, must, should, encouraged, unless, wherever practicable, predominantly, who need assistance.
- **Look for the missing fact.** Some items are Cannot Say because a date or threshold was never given.
- **Separate rules with escape routes from rules without them.** The first gives False when you assume a breach; the second gives True.
- **Distinguish "consider whether X" from "recommend X".** Review prose runs on this.
- **Treat declared silence as silence.** A review that did not examine something has told you nothing about it.
- **Practise on real documents.** Take a page of the Service Manual and write three statements — one True, one False, one Cannot Say.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Answering from world knowledge.** The most frequent cause of lost marks by a distance.
- **Avoiding Cannot Say.** It converts good reading into wrong answers.
- **Reading "encouraged" as "required".** Policy prose puts soft aspirations next to hard rules.
- **Dropping a qualifier.** "Who need assistance" and "during published hours" are doing real work.
- **Treating association as causation.** A passage that names confounds is warning you off.
- **Inferring from what a review did not examine.** Declared silence is not evidence.
- **Assuming an escape route exists.** Some requirements are unconditional.
- **Confusing a true detail with the main point.** Main-point items punish accurate but partial options.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment presents realistic workplace scenarios and asks what you would do. There is no passage to decode and no arithmetic. You get a dilemma of the kind your role produces, four or five candidate responses, and a question asking which is most effective and often which is least. Some versions ask for a ranking; others ask you to rate each option on a scale from counterproductive to highly effective.

This section measures judgement rather than knowledge, and specifically whether your instincts match what the profession considers good practice at your level. Level is the whole calibration. Your role description places you as someone who explains design decisions, works collaboratively, has responsibility for a service, works independently after being given direction, and independently identifies user issues and important needs. So the strongest answers involve genuine independent action within your remit, combined with prompt honesty about anything beyond it. Neither struggling in silence nor escalating everything scores well.

Four principles carry most scenarios, and they generalise well beyond any single item.

Users come first, and in service design that specifically means the users who are struggling most. When an option protects someone who cannot complete a journey, it will almost always outrank one protecting a schedule, a budget or somebody's convenience. Whole journeys fail people even when every individual part looks acceptable, and noticing that is what your discipline is for.

Act within your remit and be transparent at its edges. At your level you are expected to make design decisions, run a design session, handle routine stakeholder conversations and identify user needs yourself. You are not expected to overrule a policy requirement, commit the organisation, or absorb a serious accessibility problem without telling anyone. The pattern is: do what you competently can, then tell the right person promptly.

Address problems directly, early, with the person involved. Private, specific, non-accusatory conversation beats public correction, silence, or routing around someone.

Zoom out. This is the distinctively service-design signature of a strong answer: when one option addresses the screen or the letter, and another asks what happens before and after it, the second is usually stronger.

One trap worth naming. Options that are pleasant, and options that are decisive, are both attractive for reasons unrelated to effectiveness. Neither agreeableness nor boldness is the criterion.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The judgement dimensions map onto your named skills:

- **Explaining and defending design decisions** maps to **Design communication**, where you clearly explain problems, ideas and decisions using appropriate tools and methods.
- **Protecting inclusion** maps to **Designing for everyone**, where you work with others to deliver inclusive, accessible and environmentally sustainable services meeting the needs of all users.
- **Working with others' perspectives** maps to **Designing together**, where you explain techniques for bringing together multiple perspectives and the importance of constructive feedback.
- **Using evidence honestly** maps to **Evidence-based design**, where you analyse and clearly explain evidence and work with researchers and analysts.
- **Working in cycles and adapting** maps to **Iterative design**, where you apply iterative principles and improve designs across successive rounds of research.
- **Knowing where design sits** maps to **Designing strategically** and **Leading design**, where you align to organisational goals and identify the leaders designers need to work with.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — A live problem you did not cause**

A caseworker emails to say the new form is producing cases they cannot process, and they have been handling them manually for three days. You designed the form section. What is the most effective response?

- A) Explain that the section was based on research and ask them to raise a ticket.
- B) Thank them, ask for specific examples of what users are entering, look at whether the design invites that misunderstanding, and take a proposed fix to your senior designer quickly — while asking how many cases are affected so the scale is understood.
- C) Wait for the next research round to confirm the problem.
- D) Ask for the section to be reverted.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A caseworker reporting the same problem repeatedly is high-quality evidence arriving free, and the first move is to treat it that way. Option B gathers the specifics, because the actual wrong entries usually reveal the misunderstanding immediately, then diagnoses and proposes rather than merely reporting. Asking about scale matters too: three days of manual handling might mean six cases or six hundred, and the response should differ. Option A is least effective — defending a design against evidence that it is not working is the fastest way to lose both the design and the caseworker's willingness to tell you anything next time. Option C wastes weeks when the problem is visible now. Option D reverts before understanding, which may reintroduce whatever the section was solving.

**Question 2 (easy) — A gap in a journey nobody has noticed**

Reviewing the journey, you realise nothing has been designed for what happens if a decision letter does not arrive. What should you do?

- A) Assume the operational team has a process and move on.
- B) Find out what actually happens today by asking the operational team, and if nothing exists, raise it with your senior designer as a gap with a proposed approach.
- C) Design a solution and add it to the journey map.
- D) Note it in the next retrospective.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A letter that does not arrive leaves a person with no decision, no notification and often no obvious route to ask — which is exactly the kind of gap service design exists to catch. Option B does the right two things in the right order: establish what happens today, because there may well be an unmapped process, and only then treat it as a gap. Going to the operational team first is what distinguishes this from guessing, and it usually produces a better answer than you would have designed alone. Option A is least effective because "someone must have thought about it" is precisely how these gaps survive for years. Option C designs a solution to a problem you have not yet characterised and adds it unilaterally to a shared artefact. Option D delays a five-minute conversation for no reason.

**Question 3 (easy) — Conflicting direction**

Your senior designer asked you to finish the blueprint today. Your delivery manager then asks you to spend the day preparing for tomorrow's show and tell, calling it urgent. What is the most effective response?

- A) Do the show and tell preparation; the delivery manager is more senior.
- B) Do the blueprint and tell the delivery manager you are unavailable.
- C) Tell the delivery manager about the existing task, offer a specific workable option, and ask them and your senior designer to confirm the priority.
- D) Attempt both and work late.

**Correct answer: C. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Two legitimate instructions have collided, and while resolving them is not yours to impose, surfacing the conflict promptly and helpfully is. What lifts option C above bare escalation is the offer of a specific option — "I could prepare the show and tell this morning and have the blueprint by four" — which makes you part of the solution. Option A ranks by seniority; the delivery manager probably does not know about the blueprint. Option B is abrupt and leaves a real need unmet. Option D is least effective because working late hides the conflict, normalises over-commitment, and means the team never learns it is asking one person for two days. In an agile team, making competing demands visible is a contribution rather than a complaint.

**Question 4 (moderate) — A user problem outside your service**

Research shows users routinely fail at a step belonging to another department's service, before reaching yours. Your team says it is out of scope. What is the most effective response?

- A) Accept it; the team cannot fix another department's service.
- B) Document the problem and its effect on your users with evidence, raise it with your senior designer and ask about a route to the other department — and separately, consider what your own service can do to reduce the harm, such as explaining the earlier step or handling people who arrive without having completed it.
- C) Contact the other department directly yourself.
- D) Note it in the findings and move on.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** This is the characteristic service design situation and it has a characteristic answer: the user's journey does not respect organisational boundaries even though budgets do. Option B is strongest because it does three things — evidences the problem, seeks a legitimate route to the other department through someone with standing to use it, and asks what your own service can do in the meantime, which is usually more than a team assumes. Option A is least effective, because "out of scope" describes a budget, not a person's experience, and a user who fails before reaching you has still failed to get the thing government said they could have. Option C acts beyond your remit and risks cutting across relationships and conversations you cannot see. Option D records the problem without addressing it.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Pressure to remove inconvenient detail**

You have mapped a journey that appears to take thirty minutes but actually takes six weeks because of two waiting periods. A stakeholder asks you to remove the waits because "they are not design". What should you do?

- A) Remove them.
- B) Explain that the waits are the largest part of the user's experience and that removing them makes the map describe something users would not recognise — and offer a visual treatment, such as showing them greyed out or compressed, so the design steps stay legible.
- C) Refuse and send the map unchanged.
- D) Produce two versions.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** The stakeholder has a legitimate underlying concern — they want the design work legible rather than lost in grey — and the wrong solution. Option B holds the substance while addressing the concern, and the offer of a visual treatment turns a disagreement into a design problem, which is both more likely to succeed and more pleasant. It also makes the key point in a sentence: a map omitting six weeks of waiting is not a map of the user's experience. Option A means nobody designs for the waiting, and waiting is precisely where users give up, telephone, or assume they have been forgotten. Option C protects the map and discards the relationship and the chance to explain. Option D is least effective: two versions of the truth means the convenient one gets used, and you have quietly conceded that the inconvenient one is optional.

**Question 6 (moderate) — An assumption in the room**

In a design session someone says, "we don't need to worry about people without smartphones — everyone has one now". You know that a significant minority of the service's users currently apply by post. What is the most effective response?

- A) Let it go; the team has decided the direction.
- B) Bring the specific fact into the room — that a significant minority currently apply by post — and ask what the team thinks should happen to those users, so the decision is made knowingly rather than by assumption.
- C) Argue that all services must be accessible to everyone.
- D) Raise it privately with your senior designer afterwards.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** The comment reveals an assumption, and assumptions dissolve far more readily against a specific fact than against a principle. Option B supplies the fact and then asks a question, which keeps the conversation about the design rather than about who is right, and leaves room for the team to have a good answer. Option A is least effective, because a decision made on an assumption that is factually wrong will exclude real people and you had the fact available. Option C states a true principle in a way that invites a debate about principles, which rarely changes a room and often hardens it. Option D is a reasonable backup and misses the moment. Your role names independently identifying user issues and important needs, and this is the everyday form of it: noticing whose needs get assumed away when nobody in the room shares them.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Feedback that stings**

In a critique, a senior designer says your blueprint is "confusing and doesn't show the offline channel". You believe it does. What is the most effective response?

- A) Point out where the offline channel appears.
- B) Ask them to show you where they got lost, on the reasoning that if an experienced designer cannot find the offline channel then the blueprint is not communicating it — then fix that.
- C) Accept the criticism and redo it.
- D) Ask for the feedback in writing to consider later.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Both things can be true: the offline channel is on the blueprint, and the blueprint does not communicate it. Option B recognises that and converts the feedback into information, which is far more useful than establishing who was right — and it makes senior colleagues willing to review your work again. Option A is least effective because winning the argument leaves the artefact exactly as confusing as it was and teaches the room that giving you feedback is effortful. Option C accepts the verdict without extracting the detail, so you may redo it and reproduce the same problem. Option D is not unreasonable and defers a two-minute conversation. Your role names the importance of constructive feedback in the design process, and receiving it well is at least half of that skill.

**Question 8 (moderate) — An accessibility problem found late**

Ten days before launch you discover a key step cannot be completed with a screen reader. Fixing it delays launch. What is the most effective response?

- A) Launch and add it to the backlog.
- B) Raise it immediately with your senior designer and delivery manager, describe precisely what cannot be done and by whom, and ask for a decision — while offering any interim route that would let affected users complete the journey another way.
- C) Try to fix it yourself in the ten days.
- D) Launch and publish an accessibility statement noting the gap.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A step that cannot be completed with a screen reader means some people cannot use the service at all, and weighing a delay against an exclusion is a decision for people with the authority to make it — which is not you, though raising it certainly is. Option B does everything within your remit: it surfaces the problem while there is time, describes the impact precisely rather than vaguely, and looks for an interim route so the choice is not simply launch-or-delay. Option A is least effective because "add it to the backlog" is where accessibility problems go to remain unfixed while real people cannot use a public service. Option D looks more responsible and often is not: an accessibility statement is for gaps you are genuinely closing, not a mechanism for launching with a known blocker. Option C is well-intentioned and risky, and doing it quietly means nobody with authority knows.

**Question 9 (harder) — A requirement you doubt**

A policy colleague says the service must ask for three years of address history because "the policy requires it". Research shows the question causes significant abandonment. What is the most effective response?

- A) Design the question as well as possible and accept it.
- B) Share the abandonment evidence, ask what the requirement is for and where it comes from, and explore whether the underlying need could be met another way — while being ready to accept the constraint if it turns out to be fixed.
- C) Challenge the requirement and ask for it to be removed.
- D) Escalate to your senior designer without speaking to the policy colleague.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** "The policy requires it" is sometimes exactly true, sometimes a summary of a need that could be met differently, and sometimes an inherited practice nobody has revisited — and you cannot tell which without asking. Option B asks the productive question, which is not "must we?" but "what is this for?". Knowing that the underlying need is identity verification or fraud prevention opens alternatives that arguing about the question never will, and bringing the abandonment evidence makes the cost concrete. The closing clause matters: some constraints are genuinely statutory, and a designer who cannot accept one loses credibility for the ones that are negotiable. Option C is least effective — a head-on challenge without understanding the basis will not succeed and makes the next conversation harder. Option A gives up before asking. Option D bypasses a colleague who has not had a chance to explain.

**Question 10 (harder) — Findings the team does not want**

Your research shows a feature the team has built over six weeks causes problems for eight of ten participants. At the show and tell you have five minutes. What is the most effective approach?

- A) Lead with the positives and mention the difficulties briefly.
- B) Present what happened clearly and without blame — what participants tried, where they got stuck, what they said — and frame the next step as understanding why, inviting the team into the diagnosis.
- C) Present the failure rate starkly so the team takes it seriously.
- D) Ask your senior designer to present it instead.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Difficult findings are the most valuable findings, and how you deliver them determines whether they get acted on or defended against. Option B keeps the focus on observed behaviour rather than on the quality of anyone's work, which lets the team engage with the problem instead of their feelings about it, and ending on a question invites the people who built it into the analysis — where they will often see causes you cannot. Option A is least effective: softening an eight-in-ten failure means six weeks of effort continues down the same path, which serves nobody, least of all the people who built it. Option C is accurate and adversarial, and a defensive room learns nothing. Option D is understandable and deprives you of the experience; asking your senior to be present is fine, handing over the whole message is not.

**Question 11 (harder) — A mistake of your own**

You realise a blueprint you shared a fortnight ago omits a step where many users telephone the contact centre after receiving a letter. The team has planned two sprints against it. What should you do?

- A) Update it and mention it at stand-up.
- B) Tell the team immediately, explain exactly what was missing, flag which planning decisions may have relied on the incomplete picture, and note that a letter reliably generating calls is itself a finding worth investigating.
- C) Update it quietly.
- D) Raise it in the retrospective.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** Two sprints have been planned against an incomplete picture, so the correction alone is insufficient — the important element is flagging which decisions may have relied on the gap, which people skip because it is the most uncomfortable part. Option B also does something distinctively service-design-shaped: the omitted step is not merely a documentation gap but a finding. A letter that reliably generates a phone call is a letter that is not doing its job, and the omission was concealing a real problem as well as a real step. Option C is least effective, leaving downstream consequences unexamined and risking the discovery being made by someone else later. Option A treats it as housekeeping. Option D defers to a scheduled meeting when the information is needed now.

**Question 12 (harder) — Design work that never lands**

For three sprints your design work has arrived after the relevant build decisions were made. The team is appreciative but nothing changes. What is the most effective response?

- A) Accept it; the pace is set by the product manager.
- B) Bring specific examples to your senior designer and to the team's retrospective, showing where earlier design input would have changed a decision, and propose a concrete change such as a design checkpoint before stories are committed.
- C) Work faster.
- D) Stop producing design work until the team commits to using it.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** This is a process problem and needs a process forum, evidence and a proposal. Option B supplies all three: specific examples make the cost visible in a way general complaint cannot, the retrospective is the team's own mechanism for exactly this, and a named change gives people something to agree to. Involving your senior designer first gives you support and a sense check. Option A gives up on something your role genuinely can influence — applying iterative principles and agile methodologies is a named skill, and shaping how design fits the cycle is part of it. Option C treats a sequencing problem as a speed problem and will exhaust you without fixing anything. Option D is least effective because withholding design work punishes users and the team rather than the process, and it costs you the relationships you need to change it.

### Preparation tips

- **Answer as a junior with real responsibility.** Competent independent action within your remit, plus prompt honesty beyond it.
- **Start from the struggling user.** When an option protects someone who cannot complete the journey, assume it is strongest until you find a reason otherwise.
- **Zoom out.** If one option addresses the step and another asks what happens around it, the second is usually stronger.
- **Bring the fact, not the principle.** A specific number changes a room; a general principle invites a debate.
- **Ask what a requirement is for.** It opens doors that "must we?" never will.
- **Bring an option, not just a problem.** Escalation with a proposed solution scores higher than escalation alone.
- **Read the least-effective question on its own terms.** The worst option usually conceals something or lets harm continue.
- **Talk through real scenarios with colleagues.** Your organisation's actual norms are what this section measures.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Defending a design against evidence.** It costs the design and the goodwill of whoever told you.
- **Accepting "out of scope" as an answer about a person's experience.** Budgets have boundaries; journeys do not.
- **Making two versions of the truth.** The convenient one gets used.
- **Treating an accessibility statement as a way to launch with a blocker.** It is for gaps you are genuinely closing.
- **Winning the argument about feedback.** If an experienced designer got lost, the artefact is unclear regardless.
- **Softening difficult findings.** It lets wasted effort continue and helps nobody.
- **Delaying disclosure of your own error.** Speed and precise scoping turn a mistake into evidence of integrity.
- **Challenging a policy requirement before understanding it.** It fails, and it makes the next conversation harder.

## Conclusion

You have reached the end, and it is worth pausing on what that involved: forty-eight practice questions across four assessment types, with the reasoning worked through on each. That is a substantial piece of preparation and rather more than most people do.

Look back at the ground you covered. In the cognitive section you audited a blueprint against its standard, reconciled analytics that appeared to contradict research, chose a prototype fidelity from the question rather than the habit, prioritised by what could still change a decision, and traced how a smartphone-only upload would exclude exactly the users a service was built for. In the numeric section you measured drop-off against the right denominator, compared channels fairly, worked out what a journey actually costs in elapsed weeks rather than minutes, distinguished a percentage from a percentage point, and declined to endorse a comparison too small to detect what it was built to find. In the verbal section you read "encouraged" as encouragement, spotted a missing date that made a question unanswerable, separated a rule with an escape route from one without, and caught "consider whether" pretending to be a recommendation. And in the situational judgement section you handled a caseworker reporting your own design failing, held the waiting periods on a map under pressure, brought a fact that dissolved an assumption, and told a team that a blueprint they had planned two sprints against was incomplete.

Notice how little of that was really about tests. Every one is a working skill. A designer who calculates against the right denominator, reads the standard as written, holds the whole journey in view, brings the fact rather than the principle, and says so within ten minutes of finding their own mistake — that is simply a good designer. Preparing for these assessments and getting better at the job turn out to be very nearly the same activity, which is the most encouraging thing about the exercise.

A word about where you sit. Junior service designer is a genuinely good stage. You are past needing someone beside you for everything, and not yet expected to have an answer for everything. Your role captures it well: you explain design decisions, work collaboratively, have responsibility for a service, and work independently after being given direction. Use that direction deliberately. The designers who progress fastest are not the ones who ask for the least help; they are the ones who ask precise questions, act on the answers, and gradually need to ask less.

If you want to build on this, a few things repay effort more than rereading. Walk a real service end to end as a user would, including the offline parts, and time it honestly — the result is usually startling. Read the letters your service sends, because letters are frequently the part of a journey nobody has designed and the part that generates most of the phone calls. Spend an hour listening to contact centre calls. Ask an analyst how a metric you rely on is actually defined, not calculated. And practise stating findings out loud at the right strength — "eight of twelve participants", not "most users" — until the accurate version is the one that comes naturally.

Finally, for the day itself. An assessment is a snapshot taken on one morning under artificial conditions. It is not a verdict on your ability or your future in this profession. Read the stem twice, find the denominator, trust Cannot Say, add up the waiting as well as the doing, and remember that careful beats fast.

Good luck. Go carefully, ask well, and keep zooming out.
