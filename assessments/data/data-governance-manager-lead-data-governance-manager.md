## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive ability assessment is a targeted psychometric test designed to measure the exact mental processes needed for a specific role. Unlike a generic IQ test, it uses practical workplace scenarios and data drawn from the job itself to predict performance. For a lead data governance manager, that means the questions will look and feel like your working day: data asset catalogues with inconsistencies to spot, RACI matrices with logical gaps, retention schedules with conflicting rules, and remediation programmes that need sequencing under constraint.

Typical characteristics of these assessments include:

- **Role-targeted content.** Questions mimic the everyday tasks and data formats of the actual job - in your case, governance frameworks, data quality reports, metadata records, risk registers and standards compliance logs.
- **Timed format.** You will usually have 15 to 30 minutes to complete the test online, which places a premium on working accurately at pace.
- **Objective scoring.** Your results are compared against an industry or professional benchmark, often with a breakdown of speed versus accuracy rather than a single score.
- **Validated design.** Questions are vetted to ensure they predict job success without bias, which is why they focus on realistic artefacts rather than trick puzzles.

For a senior role such as yours, cognitive assessments matter because the core of lead-level data governance work is applied reasoning: deducing which record in a catalogue breaks a rule, recognising the pattern behind a cluster of data incidents, checking that a policy's logic is internally consistent, and deciding what to fix first when everything looks urgent. Assessors use these tests because performance on them correlates strongly with performance on exactly those tasks.

### What it measures for your role

Mapped to the skills in your role profile, a job-specific cognitive assessment for a lead data governance manager typically measures:

- **Logical deduction and rule application** - central to your **data governance** skill (implementing and monitoring governance using standard methodology throughout the data life cycle) and your **data standards** skill (monitoring compliance with policies and standards, and recommending how to resolve breaches). Many questions present a rule set and ask which cases comply.
- **Pattern recognition across datasets and processes** - supporting your **data life cycle** skill (designing continuous improvements across the life cycle) and your **data maturity** skill (monitoring and improving areas of data maturity). You may be asked to identify the trend or anomaly that explains a series of quality incidents.
- **Error checking and attention to detail** - underpinning **data management** (monitoring and improving data management practices) and the cataloguing duties of your role, such as catching mismatches between a data asset register and its RACI matrix.
- **Prioritisation and problem solving under constraint** - reflecting your **enabling and informing risk-based decisions** skill (working with higher impact or more complex risks and advising whether they sit within risk tolerance) and your **strategic thinking** skill (evaluating strategies against business requirements).
- **Structured reasoning about responsibilities and dependencies** - reflecting your duty to assign ownership of data and ensure a clear RACI (responsible, accountable, consulted and informed) matrix across the organisation.

### Practice questions

Work through these questions under light time pressure - aim for two to three minutes each - then study the explanations.

#### Question 1 (easy) - RACI logic

Your department's RACI standard states: every data asset must have exactly one Accountable role; the Accountable role must be a data owner at deputy director level or above; a role may not be both Responsible and Consulted for the same asset; and every asset must have at least one Responsible role.

You review four catalogue entries:

- Asset W: Accountable - Deputy Director (Casework); Responsible - Data Steward; Consulted - Policy Lead.
- Asset X: Accountable - Deputy Director (Finance) and Head of Reporting; Responsible - Finance Analyst.
- Asset Y: Accountable - Deputy Director (Operations); Responsible - Operations Steward; Consulted - Operations Steward.
- Asset Z: Accountable - Grade 7 Data Manager; Responsible - Data Steward.

Which assets breach the standard?

A) X only
B) X and Y only
C) X, Y and Z
D) Y and Z only

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Test each rule against each entry. Asset W satisfies all four rules: one Accountable at the right level, a Responsible role, and no role duplicated across Responsible and Consulted. Asset X has two Accountable roles, breaching the "exactly one Accountable" rule. Asset Y names the Operations Steward as both Responsible and Consulted, breaching the separation rule. Asset Z's Accountable role is a Grade 7 Data Manager, below the required deputy director level. Three entries breach the standard, so C is correct. The skill being tested is systematic rule application: strong candidates check every entry against every rule rather than stopping at the first breach they find.

#### Question 2 (easy) - Catalogue error checking

Your data asset catalogue requires that each entry's "review date" is no more than 12 months after its "last certified date". You spot-check five entries:

1. Case Management Data: certified 3 May 2025, review 30 April 2026
2. Grants Register: certified 12 June 2025, review 12 June 2026
3. Payments Reference Data: certified 1 February 2025, review 28 February 2026
4. Staff Directory: certified 15 August 2025, review 1 August 2026
5. Correspondence Index: certified 20 October 2025, review 20 October 2026

Which entries fail the check?

A) 3 only
B) 2, 3 and 5
C) 3 and 5 only
D) None - all entries pass

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The rule allows a maximum of 12 months between certification and review. Entry 1 is just under 12 months - pass. Entry 2 is exactly 12 months - the rule says "no more than 12 months", so exactly 12 months passes. Entry 3 runs from 1 February 2025 to 28 February 2026, which is 12 months and 27 days - fail. Entry 4 is under 12 months - pass. Entry 5 is exactly 12 months - pass. Only entry 3 fails, so A is correct. This question rewards careful reading of boundary conditions: "no more than" includes the boundary, a distinction that matters just as much when you are drafting data standards as when you are being assessed on them.

#### Question 3 (moderate) - Pattern recognition in quality incidents

Over six months, your data quality team logs incident counts by root cause:

| Month | Missing metadata | Duplicate records | Schema drift | Access errors |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Jan | 14 | 22 | 3 | 9 |
| Feb | 15 | 21 | 6 | 8 |
| Mar | 13 | 23 | 12 | 10 |
| Apr | 16 | 20 | 24 | 9 |
| May | 14 | 22 | 48 | 8 |
| Jun | 15 | 21 | 96 | 9 |

Assuming the underlying patterns continue, which statement best characterises the situation?

A) All incident types are stable, so no intervention is needed
B) Schema drift incidents are doubling monthly while other causes are stable, indicating an escalating systemic failure
C) Duplicate records are the priority because they have the highest average count
D) Access errors are declining and will soon reach zero

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Scan each column for its pattern. Missing metadata oscillates around 14-16: stable. Duplicates oscillate around 20-23: stable. Access errors oscillate around 8-10: stable. Schema drift goes 3, 6, 12, 24, 48, 96 - a doubling (exponential) pattern. Option C is tempting because duplicates currently have a high average, but the question asks which statement best characterises the situation given continuing patterns: by July, schema drift would reach roughly 192 incidents, dwarfing everything else. Option D misreads a flat series as declining. This is exactly the lead-level judgement your role demands: distinguishing a noisy-but-stable baseline from an exponential failure signature, and directing remediation effort to the trajectory rather than the snapshot.

#### Question 4 (moderate) - Logical deduction from policy rules

Your retention policy contains these rules: (1) If a dataset contains personal data, it must have a retention schedule. (2) If a dataset has a retention schedule, it must have a named data owner. (3) Datasets without personal data may optionally have a retention schedule. (4) Any dataset with a named data owner appears in the master catalogue.

Dataset Q contains personal data. Dataset R appears in the master catalogue. Dataset S has no named data owner.

Which conclusion is logically certain?

A) Dataset R contains personal data
B) Dataset S does not appear in the master catalogue
C) Dataset Q appears in the master catalogue
D) Dataset R has a retention schedule

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Chain the rules forward for Q: personal data → retention schedule (rule 1) → named data owner (rule 2) → appears in master catalogue (rule 4). So C is certain. Option A reverses rule 4: having an owner guarantees catalogue membership, but nothing says catalogue membership implies an owner, let alone personal data - R could be listed for other reasons. Option B makes the same converse error in the other direction: rule 4 says owner → catalogue, so lacking an owner removes one guaranteed route into the catalogue, but it does not prove S is absent; the rules never say the catalogue contains only owned datasets. Option D again treats catalogue membership as evidence of upstream properties it does not entail. The trap throughout is affirming the consequent - reasoning backwards along a one-way rule. Lead-level governance work involves exactly this discipline: when a policy says "all X must be Y", you cannot conclude that everything Y is X, and audits that make that slip produce false assurance.

#### Question 5 (moderate) - Prioritisation under constraint

You have capacity for exactly two remediation initiatives this quarter. Four are proposed:

- Initiative 1: Fix the schema drift pipeline defect. Impact: prevents an exponentially growing incident class. Effort: medium. Dependency: none.
- Initiative 2: Complete metadata backfill for the top 50 assets. Impact: improves discoverability. Effort: high. Dependency: requires the new metadata tool, which arrives next quarter.
- Initiative 3: Deduplicate the customer reference dataset. Impact: removes a stable but persistent incident class. Effort: medium. Dependency: none.
- Initiative 4: Draft the data ethics review template. Impact: needed for a statutory audit in five weeks. Effort: low. Dependency: none.

Which two initiatives should you select?

A) 1 and 2
B) 1 and 4
C) 2 and 3
D) 3 and 4

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Eliminate first: Initiative 2 cannot succeed this quarter because its dependency (the metadata tool) arrives next quarter - any option containing 2 fails on feasibility, removing A and C. Between the remaining pairings, compare urgency and impact. Initiative 1 addresses the exponential failure (as in Question 3): every month of delay doubles the incident load, so deferring it is the most expensive choice available. Initiative 4 is low effort and has a hard statutory deadline in five weeks - missing it creates compliance exposure. Initiative 3 addresses a stable problem: real, but safely deferrable a quarter without worsening. So 1 and 4 (option B) maximises risk reduction within capacity. This mirrors your risk-based decisions skill: sequence by trajectory and deadline, not by how long a problem has been irritating people.

#### Question 6 (moderate) - Data life cycle sequencing

A new dataset moves through your governance life cycle in this fixed order: Acquire → Classify → Assign ownership → Quality assess → Publish to catalogue → Periodic review. Your process rules state: classification must be complete before ownership can be assigned; quality assessment cannot start until ownership is assigned; publication requires both a completed quality assessment and a completed privacy screening; privacy screening can run any time after classification.

A team reports the following status for a new dataset: classification complete, privacy screening complete, quality assessment complete, publication complete, ownership not yet assigned.

What does this status tell you?

A) The process was followed correctly because all publication prerequisites were met
B) The process was breached: quality assessment ran before ownership was assigned, invalidating the chain that publication relied on
C) The process was breached only at the publication step
D) Nothing can be concluded without knowing the dates of each step

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The rules create a dependency chain: ownership requires classification; quality assessment requires ownership; publication requires quality assessment plus privacy screening. The reported status says quality assessment is complete but ownership is not assigned - which is impossible under the rules unless the quality assessment step was performed out of order. That breach propagates: publication relied on a quality assessment that should not have existed yet. Option A ignores the broken prerequisite beneath publication. Option C mislocates the breach - publication's own listed prerequisites (quality assessment and privacy screening) were nominally "complete"; the violation happened upstream. Option D is over-cautious: the status alone proves a breach because a prerequisite state is missing while its dependent state is complete, regardless of dates. Lead-level governance work constantly requires this kind of dependency audit: when a downstream state exists without its upstream preconditions, the process integrity is broken even if the final artefact looks fine.

#### Question 7 (moderate) - Spotting the inconsistency in a standards document

You are reviewing a draft data standard before it goes to the design authority. It contains these clauses:

- Clause 2.1: All reference data codes must be eight characters long.
- Clause 2.4: Country codes must follow the international two-letter standard.
- Clause 3.2: Country codes are a type of reference data code.
- Clause 4.1: Legacy codes are exempt from clause 2.1 until March 2027.

What is the core defect in this draft?

A) Clause 4.1 creates an unmanaged exemption
B) Clauses 2.1, 2.4 and 3.2 are jointly contradictory: country codes cannot be both two characters and eight characters
C) Clause 3.2 is redundant and should be deleted
D) There is no defect; the exemption in 4.1 resolves everything

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Combine the clauses: 3.2 makes country codes a subset of reference data codes; 2.1 then requires them to be eight characters; but 2.4 requires them to be two letters. Both cannot hold, so the standard is internally contradictory - option B. Option A identifies a genuine weakness (time-boxed exemptions need an owner and a plan), but it is a quality concern, not the core logical defect. Option C is wrong because 3.2 is doing real work - it is precisely what creates the contradiction, and deleting it without analysis would hide rather than resolve the conflict; the proper fix is an explicit exception in 2.1 for code types governed by international standards. Option D fails because 4.1 exempts only legacy codes, not country codes generally. Your data standards skill - creating standards and ensuring senior leaders understand them - depends on exactly this ability to detect when individually reasonable clauses combine into contradiction before they are ratified.

#### Question 8 (challenging) - Deduction across a governance framework

Your governance framework assigns datasets to one of three tiers. The tiering rules are: Tier 1 datasets are those that are business-critical AND contain personal data. Tier 2 datasets are business-critical but contain no personal data, OR contain personal data but are not business-critical. Tier 3 datasets are neither business-critical nor contain personal data. Tier 1 requires quarterly quality reviews; Tier 2 requires annual reviews; Tier 3 requires reviews only on change.

You learn the following: Dataset M receives quarterly quality reviews as required by its tier. Dataset N is business-critical and its tier requires annual reviews. Dataset P contains personal data and is reviewed only on change.

Which statement must be true?

A) Dataset M contains personal data, and Dataset N contains no personal data
B) Dataset P is correctly tiered
C) Dataset N contains personal data
D) Dataset M is business-critical but may not contain personal data

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Work tier by tier. M receives quarterly reviews "as required by its tier", so M is Tier 1, which by definition means business-critical AND personal data - so M contains personal data. N is business-critical with annual reviews, so N is Tier 2; the only Tier 2 route for a business-critical dataset is "business-critical but no personal data" - so N contains no personal data. Both halves of A are forced, so A must be true. Check the others: C directly contradicts the Tier 2 deduction. D contradicts the Tier 1 definition, which requires personal data. B is the subtle one: P contains personal data, so P should be Tier 1 or Tier 2, both of which require scheduled reviews; "reviewed only on change" is the Tier 3 regime, so P is mis-tiered or mis-reviewed - B is false, and P is exactly the kind of compliance breach your monitoring duty exists to catch. This question combines definition unpacking with exhaustive case checking, the core cognition behind auditing any tiered governance framework.

#### Question 9 (challenging) - Root cause isolation

Three data quality problems surface in the same week: (i) the payments dashboard shows customer counts 8% higher than the finance system; (ii) the marketing extract contains customers deleted months ago; (iii) the fraud model flags a spike in "new" customers whose details match existing ones.

Your team proposes four hypotheses. Which single hypothesis best explains all three problems?

A) The finance system is dropping records during its nightly load
B) The customer master data management (MDM) matching service has stopped merging duplicate records
C) The marketing team is using an out-of-date extract schedule
D) The fraud model's threshold was recently retuned

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Test each hypothesis against all three symptoms, not just one. If MDM matching stopped merging duplicates (B): re-registering customers create duplicate records, inflating counts in systems that read the raw master (dashboard 8% high versus finance, which may deduplicate downstream) - explains (i); deletion signals propagated through merge logic would stop being applied, so deleted customers linger in extracts - explains (ii); duplicates look like "new" customers with matching details - explains (iii) precisely. Hypothesis A explains (i) only if finance were high rather than low, and explains neither (ii) nor (iii). Hypothesis C explains (ii) alone. Hypothesis D explains (iii) alone, and only weakly - retuning changes sensitivity, but the flagged pattern (new customers matching existing ones) is a data phenomenon, not a threshold artefact. The reasoning skill is abductive: prefer the single cause with the greatest explanatory coverage before multiplying causes. In your role this is how you turn a week of scattered incident tickets into one targeted conversation with the MDM service owner.

#### Question 10 (challenging) - Prioritising a risk register

Your organisational data risk register shows four open risks. Your risk methodology scores likelihood and impact each from 1 (low) to 5 (high), with exposure = likelihood × impact. Risk appetite: any risk with exposure above 12 must be escalated to the board; risks at 12 or below are managed locally. Mitigations reduce likelihood only.

- Risk A: likelihood 4, impact 4. Proposed mitigation reduces likelihood to 2.
- Risk B: likelihood 5, impact 2. Proposed mitigation reduces likelihood to 3.
- Risk C: likelihood 3, impact 5. Proposed mitigation reduces likelihood to 2.
- Risk D: likelihood 2, impact 5. No mitigation available.

You can fund exactly one mitigation this month. Which single action is most defensible?

A) Mitigate Risk B, because it has the highest likelihood
B) Mitigate Risk A, because it removes the largest amount of exposure and takes a board-escalation risk back within local tolerance
C) Mitigate Risk C, because impact-5 risks always take precedence
D) Mitigate nothing and escalate all four risks to the board

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Compute exposures first. A: 4×4 = 16 (above 12, board escalation). B: 5×2 = 10 (local). C: 3×5 = 15 (above 12, board). D: 2×5 = 10 (local). Now compute post-mitigation exposure: A drops from 16 to 2×4 = 8, a reduction of 8 and a move from board-level to local tolerance. B drops from 10 to 6, reduction of 4, and was already within tolerance. C drops from 15 to 10, reduction of 5, also crossing back into tolerance. A's mitigation both removes the most exposure (8 versus 5) and neutralises the worse of the two escalations (16 versus 15), so B is the most defensible choice - and note you must still escalate Risk C, since it remains above appetite. Option C's "impact-5 always wins" is a heuristic, not the agreed methodology; your risk-based decisions skill requires applying the methodology in proportion to the risk, not overriding it with instinct. Option D abdicates: two risks are demonstrably within local tolerance, and escalating everything erodes the board's trust in your triage.

#### Question 11 (challenging) - Metadata consistency audit

Your metadata standard requires: every published dataset has a completed "description" field; every dataset with personal data has "lawful basis" recorded; "lawful basis" may only be recorded if the "data protection review" flag is set to complete. An audit query returns counts across 400 published datasets: 400 have descriptions; 130 have personal data; 118 have lawful basis recorded; 122 have the data protection review flag complete.

What is the minimum number of datasets that are definitely non-compliant with at least one rule?

A) 4
B) 8
C) 12
D) 16

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Rule 1 (descriptions) is fully met: 400 of 400. Rule 2 requires all 130 personal-data datasets to have lawful basis, but only 118 datasets in total have it recorded; even if every one of those 118 were a personal-data dataset, at least 130 − 118 = 12 personal-data datasets lack lawful basis. Rule 3 requires lawful basis only where the review flag is complete: 118 recorded against 122 complete flags means rule 3 can be fully satisfied (all 118 could sit inside the 122). So the minimum guaranteed non-compliance is 12, option C. The technique is worst-case-favourable counting: to find the minimum defect count, arrange the overlaps as generously as possible and see what shortfall remains unavoidable. Lead governance managers use this constantly when audit tooling returns aggregates rather than record-level detail - you can often prove a floor of non-compliance from counts alone, which is exactly the evidence you need to justify a remediation programme to data owners.

#### Question 12 (challenging) - Strategic pattern synthesis

Your annual data maturity assessment scores five dimensions from 1 to 5 over three years:

| Dimension | Year 1 | Year 2 | Year 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Governance & ownership | 2 | 3 | 4 |
| Data quality | 2 | 3 | 4 |
| Metadata & cataloguing | 2 | 2 | 2 |
| Skills & literacy | 3 | 3 | 3 |
| Tools & architecture | 1 | 3 | 5 |

Senior leaders read the average score (2.0 → 2.8 → 3.6) and conclude the strategy is working uniformly. As the lead data governance manager, what is the most accurate strategic reading?

A) The averages confirm uniform progress; continue the current strategy unchanged
B) Progress is concentrated in governance, quality and tools, while metadata and skills are flat - and the growing gap between advanced tooling and static skills is itself an emerging risk
C) Tools & architecture should be deprioritised because it has already reached 5
D) Metadata scores are stable, which demonstrates that dimension is under control

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Averages hide distribution. Reading row by row: governance, quality and tools are rising strongly; metadata is flat at 2 (a weak score, not a controlled one - option D confuses stability with adequacy); skills is flat at 3. The sharpest insight is the interaction: tooling has sprinted from 1 to 5 while the people expected to use those tools have not moved from 3, and the organisation's ability to find data (metadata, stuck at 2) has not improved either. Sophisticated platforms plus static literacy plus poor discoverability is a classic failure pattern - expensive capability that the organisation cannot exploit, and a widening dependency on a few experts. Option B captures both the uneven progress and the emergent risk. This is your strategic thinking skill in miniature: evaluating whether the strategy meets business requirements means reading beneath the headline metric, and your data maturity skill means communicating precisely this kind of nuance to leaders who saw only the average.

### Preparation tips

- **Practise with your own artefacts.** Before the assessment, spend an hour auditing a real RACI matrix, a slice of your data catalogue, or a section of your risk register against their governing rules. The mental motions - rule application, exception spotting, dependency checking - are exactly what the test compresses.
- **Rehearse boundary conditions.** Many items turn on "at least", "no more than", "exactly one". Slow down for ten seconds on the rule statement; it saves a minute of confusion later.
- **Work eliminations first.** At lead level, distractor options are plausible. Eliminating infeasible options (broken dependencies, contradicted rules) is usually faster than proving the right one from scratch.
- **Manage the clock deliberately.** With 15 to 30 minutes total, decide in advance how long a single question deserves. Bank the easy rule-checking items quickly to buy time for multi-step deduction items.
- **Trust structured reasoning over familiarity.** You know governance deeply; the test rewards reasoning from the rules on the page, which occasionally differ from your organisation's conventions. Answer the framework in front of you.
- **Warm up.** Do two or three practice items immediately before the real test so your first scored question is not also your first thinking of the day.

### Common pitfalls

- **Stopping at the first breach.** Compliance questions often have multiple violations; candidates who stop scanning after one hit choose undersized answers (as in Question 1).
- **Reasoning backwards along one-way rules.** "All personal data assets need owners" does not mean "all owned assets contain personal data". Affirming the consequent is the most common error in framework-logic items (Question 4).
- **Reading the snapshot instead of the trajectory.** A high current count is not the same as a growing problem; assessors deliberately pit a large stable number against a small exponential one (Question 3).
- **Confusing stability with adequacy.** A flat score at a low level is a stagnation signal, not an assurance signal (Question 12).
- **Over-calculating.** Some items yield to elimination or worst-case counting in seconds; grinding through full computation is a time sink that costs you later questions.
- **Importing outside knowledge.** Answer from the rules and data given, even where your department's real policy differs - the test measures reasoning, not recall.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment evaluates your ability to analyse, interpret, and make sound decisions using numerical data. It mimics the data-handling demands of your actual role rather than testing abstract formulae. For a lead data governance manager, the raw material is instantly recognisable: data quality dashboards, completeness percentages, training uptake figures, governance programme budgets, risk exposure scores, and the capacity arithmetic of stewardship teams.

Typical characteristics include:

- **Real-world business data.** Question inputs are tables, charts, budget lines and metrics of the kind that cross your desk - not trigonometry.
- **Basic mathematics, applied carefully.** The operations are percentages, ratios, weighted averages, cost comparisons and projections. The difficulty lies in extracting the right numbers and applying the right operation, not in the arithmetic itself.
- **Calculator friendly.** Most assessments allow or provide an on-screen calculator, prioritising interpretation over mental arithmetic.
- **Strict time limits.** Expect roughly one to two minutes per question, simulating the pace at which you skim a dashboard before a board meeting.

For your role this assessment matters directly. A lead data governance manager who cannot rapidly and accurately read quantitative evidence cannot credibly monitor compliance, build a business case for metadata tooling, judge whether a risk sits within tolerance, or challenge a data owner's optimistic quality figures. Numeric fluency is also the backbone of your advocacy: when you tell the executive committee that data literacy training pays for itself, you need the numbers to hold.

### What it measures for your role

Mapped to the skills in your role profile, this assessment typically measures:

- **Interpreting quality and compliance metrics** - your **data governance** and **data standards** skills depend on reading completeness, accuracy and compliance percentages correctly, and knowing when a change is material (monitoring compliance with policies and standards across the organisation).
- **Quantifying and comparing risks** - your **enabling and informing risk-based decisions** skill requires you to work with likelihood-impact scores, expected losses and mitigation costs in proportion to the risk.
- **Budget and business-case arithmetic** - implementing wider data management competencies (data quality assurance, metadata management, master data management) means costing programmes, comparing options and defending return-on-investment figures.
- **Measuring maturity and literacy improvement** - your **data maturity** and **data literacy improvement** skills involve tracking scores, training uptake and capability growth over time, and communicating those trends honestly.
- **Turning numbers into narratives** - your **communicating analysis and insight** skill (turning complex data into compelling, clear and actionable stories) starts with getting the underlying calculation right; a wrong percentage in a board paper undermines a year of credibility.

### Practice questions

An on-screen calculator is assumed. Show your own working, then compare it with the worked arithmetic in each explanation.

#### Question 1 (easy) - Completeness percentage

Your data catalogue contains 1,250 registered data assets. Of these, 1,050 have a named data owner. Your governance framework's target is 90% ownership coverage. What is the current coverage, and how many additional assets need owners to hit the target?

A) 84%; 75 more assets
B) 84%; 125 more assets
C) 88%; 25 more assets
D) 84%; 200 more assets

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Coverage = 1,050 ÷ 1,250 = 0.84 = 84%. The target is 90% of 1,250 = 1,125 assets with owners. Shortfall = 1,125 − 1,050 = 75. So A is correct. Option B miscalculates the shortfall as 10% of 1,250 (125), forgetting to credit the existing coverage above 80%. Option D confuses the shortfall with the total unowned population (1,250 − 1,050 = 200): you do not need every asset owned to hit 90%. The habit to build is always converting a percentage target back into an absolute headcount before quoting a gap - the same discipline you use when reporting ownership coverage to the design authority.

#### Question 2 (easy) - Percentage change in incidents

Data quality incidents fell from 340 in the last quarter to 289 this quarter. Your improvement plan promised a 20% reduction. Did you meet the target?

A) Yes - incidents fell by exactly 20%
B) No - incidents fell by 15%
C) No - incidents fell by 17.6%
D) Yes - incidents fell by 51%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Reduction = 340 − 289 = 51 incidents. Percentage reduction = 51 ÷ 340 = 0.15 = 15%. The promised 20% would have required a fall of 0.20 × 340 = 68 incidents, to 272. So the target was missed: B. Option C divides by the new figure (51 ÷ 289 ≈ 17.6%) - a classic denominator error; percentage change is always measured against the starting value. Option D mistakes the absolute fall (51) for a percentage. When you report progress to data owners, being scrupulous about the baseline denominator is what keeps your "communicating negative and positive information" honest - a 15% fall is genuinely good news, but calling it 20% would be a false claim someone will eventually check.

#### Question 3 (moderate) - Weighted data quality score

Your data quality framework scores each dimension out of 100 and weights them: completeness 30%, accuracy 30%, timeliness 20%, consistency 20%. The customer master dataset scores: completeness 92, accuracy 78, timeliness 85, consistency 60. What is its weighted overall score, and does it meet the publication threshold of 80?

A) 78.8; below threshold
B) 80.0; meets threshold
C) 78.75; below threshold
D) 82.1; meets threshold

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Compute term by term: completeness 92 × 0.30 = 27.6; accuracy 78 × 0.30 = 23.4; timeliness 85 × 0.20 = 17.0; consistency 60 × 0.20 = 12.0. Sum: 27.6 + 23.4 + 17.0 + 12.0 = 80.0. A threshold of 80 is met by a score of exactly 80.0, so B is correct. The distractors are seeded from the most common slips: writing 78 × 0.30 as 22.2 gives 78.8 (option A); treating all four weights as equal gives the unweighted mean 78.75 (option C). Two habits protect you: compute each weighted term explicitly rather than in your head, and re-add the terms in a different order as a check. And note the boundary reading: "meet the threshold of 80" includes 80 itself - the same boundary discipline as in the cognitive section. When a board paper hinges on a dataset clearing a publication threshold, this is arithmetic worth doing twice.

#### Question 4 (moderate) - Training programme cost per outcome

You run two data literacy training options across the year:

| Option | Cost | Staff trained | Passed assessment |
|---|---|---|---|
| E-learning licence | £48,000 | 800 | 480 |
| Workshop programme | £36,000 | 300 | 240 |

Which option has the lower cost per successful outcome (staff passing), and by how much per pass?

A) E-learning; £50 cheaper per pass
B) Workshop; £50 cheaper per pass
C) E-learning; £30 cheaper per pass
D) They are equal

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** E-learning: £48,000 ÷ 480 passes = £100 per pass. Workshops: £36,000 ÷ 240 passes = £150 per pass. E-learning is therefore £50 cheaper per successful outcome: option A. Notice the denominator discipline: cost per *trained* member of staff would give £60 (e-learning) versus £120 (workshops), but the question asked for cost per *pass* - always confirm which outcome the denominator counts before dividing. Note also what the headline numbers conceal: workshops convert 80% of attendees to passes versus 60% for e-learning, so if your priority were pass *rate* rather than cost efficiency (say, for a small senior cohort whose time is the scarce resource), the recommendation could reverse. Your data literacy improvement skill is precisely this: identifying training options and judging them against the outcome that matters, then telling the honest quantitative story to the budget holder.

#### Question 5 (moderate) - Risk exposure arithmetic

Your risk methodology estimates expected annual loss as probability × impact. Risk R1: 20% annual probability of a data breach costing £600,000. Risk R2: 60% annual probability of regulatory rework costing £150,000. A control costing £70,000 per year would halve the probability of R1. Is the control worth funding on expected-loss grounds alone?

A) No - the control costs more than the expected saving of £60,000
B) Yes - the expected saving is £60,000, nearly covering its cost
C) No - R2 has higher expected loss and should be treated first
D) Yes - the expected saving is £120,000, exceeding the cost

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Expected annual loss of R1 = 0.20 × £600,000 = £120,000. Halving the probability (to 10%) gives a new expected loss of £60,000, so the expected saving is £120,000 − £60,000 = £60,000 per year. The control costs £70,000, which exceeds the £60,000 saving, so on pure expected-loss grounds it is not worth funding: A. Option D forgets that halving the probability removes only half the expected loss, not all of it. Option C is a distraction: R2's expected loss (0.60 × £150,000 = £90,000) is lower than R1's £120,000, and in any case the question asks about this control, not portfolio ordering. In practice you might still fund the control - expected loss ignores reputational harm, tolerance thresholds and the non-linearity of large breaches - but your risk-based decisions skill starts with getting the base arithmetic right before layering judgement on top.

#### Question 6 (moderate) - Reading a maturity chart's axes

A supplier's slide shows your metadata completeness rising from 40% to 44% and headlines it as "10% improvement". Your deputy director asks whether that claim is fair. Which reading is correct?

A) The claim is fair: completeness rose 10%
B) The claim is misleading: completeness rose 4 percentage points, which is a 10% relative increase - both numbers should be reported
C) The claim is false: the increase is 4%, not 10%
D) The claim understates progress: the true improvement is 40%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** From 40% to 44% is an absolute rise of 4 percentage points. As a relative change it is 4 ÷ 40 = 10%. So "10% improvement" is arithmetically defensible as a relative claim but rhetorically slippery, because most readers of a percentage metric assume percentage-point movement. The professional answer is B: report both ("up 4 percentage points, a 10% relative improvement"). Option C is itself imprecise - "4%" conflates points with percentages in the other direction. This distinction is a staple of numeric reasoning tests and of real governance reporting alike: your communicating analysis and insight skill includes refusing to let a technically true number tell a false story, whether the slide is yours or a supplier's.

#### Question 7 (challenging) - Stewardship capacity planning

Your governance operating model requires each data steward to manage no more than 45 catalogued assets. The catalogue holds 1,250 assets today and is growing at 12% per year. You employ 26 stewards. How many additional stewards must you recruit to remain compliant one year from now?

A) 2
B) 5
C) 3
D) 6

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Assets in one year = 1,250 × 1.12 = 1,400. Stewards required = 1,400 ÷ 45 = 31.1, which must round *up* to 32, because 31 stewards could cover only 31 × 45 = 1,395 assets - five short and therefore non-compliant. Additional recruits = 32 − 26 = 6: option D. Two habits matter here. First, capacity ratios round up, never to the nearest whole number - 31.1 stewards means 32 people, because you cannot employ a tenth of a steward. Rounding down (or to nearest) gives 31 − 26 = 5, which is exactly how distractor B is built. Second, apply growth *before* dividing: candidates who divide first (1,250 ÷ 45 = 27.8 → 28, then try to inflate the result) get tangled. This is the everyday arithmetic of implementing governance frameworks at organisational scale: headcount cases stand or fall on whether your rounding logic survives scrutiny by finance.

#### Question 8 (challenging) - Master data deduplication benefits

Your customer master data holds 2.4 million records, of which analysis suggests 6% are duplicates. Each duplicate record costs an estimated £3.20 per year in storage, mailing errors and reconciliation effort. An MDM matching service costs £250,000 in year one (including implementation) and £90,000 per year thereafter, and is expected to eliminate 85% of duplicates from year one onwards. What is the approximate net benefit over three years?

A) £142,000
B) £362,000
C) £745,000
D) £1,175,000

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Work the cashflows step by step. Duplicates = 2,400,000 × 0.06 = 144,000 records. Annual cost of duplicates = 144,000 × £3.20 = £460,800. Eliminating 85% saves 0.85 × £460,800 = £391,680 per year. Three-year saving = 3 × £391,680 = £1,175,040. Three-year cost = £250,000 + £90,000 + £90,000 = £430,000. Net benefit = £1,175,040 − £430,000 = £745,040, so C. The distractors are diagnostic: D (£1,175,000) is the gross saving with the costs forgotten; A is roughly what you get if you count only one year of savings against three years of cost - the most common error in multi-year business cases; B comes from treating the £250,000 as recurring in all three years. When you build the real MDM business case - establishing master data management competencies is a named responsibility of your role level - lay out the yearly cashflows in a simple table before summing; it prevents exactly these collapses.

#### Question 9 (challenging) - Compliance sampling

You audit standards compliance by sampling. From 1,600 in-scope datasets you sample 200 and find 24 non-compliant. Assuming the sample is representative: (i) what is the estimated organisation-wide non-compliance rate, and (ii) approximately how many non-compliant datasets does that imply in total?

A) 12%; 192 datasets
B) 12%; 24 datasets
C) 8%; 128 datasets
D) 15%; 240 datasets

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Sample non-compliance rate = 24 ÷ 200 = 0.12 = 12%. Scaling to the population: 0.12 × 1,600 = 192 datasets. So A. Option B forgets to scale beyond the sample. Option C divides 24 by an imagined 300. This is the routine arithmetic of monitoring compliance across a large organisation, where full audits are unaffordable: you sample, estimate, and extrapolate. At lead level you should also carry the caveat in your head (and your report): a sample of 200 gives a margin of error of several percentage points, so quote "around 190, plausibly 150-240" rather than a falsely precise figure - honest uncertainty is part of communicating analysis and insight to decision-makers.

#### Question 10 (challenging) - Budget reallocation under a cut

Your data governance programme budget is £900,000, split: tooling 40%, staff 45%, training 15%. A spending review cuts the total by 10%, but the staff allocation is contractually fixed in pounds. If tooling and training absorb the whole cut in proportion to their original shares, what is the new training budget?

A) £121,500
B) £110,455
C) £135,000
D) £113,400

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Original allocations: tooling £360,000; staff £405,000; training £135,000. The cut = 10% × £900,000 = £90,000, borne entirely by tooling and training in proportion to their original shares. Their combined original budget is £360,000 + £135,000 = £495,000; training's proportion of that pool = 135 ÷ 495 = 27.27%. Training's share of the cut = 0.2727 × £90,000 = £24,545. New training budget = £135,000 − £24,545 = £110,455: option B. Verify by completing the picture: tooling's share of the cut = 360/495 × £90,000 = £65,455, so tooling falls to £294,545, staff stays protected at £405,000, and £294,545 + £405,000 + £110,455 = £810,000 = 90% of the original budget ✓. Distractor A (£121,500) is training minus a flat 10% of itself, ignoring that the protected staff line pushes a larger burden onto the unprotected lines; distractor D pro-rates against the whole £900,000 pool instead of the flexible £495,000. This "protected line" structure appears constantly in public sector budgeting, and the reasoning pattern - identify the pool that actually absorbs the change, then pro-rate within it - is one to rehearse until automatic.

#### Question 11 (challenging) - Data maturity trajectory

Your data maturity score (scale 1-5) has moved 2.1 → 2.4 → 2.7 over three annual assessments. The departmental strategy commits to reaching 4.0. If the current linear trend continues, in how many more years do you reach 4.0, and what should you tell the executive committee?

A) About 4.3 more years; the trajectory meets a 2030 target only if progress stays linear, which typically gets harder at higher maturity
B) About 2 more years; commit confidently to 4.0
C) About 13 more years; the strategy has failed
D) It cannot be estimated

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The trend is +0.3 per year. Gap to target = 4.0 − 2.7 = 1.3. Years required = 1.3 ÷ 0.3 ≈ 4.33 years. So arithmetically about four and a third more years - option A, which also carries the professional caveat: maturity gains typically get harder at higher levels (early wins come from fixing absences; later gains require culture change), so linear extrapolation is a planning assumption, not a promise. Option B halves the gap arithmetic. Option D is over-timid: an estimate with stated assumptions is exactly what executives need. This item tests two things at once - the extrapolation arithmetic and the strategic thinking to present it with appropriate confidence bounds, which is how a lead communicates opportunities to improve data maturity without overpromising.

#### Question 12 (challenging) - Multi-source dashboard reconciliation

Three systems report "active customer records" for the same reporting date: the CRM shows 412,000; the finance system shows 396,500; the data warehouse shows 405,200. The warehouse figure is the governed golden source. What are the CRM's and finance system's percentage deviations from the golden source, and which breaches your 2% reconciliation tolerance?

A) CRM +1.7%, finance −2.1%; finance breaches
B) CRM +1.7%, finance −2.1%; both breach
C) CRM +3.9%, finance −2.2%; both breach
D) CRM +1.6%, finance −2.2%; finance breaches

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Deviations are measured against the golden source (405,200). CRM: (412,000 − 405,200) ÷ 405,200 = 6,800 ÷ 405,200 = 0.01678 ≈ +1.7%, inside tolerance. Finance: (396,500 − 405,200) ÷ 405,200 = −8,700 ÷ 405,200 = −0.02147 ≈ −2.1%, outside the 2% tolerance. So A: only finance breaches. Option D's figures come from using the wrong denominator (each system's own count); reconciliation deviations are always computed against the agreed golden source - that is the entire point of designating one. This is master data management in numeric form: the test of a golden source regime is whether everyone, including you under exam pressure, measures drift from the same reference.

#### Question 13 (challenging) - Ratio reasoning about literacy uptake

Your data literacy programme aims for a 3:2 ratio of non-specialist to specialist staff among course completions, to ensure the culture change reaches beyond the data profession. This year 1,850 staff completed courses, of whom 780 were specialists. How many more non-specialist completions would have been needed to achieve the target ratio, holding specialist numbers constant?

A) 70
B) 100
C) 130
D) 40

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Non-specialist completions this year = 1,850 − 780 = 1,070. The target ratio of 3:2 against 780 specialists requires non-specialists = 780 × (3 ÷ 2) = 1,170. Shortfall = 1,170 − 1,070 = 100: option B. Distractor A comes from mis-splitting the total 1,850 in a 3:2 ratio (which gives 1,110 : 740) and comparing against the wrong baseline; distractor D compares 1,110 with 1,070, i.e. re-splitting the existing total rather than holding specialists constant as the question instructs. The key reading skill is spotting the phrase "holding specialist numbers constant" - it tells you which quantity anchors the ratio. In your data literacy improvement work, this is more than arithmetic: the ratio is a proxy for whether data-driven culture is spreading across all levels of the organisation or merely deepening within the data community, and being able to quantify the gap ("we need about 100 more non-specialist completions") turns an aspiration into a recruitment target for the next course intake.

### Preparation tips

- **Rebuild the classics until they are reflexes.** Percentage change (always divide by the *starting* value), percentage points versus percent, weighted averages, cost-per-outcome, and round-up capacity arithmetic cover most of what these tests ask. Practise each until the method is automatic.
- **Use your real dashboards as practice material.** Take last quarter's data quality report and recompute its headline figures from the raw counts. Any number you cannot reproduce is both a study exercise and, possibly, a reporting defect worth raising.
- **Write the calculation before pressing buttons.** Under time pressure, jotting "1,400 ÷ 45, round up" before touching the calculator prevents the most common slips: wrong denominator, wrong pool, wrong direction of rounding.
- **Estimate first, compute second.** A rough order-of-magnitude estimate ("savings should be near £400k a year, so three-year net must exceed £700k") catches keying errors instantly.
- **Bank easy marks.** Do the single-step percentage items fast, and be willing to abandon a multi-stage business-case item and return to it. One stubborn question should never cost you three easy ones.
- **Check the units and scale qualifiers.** "In thousands", "per year", "per pass" - misreading a qualifier is the top scoring leak at every level, including yours.

### Common pitfalls

- **Denominator errors.** Measuring change against the new value instead of the baseline (Question 2), or deviation against the wrong reference instead of the golden source (Question 12).
- **Confusing percentage points with percentages.** A 4-point rise from 40% is a 10% relative improvement; conflating the two misleads either way (Question 6).
- **One year of benefit versus multi-year cost.** In business-case items, always line up cashflows over the same horizon before netting (Question 8).
- **Rounding capacity the wrong way.** People and licences round up; anything else leaves you non-compliant on paper and in reality (Question 7).
- **Over-calculating.** Performing precise multi-decimal computation when the options are far apart wastes time; estimation plus elimination is often enough.
- **Ignoring protected lines and fixed constraints.** When part of a budget or population cannot move, the change is absorbed by the remainder - pro-rate within the pool that actually flexes (Question 10).
- **Time sink questions.** Getting stuck on one multi-stage problem instead of skipping and returning is the most common self-inflicted wound in timed numeric tests.

A final calibration note for your level: assessors for lead roles are less interested in raw calculation speed than in whether you extract the *decision-relevant* number. A mid-level analyst is scored on getting £745,040 right; a lead is additionally scored on knowing that the business case survives even if the duplicate estimate is a third too high, and on saying so. As you practise, add one sensitivity question to each item you complete: "which input, if wrong by 20%, would change the conclusion?" That habit costs seconds, sharpens your test answers, and is exactly the quantitative maturity that distinguishes a lead data governance manager's advice - the point is never the number, it is the decision the number should drive.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment evaluates how well you understand, analyse and draw correct conclusions from written business information. It mirrors the exact reading demands of your role. As a lead data governance manager, your working week is saturated with dense text: data protection legislation and guidance, departmental data policies, governance framework documents, audit reports, ministerial correspondence about data incidents, supplier contracts for data tooling, and emails from stakeholders whose meaning is not always what it first appears.

Typical characteristics include:

- **Context-specific text.** Passages use genuine industry material: policy clauses, standards, board papers, emails and reports.
- **Fact-based logic.** You must answer based strictly on the text provided, setting aside your own expert knowledge - which is often the hardest discipline for experienced professionals.
- **Strict answer options.** The classic format is True / False / Cannot Say: True means the passage logically guarantees the statement; False means the passage logically contradicts it; Cannot Say means the passage provides insufficient information either way.
- **Time pressure.** Tests usually allow less than a minute per question once you have read the passage, simulating the pace of real document review.

For your role this assessment is arguably the most job-relevant of the four. Data governance is fundamentally a discipline of precise language: the difference between "must" and "should" in a standard, between "personal data" and "special category data" in a policy, or between "the supplier will delete" and "the supplier may delete" in a contract can be the difference between compliance and a breach. Assessors know that leads who misread documents write frameworks that fail.

### What it measures for your role

Mapped to the skills in your role profile, this assessment typically measures:

- **Precision reading of policies and standards** - your **data governance** and **data standards** skills require extracting exactly what a clause obliges, permits or forbids, and spotting when a proposed action is not actually covered by the text (monitoring compliance and recommending how to resolve breaches of standards).
- **Distinguishing evidence from inference** - your **data ethics and privacy** skill includes assessing and constructively challenging proposed policies, which means separating what a document establishes from what its author merely implies or hopes.
- **Interpreting stakeholder communications** - your **communicating between the technical and non-technical** skill and your **stakeholder relationship management** skill both begin with accurately reading what technical and non-technical stakeholders have actually said, including tone and reservation, before you respond.
- **Summarising faithfully** - your **communicating analysis and insight** skill (turning complex material into clear, actionable stories) depends on compressing documents without distorting them; multiple-choice "best summary" items test exactly this.
- **Reading risk language accurately** - your **enabling and informing risk-based decisions** skill involves parsing audit findings and risk statements where hedged wording ("may", "in some circumstances", "where practicable") carries the real meaning.

### Practice questions

Answer strictly from the passage in front of you. For True / False / Cannot Say items, remember: True = guaranteed by the text; False = contradicted by the text; Cannot Say = the text is insufficient.

#### Passage A - Data ownership policy extract

*"Each information asset listed in the corporate data catalogue must be assigned to a named data owner at deputy director level or above. Data owners are accountable for the quality, security and lawful use of their assets. Data owners may delegate day-to-day stewardship duties to data stewards, but accountability itself may not be delegated. Where an asset is shared between two or more business units, the Data Governance Board will designate a single owner. Assets awaiting designation must not be used to support new services."*

#### Question 1 (easy) - True / False / Cannot Say

Statement: "Under this policy, a data owner can transfer accountability for an asset to a data steward."

A) True
B) False
C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B (False)**

**Explanation:** The passage states explicitly that owners "may delegate day-to-day stewardship duties" but that "accountability itself may not be delegated". The statement claims a transfer of accountability to a steward, which the text directly contradicts, so the answer is False. It is not Cannot Say, because the text does address the point - unambiguously and negatively. Notice the assessment habit: locate the exact clause ("accountability itself may not be delegated") and test the statement against its wording, rather than answering from your general knowledge of how accountability usually works in governance frameworks - the text, not your expertise, is the authority here.

#### Question 2 (easy) - True / False / Cannot Say

Statement: "Most assets in the corporate data catalogue are shared between business units."

A) True
B) False
C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C (Cannot Say)**

**Explanation:** The passage tells you what happens *when* an asset is shared (the Board designates a single owner), but it says nothing about how many assets are shared - "most", "some" or "few" are all consistent with the text. A statement about proportion cannot be verified or contradicted from a clause about procedure, so Cannot Say. This is the most common trap pattern in verbal reasoning: the passage mentions a topic, and the statement makes a quantified claim about that topic which the passage never supports. Mentioning is not measuring.

#### Question 3 (moderate) - True / False / Cannot Say

Statement: "An asset shared between three business units that has not yet been assigned an owner by the Data Governance Board cannot be used to support a new service."

A) True
B) False
C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A (True)**

**Explanation:** Chain two clauses. Shared assets receive their single owner by Board designation; an asset that "has not yet been assigned an owner by the Board" is therefore an asset awaiting designation. The final sentence states that "assets awaiting designation must not be used to support new services". The statement's scenario (three business units, no designation yet, new service) falls squarely within that prohibition, so True. The skill here is legitimate inference: True does not require the statement to be quoted verbatim, only to be logically guaranteed by combining what the passage says. Contrast this with Question 2, where the required fact was absent altogether - the boundary between valid deduction and unsupported assumption is precisely what the test measures, and precisely what you exercise when applying policy clauses to real cases.

#### Passage B - Audit finding on retention compliance

*"Our review examined 60 datasets across four directorates. In 22 cases, retention schedules were either absent or had not been reviewed within the mandated three-year cycle. We found no evidence that data had been unlawfully retained; however, the absence of current schedules means the department cannot demonstrate that retention is lawful. Directorate leads attributed the lapses to the migration to the new records system, which is scheduled to complete in October. We recommend that the data governance function verify all retention schedules within six months of migration completing, prioritising datasets containing personal data."*

#### Question 4 (moderate) - True / False / Cannot Say

Statement: "The audit found that the department had unlawfully retained data."

A) True
B) False
C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B (False)**

**Explanation:** The passage says "we found no evidence that data had been unlawfully retained". The statement asserts the opposite of the audit's stated finding, so within the world of the passage it is False. Read the nuance carefully though: the auditors did not certify that all retention *is* lawful - they said the department "cannot demonstrate" lawfulness. The statement, however, claims the audit *found* unlawful retention, and the text directly denies making such a finding. Distinguishing "no evidence of X" from "evidence of no X", and both from "cannot demonstrate not-X", is exactly the reading precision your data ethics and privacy skill demands when you brief senior leaders on audit outcomes without either overstating or understating the position.

#### Question 5 (moderate) - True / False / Cannot Say

Statement: "More than a third of the datasets reviewed had retention schedule failings."

A) True
B) False
C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A (True)**

**Explanation:** The audit reviewed 60 datasets and found failings (schedules absent or not reviewed on cycle) in 22 cases. One third of 60 is 20; 22 exceeds 20, so "more than a third" is guaranteed by the text: True. Verbal reasoning at senior level often embeds light arithmetic inside comprehension - the test is whether you convert the fraction correctly and compare it with the stated count rather than estimating by feel. Note also what would make the statement Cannot Say: if it had said "more than a third of the department's datasets" (rather than "reviewed"), the passage could not confirm it, because 60 datasets are a sample, not the estate.

#### Question 6 (moderate) - Multiple choice comprehension

Which of the following best summarises the audit recommendation?

A) Complete the records system migration by October
B) After migration completes, verify all retention schedules within six months, starting with personal data datasets
C) Immediately verify retention schedules for all 60 reviewed datasets
D) Discipline the directorate leads responsible for the lapses

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The recommendation sentence contains three elements: the trigger (within six months of migration completing), the scope (all retention schedules), and the priority order (datasets containing personal data first). Option B reproduces all three faithfully. Option A confuses background fact (migration is scheduled to complete in October) with the recommendation itself - the auditors do not recommend the migration, they anchor their deadline to it. Option C distorts both timing ("immediately") and scope (the recommendation covers all schedules, not just the sample of 60). Option D invents content entirely absent from the passage. Faithful summary - keeping trigger, scope and priority intact under compression - is the verbal core of your communicating analysis and insight skill; a board paper that turned this finding into option A's meaning would misdirect the department's response.

#### Passage C - Email from a directorate data owner

*"Thanks for sharing the draft metadata standard. I can see the rationale for mandatory business definitions on all critical data elements, and my team supports the direction of travel. I have to be honest, though: with the case management replatforming absorbing my best people until at least March, I cannot commit to the September deadline for full compliance. If the Board holds firm on September, we will need either a formal exemption for the replatforming period or additional resource from the centre. I'd rather we found a pragmatic phased date than start logging exemptions, which never look good at audit. Can we discuss before Thursday's Board?"*

#### Question 7 (moderate) - True / False / Cannot Say

Statement: "The data owner opposes making business definitions mandatory for critical data elements."

A) True
B) False
C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B (False)**

**Explanation:** The email says the owner "can see the rationale" for the mandate and that the team "supports the direction of travel". The objection raised is entirely about *timing and resources* (the September deadline during replatforming), not about the substance of the requirement. The statement claims opposition to the policy substance, which the text contradicts: False. This distinction - substance objection versus implementation objection - is one you navigate weekly in stakeholder relationship management, and misreading one as the other is how avoidable conflicts start: a lead who reported this owner as "opposed to the standard" would poison a supportive relationship and misinform the Board.

#### Question 8 (challenging) - True / False / Cannot Say

Statement: "The data owner will fail to comply with the metadata standard by September."

A) True
B) False
C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C (Cannot Say)**

**Explanation:** The owner says they "cannot commit to" the September deadline under current conditions, and sets out alternatives (exemption, extra central resource, phased date). None of that guarantees eventual failure: with additional resource, compliance by September might be achieved; equally, nothing guarantees success. A prediction about a future outcome that depends on undecided conditions is not settled by a statement of present inability to commit. So Cannot Say. Candidates frequently mark this False ("they might comply") or True ("they said they can't") - both import certainty the text does not contain. Reading commitments precisely - distinguishing "cannot commit" from "will not deliver" - is essential when you manage expectations between technical teams and senior stakeholders.

#### Question 9 (challenging) - Multiple choice inference

What is the data owner primarily asking the recipient to do?

A) Grant a formal exemption for the replatforming period
B) Provide additional resource from the centre
C) Engage in a conversation before Thursday's Board to agree a pragmatic phased compliance date
D) Postpone the Board meeting

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The email presents exemption and central resource as fallback options *if* the Board holds firm, but states a clear preference: "I'd rather we found a pragmatic phased date than start logging exemptions", followed by the direct request "Can we discuss before Thursday's Board?". The primary ask is therefore the pre-Board conversation aimed at a phased date: option C. Options A and B elevate the contingencies over the stated preference; option D is never requested. Reading the *shape* of a stakeholder email - context, options, preference, ask - is a core skill for hosting difficult discussions with senior stakeholders: respond to the real request, not the loudest sentence.

#### Passage D - Draft data ethics policy clause

*"Before any new automated decision-making process is deployed, the responsible team must complete an ethics assessment using the departmental template. Where the assessment identifies a high residual risk to individuals, deployment may proceed only with the written approval of the Data Ethics Committee. The Committee will normally respond within 15 working days. Teams should note that approval under this clause does not remove the need to comply with data protection legislation, and that the Committee may attach conditions to any approval. This clause does not apply to processes that merely support human decision-makers without determining outcomes."*

#### Question 10 (challenging) - True / False / Cannot Say

Statement: "A new tool that ranks benefit claims for caseworkers to review, without deciding any claim itself, requires Data Ethics Committee approval before deployment."

A) True
B) False
C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B (False)**

**Explanation:** The final sentence carves out "processes that merely support human decision-makers without determining outcomes". A ranking tool whose outputs caseworkers review, and which decides nothing itself, sits within that carve-out, so the clause - including its Committee approval requirement - does not apply. The statement asserts that approval *is* required, which the text contradicts: False. Two subtleties reward careful readers: first, even in-scope processes need Committee approval only where the ethics assessment finds *high residual risk*, so the statement would over-reach even without the carve-out; second, "merely support... without determining outcomes" is doing precise work - if the ranking in practice determined outcomes (caseworkers rubber-stamping), the carve-out might not apply, but the statement as written matches the exemption. Assessing and constructively challenging proposed ethics policies means noticing exactly these scope boundaries.

#### Question 11 (challenging) - True / False / Cannot Say

Statement: "The Data Ethics Committee must respond to approval requests within 15 working days."

A) True
B) False
C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B (False)**

**Explanation:** The passage says the Committee "will normally respond within 15 working days". The word "normally" makes the timescale an expectation with implicit exceptions, not an obligation. The statement converts it into a strict duty ("must... within 15 working days"), which the text does not support - and because the text affirmatively frames the timescale as a norm rather than a rule, the strict-duty claim is contradicted rather than merely unverifiable. Modal-verb precision ("must" versus "should" versus "will normally") is the single most tested distinction in policy-based verbal reasoning, and the most consequential one in your standards work: an SLA and an aspiration are different instruments, and drafting or reading one as the other creates false expectations at audit.

#### Question 12 (challenging) - Multiple choice comprehension

According to the passage, which of the following is true of Committee approval?

A) It exempts the team from data protection legislation for the approved process
B) It may carry conditions, and it does not displace data protection obligations
C) It is required for every automated decision-making process
D) It is granted automatically if no response arrives within 15 working days

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage states both limbs of option B directly: "the Committee may attach conditions to any approval" and "approval under this clause does not remove the need to comply with data protection legislation". Option A asserts the opposite of the second limb. Option C over-extends scope: approval is required only where the ethics assessment identifies high residual risk (and not at all for the carve-out processes). Option D invents a deemed-approval mechanism the text nowhere creates - silence is not consent in this clause. The reasoning pattern is compositional: correct answers to "which is true" items usually stitch together two separately stated facts, while distractors negate, over-generalise or fabricate. That is also a fair description of how misreadings of governance policy propagate in real organisations - which is why your role exists.

#### Passage E - Board paper extract on data strategy

*"The data strategy refresh proposes consolidating the department's eleven data quality tools to a maximum of three within two years. Directorates raised concerns that consolidation could disrupt statutory reporting during the transition. The programme board has therefore agreed that no directorate will be required to migrate during its statutory reporting window, and that the two legacy tools supporting statutory returns will be decommissioned last. Funding for the consolidation is approved for year one only; continuation funding is subject to the spending review and to the programme demonstrating benefits against the agreed baseline by Q4."*

#### Question 13 (challenging) - True / False / Cannot Say

Statement: "The consolidation programme is fully funded for the two-year period."

A) True
B) False
C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B (False)**

**Explanation:** The passage states funding "is approved for year one only", with continuation "subject to the spending review and to the programme demonstrating benefits... by Q4". A programme whose second year of funding is explicitly conditional is not "fully funded for the two-year period", so the statement is contradicted: False. It is not Cannot Say, because the text speaks directly to the funding position rather than leaving it open. This structure - a confident strategic commitment resting on conditional funding - is one you will meet repeatedly when defining strategies and policies; reading it accurately is the difference between telling stakeholders "we are funded" and the truthful "we are funded for year one, with a benefits case to make by Q4".

#### Question 14 (challenging) - True / False / Cannot Say

Statement: "At least one directorate will be permitted to delay its migration."

A) True
B) False
C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C (Cannot Say)**

**Explanation:** The board agreed that no directorate will be *required* to migrate during its statutory reporting window. That establishes a protection, but whether it results in any actual delay depends on facts the passage does not give: migration schedules might be planned entirely outside reporting windows, in which case no directorate ever needs to invoke the protection. A guarantee of permission-if-needed is not a guarantee that the need arises, so the statement is neither confirmed nor contradicted: Cannot Say. The tempting wrong answer is True, reasoning that with eleven tools and multiple directorates some clash is inevitable - but that is probabilistic real-world reasoning, not textual entailment. Verbal reasoning rewards the narrower question: what does this text, alone, guarantee?

### Preparation tips

- **Drill the three-way distinction daily.** True = the text guarantees it; False = the text contradicts it; Cannot Say = neither. Ten minutes a day with any dense document - a policy, an audit report, a contract clause - asking "what does this text *guarantee*?" builds the reflex fast.
- **Suspend your expertise deliberately.** You know more about data governance than most passage authors. That knowledge is precisely what generates wrong answers here: the test asks what *this text* establishes, not what good practice would be. Practise answering as if you knew nothing beyond the page.
- **Highlight modal verbs and qualifiers as you read.** "Must", "should", "may", "normally", "where practicable" - these words carry the legal and logical weight. Most challenging items turn on one of them.
- **Watch scope words.** "All", "some", "most", "only", "reviewed datasets" versus "all datasets" - statements that quietly widen or narrow the passage's scope are the standard hard distractor.
- **Read the statement before re-reading the passage.** For each question, scan the statement first, then hunt the relevant clause. Re-reading whole passages for every item is the classic time leak.
- **Practise faithful compression.** Summarise a real policy paragraph in one sentence, then check: did you keep the trigger, the scope and the conditions? This mirrors both the multiple-choice summary items and your day job of briefing senior leaders.

### Common pitfalls

- **Bringing in outside knowledge.** Answering from your professional experience instead of the passage - the most common error for senior candidates, because your experience is usually right about the world and wrong about the text.
- **Confusing "no evidence of X" with "X is false".** Audit and assurance language is full of these gradations; the passage's exact epistemic claim is the answer's boundary (Question 4).
- **Treating mentions as measurements.** A passage that discusses shared assets says nothing about how many assets are shared (Question 2).
- **Converting norms into duties.** "Will normally" is not "must"; "should" is not "shall". Statements that harden or soften the modality are False or Cannot Say traps (Question 11).
- **Predicting outcomes from stated intentions.** "Cannot commit to the deadline" does not settle whether the deadline will be missed (Question 8).
- **Misreading tone in correspondence.** An email can support a policy while resisting its timeline; collapsing that distinction misrepresents the stakeholder (Question 7).
- **Poor time management.** Spending too long absorbing the whole passage up front instead of locating the clause each statement actually turns on.

One last point of encouragement specific to your role: verbal reasoning is the assessment where lead data governance managers most often outperform, because the discipline it demands - answering from the text, the whole text and nothing but the text - is the discipline you already apply when interpreting legislation, standards and audit findings. The risk for you is not lack of skill but excess of context: the more you know about how governance *should* work, the more actively you must silence that knowledge when the passage says something narrower, looser or simply different. Treat every passage as a new department whose policies you have just inherited: your job is to establish exactly what the documents commit it to, before you bring any judgement about whether those commitments are wise. Get that stance right and the question formats largely take care of themselves.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment evaluates your decision-making, interpersonal skill and professional conduct. Unlike the reasoning tests, it presents realistic workplace dilemmas - conflicts, ethical grey areas, competing priorities - and asks how you would respond. There is rarely a single objectively right answer; instead, some responses fit the values and demands of the role better than others, and the scoring reflects the judgement of experienced practitioners and occupational psychologists.

Typical characteristics include:

- **Scenario-based items.** Short, realistic dilemmas drawn from the role: a data owner resisting a standard, an ethics concern surfacing late in a delivery, a minister's office wanting data faster than governance allows.
- **Most/least effective or ranking formats.** You typically select the most effective and least effective responses from four options, or rank all options from best to worst.
- **No trick questions, but real tension.** Several options usually look reasonable; the discrimination is between good and best, or between superficially decisive and genuinely effective.
- **Untimed or generously timed.** These assessments measure your natural judgement rather than your speed.

For a lead data governance manager, situational judgement is where the role's true difficulty lives. Your reasoning skills tell you what the framework says; your judgement determines whether the organisation actually follows it. You operate almost entirely through influence: data owners outrank you in their own directorates, delivery teams control their own backlogs, and executives control the budget. The scenarios below test how you convert governance authority-on-paper into governance reality, while honouring UK public sector values: user needs first, transparency, collaboration, accessibility and inclusion, and honest handling of risk.

### What it measures for your role

Mapped to the skills in your role profile, this assessment typically measures:

- **Influencing without authority** - your **stakeholder relationship management** skill (identifying and resolving issues, influencing stakeholders, building long-term strategic relationships) is tested through scenarios where compliance cannot simply be ordered.
- **Handling difficult conversations** - your **communicating between the technical and non-technical** skill includes supporting or hosting difficult discussions with diverse senior stakeholders, and several scenarios turn on how you deliver unwelcome messages.
- **Ethical judgement under pressure** - your **data ethics and privacy** skill (identifying and addressing ethical and privacy concerns with stakeholders) is tested where delivery pressure, political pressure or convenience pull against doing the right thing.
- **Proportionate risk decisions** - your **enabling and informing risk-based decisions** skill is tested through dilemmas about escalation, tolerance and when to accept versus resolve a risk in coordination with data owners.
- **Leading culture change** - your **data literacy improvement**, **data maturity** and **advocacy** duties are tested through scenarios about building capability and buy-in rather than policing.
- **Strategic balance** - your **strategic thinking** skill appears in scenarios where a locally sensible decision would undermine the organisation-wide framework you are accountable for.

### Practice questions

For each scenario, choose the MOST effective and LEAST effective responses before reading the analysis.

#### Question 1 (moderate) - The resistant data owner

You are implementing the new data governance framework. A deputy director who owns several critical datasets tells you privately: "My teams are flat out on the minister's priority programme. Your stewardship requirements are a distraction, and I don't intend to resource them this year."

Options:

A) Escalate immediately to the Data Governance Board, noting the deputy director's refusal in the minutes.
B) Meet the deputy director to understand their delivery pressures, then propose a phased stewardship plan that protects the two or three highest-risk datasets now and defers the rest, agreeing a review point.
C) Accept the position for this year and record the datasets as temporarily out of scope of the framework.
D) Ask members of the deputy director's team to complete the stewardship tasks quietly, without requiring their director's sign-off.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D.**

**Explanation:** Option B does what a lead should do: it treats the resistance as information (a genuine resource conflict), engages with the stakeholder's reality, and converts a refusal into a risk-based, phased commitment - protecting the highest-risk assets now while preserving the relationship and the framework's credibility. It exemplifies influencing, proportionate risk management and collaboration. Option D is the least effective by a distance: working around a senior stakeholder covertly destroys trust when discovered (and it will be), undermines the ownership model your entire framework depends on - stewardship without owner accountability is precisely what your RACI work exists to prevent - and models exactly the shadow-governance behaviour you are employed to eliminate. Option A is premature: escalation is a legitimate tool, but using it before attempting resolution burns influence and teaches stakeholders that talking to you is dangerous; it may become right later, after B fails. Option C is passive: silently descoping critical datasets converts an unmanaged risk into an invisible one and would misrepresent coverage to the Board.

#### Question 2 (moderate) - The ethics concern near go-live

A delivery team is two weeks from launching a service that uses postcode-level data to prioritise inspections. A junior analyst emails you directly: "I'm worried this effectively deprioritises areas with certain demographic profiles, but my team lead says the ethics assessment was signed off months ago and we can't reopen it now."

Options:

A) Reply that the signed-off ethics assessment stands, and that reopening assessments late in delivery would set an unworkable precedent.
B) Thank the analyst, review the original ethics assessment against the concern raised, and if the concern was not considered, convene a rapid proportionate review with the team and the ethics function before launch - keeping the analyst's identity out of it unless they consent.
C) Escalate to the senior responsible owner demanding the launch be halted pending a full new ethics assessment.
D) Advise the analyst to raise the issue through their own line management chain, since going around their team lead is a matter for that directorate.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A.**

**Explanation:** Option B is the professional standard for lead-level ethics practice: it takes the concern seriously without presuming the answer, tests it against what the original assessment actually covered, respects proportionality (a rapid focused review, not an automatic halt), protects the person who spoke up, and keeps the possibility of launching on time if the concern proves already-addressed. It embodies your skill of working with stakeholders to identify and address ethical concerns, and public sector values of inclusion and transparency. Option A is least effective: it elevates process finality over the substance of a potential discriminatory impact - sign-off is evidence a question was asked, not that this question was answered - and it teaches the organisation that raising concerns is pointless, corroding the culture your role depends on. Option C is disproportionate as a first move: demanding a halt before establishing whether the concern is new or already covered spends credibility you may need, and positions ethics as a blocker rather than a partner. Option D is procedural abdication: the analyst came to the ethics route precisely because the line route had closed; redirecting them back into it looks tidy and achieves nothing.

#### Question 3 (moderate) - The impatient executive

At the executive committee, a director general says: "This governance programme has run for a year and I still can't get a straight answer on what data we hold. Why am I funding a bureaucracy?" You have made real progress - ownership coverage up from 40% to 84% - but the data catalogue's search function is poor and the DG's office has struggled to use it.

Options:

A) Defend the programme by walking the committee through your governance framework documentation and delivery milestones.
B) Acknowledge the DG's experience is a fair test the programme currently fails, give the one headline of real progress, and commit to a specific fix: a usable catalogue search and a named contact for the DG's office, reporting back in a month.
C) Explain that catalogue search is the tooling team's responsibility, not data governance's, and offer to raise it with them.
D) Agree the programme has underdelivered and offer to reduce its budget request accordingly.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C.**

**Explanation:** Option B demonstrates the communication skill your profile emphasises: communicating negative and positive information honestly, translating between the technical (ownership coverage) and the non-technical (a DG who just wants to find data), and converting criticism into a concrete, time-boxed commitment. It treats the DG's frustration as legitimate user feedback about the service your programme provides - the user-needs lens applied to governance itself. Option C is least effective: deflecting to another team may be organisationally accurate but is strategically fatal - to the executive, you are accountable for the outcome, and boundary-drawing in front of the committee reads as exactly the bureaucracy being criticised. It also violates the spirit of your role: leads own outcomes across organisational seams. Option A answers a question nobody asked; frameworks and milestones are the language that provoked the criticism. Option D overcorrects: conceding budget without analysis abandons the programme's genuine progress and your duty to advocate for better data management - honest acknowledgement does not require capitulation.

#### Question 4 (moderate) - The standards breach with a deadline

A team preparing a statutory return tells you they have discovered their submission uses a customer classification that breaches the new data standard - the one you authored. Correcting it properly would take three weeks; the statutory deadline is in one week. Submitting the non-compliant version would be materially accurate but inconsistent with the standard.

Options:

A) Insist the return is corrected to full compliance before submission, accepting the missed statutory deadline.
B) Approve submission on the existing classification, log a formal, time-bound exception against the standard with a remediation date for the next cycle, and inform the data owner and the return's recipient of the known inconsistency.
C) Approve submission and deal with the classification quietly in the next cycle, since the return is materially accurate.
D) Tell the team that as the standard's author you cannot advise them to breach it, and the decision is theirs.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D.**

**Explanation:** Option B is proportionate governance in action: it weighs a statutory obligation against standards compliance correctly (missing a statutory deadline is the greater harm here), but keeps the framework intact by making the deviation visible, time-bound and owned - a formal exception with a remediation date, with the data owner and recipient informed. That is transparency doing real work: the standard survives because deviations from it are governed, not hidden. Option D is least effective: it is abdication wearing the costume of integrity. You are the organisation's senior governance judgement; refusing to exercise it at the moment it is most needed leaves a delivery team to make a governance call without governance support, and signals that your framework functions only in easy conditions. Option A treats the standard as more important than the legal obligation it exists to support - purity at the cost of proportionality, and a misreading of risk tolerance. Option C reaches the right submission decision by the wrong route: silent non-compliance corrodes the standard (others will learn deviations are fine if unspoken) and denies the recipient information they may need.

#### Question 5 (challenging) - The data sharing request from the centre

A cross-government unit urgently requests a bulk extract of your department's case data, citing a high-profile policy priority with ministerial interest. The request arrives without a data sharing agreement, and the stated purpose is broad ("policy analysis"). Your team can produce the extract in a day. The unit's director calls you directly: "We need this by Friday. Don't let process get in the way of the mission."

Options:

A) Provide the extract by Friday, given the ministerial interest, and complete the data sharing agreement retrospectively.
B) Refuse until a full data sharing agreement is executed through the standard process, which typically takes six weeks.
C) Engage the unit that day to pin down the specific purpose and minimum data needed, provide what can be shared lawfully at speed - such as aggregated or de-identified data under an expedited agreement - and escalate in parallel to your data owner and information rights team to fast-track the remainder.
D) Ask the unit to have the minister's office direct your permanent secretary to authorise the release, so the accountability sits at the right level.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: A.**

**Explanation:** Option C shows what "urgent and lawful" looks like: it takes the mission seriously (same-day engagement, aggregated or de-identified data at pace) while applying the discipline that protects citizens - purpose specification and data minimisation - and it mobilises the right accountable people (data owner, information rights team) rather than deciding alone. It treats governance as an enabler with a fast lane, which is the strongest possible advocacy for your framework. Option A is least effective: bulk personal-level case data released without an agreed lawful basis or specified purpose is precisely how departments end up in breach, and "retrospective agreement" is not a lawful basis - ministerial interest raises the stakes of getting it right, it does not lower the bar. Option B is the second error the scenario invites: pure process refusal is defensible on paper but fails the organisation - it hands ammunition to everyone who says governance blocks delivery, when faster lawful routes exist. Option D games the accountability system: it seeks cover rather than compliance, and a permanent secretary's instruction would not cure an unlawful share anyway.

#### Question 6 (challenging) - The quality figures you doubt

Preparing the quarterly report to the Data Governance Board, you notice a directorate has reported 97% data quality compliance - up from 71% last quarter. Informally, you learn they changed their measurement method: they now sample only records created in the last month, excluding the legacy backlog. The directorate's data owner, a peer-grade colleague you work well with, asks you to "keep the good news story intact - my director needs a win".

Options:

A) Report the 97% figure as submitted; measurement methods are the directorate's own responsibility.
B) Report the figure with a clear methodology note explaining the change and its effect, tell the data owner beforehand exactly what the note will say, and propose defining a consistent measurement standard for all directorates so future comparisons are honest.
C) Replace the figure with your own recalculation including the legacy backlog, and flag the directorate's methodology change to the Board as attempted misrepresentation.
D) Ask the data owner to withdraw the figure and resubmit using the old method before the Board pack is issued.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A.**

**Explanation:** Option B holds the line on honest reporting while treating the colleague as a partner: the figure is not suppressed (recent-record quality genuinely improved), but the Board sees what it means; the owner is told before, not ambushed; and the systemic gap - no common measurement standard - is fixed so this cannot recur. That is communicating negative and positive information to stakeholders, monitoring compliance, and creating data standards, all in one move. Option A is least effective: knowingly passing a misleading comparison to your own governance board is the single most damaging act available to you here - the Board's decisions are only as good as the data you certify, and your role's entire value rests on the trustworthiness of that channel. Option C reaches honesty but poisons the well: recalculating unilaterally and framing a defensible-if-convenient methodology change as "misrepresentation" converts a fixable measurement problem into a public accusation, breaching the relationship your influence depends on. Option D quietly restores comparability but hides the underlying issue and forfeits the improvement the new method legitimately shows; it also leaves the measurement inconsistency unresolved for every other directorate.

#### Question 7 (challenging) - Ranking: the literacy programme nobody attends

Your flagship data literacy programme has 30% attendance against a 75% target. Feedback says sessions are "too basic for analysts, too abstract for operational staff". The executive sponsor asks for your plan. Rank these four responses from most to least effective:

A) Segment the programme: role-based pathways (operational, managerial, analytical) built with each community, with content drawn from their real datasets and decisions, and attendance targets reset per pathway.
B) Make attendance mandatory via performance objectives, keeping the current content.
C) Pause the programme entirely and commission a six-month training needs analysis before relaunching.
D) Keep the programme voluntary but double the communications effort promoting it.

**Most effective ranking: A, C, D, B.**

**Explanation:** A wins because it responds directly to the diagnostic the feedback hands you: one-size-fits-none. Role-based pathways using real organisational data are established good practice for data-driven culture change, and co-building with each community creates the pull that mandates cannot. C is second: it takes the evidence seriously and would eventually produce a sound design, but it stalls momentum for six months and over-solves a problem whose shape the feedback already reveals - analysis is a means here, not the plan. D is third: it mistakes an offer problem for an awareness problem; louder promotion of content people have judged irrelevant yields diminishing returns and mild cynicism, though it at least does no lasting harm. B is last: mandating attendance at training that misses every audience manufactures resentment, converts literacy - which you want to be identity-level culture - into compliance theatre, and produces attendance statistics that mask non-learning. Your data literacy improvement skill is about recommending literacy as a *valuable* skill; value must be real before it is required.

#### Question 8 (challenging) - The innovation pressure

Your chief data officer is enthusiastic about an AI-powered data cataloguing tool a vendor demonstrated, which auto-generates metadata and classifications. They want it deployed across the estate this quarter and see you as the natural sponsor. Your assessment: the tool is genuinely promising, but its auto-classifications are unvalidated on your data, and misclassification of sensitive datasets would create real exposure.

Options:

A) Sponsor the full deployment as asked; the CDO's backing is an opportunity for data innovation, and problems can be fixed in flight.
B) Decline to sponsor it, explaining that unvalidated AI classification is too risky for governance-critical metadata.
C) Propose sponsoring a bounded pilot this quarter: the tool runs across two directorates' catalogues with human review of every auto-classification, explicit accuracy thresholds for sensitive-data detection, and a go/no-go decision for estate-wide rollout based on measured results.
D) Agree to deploy, but configure the tool to run only on datasets already classified as non-sensitive, avoiding the risk area entirely.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: A.**

**Explanation:** Option C is your data innovation skill as the framework defines it: identifying promising tools *and recognising appropriate timing for adoption*. The pilot preserves the CDO's momentum (it starts this quarter), generates the validation evidence that is currently missing, and converts an enthusiasm-driven decision into an evidence-driven one with defined thresholds - the risk methodology applied in proportion to the risk. Option A is least effective: estate-wide deployment of unvalidated auto-classification puts the organisation's sensitive-data controls downstream of an unmeasured model; misclassification exposure is not a "fix in flight" problem because the harm (wrongly-classified sensitive data flowing under weaker controls) occurs silently and immediately. Option B is the mirror-image failure: it may be the safest sentence to say, but it wastes a genuine innovation opportunity, damages your relationship with the CDO, and casts governance as the department of no - the reputation that makes future influence harder. Option D looks clever but quietly guts the value case (auto-classification matters most where classification is hardest) while still deploying an unvalidated tool, and "already classified as non-sensitive" assumes the very classifications the tool was meant to improve.

#### Question 9 (challenging) - The steward burnout signal

One of your best data stewards - who supports three directorates single-handedly - tells you they are exhausted and considering leaving. Their directorates all rate stewardship support as excellent. Your recruitment request for two more stewards was rejected last quarter as "unaffordable".

Options:

A) Persuade the steward to stay by emphasising how critical they are and promising the resourcing picture will improve.
B) Rebalance the steward's workload immediately by pausing lowest-risk stewardship activities, document the single-point-of-failure risk in the organisational risk register with the service impact of their departure quantified, and use that risk to reopen the resourcing case with specific evidence.
C) Accept the situation; budgets are set, and the steward's departure would at least prove the resourcing point.
D) Quietly redistribute some of the steward's tasks to stewards in other areas who are also fully loaded.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C.**

**Explanation:** Option B operates on all three required levels at once: human (immediate, real load reduction - not promises), governance (a single point of failure across three directorates is an organisational data risk, and your role level is explicitly responsible for understanding and managing organisational data risks), and strategic (a rejected business case reopened with quantified risk evidence is a different case; "unaffordable" was a judgement about a cost, and B re-frames it as a judgement about an exposure). Option C is least effective: consciously spending a person's wellbeing to strengthen a budget argument is an ethical failure first and a leadership failure second - and operationally reckless, since the "proof" arrives only after the capability is lost and three directorates' governance degrades. Option A is the road to C: retention-by-flattery without load change typically buys weeks, and the promise it rests on is one you cannot currently keep. Option D just relocates the burnout while hiding the capacity problem from the people who could fund its solution - the same invisibility error, one level down.

#### Question 10 (challenging) - The framework you inherited

Three months into a new post, you conclude that the data governance framework you inherited is over-engineered: 40 mandatory artefacts per dataset, widely ignored in practice, with directorates maintaining paperwork compliance that bears little relation to actual data handling. Your predecessor, who designed it, is now a senior figure in another part of the organisation and sits on the Data Governance Board.

Options:

A) Implement the framework as designed with renewed enforcement energy; it was approved by the Board and consistency matters more than elegance.
B) Redesign the framework unilaterally and present the Board with the finished replacement.
C) Bring the Board evidence of the compliance-reality gap - artefact completion versus actual practice - propose a risk-based simplification with a target of the vital few artefacts, and involve your predecessor early as a source of design history rather than positioning them as the author of a failure.
D) Leave the framework formally intact but signal informally to directorates which artefacts you actually care about.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: D.**

**Explanation:** Option C leads the "formation, implementation and management of data governance frameworks" the way your role level describes: with evidence (the paperwork-versus-practice gap is measurable and unarguable), with proportionality (vital few artefacts, risk-based), through legitimate authority (the Board that owns the framework), and with the political intelligence to convert the predecessor from a threatened author into a consulted expert - stakeholder relationship management applied inside the governance system itself. Option D is least effective, and it is worth seeing why it beats even A's rigidity to the bottom: a framework that is formally mandatory but informally optional is the worst of all governance states - it destroys the meaning of "mandatory" across everything you govern, creates unequal treatment (directorates in the know versus those diligently completing 40 artefacts), and is indefensible at audit. Option A enforces waste: energy spent making people comply with artefacts that do not change data handling is energy taken from real risk, and enforcement will harden the cynicism the gap already reveals. Option B might produce a better design but arrives at the Board as an ambush - and Board members surprised in public, including your predecessor, defend positions rather than evaluate evidence.

### Preparation tips

- **Anchor on the role's operating reality.** Before the assessment, re-read your role description: lead-level governance works through influence, evidence and proportionality. Options that order, hide, or abdicate almost always score poorly; options that engage, make risk visible, and preserve relationships while holding the line almost always score well.
- **Look for the option that does two things at once.** Strong lead-level responses typically solve the immediate problem *and* fix the system that produced it (a methodology note *plus* a measurement standard; a workload fix *plus* a risk register entry). Practise spotting the dual-action option.
- **Check every option for hidden costs.** Ask of each: what does this do to trust? To the framework's credibility? To the next person who raises a concern? Options that look efficient often spend one of these invisibly.
- **Distinguish escalation from abdication.** Escalating with evidence after attempting resolution is leadership; escalating first, or pushing decisions to others without your judgement attached, is not. Assessors at your level test this line repeatedly.
- **Rehearse the values.** UK public sector scenarios reward user needs, transparency about problems, collaboration across boundaries, accessibility and inclusion, and honest treatment of risk. When two options seem close, the one that keeps information visible to accountable people usually wins.
- **Answer as the role, not as a hero.** The best response is rarely the most dramatic. Halting launches, refusing outright, and grand confrontations are usually the second-best or worst options; calibrated, proportionate moves win.

### Common pitfalls

- **Choosing the idealistic option over the effective one.** "Refuse until every process is complete" sounds principled but often fails the organisation when a lawful faster route exists (Question 5). Effectiveness includes finding that route.
- **Selecting passive or deferring options.** Responses that push the problem to line management, another team, or "the next cycle" score poorly - they are the classic weak choices in situational judgement design (Questions 2 and 4).
- **Ignoring the relationship dimension.** Options that reach the right technical outcome while ambushing, exposing or working around a stakeholder (unilateral recalculations, covert workarounds) are scored down heavily at lead level, where influence is the working capital (Questions 1 and 6).
- **Treating rules as ends rather than means.** Enforcing a standard into a missed statutory deadline, or a framework into performative paperwork, mistakes the instrument for the purpose (Questions 4 and 10).
- **Making the invisible-risk trade.** Several weak options share one signature: they make a problem less visible (silent descoping, quiet workload shuffles, informal signals about which rules count). Governance leadership runs in exactly the opposite direction.
- **Forgetting proportionality.** Both over-reaction (halt everything, full reassessment) and under-reaction (sign-off stands, methods are their business) lose marks; the scored best answers consistently match the response to the size and reversibility of the risk.

## Conclusion

You have now worked through the four assessment types most relevant to your role as a lead data governance manager: cognitive reasoning with the frameworks, catalogues and risk registers you manage; numeric reasoning with the compliance metrics, budgets and business cases you defend; verbal reasoning with the policies, audit findings and stakeholder correspondence you interpret; and situational judgement across the influence, ethics and leadership dilemmas that define the lead level.

Two themes run through everything you have practised. The second is proportionality: matching remediation to trajectory rather than snapshot, escalation to evidence, and governance effort to risk. These are not test-taking tricks - they are the professional substance of your role, compressed into assessable form. That is exactly why practice here transfers back into your working week: every worked explanation you compared against your own reasoning is also a rehearsal for your next board paper, audit response or difficult stakeholder conversation.
