# Digital Evaluator (Head of Digital Evaluation) - Psychometric Assessment Resources

## Introduction

Welcome. This document is a practical, encouraging companion for you as a head of digital evaluation preparing for psychometric assessments, whether you are applying for a new post, taking part in a development centre, or simply sharpening the professional judgement you rely on every day.

Psychometric assessments are structured, standardised exercises that measure how you think, reason, and decide. Employers across the UK public sector use them because they are fair, evidence-based predictors of performance: they compare every candidate against the same benchmark, reduce the influence of unconscious bias, and reveal capabilities that interviews alone can miss. For a leadership role such as yours, they are also a mirror. The same faculties they test — spotting flaws in a theory of change, interpreting an evaluation dataset under time pressure, extracting the precise meaning of Magenta Book guidance, and choosing the wisest course of action when a minister's office wants findings before quality assurance is complete — are the faculties you exercise daily as the leader of your organisation's digital evaluation community.

This document is organised into four assessment areas, each tailored specifically to your role:

1. **Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment** — pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, and prioritisation using the artefacts you genuinely handle: theories of change, evaluation plans, quality assurance frameworks, and logic models.
2. **Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment** — interpreting evaluation budgets, effect sizes, response rates, benefit-cost ratios, and performance indicators, with full worked arithmetic.
3. **Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment** — dense passages drawn from evaluation strategies, government guidance, data ethics frameworks, and stakeholder correspondence, followed by True / False / Cannot Say and comprehension questions.
4. **Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment** — realistic leadership dilemmas involving delivery teams, policy colleagues, senior stakeholders, analytical professions, and the evaluators you lead.

Each section explains what the assessment measures, maps it explicitly to the skills in your capability framework, offers substantial practice questions with worked answers, and closes with preparation tips and common pitfalls.

How should you use it? Three ways. First, as practice: attempt every question under gentle time pressure before reading the explanation. Second, as self-reflection: where an explanation surprises you, ask what that reveals about your habits of reasoning. Third, as preparation: revisit the tips shortly before any real assessment so the formats feel familiar rather than foreign.

You already operate at the head-of-profession level. These exercises will not teach you your job — they will help you demonstrate, calmly and confidently, the judgement you have spent a career building. Take your time, enjoy the challenge, and let us begin.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive ability assessment measures the exact mental processes your role demands, using practical scenarios rather than abstract puzzles. Unlike a generic intelligence test, every question here mimics the everyday artefacts and decisions of a head of digital evaluation: logic models with missing links, evaluation plans containing internal contradictions, quality assurance registers that need error checking, and competing demands on your community's limited capacity that must be prioritised on defensible grounds.

Typical delivery is online and timed — commonly 15 to 30 minutes for 20 to 30 questions — with objective scoring against a norm group of comparable professionals. For senior appointments in government, the assessment usually forms one strand of a wider process alongside interviews and, sometimes, staff engagement exercises. Modern platforms may adapt question difficulty to your answers and report speed and accuracy separately, so a considered, accurate performance is valued as much as a fast one.

Why does it matter for your role? Because cognitive rigour is the engine of everything you do. When you assure the evaluation work of digital evaluator teams, you are error checking. When you scrutinise a theory of change, you are testing logical deduction: do the activities really lead to the outputs, do the outputs plausibly generate the outcomes, and what unintended effects has the team missed? When you act as an escalation point and remove blockers, you are prioritising under uncertainty. When you set the digital evaluation strategy, you are recognising patterns across a portfolio of products and services and abstracting them into a coherent direction. This assessment simply isolates those processes and measures them cleanly.

### What it measures for your role

The assessment dimensions map directly onto the skills in your role summary:

- **Logical deduction → Product and service monitoring and Evaluation delivery.** You provide guidance on developing theories of change and define best practice methodologies. Deduction questions test whether you can trace causal chains, spot non sequiturs, and identify assumptions that break a logic model.
- **Error checking → Quality assurance of data and analysis.** You set up tools and systems for assuring data and analysis across multiple teams, and you verify and validate analysis. Error-checking questions present registers, tables, and specifications containing deliberate inconsistencies for you to find quickly.
- **Prioritisation and problem solving → Evaluation planning and strategy and your head-of-level duties.** You remove blockers, act as an escalation point, and manage the organisation's digital evaluation capability through business planning. Prioritisation questions give you more demands than capacity and ask which allocation best serves quality standards and strategic goals.
- **Pattern recognition → Monitoring and evaluation across the product life cycle.** You identify improvements to current approaches and raise evaluation or product risks at any life cycle stage. Pattern questions ask you to detect trends, anomalies, and structural similarities across evaluation portfolios.
- **Abstract and strategic reasoning → Communicating analysis and insight.** Making complex recommendations at tactical and strategic level requires you to move fluently between the detail of a finding and the shape of the argument it supports.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Logic model sequencing.** A delivery team drafts a theory of change for a new digital licensing service with these elements, presented out of order: (1) fewer incomplete applications are submitted; (2) applicants receive real-time validation messages while completing the online form; (3) build inline validation into the application form; (4) caseworker time spent chasing missing information falls, reducing average decision time from 20 days to 12 days; (5) applicants trust the service more and channel shift from paper increases. Which ordering correctly runs activity → output → intermediate outcome → outcome → longer-term outcome?

- A. 3, 2, 1, 4, 5
- B. 2, 3, 1, 5, 4
- C. 3, 1, 2, 4, 5
- D. 3, 2, 4, 1, 5

**Correct answer: A.** The activity is the thing the team does (3, build validation). The output is what the activity directly produces for users (2, real-time messages). The intermediate outcome is the first behavioural change (1, fewer incomplete applications). That drives the operational outcome (4, faster decisions), which in turn supports the longer-term outcome (5, trust and channel shift). Options C and D break the chain by placing a behavioural change before the mechanism that causes it; option B begins with an output before any activity exists to generate it. As the person who provides guidance on developing theories of change, you would coach a team through exactly this sequencing discipline.

**Question 2 (easy) — Error checking a QA register.** Your quality assurance framework requires every published evaluation to record: peer reviewer named, data sources logged, code review completed, and limitations section present. You scan four entries in the assurance register:

- Report Alpha: reviewer J. Achebe; 3 data sources; code review "complete"; limitations section "present".
- Report Beta: reviewer "TBC"; 5 data sources; code review "complete"; limitations section "present".
- Report Gamma: reviewer M. Osei; 2 data sources; code review "complete"; limitations section "present".
- Report Delta: reviewer L. Fraser; 4 data sources; code review "n/a — qualitative study"; limitations section "present".

Which report clearly breaches the framework as written?

- A. Alpha
- B. Beta
- C. Gamma
- D. Delta

**Correct answer: B.** "TBC" means no peer reviewer is named, which directly breaches a mandatory field. Delta is the tempting distractor: "n/a" for code review looks like a gap, but a qualitative study may legitimately have no code to review — that is a judgement call for your framework to clarify, not an unambiguous breach of the register as written. Error checking at your level means distinguishing definite non-compliance from items needing a policy ruling, and then improving the framework so the ambiguity cannot recur.

**Question 3 (moderate) — Logical deduction from assurance rules.** Your organisation's evaluation standards state: "Every impact evaluation must include a counterfactual. Every evaluation with a counterfactual must be peer reviewed by an analyst outside the commissioning team. Process evaluations are not required to include a counterfactual." The passport renewal team has completed an evaluation that was peer reviewed by an analyst inside the commissioning team. Which conclusion follows logically?

- A. The evaluation is a process evaluation.
- B. The evaluation did not include a counterfactual, or it breaches the standards.
- C. The evaluation breaches the standards.
- D. The evaluation is not an impact evaluation.

**Correct answer: B.** Work the chain backwards. If the evaluation included a counterfactual, it needed external peer review; it got internal review only, so it would breach the standards. If it lacked a counterfactual, the external-review rule never triggers — but then it cannot be a compliant impact evaluation, though it might be a compliant process evaluation. So the only certainty is the disjunction in B: either no counterfactual, or a breach. Options A, C, and D each assert one branch of that disjunction as fact, which the premises do not support. This is precisely the reasoning you use when assuring evaluation work: identifying what a set of facts proves, versus what it merely suggests.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Pattern recognition across a portfolio.** As head of digital evaluation you review the year's completed evaluations across your organisation's portfolio. You notice: the benefits calculator evaluation underestimated uptake because it ignored word-of-mouth referral; the appointment booking evaluation underestimated demand because it ignored referrals from charities; and the grants portal evaluation underestimated volume because it ignored signposting from local authorities. What is the generalisable pattern you should feed into best practice, and what improvement does it imply?

- A. Teams overestimate demand; guidance should mandate conservative forecasting.
- B. Teams systematically omit indirect and third-party referral routes from their theories of change; guidance should require mapping all entry channels, including intermediaries, at the planning stage.
- C. Teams lack quantitative skills; recruitment should prioritise statisticians.
- D. The three services are unusually popular; no general lesson exists.

**Correct answer: B.** Each case shares the same structure: an unmodelled indirect entry route causing underestimation. Abstracting from three instances to a structural cause — and then converting it into a reusable planning requirement — is exactly what "lead and champion best practice, and identify improvements to current approaches" means in your skill set. Option A misreads the direction of every error (they underestimated). Option C invents a cause the evidence does not show; the omissions were conceptual, not computational. Option D refuses the inductive step that leadership requires.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Prioritisation under capacity constraint.** Your community has capacity for exactly two major assurance reviews this quarter. Four requests arrive: (i) a politically high-profile evaluation publishing in five weeks with known methodological disputes; (ii) a routine process evaluation publishing next quarter; (iii) an impact evaluation whose findings will decide whether a £40 million programme is scaled, publishing in eight weeks; (iv) a retrospective review of a service that was decommissioned last year. Which pair should you prioritise?

- A. (i) and (ii)
- B. (i) and (iii)
- C. (iii) and (iv)
- D. (ii) and (iv)

**Correct answer: B.** Prioritise by consequence and urgency together. Request (i) combines imminent publication with known methodological risk and political exposure — assurance failure there damages the credibility of the whole evaluation function you lead. Request (iii) carries the largest decision consequence: £40 million of public money turns on findings that must therefore be sound. Request (ii) can be scheduled next quarter, when it publishes. Request (iv) has historical value but no live decision or deadline depends on it. Note the reasoning is not "loudest stakeholder wins"; it is a defensible framework of risk, consequence, and timing — the kind you would articulate when influencing senior stakeholders about where assurance effort goes.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Spotting the flawed inference.** A delivery team's report states: "Following the redesign of the appeals service, user satisfaction rose from 61% to 79%. The redesign therefore caused an 18 percentage point improvement in satisfaction." As the assurer, which single weakness most undermines the causal claim?

- A. Percentages should never be subtracted.
- B. The before-and-after comparison has no counterfactual, so other concurrent changes (seasonality, case mix, a media story, survey method changes) could explain some or all of the rise.
- C. Satisfaction is not worth measuring.
- D. The sample sizes are not stated, so the result is certainly wrong.

**Correct answer: B.** The claim leaps from correlation across time to causation without any control for what would have happened anyway — the core weakness a counterfactual addresses. Option A is false; subtracting percentage points is legitimate arithmetic (though the report should say "percentage points", a wording refinement you might also note). Option C is dismissive and wrong. Option D overreaches: missing sample sizes weaken confidence but do not make a result "certainly wrong". Precise diagnosis matters because your coaching is only as credible as the accuracy of your critique.

**Question 7 (hard) — Deduction with interacting rules.** Your data ethics framework contains three rules: (R1) Any evaluation using linked administrative data must complete an ethics review before data access. (R2) Any ethics review involving children's data must go to the full ethics board, not the fast-track panel. (R3) Fast-track decisions take two weeks; full board decisions take six weeks, and the board meets only in the first week of each month. On 1 September, a team asks to start analysis on 1 November for an evaluation linking school attendance records with a digital tutoring service's usage logs. Can they meet their date, and what is the binding constraint?

- A. Yes, comfortably — fast-track takes two weeks.
- B. Yes, but only if the full board reviews the case at its early-September or early-October meeting, because children's data mandates the full board and its six-week decision means only the September meeting guarantees a decision before 1 November, making the September board the binding constraint.
- C. No — six weeks from 1 November is mid-December.
- D. Yes — ethics review can run in parallel with analysis.

**Correct answer: B.** Chain the rules: linked administrative data triggers review (R1); school attendance records are children's data, so the full board is mandatory (R2), eliminating option A. The board meets the first week of each month and takes six weeks to decide (R3). A first-week-of-September review concludes around mid-October — in time. A first-week-of-October review concludes around mid-November — too late. So the team can start on 1 November only by making the September board, which is the binding constraint and requires immediate action in the very week they asked. Option D violates R1, which requires review before data access. This is the multi-rule scheduling deduction you perform whenever you remove blockers: finding the one step that actually determines whether a team's date survives.

**Question 8 (hard) — Error checking interlocking figures.** An evaluation plan you are assuring states: "We will survey 1,200 users, expecting a 40% response rate, giving about 480 responses. We will interview 5% of respondents, approximately 30 interviews. Fieldwork costs £25 per survey response and £150 per interview, so the fieldwork budget is £15,600." Which element is internally inconsistent?

- A. The 480 responses figure.
- B. The 30 interviews figure.
- C. The £15,600 budget figure.
- D. All figures are consistent.

**Correct answer: B.** Check each: 1,200 × 40% = 480 responses — consistent. 5% of 480 = 24 interviews, not "approximately 30" — inconsistent; 30 would be 6.25% of respondents. The budget: 480 × £25 = £12,000; using the plan's own 30 interviews, 30 × £150 = £4,500, totalling £16,500, and using the corrected 24 interviews, 24 × £150 = £3,600, totalling £15,600 — so the budget line is consistent with the correct interview number, which confirms the "30" is the typo. Notice the elegant diagnostic: the budget silently used 24, revealing which figure the authors actually intended. Reconciling documents against themselves like this is the heart of verifying and validating analysis.

**Question 9 (hard) — Prioritisation with strategic trade-offs.** You are finalising next year's business plan for the digital evaluation capability. You can fund three of the following five initiatives: (i) a coaching programme teaching delivery teams to build their own theories of change; (ii) a central tool that automates quality assurance checks on analytical code across all teams; (iii) a high-visibility summative evaluation of the department's flagship digital service; (iv) a data ethics training refresh for the evaluation community; (v) a conference stand to advocate for digital evaluation across government. Your strategy's stated priorities are, in order: raise quality, build capability, grow influence. Which three best fit the strategy?

- A. (iii), (v), (i)
- B. (ii), (i), (iv)
- C. (ii), (i), (v)
- D. (i), (iii), (iv)

**Correct answer: C.** Map each initiative to the strategy's ordered priorities. Raise quality: (ii) is the strongest quality lever because it scales assurance across every team rather than one product — it directly operationalises "set up tools and systems for effectively assuring data and analysis across multiple teams". Build capability: (i) is the clearest capability multiplier, embedding theory-of-change skill in delivery teams so quality rises at source. Grow influence: (v) serves the advocacy duty explicitly. Option B is defensible but doubles up on internal capability ((i) and (iv) overlap as community development) while leaving the influence priority unserved. Options A and D spend a scarce slot on (iii), a single evaluation — valuable, but a delivery output rather than a strategic investment in the function, and one that could be resourced through normal portfolio channels. Strategy questions reward reading the priority ordering as a decision rule, not a mood.

**Question 10 (hard) — Detecting a subtle metric artefact.** A dashboard shows that after a service moved from monthly to weekly release cycles, the "defects per release" metric fell from 12 to 4, and the product manager claims quality has tripled. Weekly releases are roughly a quarter the size of monthly ones. What should you conclude?

- A. Quality tripled, as claimed.
- B. Normalising by release size, defects per unit of change went from 12 per month-sized release to approximately 16 (4 × 4) per equivalent volume — so defect density may actually have worsened, and the metric needs re-basing before any quality claim is made.
- C. Weekly releases are always better.
- D. The metric is fine because fewer defects is always good.

**Correct answer: B.** The denominator changed. Four defects in a release a quarter of the size is equivalent to about 16 defects per month-sized volume of change, versus 12 before — a possible worsening masked by re-slicing. The honest conclusion is that the metric is no longer comparable across the boundary and must be normalised (for example, defects per hundred story points or per thousand lines changed). This is a classic pattern you champion under "define best practice and methodologies for measuring indicators... and communicating limitations": whenever a process changes shape, every rate metric that spans the change must be re-based. Options A, C, and D all accept the surface number uncritically.

**Question 11 (moderate) — Life cycle risk identification.** A digital service is about to move from public beta to live. You review its evaluation status: user research is extensive; a process evaluation ran during beta; baseline data for the key outcome (reduced fraud) was never collected before beta launched; the monitoring dashboard tracks uptake and satisfaction but not fraud outcomes. What is the most important evaluation risk to raise at the live assessment?

- A. Satisfaction may dip after launch.
- B. Without a pre-launch baseline and outcome monitoring, the service will never be able to demonstrate its impact on fraud — the core benefit in its business case — so an urgent decision is needed on constructing a retrospective baseline or comparison group while that is still possible.
- C. The dashboard has too many metrics.
- D. Process evaluations are unnecessary.

**Correct answer: B.** The service's central claim — fraud reduction — is currently unmeasurable, and each month that passes makes retrospective baseline construction harder as the pre-intervention period recedes and data retention limits bite. Raising this now, at a life cycle gate where the team must respond, is exactly "identify and raise evaluation or product risks during any stage of the product life cycle". Option A is a minor, monitorable issue. Options C and D misdescribe the situation. The skill being tested is severity ranking: many things are imperfect, but only one gap destroys the ability to ever answer the business-critical question.

**Question 12 (hard) — Deductive reasoning about missing evidence.** Three teams submit annual evaluation summaries. Team X reports on all five of its committed indicators. Team Y reports on four of five, omitting its economic outcome indicator, and notes "data supplier delay". Team Z reports on three of five, omitting both indicators that showed decline at mid-year, with no explanation. Applying the principle that omissions correlated with unfavourable results are a stronger assurance concern than omissions with documented external causes, which team warrants the most urgent assurance attention, and why?

- A. Team X, because complete reporting may hide manipulation.
- B. Team Y, because economic outcomes matter most.
- C. Team Z, because the pattern of omissions — precisely the two declining indicators, unexplained — is consistent with selective reporting, which threatens the integrity of the evidence base itself.
- D. All three equally.

**Correct answer: C.** Team Z's omissions are not random: they select exactly the unfavourable indicators and offer no cause. That pattern is the signature of selective reporting — the single most corrosive threat to an evaluation function's credibility, because it converts evaluation from evidence into advocacy. Team Y's omission has a documented, checkable external cause; you would verify the supplier delay, but it is second order. Option A inverts the principle: completeness is not itself suspicious. Option D refuses to prioritise, which an escalation point cannot afford to do. In your assurance role you would follow up with Team Z supportively but firmly — the reasoning here is deductive; the handling of it is leadership.

**Question 13 (moderate) — Problem solving a coaching bottleneck.** You personally coach teams on evaluation techniques, but demand has grown to twelve teams and you have time for three. Coaching quality drops sharply when a session exceeds eight participants. Lead evaluators (you have four) can each coach two teams to a good standard after a short train-the-trainer investment from you. What allocation solves the bottleneck this quarter while protecting quality?

- A. Coach all twelve teams yourself in one large group.
- B. Coach three teams yourself and tell the other nine to wait.
- C. Invest your time in training the four lead evaluators (who then coach eight teams), coach the three most complex or highest-risk teams yourself, and schedule the one remaining team for next quarter.
- D. Send all teams a written guide instead of coaching.

**Correct answer: C.** This is a multiplication problem disguised as an allocation problem. Using your scarce time to create coaches yields 4 × 2 = 8 coached teams plus your own 3 = 11 of 12 this quarter, with only one deferred — and it builds durable capability in your leads, serving "coach people across the organisation" at scale. Option A violates the stated quality constraint (twelve people far exceeds eight). Option B leaves 75% of demand unmet when a better option exists. Option D substitutes a weaker intervention for all rather than for the margin. Heads of profession repeatedly face this structure: the highest-leverage use of expert time is usually creating more experts.

### Preparation tips

- **Rehearse with your own artefacts.** Take a real theory of change, evaluation plan, or QA register from your organisation (respecting confidentiality) and deliberately hunt for the weakest causal link, the internal inconsistency, or the missing mandatory field. Ten minutes a day of this converts your professional instinct into fast, examinable technique.
- **Verbalise your decision rules.** For prioritisation items, practise stating the rule before choosing: "consequence times urgency, tie-break on reversibility". Assessments reward consistent frameworks over case-by-case intuition, and so do senior selection panels.
- **Check arithmetic relationships, not just values.** As Question 8 showed, documents often betray their own errors when two figures imply a third. Train yourself to compute the implied figure quickly.
- **Manage the clock like a portfolio.** Answer the questions you find quick first; flag and return to the multi-rule deductions. A stuck three minutes on one item is three easier items lost.
- **Stay calm about difficulty spikes.** Adaptive tests get harder because you are doing well. Treat a brutal question as a compliment, not a crisis.
- **Sleep and setting matter.** Do the test somewhere quiet, on a reliable connection, at your best time of day. Senior candidates often squeeze assessments between meetings; give it the same protected time you would give a board paper.

### Common pitfalls

- **Answering from experience instead of the stimulus.** You have deep evaluation knowledge, and it will tempt you to conclude what is usually true rather than what the given rules and data actually prove. Questions 3 and 12 are only answerable from the stated premises. Discipline yourself to the page.
- **Confusing the plausible with the proven.** Distractor options are frequently sensible-sounding professional judgements that simply do not follow logically. Ask "does this follow?", not "would this be reasonable advice?".
- **Missing changed denominators.** Rate and ratio artefacts (Question 10) are the most common trap in role-specific cognitive items for analytical leaders, because they exploit the exact dashboards you read daily.
- **Over-flagging.** In error-checking items, marking ambiguous entries (like Delta in Question 2) as breaches costs marks. The tested skill is precision: definite breach versus judgement call.
- **Perfectionism under time pressure.** At head-of level you are used to polishing. In a timed test, a good answer now beats a perfect answer never — exactly the proportionality principle you apply to evaluation itself.
- **Ignoring the scoring model.** If the platform reports speed and accuracy separately, wild guessing damages the accuracy profile. Prefer reasoned elimination; guess only when the clock forces it.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment evaluates how well you analyse, interpret, and make sound decisions from numerical data presented in the formats your role really uses. It is emphatically not a test of abstract mathematics: the arithmetic rarely goes beyond percentages, ratios, weighted averages, and rates. What it tests is whether you can extract the right numbers from a table, transform them correctly, and — crucially at your level — judge what they do and do not support.

Expect real-world inputs: evaluation budgets, survey response tables, benefit-cost calculations, uptake dashboards, capacity plans, and performance indicator time series. Most platforms allow or provide an on-screen calculator, prioritising interpretation over mental arithmetic, but impose strict time limits — often around a minute to ninety seconds per question — so quick, accurate data extraction matters. Scores are normed against comparable professional groups.

For a head of digital evaluation, numeric fluency is a leadership credential, not a technicality. You influence senior stakeholders to secure resources — which means constructing and defending budgets and benefit-cost arguments in numbers. You define best practice for measuring indicators, including economic outcomes, and for communicating their limitations — which means you must personally model impeccable quantitative hygiene. You assure the data and analysis of multiple teams — which means catching numerical errors that survived the teams' own checks. And when you publish findings, the credibility of the entire digital evaluation community rests on every figure in the report being right. The assessment distils these demands into clean, checkable exercises.

### What it measures for your role

- **Interpreting performance metrics and indicators → Product and service monitoring.** You define methodologies for measuring indicators, including economic outcomes. Questions use uptake rates, completion rates, unit costs, and trend data from digital services.
- **Budgets, resourcing, and capacity arithmetic → Evaluation planning and strategy and your business-planning duty.** You manage the capability through business planning, development, and recruitment. Questions cover evaluation project budgets, day rates, team capacity, and recruitment costs.
- **Verifying and validating figures → Quality assurance of data and analysis.** You use appropriate approaches for verifying and validating data. Questions ask you to reconcile totals, check percentages, and spot implausible values.
- **Quantitative research design arithmetic → Evaluation delivery.** You provide guidance on evaluation techniques, so sample sizes, response rates, confidence in subgroup estimates, and effect sizes appear throughout.
- **Presenting numbers honestly → Communicating analysis and insight and Data ethics and privacy.** Several questions test whether a numerical claim is a fair representation of the data — the quantitative face of ethical communication.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Response rates.** Your community surveys users of a digital benefits calculator. Of 8,500 invitations, 2,210 complete responses are received. What is the response rate?

- A. 22%
- B. 26%
- C. 30%
- D. 34%

**Correct answer: B.** Response rate = responses ÷ invitations = 2,210 ÷ 8,500 = 0.26, so 26%. A quick sanity check: 25% of 8,500 would be 2,125, and 2,210 is slightly more, so "just over a quarter" confirms 26%. Building the habit of a one-line sanity check after every calculation is the same verification discipline you coach into teams.

**Question 2 (easy) — Percentage change in an indicator.** A digital licensing service's median transaction time falls from 14 minutes to 10.5 minutes after a redesign. What is the percentage reduction?

- A. 20%
- B. 25%
- C. 30%
- D. 35%

**Correct answer: B.** Reduction = (14 − 10.5) ÷ 14 = 3.5 ÷ 14 = 0.25 = 25%. The classic error is dividing by the new value (3.5 ÷ 10.5 ≈ 33%), which overstates improvement — exactly the kind of flattering slip your assurance systems exist to catch before publication. Percentage change is always relative to the starting value unless explicitly stated otherwise.

**Question 3 (moderate) — Evaluation budget composition.** An impact evaluation budget totals £240,000: staff costs are 55%, data acquisition 20%, fieldwork 15%, and dissemination the remainder. The dissemination line must fund a published report (£9,000) plus stakeholder events. How much remains for stakeholder events?

- A. £12,000
- B. £15,000
- C. £24,000
- D. £33,000

**Correct answer: B.** Dissemination share = 100% − 55% − 20% − 15% = 10%. So dissemination = 0.10 × £240,000 = £24,000. Subtract the report: £24,000 − £9,000 = £15,000. Two-step questions like this reward writing the intermediate figure down; the distractor £24,000 is there for candidates who stop a step early, and £33,000 for those who add instead of subtracting the report cost.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Weighted average across services.** You aggregate user satisfaction across three digital services for a strategy paper: Service A, 82% satisfied from 12,000 respondents; Service B, 74% from 6,000; Service C, 90% from 2,000. What is the overall satisfaction rate across all respondents?

- A. 79.8%
- B. 82.0%
- C. 80.4%
- D. 84.7%

**Correct answer: C.** Weight by respondents: satisfied users = (0.82 × 12,000) + (0.74 × 6,000) + (0.90 × 2,000) = 9,840 + 4,440 + 1,800 = 16,080. Total respondents = 20,000. Overall rate = 16,080 ÷ 20,000 = 80.4%. The unweighted mean of the three percentages, (82 + 74 + 90) ÷ 3 = 82.0%, is the trap in option B. When you define best practice for aggregating indicators across a portfolio, insisting on denominators is rule one: an average of averages is only valid when the groups are the same size.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Benefit-cost ratio for a resourcing case.** You are influencing senior stakeholders to fund an evaluation-informed improvement programme. The programme costs £1.8 million over two years. Evaluation evidence projects annual savings of £1.35 million once implemented, starting in year two, with year-two savings at only 60% of the full rate. What is the simple benefit-cost ratio over the first three years (ignoring discounting)?

- A. 0.75
- B. 1.20
- C. 1.50
- D. 2.25

**Correct answer: B.** Benefits: year one £0; year two 0.60 × £1.35m = £0.81m; year three £1.35m. Total benefits = £2.16m. Costs = £1.8m. BCR = 2.16 ÷ 1.8 = 1.20. Options C and D come from crediting full savings too early. In a real submission you would add discounting and sensitivity ranges in line with government appraisal guidance, and you would say so — communicating limitations is part of the skill — but the assessment layer tests whether the core ratio is built from the correctly phased flows.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Reconciling a data table.** A team's monitoring return reports quarterly digital uptake for a service:

| Quarter | Digital applications | Paper applications | Digital share |
|---------|---------------------|--------------------|---------------|
| Q1      | 18,400              | 9,600              | 65.7%         |
| Q2      | 21,150              | 8,850              | 70.5%         |
| Q3      | 23,400              | 7,800              | 75.0%         |
| Q4      | 26,000              | 6,500              | 78.0%         |

You verify the digital share column. Which quarter's share is misreported?

- A. Q1
- B. Q2
- C. Q3
- D. Q4

**Correct answer: D.** Verify every row rather than stopping at the first plausible suspect. Q1: total = 18,400 + 9,600 = 28,000; 18,400 ÷ 28,000 = 65.7% — correct. Q2: total = 30,000; 21,150 ÷ 30,000 = 70.5% — correct. Q3: total = 31,200; 23,400 ÷ 31,200 = 75.0% — correct. Q4: total = 32,500; 26,000 ÷ 32,500 = 80.0%, not the printed 78.0% — misreported, and understated. The systematic row-by-row check mirrors real assurance practice: distractors in these items are engineered so that a hasty checker who eyeballs "biggest numbers" or trusts the first row that feels wrong picks a wrong option. Row-by-row verification is slower by twenty seconds and safer by a full mark — the same trade you make when signing off a publication.

**Question 7 (hard) — Sample size and subgroup precision.** An evaluation plan proposes a survey of 1,600 users, with results reported for four nations. Expected respondent distribution: England 1,120, Scotland 240, Wales 160, Northern Ireland 80. The margin of error at 95% confidence is roughly 1 ÷ √n. Approximately what is the margin of error for the Northern Ireland estimate, and what should your guidance say?

- A. About ±2.5%; subgroup reporting is fine.
- B. About ±11%; the plan should either boost the Northern Ireland sample, combine reporting periods, or clearly flag the estimate as indicative only.
- C. About ±5%; identical to the whole sample.
- D. Margins of error do not apply to subgroups.

**Correct answer: B.** For n = 80: √80 ≈ 8.94, so margin ≈ 1 ÷ 8.94 ≈ 0.112, roughly ±11 percentage points. A satisfaction estimate of, say, 70% for Northern Ireland could plausibly be anywhere from about 59% to 81% — far too wide to support national comparisons without caveats. The whole-sample margin is 1 ÷ √1,600 = 1 ÷ 40 = 2.5% (option A's figure, valid only for the total). Your guidance role — "provide guidance on evaluation techniques" and "communicating limitations" — is precisely to catch designs that promise subgroup findings their sample cannot honestly deliver, and to offer the three standard remedies: boost, pool, or caveat.

**Question 8 (hard) — Unit cost with phased volumes.** A digital service's running cost is £4.2 million per year, fixed. Transaction volumes were 1.2 million in year one and are forecast to grow 25% in year two and a further 20% in year three. What is the forecast cost per transaction in year three, to the nearest penny?

- A. £2.33
- B. £2.80
- C. £2.92
- D. £3.50

**Correct answer: A.** Year-two volume = 1.2m × 1.25 = 1.5m. Year-three volume = 1.5m × 1.20 = 1.8m. Cost per transaction = £4,200,000 ÷ 1,800,000 = £2.33 (to the nearest penny). Option D is the year-one unit cost (4.2 ÷ 1.2 = £3.50); option B is the year-two figure (4.2 ÷ 1.5 = £2.80); option C comes from wrongly compounding 25% + 20% as 45% (1.2m × 1.45 = 1.74m; 4.2 ÷ 1.74 ≈ £2.41 — close to but not exactly C, which catches other slips). Sequential percentage growth compounds multiplicatively, never additively — a point worth a coaching note whenever teams forecast benefits.

**Question 9 (hard) — Capacity planning for the community.** Your business plan must cover assurance reviews next year. Each lead evaluator delivers 1.5 assurance reviews per month and each senior evaluator 1.0 per month, but 20% of all evaluator time is reserved for coaching and community activities. You have 3 leads and 6 seniors. The portfolio forecast requires 108 assurance reviews in the year. What is your capacity position?

- A. Surplus of about 6 reviews.
- B. Exactly at capacity.
- C. Shortfall of about 6 reviews.
- D. Shortfall of about 18 reviews.

**Correct answer: C.** Gross monthly capacity = (3 × 1.5) + (6 × 1.0) = 4.5 + 6.0 = 10.5 reviews. Net of the 20% reservation: 10.5 × 0.80 = 8.4 per month. Annual net capacity = 8.4 × 12 = 100.8 reviews. Against the 108 required, that is a shortfall of 108 − 100.8 = 7.2, closest to option C's "about 6". The chief trap is forgetting the utilisation haircut entirely: 10.5 × 12 = 126 produces a phantom surplus and lures you to option A. (Applying the 20% reservation before or after annualising gives the same result; omitting it does not.) In business planning, a shortfall of roughly seven reviews is your cue to recruit, re-prioritise, or renegotiate the portfolio — with the numbers as your case to senior stakeholders.

**Question 10 (moderate) — Recruitment funnel arithmetic.** To close a capability gap you run a recruitment campaign for senior digital evaluators. Historical funnel rates: 40% of applicants pass sift, 50% of sifted candidates pass interview, and 75% of passers accept an offer. You need 6 hires. Approximately how many applicants must the campaign attract?

- A. 20
- B. 30
- C. 40
- D. 60

**Correct answer: C.** Overall conversion = 0.40 × 0.50 × 0.75 = 0.15, so each applicant yields 0.15 hires on average. Required applicants = 6 ÷ 0.15 = 40. Working forwards as a check: 40 applicants → 16 pass sift → 8 pass interview → 6 accept. The chain-multiplication of funnel rates is the same structure as compound growth in Question 8, and it appears constantly in the workforce planning strand of "manage the organisation's digital evaluation capability through business planning, development and recruitment".

**Question 11 (moderate) — Interpreting an effect size honestly.** A randomised encouragement trial finds a digital reminder increases form completion from 64.0% in the control group (n = 5,000) to 66.5% in the treatment group (n = 5,000). A colleague drafts the headline: "Reminders boost completion by 4%." Which statement is the most accurate and honest headline figure?

- A. "Completion rose by 2.5 percentage points (from 64.0% to 66.5%), a relative increase of about 3.9%."
- B. "Completion rose 4%."
- C. "Completion rose 2.5%."
- D. "Two-thirds of users now complete."

**Correct answer: A.** The absolute change is 66.5 − 64.0 = 2.5 percentage points. The relative change is 2.5 ÷ 64.0 ≈ 0.039 = 3.9%. The draft headline's "4%" rounds the relative figure and, worse, invites readers to hear "4 percentage points" — a 60% overstatement of the true absolute effect. Option C commits the mirror error, presenting percentage points as a percentage. Option A gives both, correctly labelled — the formulation your best-practice guidance on communicating indicators should mandate. This is where numeracy meets your data ethics duty: figures that are technically defensible but predictably misread fail the ethical communication test.

**Question 12 (hard) — Two-variable trade-off from a dashboard.** An evaluation dashboard for a digital grants service shows two options for next year's improvement budget of £600,000. Option X: spend all of it reducing failure demand; evidence suggests each £100,000 cuts failed applications by 3,000, and each failed application costs £45 in reprocessing. Option Y: spend all of it on uptake; each £100,000 attracts 5,000 additional digital applications, each saving £22 versus the paper channel. Which option yields the larger annual saving, and by how much?

- A. X, by £150,000
- B. Y, by £150,000
- C. X, by £270,000
- D. They are equal

**Correct answer: A.** Option X: (£600,000 ÷ £100,000) = 6 units × 3,000 = 18,000 fewer failures × £45 = £810,000 saved. Option Y: 6 units × 5,000 = 30,000 extra digital applications × £22 = £660,000 saved. X saves £810,000 against Y's £660,000, so X wins by £150,000. If you reached for B, you likely compared the per-unit counts (5,000 beats 3,000) and let the larger volume dominate — but the value per case (£45 versus £22) more than compensates. Multiplying volume by value before comparing is elementary, yet under time pressure the bigger raw number exerts real pull; this trap appears in genuine investment cases just as it does in tests.

**Question 13 (moderate) — Data quality rate with unequal batches.** Two teams submit records to your assured analysis pipeline. Team P submits 12,000 records with a 2.5% error rate; Team Q submits 4,000 records with a 6% error rate. What is the overall error rate across all records, and which single intervention cuts total errors most: halving P's rate or halving Q's rate?

- A. 4.25% overall; halving Q's rate helps most.
- B. 3.375% overall; halving P's rate helps most.
- C. 3.375% overall; halving Q's rate helps most.
- D. 4.25% overall; halving P's rate helps most.

**Correct answer: B.** Errors: P = 12,000 × 0.025 = 300; Q = 4,000 × 0.06 = 240. Total = 540 errors in 16,000 records = 540 ÷ 16,000 = 3.375%. The naive average of the two rates, (2.5 + 6) ÷ 2 = 4.25%, is the distractor in options A and D. Halving P's rate removes 150 errors; halving Q's rate removes 120. So despite Q's rate looking more alarming, the higher-volume stream P offers the bigger absolute gain — option B. When you set up tools and systems for assuring data across multiple teams, targeting by absolute error contribution rather than by headline rate is exactly the prioritisation this question rehearses.

**Question 14 (easy) — Reading scale qualifiers.** A spending table headed "Evaluation programme costs (£ thousands)" shows the digital evaluation line as 1,240. A briefing draft states the programme costs "£1,240". What is the correct figure and the error type?

- A. £1,240; no error.
- B. £124,000; decimal slip.
- C. £1,240,000; ignored scale qualifier.
- D. £12,400; transcription error.

**Correct answer: C.** The header says thousands, so 1,240 means £1,240,000 — roughly £1.24 million. Ignoring scale qualifiers ("in thousands", "in millions") is among the most frequent real-world numerical errors in briefings and one of the most common engineered traps in numeric reasoning tests. It is also disproportionately damaging at your level: a thousand-fold error surviving into a ministerial briefing is precisely the failure your quality assurance framework exists to make impossible.

**Question 15 (hard) — Phased evaluation spend against a fixed envelope.** Your evaluation strategy commits £1.5 million over three years across a portfolio, split 20% in year one (scoping and baselines), 45% in year two (fieldwork), and 35% in year three (analysis and reporting). At the end of year two, actual cumulative spend is £1,040,000. Assuming year one spend was exactly on plan, by how much did year two overspend or underspend against its allocation?

- A. Overspend of £65,000
- B. Underspend of £65,000
- C. Overspend of £140,000
- D. On plan exactly

**Correct answer: A.** Year-one plan = 0.20 × £1,500,000 = £300,000, spent exactly. Year-two plan = 0.45 × £1,500,000 = £675,000. Planned cumulative by end of year two = £300,000 + £675,000 = £975,000. Actual cumulative = £1,040,000. Difference = £1,040,000 − £975,000 = £65,000 overspend, attributable to year two since year one was on plan. The strategic reading matters as much as the arithmetic: year three's allocation of 0.35 × £1,500,000 = £525,000 now has only £1,500,000 − £1,040,000 = £460,000 remaining — a £65,000 squeeze on the analysis and reporting phase, which is where quality is won or lost. A head of digital evaluation would flag this now, not at year-end, and either re-scope, secure a top-up from senior stakeholders, or re-phase the reporting plan.

**Question 16 (moderate) — Completion-rate funnel on a live service.** The monitoring dashboard for a digital appeals service shows that in one month 40,000 users start the online form, 85% reach the evidence-upload stage, 90% of those complete the upload, and 95% of those submit. The service standard requires an overall start-to-submit completion rate of at least 75%. Does the service meet the standard, and what is the rate?

- A. Yes — 76.5%
- B. No — 72.7%
- C. Yes — 90.0%
- D. No — 70.3%

**Correct answer: B.** Chain the stage rates: 0.85 × 0.90 × 0.95 = 0.72675, so about 72.7% — below the 75% standard. In volume terms: 40,000 → 34,000 → 30,600 → 29,070 submissions. Option C is the single-stage rate mistaken for the overall figure; option A comes from averaging the three stage rates ((85 + 90 + 95) ÷ 3 = 90) and then misapplying it. Beyond the arithmetic, the evaluative instinct this rehearses is decomposition: the overall shortfall is driven most by the first transition (15 points lost at evidence upload), so that stage — not the already strong submission step — is where your improvement recommendation and any follow-up user research should point.

**Question 17 (easy) — Ratio for a coaching plan.** Your development plan pairs evaluators with delivery teams for coaching at a target ratio of 1 evaluator to 4 teams. The organisation currently runs 52 delivery teams and you have 11 evaluators available for coaching duties. How many additional evaluators would you need to meet the target ratio exactly?

- A. 1
- B. 2
- C. 3
- D. 4

**Correct answer: B.** Required evaluators = 52 ÷ 4 = 13. You have 11, so you need 13 − 11 = 2 more. A common slip is dividing teams by current evaluators first (52 ÷ 11 ≈ 4.7) and concluding the ratio is "nearly met" — but "nearly 1:5" is not 1:4, and the question asks for the exact staffing gap, a number you would take straight into your recruitment case. Simple ratio questions like this open most numeric tests; bank them quickly and accurately to buy time for the multi-step items later.

### Preparation tips

- **Re-derive one published table a day.** Take a monitoring return or a published evaluation annex from your portfolio and recompute its percentages, totals, and unit costs. This is the highest-fidelity practice available, and it doubles as assurance.
- **Drill the four core moves.** Percentage change (always against the base), weighted averages (always via numerators and denominators), compound growth (always multiplicative), and rate-versus-volume trade-offs. Nearly every question in this section is one of these four in costume.
- **Write intermediate figures down.** Multi-step items (budget shares, funnels, phased benefits) punish mental juggling. One scribbled line per step converts a hard question into three easy ones.
- **Use the calculator for arithmetic, your head for structure.** Decide the operations before touching the calculator; candidates who "explore" on the calculator burn time and confidence.
- **Practise saying figures aloud precisely.** "Percentage points" versus "per cent"; "£1.24 million" versus "1,240". Verbal precision is scored implicitly in assessments and explicitly in the leadership roles they gatekeep.
- **Estimate first, compute second.** A ten-second order-of-magnitude estimate catches transposed digits and scale errors — the same habit that catches them in submissions you sign off.

### Common pitfalls

- **Misreading chart and table labels.** Monthly versus annual data, "in thousands", fiscal versus calendar years. The spec literature identifies label misreading as the single most common failure, and Question 14 shows why it is lethal at your level.
- **Averaging averages.** Unweighted means of subgroup percentages (Questions 4 and 13) are the most reliable distractor in the genre because they look so reasonable.
- **Additive compounding.** Adding sequential growth rates (25% + 20% = 45%) instead of multiplying (1.25 × 1.20 = 1.50) misstates every multi-year forecast.
- **Over-calculating.** Some items yield to estimation and elimination in twenty seconds; performing full-precision arithmetic on every option is a time sink. Match effort to what the answer options require — proportionality, again.
- **Time-sink questions.** One brutal multi-stage item can consume five easy ones' worth of time. Flag, skip, return. Your assessment score, like your assurance capacity, is a portfolio to be optimised, not a sequence to be endured.
- **Trusting the flattering number.** When a figure makes the case you hoped for, check it twice. Assessment writers, like reality, exploit motivated reasoning.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately you understand, analyse, and draw conclusions from written material of the kind your role turns on: evaluation strategies, government guidance, quality assurance frameworks, data ethics policies, stakeholder emails, and draft report text. The defining discipline is fact-based logic — you answer strictly from the passage in front of you, setting aside everything you know from outside it, however expert that knowledge is.

The dominant format is a short, dense passage followed by statements you classify as **True** (the passage logically supports it), **False** (the passage logically contradicts it), or **Cannot Say** (the passage provides insufficient information either way). Multiple-choice comprehension questions — best summary, author's main claim, precise obligation — round out the set. Timing is tight, often under a minute per question, deliberately simulating the pace at which you triage a crowded inbox before a board meeting.

Why does this matter for a head of digital evaluation? Because words are your instruments of governance. Your quality assurance framework binds teams only through what its sentences actually require. Your advice to senior stakeholders is quoted, forwarded, and acted on — precision in what you claim, and honesty about what the evidence cannot say, are the difference between influence and embarrassment. When you assure a report, you are testing whether its conclusions are supported by its own text and evidence — literally the True/False/Cannot Say judgement at professional scale. And "Cannot Say" deserves special affection from an evaluator: it is the psychometric twin of "the evidence does not support a conclusion either way", a sentence your profession exists to say when it is true.

### What it measures for your role

- **Precise reading of standards and guidance → Evaluation delivery and Quality assurance of data and analysis.** You define best practice and assure work against written standards; questions test whether you can extract exactly what a framework mandates, permits, and is silent on.
- **Distinguishing evidence from assertion → Communicating analysis and insight.** Communicating compelling stories at strategic level requires knowing precisely what your evidence licenses you to say. True/False/Cannot Say is that discipline in miniature.
- **Interpreting policy and legal text → Data ethics and privacy.** You oversee compliance with data ethics standards and legislation; questions use ethics framework and consent-wording passages where a single qualifier changes the obligation.
- **Reading stakeholder communications accurately → Evaluation planning and strategy and your escalation-point duty.** Emails from senior stakeholders compress requests, constraints, and politics into few words; questions test extraction of who has asked for what, by when, under which conditions.
- **Synthesising long documents → your strategy-setting duty.** Best-summary questions mirror the daily act of reducing a forty-page report to the three sentences a director general will read.

### Practice questions

**Passage A — from a draft organisational evaluation strategy.** "All major digital programmes must produce an evaluation plan before their beta phase begins. Plans must identify a named senior responsible analyst. Programmes classified as high-risk must additionally commission an independent peer review of their evaluation design; for other programmes, independent review is recommended but not mandatory. Where a programme processes special category personal data, the evaluation plan must be approved by the data ethics board regardless of risk classification. The head of digital evaluation may grant a deferral of the beta deadline in exceptional circumstances, provided the deferral and its rationale are recorded in the assurance register."

**Question 1 (easy).** True, False, or Cannot Say: "Every major digital programme must commission an independent peer review of its evaluation design."

**Correct answer: False.** The passage restricts the mandatory independent review to programmes "classified as high-risk"; for others it is "recommended but not mandatory". The statement generalises the obligation to every programme, which the text directly contradicts. Note the reasoning style: you do not ask whether universal review would be good practice (it might be); you ask only what this passage requires.

**Question 2 (easy).** True, False, or Cannot Say: "A medium-risk programme processing special category personal data needs data ethics board approval for its evaluation plan."

**Correct answer: True.** The passage states that where special category data is processed, ethics board approval is required "regardless of risk classification". Medium risk is a risk classification, so the requirement applies. The phrase "regardless of" is doing all the work — a one-word hinge of the kind your compliance oversight duty trains you to spot.

**Question 3 (moderate).** True, False, or Cannot Say: "Deferrals of the beta deadline have been granted more than once in the past year."

**Correct answer: Cannot Say.** The passage establishes a power to grant deferrals and a recording requirement, but says nothing about whether, or how often, the power has been exercised. A rule's existence is not evidence of its use. This distinction — between what a framework permits and what has actually happened — is one you police constantly when assuring claims like "our governance ensures quality": the machinery existing does not prove it ran.

**Question 4 (moderate).** True, False, or Cannot Say: "An evaluation plan that names a senior responsible analyst satisfies all requirements for a low-risk programme that processes no special category data."

**Correct answer: Cannot Say.** Tempting to answer True: the named analyst requirement is met, the peer review is only recommended, and ethics approval is not triggered. But the passage also requires that the plan be produced "before their beta phase begins" — and the statement says nothing about timing. The plan could name an analyst yet have been produced late. The passage neither confirms nor contradicts full compliance, so Cannot Say. This is the hardest habit in verbal reasoning: checking a claim of complete compliance against every clause, not just the clauses the statement mentions.

**Passage B — from a data ethics framework you oversee.** "Personal data collected for service monitoring may be reused for evaluation purposes only where users have been informed of this possibility at the point of collection. Anonymised data, from which individuals can no longer be identified by any reasonably available means, falls outside this restriction. Where evaluators are uncertain whether data meets the anonymisation threshold, they must consult the data protection team before access is granted. Consultation outcomes must be documented. Nothing in this framework removes obligations arising under data protection legislation."

**Question 5 (moderate).** True, False, or Cannot Say: "Evaluators may reuse fully anonymised monitoring data for evaluation without users having been informed at the point of collection."

**Correct answer: True.** The reuse restriction applies to personal data; the passage expressly places anonymised data — defined as data from which individuals "can no longer be identified by any reasonably available means" — "outside this restriction". So the point-of-collection notice condition does not apply to it. The statement tracks the passage's own definition, so it is supported. (Your professional instinct that anonymisation claims deserve scepticism is correct in life — but the question asks about this text.)

**Question 6 (hard).** True, False, or Cannot Say: "If an evaluator is confident that data is anonymised, the framework does not require them to consult the data protection team."

**Correct answer: True.** The consultation duty is triggered only "where evaluators are uncertain whether data meets the anonymisation threshold". A confident evaluator is not uncertain, so the trigger condition is absent and the framework, as written, imposes no consultation duty on them. Many strong candidates wrongly answer Cannot Say here, feeling that confidence might be misplaced — true, and a real governance gap you might well fix in the next revision — but the question is about what this framework requires, and its condition is uncertainty, not risk. Reading the trigger conditions of obligations exactly as drafted is core to overseeing compliance: it tells you both what the rule does and where it is too weak.

**Question 7 (moderate).** True, False, or Cannot Say: "The framework overrides data protection legislation where the two conflict."

**Correct answer: False.** The final sentence states the opposite: "Nothing in this framework removes obligations arising under data protection legislation." The framework explicitly subordinates itself to legislation, so a claim that it overrides legislation is directly contradicted. Savings clauses like this are easy to skim past at the end of a passage, and test writers know it — as do the drafters of real policies, which is why your ethics oversight reads final paragraphs with particular care.

**Passage C — email from a director of policy.** "Thanks for the interim findings on the digital identity pilot. I appreciate the caveats, but the programme board meets Thursday and the Minister's office has asked for a one-page summary before then. I'd like the summary to lead with the 30% reduction in processing time. I understand the fraud outcome data isn't mature yet — fine to omit that, since it's inconclusive anyway. Could your team also confirm that the evaluation supports national rollout? We can revisit the detail in the full report next quarter."

**Question 8 (moderate).** Which of the following does the director explicitly request? Select the best answer.

- A. A one-page summary before Thursday, leading with the processing-time reduction, omitting the immature fraud data, and confirmation that the evaluation supports national rollout.
- B. A full report before Thursday.
- C. Deletion of the fraud outcome data from the evaluation.
- D. A meeting with the Minister's office.

**Correct answer: A.** Every element of A is stated: the one-pager before Thursday's board ("before then"), leading with the 30% figure, omitting fraud data ("fine to omit that"), and confirming rollout support ("Could your team also confirm..."). B confuses the one-pager with the full report, which the email defers to next quarter. C overstates — omission from a summary is not deletion from the evaluation. D is never requested. Accurate extraction comes first; whether you should comply is a separate, situational-judgement question (and this exact email reappears in the next section, because in your role the two skills fire together — but they are scored apart).

**Question 9 (hard).** True, False, or Cannot Say: "The director believes the fraud outcome data will eventually support national rollout."

**Correct answer: Cannot Say.** The director calls the fraud data "inconclusive anyway" and asks for it to be omitted — which tells you their view of its current state, not their prediction of its eventual direction. Nothing in the email expresses a belief about what mature fraud data will show. Candidates who sense spin in the email often answer False, importing a motive ("they think it will look bad") the text does not state; candidates who read "inconclusive" as optimism answer True. Both are inference beyond the words. Holding the line between what a stakeholder said and what you suspect they meant is a daily discipline when you act as the contact point between your community and the organisation.

**Passage D — from published cross-government evaluation guidance.** "Departments should proportionately evaluate all significant programmes. Proportionality means the scale and rigour of evaluation should reflect the programme's cost, risk, novelty, and the decisions the evidence will inform. Untested, novel interventions warrant more rigorous designs, including experimental methods where feasible. Evaluation findings should be published within twelve months of completion unless there are compelling reasons not to, such as national security. Departments are encouraged, but not required, to pre-register evaluation protocols publicly."

**Question 10 (easy).** True, False, or Cannot Say: "Departments are required to pre-register evaluation protocols publicly."

**Correct answer: False.** The passage says departments are "encouraged, but not required" to pre-register. The statement asserts a requirement, which the text directly contradicts. The encouraged/required distinction is the bread and butter of guidance interpretation — and of the coaching you give teams on what the guidance genuinely obliges them to do versus what good citizenship suggests.

**Question 11 (moderate).** True, False, or Cannot Say: "A low-cost, low-risk programme using well-established methods requires an experimental evaluation design."

**Correct answer: False.** The passage ties rigour to cost, risk, novelty, and decision importance, and reserves the strongest push for experimental methods to "untested, novel interventions". A low-cost, low-risk, well-established programme sits at the opposite pole of every stated criterion, so the proportionality principle as described points away from requiring experimental design; the statement contradicts the passage's explicit logic. Note the reasoning is still text-bound: the passage states the rule connecting programme characteristics to rigour, and the statement violates that stated rule — this is False by the passage's own principle, not by your professional opinion of it.

**Question 12 (moderate).** True, False, or Cannot Say: "Evaluation findings must always be published within twelve months of completion."

**Correct answer: False.** The twelve-month expectation carries an explicit exception: "unless there are compelling reasons not to, such as national security". The word "always" makes the statement absolute, and the passage's exception contradicts an absolute claim. One-word qualifiers — always, never, all, only — are the highest-yield tripwires in True/False/Cannot Say items; scan for them before you scan for anything else.

**Question 13 (hard) — Best summary.** A 60-word extract from a draft evaluation report reads: "Uptake of the digital service exceeded forecast among users aged under 50 but fell materially short among older users, who cited difficulty with identity verification. Overall uptake met target only because the under-50 group over-performed. Unless verification is simplified, projected savings — which assume uniform uptake growth — are unlikely to be realised in full." Which one-sentence summary best serves a senior stakeholder?

- A. "The service met its uptake target."
- B. "Older users dislike the service."
- C. "Overall uptake met target, but only through over-performance among under-50s; the verification barrier for older users puts the assumed savings at risk unless it is fixed."
- D. "Savings will not be realised."

**Correct answer: C.** A good executive summary preserves the finding, the mechanism, and the consequence. Option A is true but dangerously incomplete — it hides the compositional fragility the passage exists to flag. Option B coarsens a specific, fixable barrier (identity verification) into an attitude claim the text does not make. Option D overstates: the passage says savings are "unlikely to be realised in full" without action, not that they will not be realised. C keeps all three load-bearing elements within one sentence — which is precisely the skill of "communicating relevant, compelling stories" without sacrificing accuracy: compression that loses no decision-relevant truth.

**Question 14 (hard).** True, False, or Cannot Say (Passage D again): "A department that delays publication of findings for fourteen months without any compelling reason is acting inconsistently with the guidance."

**Correct answer: True.** The guidance sets a twelve-month expectation, with an exception only where compelling reasons exist. The scenario stipulates fourteen months and expressly no compelling reason, so the exception cannot apply and the expectation is breached. Note how the stipulations inside the question statement ("without any compelling reason") are part of the facts you reason from — you combine them with the passage's rule. Candidates sometimes answer Cannot Say because the passage says "should" rather than "must"; but the question asks about consistency with the guidance, not legal violation, and failing a stated "should" without excuse is inconsistency with it.

**Passage E — from the terms of reference for a cross-government digital evaluation network.** "The network exists to share evaluation methods, findings, and standards across departmental digital evaluation communities. Membership is open to any government analyst or digital professional with evaluation responsibilities. The network is chaired on a rotating annual basis by a head of digital evaluation from a member department. The chair may convene working groups on specific methodological topics; working group outputs are advisory and become binding on a department only if adopted through that department's own governance. The network does not itself commission evaluations, and it holds no budget beyond secretariat costs, which are met by the chairing department in the year of its chairing."

**Question 15 (easy).** True, False, or Cannot Say: "Working group outputs are binding on all member departments once published by the network."

**Correct answer: False.** The passage states outputs "are advisory and become binding on a department only if adopted through that department's own governance". Publication by the network is not departmental adoption, so the statement contradicts the text. The advisory-until-adopted pattern is common across cross-government communities, and misreading it in either direction — treating advice as mandate, or a mandate as mere advice — is exactly the confusion your advocacy role often has to untangle for colleagues.

**Question 16 (moderate).** True, False, or Cannot Say: "A department chairing the network will incur costs that other member departments do not incur that year."

**Correct answer: True.** The passage says the network's only budgeted costs are secretariat costs, "met by the chairing department in the year of its chairing". So in any given year, the chairing department bears the secretariat costs and, by the funding rule stated, other members do not. The statement follows from combining two clauses — the cost scope and the cost bearer — which is a step up from single-sentence matching but still wholly inside the text. Multi-clause synthesis questions reward slowing down for five seconds to assemble the rule before judging the statement.

**Question 17 (hard).** True, False, or Cannot Say: "A head of digital evaluation whose department is not currently a member of the network is eligible to chair it."

**Correct answer: Cannot Say.** The passage says the chair rotates among heads of digital evaluation "from a member department" — so a head from a non-member department is not eligible while the department remains a non-member. But is the statement therefore False? Look again: membership "is open to any government analyst or digital professional with evaluation responsibilities" — it is individuals, not departments, who join under that clause, yet the chairing clause speaks of "a member department". The passage uses membership in two subtly different ways and never defines departmental membership, so whether the head's department could count as a member — and hence whether they are eligible — cannot be settled from the text. This is a deliberately drafted ambiguity of the kind you find in real terms of reference; the professionally correct answer, in the test and in the meeting, is that the text does not determine the answer, and the drafting needs fixing.

**Question 18 (moderate) — Comprehension.** According to Passage E, which of the following can the network itself do?

- A. Commission an evaluation of a cross-departmental digital service.
- B. Convene a working group on quasi-experimental methods.
- C. Compel a department to adopt a shared quality standard.
- D. Fund a departmental evaluation from its central budget.

**Correct answer: B.** The chair "may convene working groups on specific methodological topics", and quasi-experimental methods is such a topic. A is excluded ("does not itself commission evaluations"); C is excluded (outputs are advisory unless departmentally adopted); D is excluded (no budget beyond secretariat costs). Elimination against explicit exclusions is quick and certain here — and mirrors how you would brief your own leadership on what joining such a network does and does not commit the organisation to.

**Question 19 (moderate).** True, False, or Cannot Say (Passage E again): "The current chair of the network is a head of digital evaluation."

**Correct answer: True.** The passage states the network "is chaired on a rotating annual basis by a head of digital evaluation from a member department" — the rule defines who may hold the chair, so whoever currently holds it is, under the terms as written, a head of digital evaluation. Some candidates answer Cannot Say on the ground that the passage does not mention the current chair by name; but the question asks about role, not identity, and a standing constitutional rule determines the role of every incumbent. Contrast this with Question 3 in Passage A, where a power's existence told you nothing about its use: there the statement concerned events (deferrals granted), here it concerns a defining condition of the office itself. Distinguishing rules that constrain what must be true from rules that merely enable what might happen is among the most transferable reading skills this assessment measures.

### Preparation tips

- **Read the statement first, then hunt.** For True/False/Cannot Say items, read the statement before re-reading the passage, then scan for the governing sentence. This is faster than absorbing the whole passage cold, and it mirrors how you actually check a claim against a framework document.
- **Underline trigger words.** Regardless, unless, only, must, should, may, all, always, never, encouraged, required. Each is a hinge on which an answer turns; Questions 2, 6, 10, and 12 all pivot on a single such word.
- **Rehearse "Cannot Say" as a positive skill.** Practise articulating why the passage is silent — "it states the power exists, not that it was used" — rather than treating Cannot Say as a shrug. As an evaluator you already own this muscle; the test just wants it fast.
- **Suppress expertise deliberately.** Before each passage, tell yourself: "Only this text exists." Your deep knowledge of evaluation guidance and data protection is the main source of wrong answers here, because it supplies facts the passage does not.
- **Practise one-sentence summaries.** Take a real report's executive summary and compress it to a single sentence keeping finding, mechanism, consequence. Ten repetitions will noticeably sharpen both test performance and board papers.
- **Pace by passage, not by question.** Budget time per passage-block (say, four minutes for a passage plus four questions), since re-reading costs amortise across the block.

### Common pitfalls

- **Bringing outside knowledge in.** The most common failure for expert candidates. You know what the Magenta Book really says, what anonymisation really risks, what directors really mean — and none of it is admissible. Only the passage counts.
- **Confusing "likely" with "certain".** Passages often state probabilistic claims ("unlikely to be realised in full"); statements convert them to absolutes ("will not be realised"). Matching modality — the degree of certainty — is as important as matching content.
- **Answering the compliance question you wish was asked.** Question 4's trap: verifying the clauses the statement mentions and forgetting the clauses it does not. Full-compliance claims require checking every requirement in the passage.
- **Missing savings clauses and exceptions.** Final sentences that subordinate, except, or reserve ("nothing in this framework...", "unless...") reverse otherwise-correct answers. Read to the full stop.
- **Reading motives into correspondence.** Question 9's trap. Stakeholder emails invite inference about agendas; the test scores only what is written.
- **Poor time management through whole-passage reading.** Reading every passage exhaustively before seeing any question wastes the scanning advantage. Statement first, then targeted search.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment evaluates decision-making, interpersonal effectiveness, and professional integrity by presenting realistic workplace dilemmas and asking which responses you judge most and least effective. Unlike the reasoning tests, there is often no single provably correct answer; instead, responses are scored against what experienced practitioners and the organisation's values identify as stronger or weaker courses of action. Formats include selecting the most effective and least effective options from a set, or ranking or rating all options from best to worst. Timing is usually generous or absent, because the construct is natural judgement, not speed.

For a head of digital evaluation, this is arguably the assessment closest to your actual job. Your reasoning skills are exercised in private; your judgement is exercised in public, in rooms where a policy director wants a caveat softened, a delivery team wants a risk waved through, an evaluator wants backing in a methodological dispute, and a finance colleague wants your assurance budget halved. The scenarios below are built from your real duties: acting as an escalation point, defending quality standards, overseeing data ethics, influencing senior stakeholders, advocating across government, and developing the community you lead. Scoring aligns with UK public sector values: honesty, objectivity, integrity, and impartiality; user needs and accessibility; transparency; collaboration; and inclusion.

### What it measures for your role

- **Escalation handling and blocker removal → your head-of-level duties.** Scenarios test whether you act at the right altitude — neither absorbing every problem personally nor bouncing everything back down.
- **Integrity under pressure → Data ethics and privacy and Quality assurance of data and analysis.** Scenarios probe the line between being helpful to stakeholders and protecting the objectivity of evidence.
- **Influence and advocacy → Evaluation planning and strategy and Communicating analysis and insight.** Scenarios test securing resources and buy-in without overclaiming what evaluation can deliver.
- **Developing people → Evaluation delivery and your coaching duties.** Scenarios test how you correct, stretch, and back the evaluators and teams you lead.
- **Cross-government collaboration → your advocacy duty.** Scenarios involve other Government Digital and Data communities and the analytical professions.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (moderate) — The pressured summary.** The director of policy from Verbal Passage C emails again: the one-page summary for Thursday's programme board should "lead with the 30% processing-time reduction, leave out the immature fraud data, and confirm the evaluation supports national rollout". Your interim findings support the 30% figure with caveats, the fraud data is genuinely immature, and the evidence so far supports rollout to two further regions, not national rollout. Rate the options.

- A. Produce the summary as requested; the full report next quarter can correct the record.
- B. Refuse to provide any summary until the full report is complete.
- C. Provide the one-pager on time: lead with the 30% figure and its key caveat, note that fraud outcomes are not yet mature enough to report, and state that current evidence supports a staged extension to two regions, with national rollout contingent on the next evidence point. Offer to brief the director before Thursday.
- D. Escalate the director's email to their director general as attempted interference.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: A.** Option C serves the stakeholder's legitimate need (a timely, usable summary for the board) while protecting every point of evidential integrity: caveated headline, honest treatment of immature data, and an accurate statement of what the evidence supports — with a pre-briefing that treats the director as a partner, not an adversary. Option A is worst because it publishes an overclaim ("supports national rollout") into a ministerial process; "correcting the record next quarter" after decisions are taken is the textbook failure of evaluation integrity, and it would spend the credibility of your whole community. Option B is passively unhelpful: it fails a reasonable timeliness need and cedes the summary-writing to someone less careful. Option D is disproportionate as a first move — the director's request is pushy but was made openly, and escalation before conversation destroys a relationship your advocacy depends on. Note the shape of the best answer: neither compliance nor refusal, but a fast, honest alternative that meets the need.

**Question 2 (moderate) — The struggling evaluator.** A senior digital evaluator you manage submits an assurance review that a delivery team immediately disputes, correctly identifying two clear analytical errors in the review itself. The evaluator is usually reliable, has been carrying a heavy workload, and is visibly embarrassed. The delivery team's product manager has copied you, asking that the review be "withdrawn and redone by someone competent". Rate the options.

- A. Publicly defend the review to maintain your community's authority.
- B. Acknowledge the errors promptly to the product manager, commit to a corrected review within a stated time, and separately talk with the evaluator about workload, support, and what allowed the errors through — also asking what your assurance-of-assurance process should have caught.
- C. Withdraw the review, reassign it to another evaluator, and quietly reduce the original evaluator's responsibilities.
- D. Reply that everyone makes mistakes and the review largely stands.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A.** Option B does four right things at once: honesty about errors (the same standard you hold delivery teams to — an assurance function that cannot admit its own mistakes has no moral authority), a concrete remedy with a date, care for the person (workload and support, not blame), and a systemic question — your quality assurance framework should catch errors in your own community's work too. Option A is worst: defending known errors to preserve authority is the fastest way to lose it permanently, and it models exactly the defensiveness you coach teams out of. Option C fixes the artefact but quietly punishes a reliable person without development, likely damaging trust across the community you lead. Option D concedes nothing specific and repairs nothing. The distinguishing leadership move in B is treating the incident as information about the system, not just the individual.

**Question 3 (hard) — The ethics shortcut.** A high-priority evaluation needs linked data urgently. The delivery team's analyst tells you, in confidence, that a colleague has suggested proceeding with the data linkage now and completing the ethics review paperwork retrospectively, "since the board always approves this kind of thing anyway". The programme's senior responsible owner is pressing for results and is aware of the suggestion. Rate the options.

- A. Allow it this once, given the precedent that similar linkages are always approved.
- B. Stop the linkage proceeding before review; tell the analyst they were right to raise it; contact the SRO directly to explain why retrospective ethics review is not review at all, and offer practical help — an expedited board submission and interim analysis on already-approved data — so the programme keeps moving.
- C. Stop the linkage and report the analyst's colleague and the SRO to the permanent secretary for attempted misconduct.
- D. Ask the ethics board to meet retrospectively and backdate the approval to keep the paperwork tidy.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D.** Option B upholds the non-negotiable (review precedes access — the entire point of your duty to "ensure data ethics is applied appropriately"), protects and affirms the person who spoke up (essential to the data ethics culture you are charged with building — people stop reporting what leaders punish or ignore), addresses the senior sponsor directly and respectfully, and — the mark of a strong answer — reduces the incentive to cut corners by offering a legitimate faster path. Option D is worst: backdating an approval is falsifying a governance record, strictly worse than the original shortcut because it corrupts the control itself. Option A converts a prediction of approval into a substitute for approval, which erases the board's ability to ever say no. Option C leaps to the gravest escalation before any conversation, punishing openly-discussed pressure as if it were concealed misconduct; it would be remembered as an ambush and would teach the organisation to keep such conversations away from you — the opposite of an escalation point's job.

**Question 4 (moderate) — The budget challenge.** In business planning, the finance director proposes cutting your evaluation programme budget by 30%, remarking that "evaluation is a nice-to-have — the services already have analytics dashboards". You have fifteen minutes with them tomorrow. Rate the options.

- A. Accept the cut and spread the reduction evenly across all planned evaluations.
- B. Bring one page showing: the decisions next year that depend on evaluation evidence, with the money at stake in each; two concrete past cases where evaluation findings changed course and saved money; the specific decisions that would be taken blind under a 30% cut; and a proposal for a smaller, prioritised cut you could absorb by deferring the lowest-consequence evaluations.
- C. Warn that you will raise the cut with the departmental board as a breach of government evaluation expectations.
- D. Explain that dashboards only monitor and cannot attribute impact, so the premise of the cut is analytically mistaken.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A.** Option B is influence done properly: it reframes evaluation from cost to decision-insurance, quantifies consequence, concedes something real (a prioritised smaller cut), and gives the finance director a defensible position to adopt — which is how you "influence the organisation's senior stakeholders to secure resources". Option D is analytically correct but tactically weak on its own: winning the definitional argument about dashboards does not answer a finance director's actual question, which is what the money buys. Option C may eventually be necessary but as an opening move it substitutes threat for persuasion and forfeits the relationship. Option A is worst: an even spread is the abdication of exactly the prioritisation judgement your role exists to provide — it degrades every evaluation slightly instead of protecting the ones that matter, and it signals that the function has no view about its own value.

**Question 5 (hard) — The inconvenient finding.** Your team's evaluation of a flagship accessibility feature finds it is barely used, and that the users who most need it largely cannot find it. The service's director — a strong ally of evaluation who secured much of your funding — asks you to "hold the finding back until we've fixed discoverability, so the feature isn't killed off unfairly". The finding is due for publication under your organisation's transparency commitment within the month. Rate the options.

- A. Publish on schedule with no engagement, since transparency rules are rules.
- B. Agree to hold the finding back indefinitely to protect the feature and the relationship.
- C. Publish on schedule, but work with the director beforehand so publication lands alongside their discoverability improvement plan — presenting the finding accurately with the response, and framing low uptake as a fixable design issue, which is what the evidence shows.
- D. Delay publication quietly for six months and hope the fix works by then.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: B.** Option C honours the transparency commitment and the accuracy of the finding while giving the ally what they legitimately need — not suppression, but context: an accurate finding published beside a credible response usually strengthens a service's position rather than killing it, and this framing is truthful because discoverability genuinely is the mechanism the evidence identifies. Option B is worst: indefinite suppression at a sponsor's request converts your function from evaluator to protector, and the precedent — findings are negotiable for allies — would surface eventually and take your credibility and theirs with it. Option D is B with a timer and added concealment. Option A honours the rule but squanders the relationship needlessly: nothing in the transparency commitment forbids coordinating publication with the service's response, and pointless collateral damage to your strongest ally is not integrity, it is poor stewardship of influence. The pattern to internalise: the strong answer protects both the evidence and the relationship, and refuses the false choice between them.

**Question 6 (moderate) — The methodological turf war.** A lead digital evaluator in your community and a principal statistician from the analytical profession are in escalating disagreement over the evaluation design for a shared programme: the evaluator favours a rapid quasi-experimental design aligned to the delivery timetable; the statistician insists a randomised design is the only defensible option and has said the evaluation community "lacks statistical depth". Both have escalated to you. Rate the options.

- A. Back your evaluator publicly — your community's standing is at stake.
- B. Convene the two of them with a neutral structure: agree the decision criteria first (what decision the evidence must support, by when, at what confidence), then assess both designs against those criteria together, and record the trade-offs whichever way it lands.
- C. Defer entirely to the statistician, since randomisation is the stronger method.
- D. Take the design decision yourself without meeting them, and inform both professions of the outcome.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A.** Option B does what an escalation point is for: it converts a status contest into a criteria problem. Fixing decision-need, timetable, and confidence first makes the design choice follow from the programme's requirements rather than from professional identity — and recording trade-offs respects both professions' legitimate concerns. It also models the collaboration across analytical professions your advocacy duty requires. Option A is worst because it makes the dispute tribal: you would win a battle and entrench a border, and the "lacks statistical depth" jibe would harden into a standing rift. Option C capitulates on method-worship grounds — randomisation is stronger in the abstract, but an evaluation that reports after the decision it was meant to inform is methodologically pristine and practically worthless; proportionality is a value, not a compromise. Option D resolves the object-level question while teaching both communities that escalation to you means exclusion from the reasoning — guaranteeing you become the bottleneck for every future dispute.

**Question 7 (moderate) — The coaching shortcut.** A delivery team about to enter private beta asks you to simply write their theory of change for them: "You'd do it in an afternoon, and honestly yours will be better than anything we'd produce." Your diary could just about absorb it. Demand for exactly this kind of help is rising across the organisation. Rate the options.

- A. Write it for them — it is efficient and the quality will be high.
- B. Decline and send them the guidance documents.
- C. Run a two-hour facilitated session where the team drafts their own theory of change with you challenging and shaping it, then have one of your lead evaluators review the result — and note the recurring demand as a case for a standing coaching offer in next year's business plan.
- D. Tell them theories of change are their responsibility and evaluation gets involved after beta.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: D.** Option C reflects the deepest point in your skill set: "coach teams on how to create evaluation plans and strategies" — coaching, not ghost-writing — because a theory of change works only when the team owns the causal claims and can revisit them as evidence arrives. The facilitated-session pattern gets near-afternoon efficiency with genuine transfer of capability, and routing review to a lead evaluator develops your community too. Feeding the demand signal into business planning is the head-of-level reflex: recurring requests are strategy information. Option A produces a beautiful artefact nobody owns; when reality diverges from it, the team will neither notice nor care, and you will have taught the organisation that evaluation is a service you perform rather than a capability they hold. Option B is technically correct and practically useless — documents do not coach. Option D is worst: it is factually wrong about your own framework (theories of change precede beta precisely so evaluation shapes design), and it slams a door on a team actively asking to do the right thing — the rarest and most valuable moment in capability-building.

**Question 8 (hard) — The community in decline.** Exit interviews and your own observation tell you the digital evaluation community's morale is sagging: two strong senior evaluators have left for other departments, citing "no visible career path"; recruitment to a vacant lead role failed twice; and delivery teams increasingly bypass evaluators, citing slow turnaround. Your director asks for your plan. Rate the options.

- A. Request an immediate pay-flexibility case for evaluators to stem departures.
- B. Present a capability plan that addresses the system: a published career pathway with named development moves; turnaround-time standards with a triage tier for rapid requests (fixing the bypass incentive); re-running the lead recruitment with a revised, realistic role profile and active search across the cross-government community; and retention conversations with current seniors now — with measurable indicators for each strand.
- C. Commission a staff survey to gather more evidence before acting.
- D. Accept the shrinkage and re-scope the function around a smaller core.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D.** Option B treats the three symptoms — departures, failed recruitment, bypassing — as one system: people leave where they cannot grow, recruitment fails where roles are misdrawn, and teams bypass where the service is slower than their need. The plan pairs every diagnosis with an intervention and an indicator, which is "manage the organisation's digital evaluation capability through business planning, development and recruitment" made concrete — and it is, fittingly, a theory of change for your own function. Option A addresses one lever for one symptom; pay matters, but neither of the stated departure reasons was pay. Option C defers action the existing evidence already supports — evaluators, of all professionals, should recognise when the evidence threshold for acting has been met. Option D is worst as a first response: it converts a recoverable dip into a strategic retreat without testing any intervention, and heads of profession who lead with surrender rarely get the chance to lead a rebuild.

**Question 9 (moderate) — The cross-government ask.** A head of digital evaluation in another department asks you to share your organisation's quality assurance framework and coaching materials, which your team spent a year developing. A colleague on your own team objects: "We should keep the advantage — we're competing with them for the same analysts." Rate the options.

- A. Share the materials openly, offer a walkthrough session, and invite the other department to contribute improvements back — while raising the recruitment-competition concern honestly inside your own retention planning.
- B. Decline; the materials are your organisation's asset.
- C. Share an older, redacted version to appear collaborative at minimal cost.
- D. Agree to share only if the other department formally credits your team in all uses.

**Most effective: A. Least effective: C.** Option A is what "advocate for digital evaluation, working with colleagues across government" means in practice: government is one employer of the taxpayer's money, and a stronger evaluation profession anywhere raises the standard everywhere — including the standard of candidates who may one day join you. Handling the retention worry through your own planning, rather than through hoarding, addresses the real concern without corrupting the collaboration. Option B mistakes the public sector for a market competitor; the "advantage" of a QA framework is realised by use, not exclusivity. Option D converts collegiality into a licensing negotiation over materials built with public funds — credit is a courtesy to request, not a condition to impose. Option C is worst because it is dishonest collaboration: it takes the reputational benefit of sharing while deliberately delivering less than it appears to, and when discovered — these things are always discovered — it would cost more trust than declining ever would.

**Question 10 (hard) — The minister's deadline.** Your director tells you the Minister's office wants headline findings from a major evaluation two weeks before your quality assurance process completes. The analysis is done; the assurance review — which in the past has changed headline numbers in roughly one case in five — has not started. Your director says: "Give me your best numbers with a health warning. Refusing isn't an option." Rate the options.

- A. Provide the numbers with a standard caveat line in the footer.
- B. Provide a briefing that gives the current headline range rather than point estimates, states plainly that one in five assurance reviews changes headline findings, identifies which specific findings are most and least likely to move, and commits to a confirmed set by a named date — and start the assurance review immediately on the highest-consequence findings first.
- C. Refuse, citing the quality assurance framework, and offer the confirmed findings in two weeks.
- D. Quietly compress the assurance review into three days by pulling evaluators off other work, and present the result as fully assured.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D.** Option B accepts the legitimate reality that ministers' decisions do not always wait, and answers it with honest uncertainty rather than false precision: ranges instead of points, a stated base rate for how often findings move, a triaged assurance plan that de-risks the most consequential numbers first, and a firm date for confirmation. That is "communicating analysis and insight... at a tactical and strategic level" under real conditions — and it is the answer that keeps both the Minister's office and the evidence safe. Option A technically discloses but practically misleads: footer caveats do not survive contact with a red box, and you know it. Option C is principled but politically naive in the stated circumstances ("refusing isn't an option"); it also misses that your framework's purpose — decisions informed by honest evidence — can be served by option B without breaching it. Option D is worst: it degrades the control while claiming its authority, presenting rushed work as "fully assured" — a direct integrity failure that, unlike the pressure that caused it, would be entirely yours.

**Question 11 (moderate) — The accessibility blind spot.** Reviewing evaluation plans across the portfolio, you notice that none of the current quantitative research designs will produce findings about disabled users: sample frames rely on completed digital journeys, systematically excluding those who abandoned or never started them. Delivery timelines are tight, and adding recruitment strands would delay two evaluations by six weeks. Rate the options.

- A. Note the limitation clearly in each evaluation's report and proceed to timetable.
- B. Require the two highest-impact evaluations to add inclusive recruitment strands despite the six-week delay; for the rest, mandate the limitation be stated prominently (not buried), and change the evaluation planning guidance so sample-frame inclusion is checked at plan approval, before timelines are set.
- C. Delay all evaluations until every design includes disabled users.
- D. Commission a separate, single study of disabled users' experience across all services next year.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A.** Option B is proportionality applied to inclusion: fix it fully where the decisions are biggest, be loudly honest where you cannot fix it this cycle, and — the head-of-level move — repair the system so the gap cannot silently recur, by moving the check to plan approval where it costs nothing. Users who abandon a digital journey are often precisely the users the service is failing; excluding them is not a technical footnote but a validity and fairness problem, which is why option A (caveat and carry on, everywhere, indefinitely) is least effective — it treats a systematic exclusion of disabled users as a documentation issue. Option C honours the principle but at a blanket cost no portfolio can bear, and blanket delay is the outcome most likely to discredit inclusion arguments in future negotiations. Option D produces useful evidence but a year late and detached from each service's decisions — a supplement, not a substitute.

### Preparation tips

- **Score options against values, not cleverness.** Before rating, ask: which option is honest, proportionate, develops people, protects users, and repairs the system? The strongest answers usually do several at once; the weakest sacrifice a principle for speed or comfort.
- **Look for the "third way" option.** The best response to pressure is rarely comply or refuse; it is usually a fast, honest alternative that meets the legitimate need (Questions 1, 3, and 10 all share this shape). Train yourself to spot it.
- **Check the least-effective choice carefully.** Marks are lost most often on "least effective". Passive options (wait, defer, caveat-and-carry-on) and integrity-corroding options (backdate, suppress, overclaim) compete for worst; integrity failures almost always rank below passivity.
- **Read who knows what.** Options differ in who is informed, consulted, or bypassed. At your level, bypassing a person before talking to them is usually penalised; protecting someone who spoke up is usually rewarded.
- **Rehearse with real dilemmas.** Recall three genuine pressure moments from your career; write down what you did, what the ideal response was, and the gap. This calibrates you faster than any practice bank.
- **Do not overthink toward exotic answers.** Assessors reward mainstream good judgement executed cleanly, not ingenious edge-case reasoning. Your first considered ranking is usually right.

### Common pitfalls

- **Choosing the idealistic over the effective.** "Refuse on principle" options feel noble but often abandon the outcome to less careful hands (Questions 1 and 10). Effectiveness includes making the right thing actually happen.
- **Selecting passive options.** Waiting for more evidence, sending documents instead of coaching, spreading cuts evenly, delaying quietly — options that avoid the decision score poorly, and the spec literature flags passivity as the classic SJT failure.
- **Ignoring the organisation's values.** These scenarios are scored against public sector culture: transparency, user needs, accessibility, inclusion, collaboration across professions. An answer that would be shrewd in a commercial turf war (hoarding the QA framework) is wrong here.
- **Punishing the messenger or the pressured.** Escalating to formal misconduct processes before any conversation (Question 3, option C) reads as judgement failure, not rigour.
- **Protecting artefacts instead of systems.** Fixing the one flawed review, plan, or finding without asking what let it through misses the head-of-level layer that most of these questions contain.
- **Letting relationships outrank evidence — or vice versa.** The scored best answers consistently protect both; any option that trades one wholly for the other is rarely the strongest available.
