## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive ability assessment is a targeted psychometric test that measures the exact mental processes you need for your role as an associate business architect. Unlike a generic IQ test, it uses practical workplace scenarios and data formats drawn from the everyday tasks of the job, which makes it a much fairer and more useful predictor of how you will perform at work.

For your role, that means the questions will look like the artefacts you genuinely handle: capability maps, process models, organisation charts, service catalogues, project plans, and strategy documents. You will be asked to spot patterns, follow logical rules, check for errors, prioritise competing items, and solve small structured problems.

Typical format and timing: these tests are usually taken online and are timed, most often between 15 and 30 minutes. Questions are multiple choice, and your results are compared against a benchmark or norm group rather than judged in isolation. Many platforms give you a few short, ungraded practice questions first so you can get used to the interface, and some are adaptive, meaning the difficulty adjusts based on your previous answers. Employers often receive a breakdown of speed versus accuracy rather than a single score, so working carefully matters just as much as working quickly.

One reassuring point: cognitive assessments are strictly validated. Questions are vetted to ensure they predict job success without bias. You are not expected to know any secret facts; everything you need is in the question. Your job is simply to think clearly and methodically, which is exactly what you practise every day as an associate business architect.

### What it measures for your role

The cognitive assessment maps directly onto the skills in your role profile:

- **Business architecture**: supporting the production of a multidimensional view of your organisation requires you to hold several layers of information in mind at once (capabilities, processes, systems, teams) and to spot where they connect or conflict. Pattern recognition and logical deduction questions test exactly this.
- **Consultancy**: framing problems so they can easily be understood, and troubleshooting where required, depends on breaking a messy situation into clear parts. Problem-solving questions measure how well you decompose complexity.
- **Making and guiding decisions**: making decisions characterised by managed levels of risk and complexity means weighing rules and constraints correctly. Logical deduction and prioritisation questions reflect this.
- **Methods and tools**: selecting the most appropriate tools and techniques requires you to compare options against criteria systematically, which is the essence of many cognitive test items.
- **Strategic thinking**: working within a strategic context means checking whether detailed activities align with higher-level goals, a form of error checking and rule following.
- **Communicating information** and **aggregating and disaggregating complex information**: several question types below ask you to reconcile information from different sources, mirroring the duty to bring together disconnected information to inform decision making.

### Practice questions

Work through these at your own pace first, then read the explanations. Each question is labelled with an approximate difficulty.

#### Question 1 (easy) - Capability map pattern

Your department's capability map groups capabilities into three tiers. You notice the numbering follows a pattern:

- Tier 1: Strategy Development (1.0), Policy Design (2.0), Service Planning (3.0)
- Tier 2: Strategy Development has 1.1, 1.2, 1.3; Policy Design has 2.1, 2.2, 2.3
- Tier 3: Capability 1.1 has 1.1.1 and 1.1.2; capability 1.2 has 1.2.1 and 1.2.2

Following the same pattern, how many Tier 3 capabilities would you expect under Service Planning (3.0) in total?

- A) 2
- B) 3
- C) 6
- D) 9

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Each Tier 1 capability has three Tier 2 children (for example, 1.1, 1.2, 1.3). Each Tier 2 capability has two Tier 3 children (for example, 1.1.1 and 1.1.2). So Service Planning (3.0) should have three Tier 2 capabilities (3.1, 3.2, 3.3), and each of those should have two Tier 3 capabilities: 3 x 2 = 6. This is a simple example of the hierarchical decomposition you use when you break down complexity into clear outputs. The key habit is to identify the rule at each level before multiplying.

#### Question 2 (easy) - Error checking a RACI matrix

You are reviewing a RACI (Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, Informed) matrix for a business change project. The project standard says: "Every activity must have exactly one Accountable role."

| Activity | Service Owner | Business Architect | Programme Manager |
|---|---|---|---|
| Define target operating model | C | A | A |
| Approve process changes | A | R | C |
| Communicate changes to staff | I | C | A |
| Update capability map | C | A | I |

Which activity breaches the standard?

- A) Define target operating model
- B) Approve process changes
- C) Communicate changes to staff
- D) Update capability map

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Scan the table one row at a time, counting only the letter A. "Define target operating model" has two A entries (Business Architect and Programme Manager), which breaks the rule of exactly one Accountable role per activity. All other rows have exactly one A. Error-checking questions reward a disciplined, row-by-row scan rather than reading the whole table at once. In your real work, spotting a double-accountability like this is exactly the kind of cross-cutting issue you are expected to recognise and raise.

#### Question 3 (easy) - Logical deduction from architecture principles

Your organisation has three architecture principles:

1. All citizen-facing services must meet the GOV.UK Service Standard.
2. Any service that meets the GOV.UK Service Standard must have completed a service assessment.
3. The Licence Renewal service is a citizen-facing service.

Which conclusion must be true?

- A) The Licence Renewal service has completed a service assessment.
- B) All services that complete a service assessment are citizen-facing.
- C) The Licence Renewal service is the only citizen-facing service.
- D) Internal services never complete service assessments.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Chain the statements: Licence Renewal is citizen-facing (principle 3), so it must meet the Service Standard (principle 1), so it must have completed a service assessment (principle 2). Option B reverses the logic (the rules say meeting the standard requires an assessment, not the other way round). Options C and D go beyond what the statements say. In assessments, only conclude what follows strictly from the given rules, just as when you apply architectural methods following direction and wider strategy.

#### Question 4 (moderate) - Prioritisation with constraints

You have four tasks on Monday morning and can complete only three before a 14:00 governance board:

- Task W: Update the business capability heat map the board will review (takes 2 hours).
- Task X: Respond to a stakeholder email about next month's workshop (takes 30 minutes).
- Task Y: Fix a version-control error in the strategy alignment document that the board will also review (takes 1 hour).
- Task Z: Draft a first-cut process model for a project starting in three weeks (takes 2 hours).

You start at 09:00 and must take a 1-hour lunch break at 12:00. Which combination lets you complete the most board-critical work on time?

- A) W, X, Z
- B) W, Y, X
- C) Y, Z, X
- D) W, Y, Z

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** First identify what matters for the 14:00 deadline: the board will review the heat map (W) and the strategy alignment document (Y), so both are board-critical. W + Y = 3 hours. Adding X (30 minutes) gives 3.5 hours of work. From 09:00 to 12:00 is 3 hours; lunch 12:00 to 13:00; then 13:00 to 14:00 is 1 more hour, giving 4 hours available. W, Y, and X fit comfortably (3.5 hours) and cover everything the board needs. Option D also includes both board-critical items but totals 5 hours, which does not fit. Options A and C each miss one board-critical task. The lesson: separate "urgent for the deadline" from "important but later", then check the arithmetic of the time budget.

#### Question 5 (moderate) - Reconciling disconnected information

You are aggregating information for a decision paper. Three sources describe the same business area:

- The HR system says the Casework directorate has 120 staff.
- The finance report says the Casework directorate's staff costs equal 130 full-time-equivalent (FTE) salaries.
- The directorate's own organisation chart shows 118 posts, of which 10 are vacant.

Which statement best describes what you can reliably conclude?

- A) The three sources agree once vacancies are considered.
- B) The sources disagree, and the differences should be flagged before the decision paper relies on any single figure.
- C) The finance report is wrong because it shows the largest number.
- D) The HR system is correct because HR systems are authoritative for staff numbers.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Compare the figures: 120 staff (HR), 130 FTE costs (finance), 118 posts with 10 vacant, meaning 108 filled posts (org chart). Even allowing for vacancies, the numbers do not reconcile: 108 filled posts is not 120 staff, and 130 FTE of cost exceeds both. You cannot tell from the information given which source is right, so options C and D assume too much, and option A is factually wrong. The professionally sound conclusion is B: flag the discrepancy. This mirrors your duty to bring together disconnected information to inform decision making; noticing that sources conflict is often more valuable than picking a number.

#### Question 6 (moderate) - Process model logic

A target-state process model for grant applications has these rules:

- Every application goes through Validation first.
- Applications that fail Validation go to Rejection Notice.
- Applications that pass Validation go to Assessment.
- Applications scoring 70 or above in Assessment go to Approval; all others go to Panel Review.
- Panel Review can send an application to Approval or to Rejection Notice.

An application has just arrived at Approval. Which of the following must be true?

- A) It scored 70 or above in Assessment.
- B) It passed Validation.
- C) It went through Panel Review.
- D) It scored below 70 in Assessment.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Trace every route into Approval. There are two: directly from Assessment (score 70+) or via Panel Review. Both routes require the application to have passed Validation first, because only passing applications reach Assessment at all. So B must be true. A is not certain (it could have arrived via Panel Review), C is not certain (it could have come straight from Assessment), and D is only true for the Panel Review route. Process-model questions reward tracing every possible path rather than the most obvious one, a core habit when you produce and quality-check process artefacts.

#### Question 7 (moderate) - Spotting the cross-cutting issue

You review four project summaries:

- Project 1 (Benefits directorate) is building a new document-upload feature for citizens.
- Project 2 (Licensing directorate) is procuring a document-upload component from a supplier.
- Project 3 (Casework directorate) reports delays because its in-house document-upload tool failed accessibility testing.
- Project 4 (Communications) is redesigning the department's intranet home page.

As an associate business architect, which observation is the most valuable to raise?

- A) Project 4 should be stopped because intranet work is low value.
- B) Three projects are independently solving the same document-upload need, which suggests an opportunity for a shared solution.
- C) Project 3 should abandon accessibility testing to catch up.
- D) Project 2 should be praised for using a supplier.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Your role level description says you should "recognise issues that cut across different areas of the organisation and present opportunities for improvement". Projects 1, 2, and 3 are all tackling document upload separately: duplicated effort, duplicated cost, and inconsistent citizen experience. Raising this pattern is classic business architecture value. Option A makes a judgement you have no evidence for. Option C is unacceptable: accessibility is a legal and ethical requirement in UK public services. Option D is merely a comment, not an insight. The test skill here is pattern recognition across independent items; read all items before judging any one of them.

#### Question 8 (moderate) - Sequence and dependency logic

A business change roadmap has five activities with these dependencies:

- Activity B cannot start until Activity A finishes.
- Activity C cannot start until Activity A finishes.
- Activity D cannot start until both B and C finish.
- Activity E cannot start until C finishes.

Activities take: A = 2 weeks, B = 3 weeks, C = 1 week, D = 2 weeks, E = 4 weeks. All activities start as early as possible. After how many weeks does the whole roadmap finish?

- A) 7 weeks
- B) 8 weeks
- C) 9 weeks
- D) 12 weeks

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Work out the earliest finish of each activity. A finishes at week 2. B runs weeks 2 to 5. C runs weeks 2 to 3. D needs both B (week 5) and C (week 3), so it runs weeks 5 to 7. E needs C (week 3), so it runs weeks 3 to 7. The latest finisher is week 7 (both D and E). So the answer is 7 weeks. A common error is adding all durations (12 weeks), which ignores that B and C, and later D and E, run in parallel. Dependency reasoning like this underpins your work ensuring business change is implemented effectively and sequenced sensibly.

#### Question 9 (moderate) - Rule application with exceptions

Your organisation's design authority applies these rules to proposals:

1. Proposals over £100,000 require a full business case.
2. Proposals that reuse an existing approved component are exempt from rule 1, unless they involve personal data.
3. All proposals involving personal data require a Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA).

A proposal costs £150,000, reuses an existing approved component, and involves personal data. What does it require?

- A) Nothing beyond standard approval
- B) A full business case only
- C) A DPIA only
- D) Both a full business case and a DPIA

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Apply each rule in turn. Rule 1: over £100,000, so a full business case is needed. Rule 2: it reuses an approved component, which would normally grant an exemption, but the exemption does not apply because the proposal involves personal data, so the full business case is still required. Rule 3: personal data means a DPIA is required. Therefore both documents are needed. Exception-within-exception questions are common in cognitive tests and in real governance frameworks alike; slow down and check whether the exemption's own conditions are met before applying it.

#### Question 10 (hard) - Consistency across architecture views

You maintain three views of the same business area, and a colleague asks you to check them for consistency:

- The capability view says the Contact Management capability is delivered by two teams: Front Office and Digital Services.
- The process view says the "Handle enquiry" process is performed only by the Front Office team.
- The system view says the CRM system supports the "Handle enquiry" process and is administered by Digital Services.
- A note says: every team that delivers a capability must perform at least one process within that capability.

Assuming "Handle enquiry" is the only process in the Contact Management capability, which inconsistency should you flag?

- A) The CRM system should be administered by Front Office.
- B) Digital Services delivers the capability but performs no process within it, contradicting the note.
- C) Front Office should not perform "Handle enquiry".
- D) There is no inconsistency.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The rule says every team delivering a capability must perform at least one process in it. Digital Services is listed as delivering Contact Management, but the only process in that capability ("Handle enquiry") is performed solely by Front Office. So Digital Services delivers the capability while performing none of its processes: a direct contradiction. Administering a supporting system (option A's territory) is not the same as performing a process, so it does not satisfy the rule. This question mirrors real multidimensional modelling: different views of the organisation must reconcile, and finding the mismatch is a core associate-level task.

#### Question 11 (hard) - Analytical reasoning about stakeholder statements

Three stakeholders comment on a proposed target operating model:

- The Finance lead says: "The model reduces running costs and requires no new roles."
- The HR lead says: "The model requires at least one new role."
- The Operations lead says: "The model does not reduce running costs."

You have independently verified two facts: exactly one of the three stakeholders is fully correct, and the model definitely requires at least one new role. Which stakeholder is fully correct?

- A) The Finance lead
- B) The HR lead
- C) The Operations lead
- D) It cannot be determined

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Start from the verified fact: at least one new role is required. That immediately makes the HR lead's statement true, and it makes the Finance lead's statement false, because Finance's claim is a conjunction ("reduces costs AND no new roles") and the "no new roles" clause fails. So HR is correct and Finance is not. Now apply the other verified fact: exactly one stakeholder is fully correct. Since HR is already correct, the Operations lead must be wrong, meaning the model does in fact reduce running costs. Everything is consistent: costs are reduced, a new role is required, and HR alone is fully correct. The practical lesson for your role is real: compound stakeholder claims ("X and Y") should be decomposed and verified clause by clause, because one true clause often hides one false clause. When you help resolve disputes between peers and stakeholders, break every assertion into its smallest testable parts before judging who is right.

#### Question 12 (hard) - Problem decomposition

A director tells you: "Our licensing service is too slow." You must break this down before proposing any solution. Which decomposition is the most useful first artefact?

- A) A list of the five newest technologies that could speed things up
- B) A breakdown of the end-to-end licensing journey into stages, with the average elapsed time and volume at each stage
- C) A survey asking staff whether they agree the service is slow
- D) A proposal to hire more staff in the licensing team

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** "Too slow" is a symptom, not a defined problem. The associate business architect's job is to break down complexity and produce clear outputs: decomposing the journey into stages with times and volumes turns a vague complaint into measurable parts, revealing where delay actually accumulates. Option A jumps to solutions before understanding the problem. Option C gathers opinions rather than evidence about where time goes. Option D commits resources before knowing whether staffing is the bottleneck. In cognitive tests, "what should you examine first" questions reward the answer that structures the problem rather than the answer that acts fastest.

#### Question 13 (hard) - Integrated reasoning

Your organisation's strategy names three goals: improve citizen satisfaction, reduce cost per transaction, and increase staff capability. A programme proposes four initiatives:

- Initiative P: automate document checks (reduces cost per transaction; needs staff retraining)
- Initiative Q: extend call-centre hours (improves satisfaction; increases cost per transaction)
- Initiative R: launch a staff learning academy (increases staff capability; cost-neutral)
- Initiative S: replace the branding on all letters (no measured effect on any goal)

The programme can fund exactly two initiatives and must ensure every funded initiative advances at least one strategic goal without setting any goal backwards. Which pair complies?

- A) P and Q
- B) Q and R
- C) P and R
- D) R and S

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Check each initiative against the rule "advances at least one goal without setting any goal backwards". P advances cost reduction; retraining is a need, not a goal set backwards, and it even connects to staff capability: compliant. Q advances satisfaction but increases cost per transaction, setting a goal backwards: non-compliant, which eliminates options A and B. R advances staff capability and harms nothing: compliant. S advances no goal, so funding it breaches the rule that every funded initiative must advance at least one goal, eliminating D. That leaves C: P and R. This is exactly the alignment-checking you do when you ensure design work from projects and programmes aligns with strategy: test every item against every stated criterion, and let the eliminations do the work.

### Preparation tips

- **Practise with your own artefacts.** Take a capability map, RACI matrix, or roadmap from your daily work and ask yourself questions like the ones above: what pattern governs the numbering, which rows breach the rule, what is the critical path? This builds test skills and job skills at the same time.
- **Learn to trace every path.** Many wrong answers in logic questions come from following only the most obvious route through a process or dependency chain. Make it a habit to ask "is there another way this could have happened?"
- **Time yourself gently at first.** Start untimed and focus on accuracy; then introduce a timer once your method is solid. As an associate, building the right habits matters more than raw speed, and speed follows naturally.
- **Verbalise your reasoning.** Explaining your answer out loud (or in writing) is excellent practice for the "explain complex business problems using clear language" part of your role, and it exposes gaps in your logic.
- **Rest and pace yourself before the real thing.** Cognitive tests measure clear thinking, and clear thinking needs sleep, hydration, and a calm start. Do the practice questions the platform offers so the interface holds no surprises.

### Common pitfalls

- **Reading the whole table when the rule tells you what to scan for.** If the standard says "exactly one Accountable", scan for the letter A row by row; do not absorb every cell.
- **Adding durations that actually run in parallel.** Dependency questions punish serial thinking; sketch the chains before you calculate.
- **Concluding more than the rules state.** Options that reverse an if-then statement, or that generalise from one case to all cases, are classic traps.
- **Getting stuck on one hard question.** Adaptive or not, most tests reward moving on and returning later. One question is never worth a third of your time.
- **Ignoring exemption conditions.** Real governance rules, and test questions modelled on them, often contain an exception to the exception. Read to the end of every rule.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment evaluates your ability to analyse, interpret, and make logical decisions using numerical data. The good news for you: it mimics the data-handling demands of your actual role, focusing on practical business arithmetic rather than abstract formulas. There is no calculus, no algebra beyond simple rearrangement, and no memorised equations. The core toolkit is percentages, ratios, differences, averages, and careful reading of tables and charts.

For an associate business architect, the numbers in the test look like the numbers in your working week: programme budgets, staff full-time-equivalent (FTE) counts, transaction volumes, cost per transaction, benefits projections, capacity figures, and survey results. The question inputs are typically tables, simple charts, and short data statements, exactly like the evidence packs you assemble when you assess the impact of strategies on the organisation or bring together disconnected information to inform decision making.

Typical format and timing: most numeric reasoning tests are taken online with a strict time limit, often around 20 to 30 minutes for 15 to 25 questions, which works out at roughly one minute per question. Most assessments allow or provide an onscreen calculator, because the test prioritises interpretation over mental arithmetic. Speed matters, but accuracy matters more: quick and accurate data extraction is the skill being measured, and getting stuck on one hard multi-stage problem is the classic way to lose marks.

Do not worry if maths has never felt like your strongest subject. These tests reward method, not brilliance: read the table headings, note the units, identify exactly which figures the question needs, and do the arithmetic step by step. All of that can be practised, and this section gives you plenty of realistic practice.

### What it measures for your role

Numeric reasoning maps to your role profile skills as follows:

- **Business architecture**: assessing the impact of strategies on the organisation, and highlighting risks and issues, almost always involves quantifying that impact: costs, savings, headcount changes, and volume shifts.
- **Strategic thinking**: communicating how activities meet strategic goals frequently means comparing performance metrics against targets, calculating gaps, and projecting trends.
- **Consultancy**: providing advice, guidance, and recommendations is far more persuasive when grounded in correctly calculated evidence. A recommendation built on a misread percentage undermines your credibility.
- **Making and guiding decisions**: decisions characterised by managed levels of risk and complexity often hinge on numbers: which option costs less per outcome, which delivers benefits sooner, which stays within capacity.
- **Stakeholder relationship management**: working to improve stakeholder relationships "using evidence to explain decisions" means being able to extract, verify, and present figures accurately and honestly.
- **Aggregating and disaggregating complex information**: rolling figures up from teams to directorates, or breaking a departmental total down into its parts, is numerical aggregation and disaggregation in its purest form.

### Practice questions

An onscreen or desk calculator is fine for all of these. Try each question before reading the worked arithmetic.

#### Question 1 (easy) - Percentage of a budget

A business change programme has a total budget of £2,400,000. The business architecture workstream is allocated 15% of the total. How much is allocated to the business architecture workstream?

- A) £160,000
- B) £240,000
- C) £360,000
- D) £400,000

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 15% of £2,400,000 = 0.15 x 2,400,000 = £360,000. A quick sense-check: 10% would be £240,000 and 5% would be £120,000, so 15% is £240,000 + £120,000 = £360,000. Building the habit of a ten-percent sense-check helps you catch slips under time pressure, and it is exactly the mental arithmetic you use when scanning a budget table in a governance paper.

#### Question 2 (easy) - Reading a volumes table

The table shows monthly application volumes for a licensing service:

| Month | Online applications | Paper applications |
|---|---|---|
| April | 8,200 | 1,800 |
| May | 8,900 | 1,600 |
| June | 9,400 | 1,400 |

What percentage of total applications in June were made online?

- A) 84%
- B) 87%
- C) 90%
- D) 94%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** June total = 9,400 + 1,400 = 10,800. Online share = 9,400 / 10,800 = 0.8703..., which is 87% to the nearest whole percent. Note the trap in option D: 9,400 looks like "94" but the percentage is of the total, not of 10,000. Always compute the denominator explicitly; never assume it is a round number. Channel-shift percentages like this appear constantly in the strategy alignment work you support.

#### Question 3 (easy) - FTE arithmetic

A directorate's target operating model reduces its workforce from 240 FTE to 210 FTE. What is the percentage reduction?

- A) 10%
- B) 12.5%
- C) 14.3%
- D) 30%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The reduction is 240 - 210 = 30 FTE. As a percentage of the original: 30 / 240 = 0.125 = 12.5%. Option C (14.3%) is what you get if you wrongly divide by the new figure (30 / 210). Percentage change is always measured against the starting value unless the question says otherwise. Option D confuses the absolute change (30 FTE) with a percentage. Being precise about the base of a percentage is essential when you highlight the impact of a strategy on the organisation.

#### Question 4 (moderate) - Cost per transaction comparison

You are comparing two service delivery options for an evidence pack:

| Option | Annual running cost | Annual transactions |
|---|---|---|
| Current service | £1,840,000 | 460,000 |
| Proposed digital service | £1,275,000 | 425,000 |

By how much would the cost per transaction fall under the proposed option?

- A) £0.50
- B) £1.00
- C) £1.50
- D) £4.00

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Current cost per transaction = £1,840,000 / 460,000 = £4.00. Proposed cost per transaction = £1,275,000 / 425,000 = £3.00. The fall is £4.00 - £3.00 = £1.00. Option D is the trap for candidates who report the current unit cost instead of the difference. Notice also that total cost falls AND volume falls; unit-cost comparisons handle that gracefully, which is why cost per transaction is a favourite metric in public sector business cases and a favourite calculation in numeric tests.

#### Question 5 (moderate) - Ratios in a capability assessment

A capability maturity review rates 60 business capabilities. The results are in the ratio 1 : 3 : 2 for "leading", "established", and "developing" respectively. How many capabilities are rated "developing"?

- A) 10
- B) 20
- C) 30
- D) 40

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The ratio 1 : 3 : 2 has 1 + 3 + 2 = 6 parts. Each part is 60 / 6 = 10 capabilities. "Developing" has 2 parts: 2 x 10 = 20. As a check, the full split is 10 leading, 30 established, 20 developing, which sums to 60. Ratio questions are solved by finding the value of one part first. Maturity distributions like this are exactly what you summarise when supporting a multidimensional view of the organisation.

#### Question 6 (moderate) - Benefits realisation projection

A business case projects savings of £150,000 in year 1, with savings growing by 20% each year as adoption increases. What are the projected savings in year 3?

- A) £180,000
- B) £210,000
- C) £216,000
- D) £270,000

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Growth of 20% per year compounds. Year 2 = £150,000 x 1.2 = £180,000. Year 3 = £180,000 x 1.2 = £216,000. Option B (£210,000) is the result of adding 20% of the original figure twice (simple rather than compound growth): £150,000 + £30,000 + £30,000. Read growth statements carefully: "growing by 20% each year" means each year's figure is 1.2 times the previous year's figure. Compounding appears in benefits profiles, demand forecasts, and cost inflation, so it is worth being fluent with it.

#### Question 7 (moderate) - Weighted scores in an options appraisal

Your team scores two options against three criteria. Criteria weights: strategic fit 50%, cost 30%, deliverability 20%. Scores are out of 10:

| Criterion | Option A | Option B |
|---|---|---|
| Strategic fit | 8 | 6 |
| Cost | 5 | 9 |
| Deliverability | 6 | 7 |

Which option wins, and by how many weighted points?

- A) Option A wins by 0.4
- B) Option B wins by 0.4
- C) Option B wins by 3.0
- D) Option A wins by 1.0

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Multiply each score by its weight, then sum. Option A: (8 x 0.5) + (5 x 0.3) + (6 x 0.2) = 4.0 + 1.5 + 1.2 = 6.7. Option B: (6 x 0.5) + (9 x 0.3) + (7 x 0.2) = 3.0 + 2.7 + 1.4 = 7.1. Option B wins by 7.1 - 6.7 = 0.4 weighted points. Option C is the trap for candidates who ignore the weights: the unweighted totals are 19 versus 22, a difference of 3.0. Weighting changes outcomes: Option A leads on the heaviest criterion (strategic fit) but loses overall because Option B's advantages on cost and deliverability outweigh it. Weighted options appraisals are standard in the methodologies you propose in your consultancy work, so being fluent with them pays off twice.

#### Question 8 (moderate) - Aggregating directorate figures

You are rolling up quarterly transaction volumes from three directorates into a departmental total for a strategy paper:

| Directorate | Q1 | Q2 |
|---|---|---|
| Benefits | 125,000 | 138,000 |
| Licensing | 64,000 | 58,000 |
| Casework | 41,000 | 44,000 |

What was the percentage change in the departmental total from Q1 to Q2, to one decimal place?

- A) +4.3%
- B) +4.5%
- C) +10.4%
- D) -4.3%

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Aggregate first, then compare. Q1 total = 125,000 + 64,000 + 41,000 = 230,000. Q2 total = 138,000 + 58,000 + 44,000 = 240,000. Change = 240,000 - 230,000 = 10,000. Percentage change = 10,000 / 230,000 = 0.04347... = +4.3% to one decimal place. A tempting error is averaging the three directorates' individual percentage changes, which gives a different (wrong) answer because the directorates differ in size. When you aggregate complex information from across the organisation, always sum first and calculate percentages on the totals, unless you genuinely want a per-directorate view.

#### Question 9 (moderate) - Capacity and demand

A casework team can process 1,250 cases per week at full capacity. Current demand is 1,100 cases per week and is forecast to grow by 5% per quarter. After how many quarters will demand first exceed capacity?

- A) 2
- B) 3
- C) 4
- D) 5

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Compound the demand quarter by quarter. After 1 quarter: 1,100 x 1.05 = 1,155. After 2 quarters: 1,155 x 1.05 = 1,212.75. After 3 quarters: 1,212.75 x 1.05 = 1,273.39. Capacity is 1,250, so demand first exceeds capacity after 3 quarters (1,273 > 1,250; at 2 quarters, 1,213 < 1,250). This "when does the line cross" pattern appears in capacity planning, benefits tracking, and risk analysis. In a timed test, compute one step at a time and stop as soon as you cross the threshold.

#### Question 10 (hard) - Multi-step cost model

A proposed shared corporate service would replace three separate team functions:

| Current function | Annual staff cost | Annual system cost |
|---|---|---|
| Function 1 | £420,000 | £85,000 |
| Function 2 | £310,000 | £60,000 |
| Function 3 | £270,000 | £55,000 |

The shared service would cost £750,000 per year in staff and £120,000 per year for a single system. Transition costs are a one-off £280,000. In the first year (including transition costs), what is the net financial position of moving to the shared service?

- A) Net saving of £330,000
- B) Net saving of £50,000
- C) Net cost of £50,000
- D) Net saving of £610,000

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Step 1: current total = (420,000 + 310,000 + 270,000) + (85,000 + 60,000 + 55,000) = 1,000,000 + 200,000 = £1,200,000. Step 2: shared service annual cost = 750,000 + 120,000 = £870,000. Step 3: annual saving = 1,200,000 - 870,000 = £330,000. Step 4: first year includes one-off transition costs, so net position = 330,000 - 280,000 = £50,000 saving. Option A is the trap for candidates who forget the transition costs; option D is the trap for those who compare staff costs only against the full shared cost. Multi-step questions reward writing intermediate results down rather than chaining everything in your head, exactly as you would in a real impact assessment where your working must be transparent to stakeholders.

#### Question 11 (hard) - Survey data interpretation

A stakeholder survey about a new operating model receives 480 responses. 65% of respondents are from operational teams and the rest are from corporate teams. Among operational respondents, 40% support the model; among corporate respondents, 75% support it. How many respondents support the model in total?

- A) 251
- B) 276
- C) 291
- D) 312

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Operational respondents = 0.65 x 480 = 312. Corporate respondents = 480 - 312 = 168. Operational supporters = 0.40 x 312 = 124.8, which in a survey context rounds to about 125 people; keep it as 124.8 for now. Corporate supporters = 0.75 x 168 = 126. Total = 124.8 + 126 = 250.8, approximately 251. Notice the overall support rate is 251 / 480, about 52%: closer to the operational figure because operational staff dominate the sample. This is a weighted-average effect: never average 40% and 75% to get 57.5% when group sizes differ. Understanding whose voice dominates aggregated figures is vital when you use evidence to explain decisions to stakeholders fairly.

#### Question 12 (hard) - Reading a burn-down of programme budget

A transformation programme has a £3,600,000 budget over 12 months, planned to be spent evenly. At the end of month 8, actual spend is £2,700,000. If the current average monthly overspend rate continues for the remaining months, by how much will the programme exceed its total budget?

- A) £150,000
- B) £300,000
- C) £450,000
- D) £900,000

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Planned monthly spend = 3,600,000 / 12 = £300,000, so planned spend by month 8 = £2,400,000. Actual spend = £2,700,000, meaning actual monthly average = 2,700,000 / 8 = £337,500. If that continues for the remaining 4 months: 4 x 337,500 = £1,350,000. Projected total = 2,700,000 + 1,350,000 = £4,050,000. Overrun = 4,050,000 - 3,600,000 = £450,000. Option B is the trap for candidates who report only the overspend to date (2,700,000 - 2,400,000 = 300,000) without projecting forward. Read the question stem twice: "if the current rate continues" signals a projection, not a snapshot. Spotting an emerging overspend and quantifying its trajectory is exactly the risk-highlighting your business architecture skill calls for.

#### Question 13 (hard) - Disaggregating a combined figure

A department reports that its two contact channels together handled 180,000 contacts last quarter at a combined average cost of £2.90 per contact. You know the phone channel's average cost is £4.50 per contact and the digital channel's is £1.30 per contact. How many contacts came through the phone channel?

- A) 60,000
- B) 72,000
- C) 90,000
- D) 108,000

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Let P be phone contacts, so digital contacts are 180,000 - P. Total cost two ways: 4.50P + 1.30(180,000 - P) = 2.90 x 180,000. Left side: 4.50P + 234,000 - 1.30P = 3.20P + 234,000. Right side: 522,000. So 3.20P = 288,000, and P = 90,000. Sense-check: £2.90 sits exactly halfway between £1.30 and £4.50, so the channels must have equal volumes, and 90,000 each fits perfectly. This "unmixing" of a blended average is disaggregation in action: taking one combined figure and recovering the parts behind it, a skill your role profile names directly.

#### Question 14 (hard) - Percentage points versus percentages

Digital uptake for a service rose from 60% of transactions to 72% of transactions after a campaign. A colleague drafts a slide saying uptake "rose by 12%". As the reviewer, which correction is right?

- A) No correction needed; 12% is accurate.
- B) Uptake rose by 12 percentage points, which is a 20% increase on the previous level.
- C) Uptake rose by 20 percentage points, which is a 12% increase on the previous level.
- D) Uptake rose by 12 percentage points, which is a 12% increase on the previous level.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The move from 60% to 72% is a rise of 12 percentage points (72 - 60). As a relative change it is 12 / 60 = 0.20 = a 20% increase. Confusing percentage points with percentages is one of the most common numerical errors in government reporting, and assessments test it deliberately. When you communicate complex information about the organisation to stakeholders, precision here protects your credibility: "12 percentage points" and "20% increase" describe the same fact from two angles, and a good slide states which one it means.

#### Question 15 (moderate) - Choosing the right chart figure

You are preparing a one-page summary for a director. The strategy sets a target: "reduce average end-to-end processing time to 10 days by year end". The latest data shows:

| Quarter | Average processing time (days) | Cases processed |
|---|---|---|
| Q1 | 16 | 22,000 |
| Q2 | 14 | 24,500 |
| Q3 | 12.5 | 25,000 |

If the average processing time continues to fall by the same absolute amount as it did between Q2 and Q3, what will it be at the end of Q4, and does it meet the target?

- A) 11 days; target met
- B) 11 days; target not met
- C) 10 days; target met
- D) 12 days; target not met

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The fall from Q2 to Q3 is 14 - 12.5 = 1.5 days. Continuing that absolute trend, Q4 = 12.5 - 1.5 = 11 days. The target is 10 days, and 11 days is above the target, so the target is NOT met. Option A is the trap for candidates who compute the projection correctly but misread the direction of the target: "reduce to 10 days" means the figure must be at or below 10. Two separate skills are being tested at once here: trend projection (arithmetic) and target interpretation (careful reading). In your role, communicating how activities meet strategic goals means being honest when the trajectory is positive but insufficient, which is exactly the story this data tells: improving, yet still short of the goal, so further intervention may be needed. That nuance, "on track in direction but not in pace", is one of the most useful phrases a business architect can bring to a governance discussion, and it comes straight from doing this simple arithmetic correctly.

#### Question 16 (easy) - Sense-checking a stakeholder's claim

A stakeholder tells a meeting: "Digital applications have doubled this year, so we can halve the paper-handling team." Last year there were 40,000 digital applications and 30,000 paper applications; this year there are 80,000 digital and 27,000 paper. The paper-handling team's workload depends only on paper volumes. What does the data actually support?

- A) The claim is fully supported; paper work has halved.
- B) Digital applications have doubled, but paper applications have fallen by only 10%, so halving the paper team is not supported by this data.
- C) Paper applications have halved, so the team can be halved.
- D) Digital applications have not doubled.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Check each part of the claim separately. Digital: 80,000 / 40,000 = 2, so digital applications have indeed doubled; option D is wrong. Paper: 27,000 versus 30,000 is a fall of 3,000, and 3,000 / 30,000 = 10%; paper volumes have fallen only 10%, not 50%, so options A and C are wrong. The stakeholder has linked two true-sounding numbers with a false inference: growth in one channel says nothing direct about workload in the other. Option B is correct. This is perhaps the most job-relevant numeric skill of all for you: politely testing the arithmetic inside a confident claim, using evidence to explain decisions, before the claim becomes a staffing plan.

### Preparation tips

- **Refresh the core four: percentages, ratios, averages, and percentage change.** Nearly every question in this test type is built from these. Ten minutes of practice a day for two weeks beats a single long cramming session.
- **Always identify the denominator.** Before calculating any percentage, say to yourself "percentage of what?". Most traps in numeric tests live in the denominator.
- **Write down intermediate results.** Multi-step questions are much safer when you note each stage (totals, unit costs, differences) instead of chaining calculations mentally. This mirrors good professional practice: your working should be checkable.
- **Practise with real public data.** Departmental annual reports and performance dashboards on GOV.UK are full of tables just like the ones in these tests. Pick a table and ask yourself: what changed, by what percentage, and what is the unit cost?
- **Use the calculator wisely.** It removes arithmetic strain but cannot choose the right numbers for you. Extract the figures first, plan the calculation, then compute.
- **Manage the clock kindly.** Aim for a steady pace of about a minute per question, and give yourself permission to skip and return. One skipped question is a tactic; one ten-minute struggle is a trap.

### Common pitfalls

- **Misreading chart and table labels**: confusing monthly with annual data, or missing scale qualifiers like "in thousands". Read the headings and units before the numbers.
- **Using the wrong base for percentage change**: dividing by the new value instead of the original, as in Question 3 above.
- **Averaging percentages across different-sized groups**: as Question 11 showed, group sizes matter; aggregate the underlying counts instead.
- **Confusing percentage points with percentages**: a rise from 60% to 72% is 12 points but a 20% increase.
- **Over-calculating**: sometimes a quick estimate or a sense-check (like the halfway observation in Question 13) reveals the answer faster than full computation.
- **Time sink questions**: getting stuck on a single difficult multi-stage problem instead of banking easier marks first. Skip, flag, return.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment evaluates how well you understand, analyse, and draw correct conclusions from written business information. It mirrors the exact reading and comprehension demands of your role: as an associate business architect you spend much of your time reading strategy documents, policy papers, standards, meeting notes, and stakeholder emails, then explaining what they mean using clear language and artefacts. This test measures the reading half of that skill.

The classic format gives you a short, dense passage followed by statements. For each statement you choose one of three answers:

- **True**: the statement definitely follows from the passage.
- **False**: the statement definitely contradicts the passage.
- **Cannot Say**: the passage does not give enough information to decide either way.

Some tests also include multiple-choice comprehension questions, such as "which of the following best summarises the author's main point?" or "according to the passage, which action is required?".

The golden rule is fact-based logic: you must answer based strictly on the text provided, ignoring outside knowledge. Even if you know from your own experience that something is usually true in government, if the passage does not say it, the answer is Cannot Say. This discipline feels strange at first but becomes natural with practice, and it is genuinely useful professionally: separating "what the document actually says" from "what I assume it means" prevents costly misunderstandings in architecture work.

Typical format and timing: tests are usually online and time-pressured, often giving less than one minute per question to simulate workplace deadlines. Passages use relevant terminology from reports, emails, and policy documents, exactly like the material below. You will not need any specialist knowledge beyond careful reading.

### What it measures for your role

Verbal reasoning maps to your role profile skills like this:

- **Communicating information**: being "flexible and capable of proactive and reactive communication" starts with accurately understanding what others have written. Misreading an email or policy is the fastest way to communicate the wrong thing.
- **Business architecture**: clearly communicating business architecture frameworks, principles, and techniques requires you first to read and digest those frameworks precisely. Standards documents reward exact reading.
- **Consultancy**: framing problems so they can easily be understood means extracting the true problem from wordy source material, and providing recommendations means understanding precisely what guidance documents do and do not permit.
- **Stakeholder relationship management**: tailoring communication to stakeholders' needs depends on correctly interpreting what stakeholders have said and written, including nuance and qualifications.
- **Making and guiding decisions**: resolving disputes "considering all views and opinions" requires you to represent each written view accurately, not as you assume it to be.
- **Strategic thinking**: working within a strategic context means reading strategy documents closely enough to know what they commit to, what they merely aspire to, and what they leave open.

### Practice questions

For each passage, answer the statements strictly from the text. Explanations point back to the exact wording.

#### Passage A - Internal architecture standard

Read this extract from a departmental standard:

"All programmes must produce a business capability impact assessment before their first gate review. The assessment must identify every capability the programme will change, and must rate the scale of change to each capability as minor, moderate, or major. Programmes rated as changing any capability at the 'major' level must present their assessment to the Design Authority. The Design Authority meets fortnightly and may require programmes to revise their delivery plans. Programmes changing capabilities only at minor or moderate levels may proceed without Design Authority review, although they are encouraged to seek advice from the business architecture team."

#### Question 1 (easy)

Statement: Every programme must produce a business capability impact assessment before its first gate review.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A (True)**

**Explanation:** The first sentence says exactly this: "All programmes must produce a business capability impact assessment before their first gate review." The statement is a near-direct restatement, so it is True. Easy questions like this check that you can match a claim to explicit text; take the free mark and move on quickly to bank time for harder items.

#### Question 2 (moderate)

Statement: The Design Authority meets every week.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B (False)**

**Explanation:** The passage says "The Design Authority meets fortnightly", meaning every two weeks. "Every week" directly contradicts "fortnightly", so the statement is False, not Cannot Say. The distinction matters: False requires an actual contradiction with the text, which we have here. Watch for frequency words (fortnightly, quarterly, annually); tests love to swap them.

#### Question 3 (moderate)

Statement: A programme that changes three capabilities at the moderate level must present its assessment to the Design Authority.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B (False)**

**Explanation:** The trigger for Design Authority review is "changing any capability at the 'major' level". A programme changing capabilities only at moderate level, however many of them, falls under the sentence "Programmes changing capabilities only at minor or moderate levels may proceed without Design Authority review". Three moderates do not add up to one major; the passage gives no such accumulation rule. The statement contradicts the text, so it is False. This mirrors real governance reading: thresholds trigger on severity, not volume, unless the standard says otherwise.

#### Question 4 (hard)

Statement: The business architecture team must review programmes that change capabilities at the minor level.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B (False)**

**Explanation:** Careful: the passage says such programmes "are encouraged to seek advice from the business architecture team". "Encouraged to seek advice" is optional for the programme, and nothing anywhere says the business architecture team "must review" anything. Because the passage states these programmes "may proceed without Design Authority review" and only encourages advice-seeking, a mandatory review requirement contradicts the permissive wording of the text. Note the subtlety: if the statement had been "the business architecture team sometimes advises programmes", the answer would be Cannot Say (plausible but not stated as fact). Modal verbs (must, may, should, encouraged) carry the legal weight in standards; read them like a lawyer.

#### Question 5 (hard)

Statement: The Design Authority has required at least one programme to revise its delivery plans.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C (Cannot Say)**

**Explanation:** The passage says the Design Authority "may require programmes to revise their delivery plans". That establishes a power, not an event. Whether the power has ever been used is simply not stated, so we cannot say. This is the classic Cannot Say pattern: the text describes what is possible or permitted, and the statement asserts what has actually happened. Keep the distinction between capability and occurrence sharp; it appears in almost every verbal reasoning test and in plenty of real strategy documents too.

#### Passage B - Stakeholder email

Read this email from a delivery manager:

"Hi. Following yesterday's health check, the team flagged two issues. First, the process maps we received last month do not match how caseworkers actually handle exceptions; the maps show exceptions going to a supervisor queue, but in practice caseworkers resolve most exceptions themselves and only escalate complex ones. Second, the team is unclear which version of the target operating model is current, because the shared drive contains three versions with different dates. We would like the business architecture team to confirm the current version by Friday, as our sprint planning depends on it. If we do not hear back by then, we will plan against the March version, which the programme board saw."

#### Question 6 (easy)

Statement: The team raised two issues following the health check.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A (True)**

**Explanation:** The email states: "Following yesterday's health check, the team flagged two issues." The statement matches directly. Notice how the health check detail echoes your Communicating information skill, which includes identifying issues through Agile health checks and working with others to address them; the test passage is drawn from your real working world.

#### Question 7 (moderate)

Statement: According to the email, caseworkers escalate all exceptions to a supervisor queue.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B (False)**

**Explanation:** The email says the maps show exceptions going to a supervisor queue, "but in practice caseworkers resolve most exceptions themselves and only escalate complex ones". The statement claims all exceptions are escalated, which contradicts the described practice. Be careful to answer about reality as the email describes it, not about what the process maps show; the whole point of the email is that the two differ. Distinguishing the documented process from the actual process is a foundational business architecture skill.

#### Question 8 (moderate)

Statement: If the business architecture team does not reply by Friday, the delivery team will stop sprint planning.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B (False)**

**Explanation:** The email says the opposite of stopping: "If we do not hear back by then, we will plan against the March version." The stated fallback is to proceed using the March version, not to halt planning. The statement contradicts the text, so it is False. When you manage stakeholder expectations in real life, spotting a stated fallback like this is crucial: silence from your team will not pause the work; it will bake an assumption into it.

#### Question 9 (hard)

Statement: The March version of the target operating model is the current version.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C (Cannot Say)**

**Explanation:** The email tells us three versions exist with different dates, that the team is unclear which is current, and that the March version "the programme board saw" will be used as a fallback. None of that establishes which version is actually current. Board visibility is evidence of status, but the email does not state it as fact, and the sender explicitly asks for confirmation, signalling genuine uncertainty. Resist the pull of plausibility: the March version might well be current, but "might well be" is exactly what Cannot Say is for.

#### Passage C - Strategy extract

Read this extract from a departmental digital strategy:

"Over the next three years the department will consolidate its fourteen customer contact points into a single front door for citizens. This consolidation is expected to reduce failure demand, although the department recognises that some user groups, particularly those with limited digital access, will continue to need assisted routes. Consequently, telephone and face-to-face support will be retained where user research demonstrates need. Funding for the consolidation programme has been approved for the first year only; funding for years two and three is subject to the next spending review. The department aims to reduce overall contact-handling costs by 25% by the end of year three."

#### Question 10 (moderate)

Statement: The department will close all telephone support within three years.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B (False)**

**Explanation:** The passage states that "telephone and face-to-face support will be retained where user research demonstrates need". A commitment to retain telephone support under evidenced need directly contradicts a claim that all telephone support will be closed. Note the connection to public sector values: assisted digital routes protect users with limited digital access, and the strategy text reflects that duty. In tests and in strategy work alike, absolute words like "all", "never", and "every" in a statement should send you back to the text hunting for qualifications.

#### Question 11 (hard)

Statement: The consolidation programme is fully funded for three years.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B (False)**

**Explanation:** The text is explicit: "Funding for the consolidation programme has been approved for the first year only; funding for years two and three is subject to the next spending review." A programme funded for one year with the remainder subject to a future review is not fully funded for three years, so the statement contradicts the passage. Spotting funding conditionality is precisely the kind of risk you highlight when you assess the impact of strategies on the organisation: a three-year commitment resting on one year of funding is a delivery risk worth naming.

#### Question 12 (hard)

Statement: The department will achieve a 25% reduction in contact-handling costs by the end of year three.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C (Cannot Say)**

**Explanation:** The passage says the department "aims to reduce overall contact-handling costs by 25%". An aim is an intention, not an achieved or guaranteed outcome. The passage gives no information about whether the aim will be met, so we cannot say. Contrast this with Question 11, where the funding facts were stated outright. Strategy documents mix commitments, aims, expectations, and facts in adjacent sentences; your job, in the test and in your role, is to keep those categories separate when you communicate the strategy onward to stakeholders.

#### Question 13 (moderate) - Multiple choice comprehension

Which of the following best summarises the main point of Passage C?

- A) The department is abandoning non-digital contact channels to save money.
- B) The department plans to consolidate contact points into a single front door while retaining assisted routes where evidence shows need, though funding beyond year one is not yet secured.
- C) The department has already reduced contact-handling costs by 25%.
- D) User research has shown that no users need telephone support.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B captures all three elements of the passage: the consolidation plan, the retained assisted routes based on user research, and the funding conditionality. Option A contradicts the retention commitment. Option C converts a year-three aim into a completed achievement. Option D contradicts the passage's recognition that some user groups will continue to need assisted routes. Summary questions reward the option that is complete and faithful, not the one that is boldest. This is the same judgement you exercise when you aggregate complex information and communicate it to stakeholders without distortion.

#### Passage D - Governance meeting minutes

Read this extract from Design Authority minutes:

"Item 4: The Pensions Uplift programme presented its capability impact assessment. The Authority noted that the assessment identified a major change to the Payments capability and moderate changes to two other capabilities. The Authority requested that the programme provide a revised benefits profile within ten working days, and agreed that the programme may continue design work in the meantime. The business architect representing the programme confirmed that the revised profile would draw on figures agreed with Finance. Item 5 was deferred to the next meeting due to time constraints."

#### Question 14 (moderate)

Statement: The Design Authority stopped the Pensions Uplift programme's design work until a revised benefits profile is provided.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B (False)**

**Explanation:** The minutes state the Authority "agreed that the programme may continue design work in the meantime". The statement claims design work was stopped, which is a direct contradiction. In governance minutes, the difference between "paused pending X" and "continue while providing X" is enormous for delivery teams, so this is exactly the precision your stakeholders rely on you for when you relay decisions.

#### Question 15 (hard)

Statement: The revised benefits profile will use figures agreed with Finance.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A (True)**

**Explanation:** The minutes record that "the business architect representing the programme confirmed that the revised profile would draw on figures agreed with Finance". Within the world of the passage, this confirmation is stated as fact about the intended content of the profile. Answer True. If you hesitated between True and Cannot Say, note that the test asks what follows from the passage; a recorded confirmation in minutes is the passage asserting the plan. Had the statement said "Finance has approved the revised benefits profile", the answer would be Cannot Say, because agreeing figures is not the same as approving the finished profile. Precision about who agreed what, and when, keeps stakeholder relationships healthy.

#### Question 16 (moderate) - Multiple choice comprehension

According to Passage D, why was Item 5 deferred?

- A) The programme failed to attend.
- B) The Authority lacked a quorum.
- C) Time constraints.
- D) The passage does not give a reason.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The final sentence says: "Item 5 was deferred to the next meeting due to time constraints." The reason is stated explicitly, so option C is correct and option D is wrong. Quick factual questions like this are gifts in a timed test, provided you scan back to the exact sentence rather than answering from memory of your first read. Train the habit of verifying even "obvious" answers against the text; it costs five seconds and prevents careless errors.

#### Passage E - Consultancy briefing note

Read this extract from a briefing note prepared by a senior business architect:

"The review of the department's operating model options considered three approaches: a functional model organised around specialisms, a service-line model organised around citizen-facing services, and a hybrid model. The review recommends the hybrid model. Under the hybrid model, citizen-facing services would each have a dedicated multidisciplinary team, while scarce specialisms such as security architecture would remain in central pools. The review estimates that the hybrid model would require eighteen months to implement. The functional model was rejected because it scored lowest on responsiveness to user needs. No option was assessed as deliverable in under twelve months."

#### Question 17 (moderate)

Statement: The service-line model was rejected because it scored lowest on responsiveness to user needs.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B (False)**

**Explanation:** The passage attributes that reason to a different option: "The functional model was rejected because it scored lowest on responsiveness to user needs." Since only one option can score lowest, the service-line model cannot also have scored lowest, so the statement contradicts the passage. Swapping an attribute from one named item to another is one of the most frequent tricks in verbal reasoning tests, and it is defeated by matching each claim to its exact subject in the text. The same discipline protects you at work when summarising options appraisals: attach every finding to the right option, every time.

#### Question 18 (hard)

Statement: The hybrid model can be implemented in under twelve months if additional funding is provided.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B (False)**

**Explanation:** The final sentence states: "No option was assessed as deliverable in under twelve months." The hybrid model is one of the options, and the review's own estimate for it is eighteen months. A claim that it can be implemented in under twelve months contradicts the assessment recorded in the passage. The funding clause is a distraction: the passage says nothing about funding changing any timescale, and adding an unstated condition does not rescue a statement that conflicts with the text as given. When stakeholders float "couldn't we do it faster if...?" questions in real life, the honest answer starts from what the evidence actually says, which is precisely the habit this question rehearses.

#### Question 19 (moderate) - Multiple choice comprehension

According to Passage E, why would scarce specialisms remain in central pools under the hybrid model?

- A) Because central pools scored highest on responsiveness to user needs.
- B) The passage describes this as part of the hybrid model's design but does not state a reason.
- C) Because the spending review required it.
- D) Because the service-line model failed its assessment.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage says that under the hybrid model "scarce specialisms such as security architecture would remain in central pools", but it never explains why; the word "scarce" hints at a rationale, yet no reason is stated. Option B is therefore correct. Option A misattaches the responsiveness finding, which concerned the rejected functional model. Options C and D invent connections the passage never makes; there is no spending review in Passage E at all, and the service-line model's assessment is not described as failed. Questions like this train a subtle but important reading skill: noticing the difference between what a document describes and what it justifies. When you communicate business architecture frameworks and techniques to others, knowing where the source material is silent is as important as knowing what it says, because those silences are where stakeholders' questions will land.

### Preparation tips

- **Practise the True / False / Cannot Say discipline daily.** Take any paragraph from a policy or standard you are reading at work and write three statements about it: one true, one false, one undecidable. This is quick, free, and doubles as close-reading practice for your actual job.
- **Read the statement first, then hunt in the passage.** Under time pressure, scanning for the relevant sentence beats re-reading the whole passage. Keywords in the statement (fortnightly, funding, must) tell you where to look.
- **Watch the modal verbs.** Must, may, should, will, aims to, is expected to: these small words decide most answers. Highlight them mentally as you read.
- **Separate the document's world from your world.** You know a lot about government digital work; the test rewards you for setting that knowledge aside. If the passage does not say it, you cannot use it.
- **Beware absolute statements.** "All", "never", "only", and "every" in a statement usually signal that the passage contains a qualification the statement ignores.
- **Build reading stamina gently.** As an associate you are still growing your document diet. Ten minutes a day with dense material (standards, strategies, minutes) makes test passages feel familiar rather than threatening.

### Common pitfalls

- **Making assumptions**: bringing in personal knowledge instead of relying only on the provided text. The most experienced candidates are often the most tempted, because they know what documents like these usually say.
- **Confusing "likely" with "stated"**: a plausible inference (the March version is probably current) is still Cannot Say if the passage does not state it.
- **Confusing False with Cannot Say**: False needs a contradiction; absence of information is Cannot Say. If you cannot point to the contradicting words, do not answer False.
- **Misinterpreting nuance**: treating an aim or expectation in the text as an accomplished fact, as in Question 12.
- **Poor time management**: spending too long reading the whole text in detail first instead of scanning for the statement's keywords. Skim once for structure, then target.
- **Answering about the wrong layer**: when a passage contrasts what a document says with what actually happens (as in Passage B), check which layer the statement asks about.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment evaluates your decision-making, interpersonal skills, and professional conduct. Unlike logic or maths tests, it presents realistic workplace dilemmas to see how well your behavioural choices align with your organisation's values and your role's requirements. For you, that means scenarios involving project teams, programme managers, senior stakeholders, subject-matter experts, and the everyday tensions of architecture work: competing priorities, unclear direction, disagreements about approach, and the balance between doing things quickly and doing them well.

Key characteristics of this assessment type:

- **Scenario-based**: each question is a short, hypothetical workplace situation, often a conflict, an ethical wrinkle, or an operational challenge.
- **No single objectively right answer**: several responses may seem reasonable, but some fit the organisation's values and the role's expectations better than others.
- **Response formats**: you are typically asked to pick the Most Effective and Least Effective actions from a set, or to rank or rate all the options from best to worst.
- **Untimed or generously timed**: these assessments focus on natural judgement rather than speed, so you can think each scenario through calmly.

For UK public sector roles, the values that shape "effective" answers are well known: put user needs first; be open and transparent; collaborate across teams and professions; take accountability at your level while escalating appropriately; champion accessibility and inclusion; use evidence rather than opinion. As an associate, effective answers also reflect your level: you act within direction and wider strategy, you seek support when risk and complexity grow, and you recommend rather than impose when decisions exceed your remit. Choosing an option in which you unilaterally overrule a senior stakeholder is rarely "effective" at your level; equally, choosing pure passivity ("do nothing and hope") is almost never right either.

### What it measures for your role

Situational judgement maps to your role profile skills like this:

- **Stakeholder relationship management**: identifying important stakeholders, communicating clearly and regularly, tailoring communication to their needs, building consensus, and using evidence to explain decisions. Many scenarios test whether you keep stakeholders informed and bring them along rather than surprising them.
- **Communicating information**: working collaboratively, managing stakeholder expectations, being proactive and reactive in communication, and hosting or moderating difficult discussions. Scenarios probe how you handle tension in meetings and workshops.
- **Making and guiding decisions**: making decisions with managed levels of risk and complexity, recommending decisions as risk and complexity increase, and helping resolve disputes considering all views. Scenarios test your judgement about when to decide, when to recommend, and when to escalate.
- **Consultancy**: providing advice, guidance, and recommendations based on specialist knowledge, and framing problems so they can be understood. Scenarios test whether you advise honestly even when the message is unwelcome.
- **Business architecture and strategic thinking**: ensuring design work aligns with strategy and highlighting risks and issues. Scenarios test whether you protect strategic alignment without becoming a blocker.

### Practice questions

For each scenario, decide your answer before reading the discussion. Formats vary between "most and least effective" and full ranking, matching what you will meet in real assessments.

#### Question 1 (easy) - The misaligned project

You notice that a project's proposed process design conflicts with the department's target operating model, which the design team seems unaware of. The project's next design review is in two weeks. What do you do?

- A) Say nothing; the design review will probably catch it.
- B) Contact the project's lead designer now, explain the conflict with reference to the target operating model, and offer to walk through it together before the review.
- C) Email the programme director stating the project is non-compliant and should be halted.
- D) Raise it informally with a colleague on your own team and leave it with them.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A.**

**Explanation:** Option B is proactive, collaborative, and evidence-based: you communicate the issue early, cite the artefact that shows the conflict, and offer help rather than judgement. That is textbook stakeholder relationship management and exactly the "ensure design work aligns with strategy" duty in your role. Option A is the least effective: hoping a later gate catches a known problem wastes two weeks and gambles with delivery; passivity that ignores a root issue is a classic weak answer. Option C escalates disproportionately before talking to the team, damaging relationships and skipping the collaborative step; escalation is for when direct engagement fails or risk is severe. Option D delegates your insight informally with no ownership, so the issue may simply evaporate.

#### Question 2 (easy) - The jargon problem

After you present a capability model at a stakeholder meeting, an operations manager says, honestly and a little frustrated: "I did not understand half of that." What do you do?

- A) Suggest they read a business architecture textbook to get up to speed.
- B) Apologise briefly, then re-explain the key points in plain language using an example from their own operational area, and ask them to tell you where it stops making sense.
- C) Move on quickly to avoid embarrassment and cover the remaining slides.
- D) Repeat the same explanation more slowly.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A.**

**Explanation:** Your role level description says you should "explain complex business problems using clear language and artefacts", and your Communicating information skill requires tailoring communication to stakeholders' needs. Option B does both: it adapts language, grounds the model in the listener's world, and invites feedback, turning a stumble into relationship-building. Option A is the least effective because it blames the listener for your communication choice, which is disrespectful and corrodes the relationship; the obligation to be understood sits with the communicator. Option C ignores the feedback entirely, and option D changes speed but not language, misdiagnosing the problem: the issue is vocabulary and relevance, not pace.

#### Question 3 (moderate) - Conflicting stakeholder demands

Two senior stakeholders give you conflicting instructions about a business design artefact you are producing. The head of operations wants current pain points documented in detail; the programme director wants a future-state-only view and says documenting the current state is a waste of time. Both expect their version this week. What is the most effective response?

- A) Produce the version the programme director wants, since they are more senior in the programme hierarchy.
- B) Produce both versions in secret and show each stakeholder only their own.
- C) Tell both stakeholders about the conflict, propose a short three-way conversation to agree the artefact's purpose and scope, and bring a recommendation with the trade-offs of each approach.
- D) Ask your line manager to decide, and pause work until they respond.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: B.**

**Explanation:** Option C is transparent, brings the disagreement into the open, and positions you as a facilitator with a recommendation, which matches your skills in building consensus, managing expectations, and hosting difficult discussions. Bringing "a recommendation with trade-offs" also fits your decision-making level: recommend as complexity increases. Option B is the least effective: maintaining two secret versions is dishonest, unsustainable, and would destroy trust the moment it surfaced; transparency is a core public sector value. Option A resolves the conflict by seniority rather than by purpose, leaving the operations head ignored and the underlying disagreement alive. Option D is not wrong to involve a manager, but pausing all work while delegating the whole problem upward is passive for a conflict you could convene yourself; keep your manager informed rather than handing them the task.

#### Question 4 (moderate) - The optimistic report

Your programme manager asks you to soften language in your impact assessment: your draft flags a major risk that the benefits case depends on a system integration that has not been scoped. They say: "Just call it an assumption; a red risk this late looks bad at the board." What do you do?

- A) Make the change; the programme manager owns the report.
- B) Refuse loudly in the team channel so everyone can see the pressure you are under.
- C) Explain why the distinction matters, propose wording that is professionally accurate but constructive (a clearly stated risk with a mitigation plan), and if the disagreement persists, flag it to your senior business architect for guidance.
- D) Make the change but keep your original draft privately so you are protected later.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: A (with D close behind).**

**Explanation:** This scenario tests integrity under pressure, central to consultancy: "provide advice, guidance and recommendations based on your specialist knowledge". Option C holds the professional line while staying constructive: you explain the reasoning, offer wording that is honest and palatable, and escalate for guidance if needed, which is exactly right at associate level where support is part of how you work. Option A is least effective because mislabelling a known risk as an assumption misleads the board, undermines transparent decision-making, and could compromise the programme; agreeing quietly makes you complicit. Option D performs the same harm while protecting only yourself, which fails both the organisation and your own integrity. Option B protects the truth but chooses a public, confrontational channel that humiliates a colleague and damages the relationship; the goal is honest reporting, not visible victory.

#### Question 5 (moderate) - The health check finding

During an Agile health check you help facilitate, a delivery team privately tells you their velocity figures are being inflated before they reach the programme dashboard, because "leadership only wants green". What is the most effective first action?

- A) Report the team to programme leadership immediately, naming the individuals who told you.
- B) Do nothing; dashboards are not an architecture concern.
- C) Discuss with the team how the inflated figures distort decisions, encourage them to correct the data, and agree how the issue can be raised safely, offering to support them in raising it through the health check findings.
- D) Quietly correct the dashboard figures yourself using the numbers the team gave you.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: B.**

**Explanation:** Your Communicating information skill explicitly includes identifying issues through Agile health checks "and working with others to address them". Option C does exactly that: it treats the team as partners, protects the trust they showed you, and channels the issue through the legitimate health check route so leadership hears it with context. Option B is least effective: distorted data feeding governance decisions is everyone's concern, and a business architect who brings together information to inform decision making cannot shrug at corrupted information. Option A punishes candour and would teach every team to hide problems from health checks, making the organisation less transparent. Option D fixes one number while leaving the culture that inflated it untouched, exceeds your authority over another team's reporting, and does it covertly, which compounds the transparency problem it tries to solve.

#### Question 6 (moderate) - Ranking: the workshop that turns hostile

You are moderating a process-mapping workshop. A senior operations stakeholder repeatedly interrupts a junior caseworker who is describing how exceptions are really handled, saying: "That is not our process. We should not document workarounds." The room goes quiet. Rank these responses from most to least effective:

1. Acknowledge both views: explain that documenting actual practice alongside the designed process is standard method, because the gap itself is valuable information, then invite the caseworker to continue.
2. Move to the next agenda item to defuse the tension.
3. Privately decide the senior stakeholder is right and stop capturing the caseworker's input.
4. Ask the senior stakeholder to leave the workshop.

**Correct ranking: 1, 2, 3, 4.**

**Explanation:** Response 1 is the strongest: it moderates the difficult moment (a named behaviour in your Communicating information skill), protects the junior voice, and grounds the resolution in method rather than personality: the documented-versus-actual gap is core business architecture evidence, as Passage B in the verbal section illustrated. Response 2 is weak but not catastrophic: it avoids escalation yet loses the data and signals that interruption works. Response 3 is worse: it silently rewards seniority over evidence and discards exactly the information the workshop existed to capture. Response 4 is the most damaging: publicly ejecting a senior stakeholder humiliates them, poisons a relationship you must maintain for years, and exceeds the facilitator's proportionate options at your level. Notice the pattern in the ranking: engage with the substance first, avoid harm second, and relationship-destroying moves last.

#### Question 7 (moderate) - The deadline versus quality dilemma

A programme needs your capability impact assessment by Friday for a funding decision. On Wednesday you discover that a whole business area's data is missing from your analysis, and getting it properly would take another week. What is the most effective response?

- A) Deliver on Friday without mentioning the gap; the missing area is probably similar to the others.
- B) Miss the deadline and deliver the complete assessment the following week without warning anyone in advance.
- C) Tell the programme manager now: explain the gap, deliver on Friday with the missing area clearly marked as not yet assessed, state what could change when the data arrives, and agree a date for the completed version.
- D) Work through the weekend to invent reasonable estimates for the missing area so the assessment looks complete.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: A (with D equally poor).**

**Explanation:** Option C manages stakeholder expectations proactively (a named element of your Communicating information skill), preserves the funding decision's timeline, and is transparent about confidence levels: decision-makers get a usable product and know exactly where its edges are. Options A and D both present incomplete or fabricated analysis as complete, which corrupts a funding decision; A hides the gap and D fills it with invention dressed as data. In public sector work, decisions must rest on honest evidence, and "clearly marked limitations" is always more professional than false completeness. Option B protects quality but sacrifices the decision itself and blindsides the programme, failing the proactive-communication test; deadlines you cannot fully meet are managed by early conversation, not silent slippage.

#### Question 8 (hard) - The scope-creep request

A well-liked service owner asks you, as a favour, to add a recommendation to your consultancy report endorsing a tool their team has already decided to buy. Your analysis did not evaluate that tool. They say: "It would really help us get sign-off, and you would be our hero." What do you do?

- A) Add the endorsement; it maintains a valuable relationship and the tool might be fine.
- B) Decline to endorse what you have not evaluated, explain why your report's credibility depends on that boundary, and offer a genuine alternative: a quick, honest evaluation of the tool against the criteria your report already uses.
- C) Add the endorsement but phrase it vaguely so you could deny it meant approval.
- D) Report the service owner to their director for attempting to influence your report.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A (with C close behind).**

**Explanation:** This is a consultancy-integrity scenario. Your advice must be "based on your specialist knowledge and experience"; endorsing an unevaluated tool is advice based on friendship, which quietly converts your professional judgement into a favour bank. Option B declines the improper part while strengthening the relationship through real help, and it keeps the door open for the tool to earn a genuine endorsement: firm on the boundary, generous in the alternative. Option A trades long-term credibility for short-term warmth; the first time an unevaluated endorsement goes wrong, every past and future report you write is discounted. Option C is worse in character: deliberately ambiguous wording is dishonesty with an escape hatch. Option D treats a common, probably naive request as misconduct, escalating past the person instead of talking to them; proportionality matters, and most boundary-setting happens perfectly well in conversation.

#### Question 9 (hard) - The disputed method

A solution architect and a service designer on your project are in a standoff. The solution architect insists the future process must follow the constraints of the chosen platform; the service designer insists the process must follow user research findings, platform be damned. Delivery is stalling, and both have asked you to back them. What is the most effective response?

- A) Back the service designer; user needs always win in government.
- B) Back the solution architect; the platform decision was already made and constraints are constraints.
- C) Facilitate a working session in which both constraint sets are laid out against the actual process steps, identify which steps genuinely conflict, and bring any remaining irreconcilable points as a framed recommendation to the project's decision-maker.
- D) Tell them both to sort it out between themselves; you are not the referee.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: D.**

**Explanation:** Your Making and guiding decisions skill includes helping resolve technical disputes between peers "considering all views and opinions", and your consultancy skill is about framing problems so they can be understood. Option C does the architect's real job: it converts a personality standoff into an evidence question, usually revealing that only a small subset of steps truly conflict, and it recommends rather than rules on what remains, which is right at associate level. Option A misuses a true principle: user needs are central in government, but "always win, platform be damned" ignores legitimate delivery constraints, and slogans are not analysis. Option B repeats the error in the other direction. Option D is least effective: the dispute is stalling delivery and both parties have invited you in; declining to structure the problem when structuring problems is your profession is passivity at its purest.

#### Question 10 (hard) - The strategy contradiction

While mapping initiatives to strategic goals, you find that a flagship programme, sponsored by your director's own director, no longer aligns with the refreshed departmental strategy: the goal it served was dropped in the refresh. Nobody seems to have noticed. What do you do?

- A) Leave it out of your mapping; flagging it could embarrass senior people.
- B) Include the finding accurately in your mapping, brief your senior business architect first with the evidence, and let them guide how it is raised through governance.
- C) Email the sponsoring director-general directly to inform them their programme is now unaligned.
- D) Post the finding in the all-staff channel in the interests of transparency.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A.**

**Explanation:** The core duty here is "ensure design work from projects and programmes aligns with strategy", and hiding a misalignment because it is politically awkward (option A) is the least effective choice: it falsifies your artefact by omission and defeats the entire purpose of strategic mapping. Option B is right for your level: the finding stays accurate and evidenced, and the routing goes through your senior business architect, who has the standing and context to raise it well; "recommend as risk and complexity increase" includes political complexity. Option C is honest but reckless about channels: skipping several organisational layers with unwelcome news, alone, as an associate, sets the message up to fail. Option D confuses transparency with broadcast; transparency in governance means the right decision-makers see accurate information, not that sensitive findings bypass governance entirely.

#### Question 11 (hard) - The accessibility trade-off

A project you support wants to launch a staff-facing tool without completing accessibility fixes, arguing: "Only two people in the pilot group use screen readers, and we will fix it in phase two." The project asks you to reflect this plan in the business design as agreed. What is the most effective response?

- A) Reflect the plan as requested; two users is a small impact and phase two is a real commitment.
- B) Advise the project that accessibility is a legal requirement, not a preference to trade off; recommend the fixes be completed before launch or an interim assisted route be provided, and record the risk clearly in the design if the project proceeds anyway.
- C) Refuse to work with the project until they comply.
- D) Reflect the plan but anonymously alert the two affected users so they can complain.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A.**

**Explanation:** Accessibility in UK public sector services is a legal obligation under equality law and a core value of the profession, and it applies to staff-facing tools, not just citizen services. Option B gives correct specialist advice, offers a practical path (fix first, or interim assisted route), and, crucially, does what architects do when a project proceeds against advice: record the risk clearly and honestly. Option A is least effective because it treats excluding two named colleagues as an acceptable cost and launders that decision through your design document; "we will fix it in phase two" is the most broken promise in software. Option C abandons influence entirely: walking away removes the one person documenting the risk. Option D is passive-aggressive: engineering a complaint instead of giving straight advice corrodes trust in every direction. Inclusion questions in situational judgement tests reward candidates who treat accessibility as non-negotiable while remaining constructive about how compliance is achieved.

#### Question 12 (moderate) - The knowledge gap

In a stakeholder meeting, a finance lead asks you a detailed question about the costing methodology behind a target operating model. You do not know the answer; the methodology was built by a senior colleague who is on leave. What do you do?

- A) Improvise a plausible-sounding answer; admitting uncertainty looks weak.
- B) Say you do not know, commit to finding out, give a specific date for your answer, and follow up on time.
- C) Say it is not your area and suggest they wait until your colleague returns from leave in three weeks.
- D) Deflect the question by asking the finance lead what they think.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A.**

**Explanation:** Option B is honest, keeps ownership of the follow-up, and turns a gap into a demonstration of reliability, which is how trust with stakeholders is actually built: "communicate with them clearly and regularly" includes being clear about what you do not yet know. As an associate, you are expected to be still learning; nobody credible penalises "I will find out by Thursday". Option A is least effective and genuinely dangerous: an improvised costing answer to a finance lead can propagate into budgets and decisions, and being caught improvising once discounts everything you say afterwards. Option C is honest but abdicates: three weeks of stakeholder limbo for a question that likely has a documented answer fails the proactive test. Option D as a rhetorical dodge fools no one; used sincerely, asking their view can be useful, but not as a substitute for eventually answering.

### Preparation tips

- **Anchor yourself in the values before you practise.** Re-read the Civil Service values and the Service Standard's spirit: user needs first, openness, collaboration, evidence, accessibility, inclusion. Most "most effective" answers are simply these values applied under pressure.
- **Learn the shape of strong answers.** Across the questions above, effective options tend to share features: they are proactive rather than passive, they engage directly with the person concerned before escalating, they are transparent about problems, they offer a constructive path rather than a bare refusal, and they keep decisions at the right level: act, recommend, or escalate according to risk.
- **Learn the shape of weak answers too.** Least effective options are usually one of: do nothing and hope; deceive or shade the truth; escalate explosively past the person involved; act unilaterally beyond your remit; or protect yourself at the organisation's expense.
- **Rehearse with your own week.** Think of a real tension from your recent work: a disagreement, an awkward request, a deadline conflict. Write four responses of varying quality and decide which is most and least effective, and why. This builds the judgement muscle the test measures.
- **Answer as your best professional self, consistently.** The test asks what you would do; answer with the judgement you genuinely aspire to and practise, not what you fear you might do on a bad day, and not an idealised saint who has no deadlines. Consistency across scenarios matters, because wildly varying personas read as guesswork.
- **Do not rush.** These assessments are usually untimed or generously timed precisely because natural judgement is the target. Read every option fully; the difference between the best and second-best option is often a single clause.

### Common pitfalls

- **Answering idealistically rather than practically.** "Convene a full stakeholder summit" sounds noble, but for a two-person disagreement it is overkill; effective means proportionate.
- **Selecting passive options.** Choices that delay action, pass the problem wholesale to someone else, or ignore the root issue are consistently rated weakest, as in Questions 1, 5, and 9.
- **Ignoring the organisation's culture and values.** In UK government digital work, transparency, user needs, accessibility, and collaboration are not decoration; options that sacrifice them for speed or comfort rate poorly, as in Questions 4, 7, and 11.
- **Confusing escalation with communication.** Escalating can be right, but escalating first, publicly, or past the person concerned usually is not; the direct, respectful conversation almost always comes first.
- **Playing politics instead of judgement.** Choosing options to please the most senior person in the scenario, as in Question 3, misreads what is being measured: the test rewards sound process, not deference.
- **Forgetting your level.** As an associate you act within direction, recommend as complexity grows, and use the support around you. Options in which you unilaterally decide matters beyond your remit are traps, even when your instinct about the substance is right.
