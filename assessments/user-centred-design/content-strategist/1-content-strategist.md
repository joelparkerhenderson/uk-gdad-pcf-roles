## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive ability assessment measures the exact mental processes your role demands, using practical workplace scenarios rather than abstract puzzles. Unlike a generic intelligence test, it presents you with the everyday materials of your job — in your case, taxonomies, governance frameworks, publishing workflows, content audit spreadsheets, journey maps, and strategy documents — and asks you to reason quickly and accurately about them.

Typical format: an online, timed test lasting 15 to 30 minutes, with questions covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective, comparing your responses against a norm group, and modern platforms often adapt question difficulty based on your previous answers. Employers usually receive a breakdown of speed versus accuracy rather than a single score, so working both quickly and carefully matters. You will normally be offered short, ungraded practice questions before the real test begins, so you can get comfortable with the interface without pressure.

For a content strategist, cognitive ability assessment is particularly relevant because your core professional value is structured thinking about content at scale. You shape and structure the architecture of content, and that requires holding many moving parts in mind at once: which taxonomy terms map to which user needs, which workflow stages depend on which approvals, where a governance rule contradicts an established practice, and what follows logically when one element of a content model changes. A well-designed cognitive assessment simulates precisely those demands in miniature.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The assessment dimensions map directly to the skills in your capability framework:

- **Pattern recognition** maps to your **User-centred content design** skill: showing a deep understanding of end-to-end journeys and how content is affected within them means recognising recurring structures — duplicated pages, inconsistent taxonomy terms, repeated journey breakpoints — across large and messy content estates.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Strategic thinking**: leading the design and implementation of strategy, and directing the evaluation of strategies and policies to ensure business requirements are met, requires you to reason validly from governance rules, style standards, and policy intent to their consequences in specific cases.
- **Error checking** maps to **User-centred content design** and your duty to design governance, workflows and taxonomies: when you take responsibility for assuring the quality of content from more junior colleagues, you must spot inconsistencies between artefacts — a taxonomy that contradicts a navigation model, a workflow diagram with an approval loop that can never terminate.
- **Prioritisation** maps to **Agile working**: helping teams to manage and visualise outcomes, prioritise work, and adhere to agreed MVP, priorities and scope depends on rapidly ranking what matters most against user impact, deadlines, and strategic intent.
- **Problem solving** maps to **User focus** and **Stakeholder relationship management**: stakeholders bring you tangled content problems that span teams and channels, and you must decompose them, weigh the evidence about user needs, and recommend next steps that enable progress.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern recognition in a taxonomy**

Your department's taxonomy uses a consistent naming pattern for content topics. Parent topics are single nouns in plural form (e.g. "Benefits"). Child topics are named "Parent + colon + specific service area" (e.g. "Benefits: Disability support"). Grandchild topics append a further colon and task (e.g. "Benefits: Disability support: Make a claim"). Reviewing a draft taxonomy from a junior content designer, you see:

1. Visas
2. Visas: Work visas
3. Visas: Work visas: Extend your visa
4. Work visas: Visas: Skilled worker route
5. Visas: Study visas

Which entry breaks the naming pattern?

- A) Entry 2
- B) Entry 3
- C) Entry 4
- D) Entry 5

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The pattern requires the parent topic to come first, with each colon adding a deeper level of specificity. Entry 4 inverts the hierarchy: "Work visas" is a child of "Visas", so a grandchild entry must read "Visas: Work visas: Skilled worker route". Entries 2, 3 and 5 all follow the rule. This is exactly the kind of consistency check you perform when assuring the quality of content from more junior colleagues: naming conventions look trivial, but broken conventions destroy the findability and machine-readability that make a taxonomy usable — and they compound quickly once other teams start tagging content against the broken terms.

**Question 2 (easy) — Logical deduction from governance rules**

Your content governance framework states: "Every page in the benefits journey must be reviewed within 12 months of its last review date unless it has been formally archived." A content designer tells you: "Page X is in the benefits journey and has not been reviewed for 14 months." Which one of the following conclusions must be true?

- A) The team has breached the governance framework.
- B) Page X has been formally archived.
- C) Either page X has been formally archived, or the framework has been breached.
- D) Page X contains out-of-date information.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The governance rule is a conditional with one permitted exception. From the facts given, you cannot tell whether page X has been archived, so neither A nor B must be true on its own — each is merely possible. D introduces a claim about content accuracy that the facts do not support: a page can be unreviewed yet still accurate. The only conclusion guaranteed in every case consistent with the facts is the disjunction in C. Assessments reward this discipline: deduce only what the given information guarantees. In your role, the same discipline stops you accusing a team of breaching governance before checking the archive log — which protects your relationships with the content teams whose interdependencies you manage.

**Question 3 (easy) — Spotting the broken workflow step**

You are reviewing a proposed publishing workflow: (1) content designer drafts; (2) peer review by another content designer; (3) subject matter expert factcheck; (4) 2i quality review; (5) publish. The proposal adds a rule: "Any change made after step 3 must be re-factchecked before publishing." A team's process log shows a page where the 2i reviewer at step 4 rewrote a paragraph about eligibility criteria, and the page was then published. What has gone wrong?

- A) Nothing — the 2i reviewer is senior, so their changes need no further check.
- B) The page skipped peer review.
- C) The page needed to return to step 3 for re-factcheck after the step 4 rewrite, and did not.
- D) The subject matter expert should have done the 2i review.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Apply the rule mechanically: the rewrite happened at step 4, which is after step 3, so re-factcheck was required before publishing. Seniority is irrelevant — the rule contains no seniority exemption, so A fails. B and D describe process variations that the log does not show. The deeper point for your role: eligibility criteria are exactly the content where an unfactchecked rewrite creates real user harm, because a plausible-sounding paraphrase can silently change meaning. When you design governance, workflows and taxonomies, you build rules like this one precisely so that quality does not depend on any individual's confidence.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Error checking a content audit**

You maintain an audit tracing user needs to the content that meets them. An extract reads:

| User need | Meeting content | Owning team |
|---|---|---|
| UN1: Check eligibility | Eligibility checker page | Team Red |
| UN2: Apply online | Application start page | Team Blue |
| UN3: Track application | Status tracker page | Team Green |
| UN1: Check eligibility | Eligibility guidance page | Team Blue |

The content strategy states: (i) each user need is met by exactly one piece of content; (ii) Team Red owns all eligibility content. How many rows of the audit are inconsistent with the strategy?

- A) 0
- B) 1
- C) 2
- D) 3

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Check each rule against each row. Rule (i): UN1 appears twice (rows 1 and 4) with two different pieces of content, so at least one of those rows must be wrong — but the question asks how many rows are inconsistent, so identify the minimal correction. Row 4 also violates rule (ii): it assigns eligibility content to Team Blue rather than Team Red. Row 4 therefore breaches both rules, while rows 1, 2 and 3 each breach neither on their own — row 1 is only rendered problematic by row 4's existence, and removing row 4 restores full consistency. The single defective row is row 4, so the answer is 1. This mirrors real audit review: locate the minimal correction that restores consistency rather than flagging every symptom as a separate defect. It also flags a familiar strategic problem — duplicated content meeting the same need is one of the most common findings in a content audit, and consolidation is usually the fix.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Prioritisation across a governance backlog**

It is Monday morning. Four items compete for your attention:

1. The service's design authority meets Wednesday and needs your assessment of a proposed new content type, without which two teams cannot start building templates.
2. A junior content designer has asked for feedback on their first content model, due to their line manager Friday.
3. A director has emailed asking for "a quick view" of how a proposed departmental restructure would affect content ownership — no deadline stated.
4. Your quarterly review of taxonomy health metrics is due at month end, two weeks away.

Which sequencing is most defensible?

- A) 3, 1, 2, 4 — directors outrank all other stakeholders
- B) 1, 3, 2, 4 — the blocking assessment first, then the strategic request, then coaching, then routine review
- C) 1, 2, 3, 4 — strict deadline order
- D) 2, 1, 3, 4 — developing others always comes first

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Prioritisation questions test reasoning about impact and urgency together. Item 1 is both urgent (Wednesday) and high impact (it blocks two teams' delivery). Item 3 has no stated deadline but concerns a restructure affecting content ownership — strategically significant, sponsored by a director, and exactly the kind of question a content strategist exists to answer; a brief scoping reply also lets you manage the director's expectations early. Item 2 matters (coaching and guiding junior colleagues is core to your level) but has a Friday deadline and can follow. Item 4 is important, routine, and two weeks away. Option A over-weights seniority against a hard governance deadline; C treats the undated director request as lowest priority purely because it lacks a date, which confuses "no deadline" with "no urgency"; D inverts impact ordering. The strongest answer sequences by impact-weighted urgency, not by any single dimension.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Pattern recognition across journey data**

You are consolidating drop-off data for the four stages of an application journey across four service areas. Each area's journey has the same stages: Start, Eligibility, Form, Confirmation. The percentage of users abandoning at the Eligibility stage is: Area A 12%, Area B 14%, Area C 41%, Area D 13%. The four areas serve comparable users at comparable volumes. As a content strategist, what is the most reasonable first interpretation of Area C's figure?

- A) Area C's eligibility content is badly written and should be rewritten immediately.
- B) The figure is an outlier that warrants investigation — it may reflect a measurement difference, a genuinely harder eligibility rule, or a content problem.
- C) Areas A, B and D are losing too few users and their eligibility content is too permissive.
- D) The figures cannot be compared because the areas are different services.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Recognising the outlier is pattern recognition; deciding what it means requires restraint. Three comparable areas cluster at 12-14% and one sits at 41% — roughly three times the cluster. But an outlier is a prompt for inquiry, not a conclusion. Common innocent explanations include measurement differences (Area C may count users who correctly self-screen out as "abandoning" — which is the eligibility content doing its job), genuinely stricter eligibility rules, or a real content failure. Option A leaps to a rewrite without evidence and could "fix" content that is working correctly; option C invents a problem; option D wrongly abandons comparison despite the stem stating the areas are comparable. In your role, bringing insight about user needs to the business only supports decision making if you first validate that you are comparing like with like — and eligibility drop-off is the classic case where high abandonment can be success, not failure.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Logical deduction about workflow sequencing**

A content migration plan contains these dependency rules: (i) content cannot be migrated to the new publishing platform before the taxonomy mapping is complete; (ii) taxonomy mapping cannot start until the content audit finishes; (iii) editor training must occur in the same month as migration or the month immediately before it. The content audit finishes at the end of March. Taxonomy mapping takes one full month. What is the earliest month in which editor training may begin?

- A) February
- B) March
- C) April
- D) May

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Work forwards through the dependencies. The audit finishes at the end of March, so taxonomy mapping can occupy April at the earliest and completes at the end of April. Migration therefore cannot occur before May. Training must fall in the migration month (May) or the month immediately before it (April). The earliest permissible month for training is therefore April. A tempting error is to answer May by anchoring on the migration month; another is March by forgetting that mapping takes a full month. Dependency reasoning of exactly this kind underpins your governance and workflow design work — editors trained too early forget the new platform before they use it; trained too late, they block the migration itself.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Error checking a benefits chain for a content strategy**

A business case for your content consolidation programme claims: "Enabler: consolidate 400 duplicate pages into 120 → Intermediate benefit: users find the right page faster → End benefit: reduced calls to the contact centre → Strategic objective: improved staff wellbeing in the contact centre." Which link in the chain is logically weakest as drawn?

- A) Consolidation → users find the right page faster
- B) Users find the right page faster → reduced contact centre calls
- C) Reduced contact centre calls → improved staff wellbeing
- D) All links are equally strong

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The first link is mechanically plausible: fewer competing pages means less ambiguity in search results and navigation. The second is plausible with caveats: a meaningful fraction of calls are "failure demand" caused by users not finding or not understanding online content, so better findability genuinely reduces calls. The third link changes category without a mechanism: fewer calls could improve wellbeing, but it could equally lead to headcount reduction, higher call complexity per adviser (easy calls disappear first, hard ones remain), or no change at all. Nothing in the chain explains the mechanism, and the harder-calls effect is well documented in service operations. The map needs either a different end-point (e.g. an efficiency or user-experience objective) or an explicit mechanism. Spotting category errors in causal chains is central to directing the evaluation of strategies — a strategy that promises the wrong benefit will be judged a failure even when the content work succeeds.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Multi-constraint scheduling**

You must schedule three governance workshops — Vision (V), Taxonomy review (T), and Workflow design (W) — across five working days, Monday to Friday, one workshop per day at most. Constraints: (i) V must precede both T and W; (ii) the head of content is only available Monday and Thursday, and must attend V; (iii) T requires the outputs of V to be synthesised, which takes two full working days between the workshops; (iv) W must be on the day immediately after T. On which day must T take place?

- A) Tuesday
- B) Wednesday
- C) Thursday
- D) Friday

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Test V on each of the head of content's available days. If V is Thursday, then T needs two clear working days after it (Friday and the following Monday), pushing T beyond this week — impossible within Monday to Friday. So V must be Monday. Two full working days for synthesis are Tuesday and Wednesday, so T's earliest day is Thursday. Could T be Friday? Then W, which must be the day immediately after T, would fall on Saturday — not a working day. So T must be Thursday, with W on Friday. The question type rewards systematic case elimination: fix the most constrained variable first (V, pinned by the sponsor's availability), propagate the consequences, and check the boundary cases. This is precisely how you sequence real governance work around senior stakeholders' diaries — the scarcest resource constrains everything downstream.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Deduction from conflicting governance principles**

Your content governance framework contains: (P1) "Content that receives fewer than 10 views per month for six consecutive months must be archived." (P2) "Statutory content must remain published regardless of usage." (P3) "Any exception to an archiving decision must be recorded in the exceptions register." A page containing statutory guidance has received 4 views per month for the last eight months. It remains published, and the exceptions register contains no entry for it. Which statement best describes the situation?

- A) The framework has been breached because the page should have been archived under P1.
- B) The framework has been breached because the exception is unrecorded under P3.
- C) Whether the framework has been breached depends on whether P2 operating automatically counts as an "exception" requiring registration — the framework is ambiguous and needs clarifying.
- D) The framework has not been breached and needs no change.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** P1's archiving condition is met (under 10 views for more than six consecutive months), but P2 overrides it for statutory content, so keeping the page published is correct and A fails. The live question is P3: is the P2 override an "exception to an archiving decision" that must be registered, or is it a rule operating automatically, with no archiving decision ever made and hence no exception to record? Both readings are defensible from the text, which means B and D each assume an interpretation the framework does not settle. The best answer recognises the ambiguity and the strategist's responsibility: you designed or inherited this framework, and an ambiguity that surfaces in one page today will surface in hundreds during the next audit. Expert-level governance work is exactly this — noticing where rules underdetermine practice and tightening the wording before inconsistent local interpretations take root.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Applied problem solving across interdependent teams**

Three content teams share your taxonomy. Team Red proposes renaming the term "Payments" to "Money" because their user research shows users say "money". Team Blue's service auto-generates navigation from the term names, so any rename changes their live navigation instantly. Team Green has 200 pages tagged "Payments" and a content freeze for the next three weeks due to a legal deadline. Renaming is technically instant and reversible. What is the soundest first move?

- A) Approve the rename now — user research evidence beats operational convenience.
- B) Reject the rename — stability of shared infrastructure beats one team's research.
- C) Validate the research finding across all three teams' users, model the impact on Blue's navigation and Green's freeze, and schedule any rename after Green's freeze with Blue's navigation tested.
- D) Let Team Red use "Money" locally while the shared taxonomy keeps "Payments".

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** This question tests whether you treat a shared taxonomy as shared infrastructure. Option A acts on evidence from one team's users while ignoring a live navigation dependency and a legal freeze — the rename would alter Team Blue's live service untested and change tagged content during Team Green's freeze. Option B is the mirror error: it privileges stability so absolutely that validated user evidence can never change the taxonomy, which ossifies the very architecture you are meant to evolve. Option D quietly forks the taxonomy — the worst long-term outcome, because divergent local terms destroy cross-service consistency and the interdependencies you exist to manage. Option C sequences the decision correctly: validate whether the finding generalises (one team's users may not represent all three), assess impact on each dependency, and schedule the change to respect the freeze. Note that C does not refuse the rename; it routes it through exactly the governance a content strategist designs. In assessments and in practice, the strongest answers on shared-infrastructure problems combine openness to change with disciplined sequencing.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Detecting the flawed inference in a strategy paper**

A draft content strategy argues: "Our survey shows 78% of users who contacted the helpline had first visited the website. Therefore the website is failing to answer users' questions, and we should invest in rewriting the most-visited pages." Which is the most important flaw in this reasoning?

- A) The survey percentage is too low to matter.
- B) The inference ignores the users who visited the website and did not need to call — the data says nothing about the website's overall success rate.
- C) Rewriting pages is always less effective than restructuring navigation.
- D) Helpline users may be lying about visiting the website.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The statistic conditions on the wrong population. Among callers, 78% had visited the website first — but the callers are, by construction, the users for whom every channel so far has failed. The relevant question for strategy is: of all users who visited the website, what proportion resolved their need without calling? That figure could be 95% (website performing well, callers a small residue) or 40% (website failing broadly), and the survey cannot distinguish them. This is a base-rate error, and it matters because it drives investment: rewriting the most-visited pages targets pages selected by traffic, not by failure. A stronger analysis would join call reasons to the specific pages callers visited, and measure page-level failure demand. Options A, C and D each miss the structural flaw. Directing the evaluation of strategies — a named duty at your level — means catching exactly this class of error before it misdirects a programme's budget.

### Preparation tips

- **Practise with your own artefacts.** Open a real taxonomy, workflow diagram, or audit spreadsheet from your current work and interrogate it the way these questions do: Where could the naming convention break? Which rule has an undefined edge case? Which row contradicts which policy? Ten minutes a day of this converts your daily work into assessment practice.
- **Learn to separate "possible" from "necessary".** Many cognitive questions hinge on the difference between what could be true and what must be true. When you review a deduction question, ask: is there any scenario consistent with the facts where this option is false? If yes, it is not a valid conclusion.
- **Time-box, then review.** Do practice questions under a timer that allows roughly 60 to 90 seconds each, then review untimed. The timed pass trains pace; the untimed review trains depth. Both matter, because assessments report speed and accuracy separately.
- **Fix the most constrained variable first.** In scheduling and multi-constraint puzzles, identify the element with the fewest options (the stakeholder available two days a week, the immovable freeze) and place it first. The rest of the puzzle usually falls out by propagation.
- **Read the numbers' definitions, not just the numbers.** Before interpreting any figure — drop-off, views, satisfaction — ask what is being counted and who is in the denominator. As Question 12 shows, the commonest strategic error is a true number applied to the wrong population.
- **Rest and pace on the day.** Cognitive scores degrade measurably with fatigue. Take the assessment when you are fresh, in a quiet environment, and use any ungraded practice items to settle your nerves before the clock starts.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Overriding the rules with experience.** You know how governance should work, and that knowledge can tempt you to answer what a well-run organisation would do rather than what the stated rules entail. Assessments score against the stem's rules. Apply them mechanically, even when they are imperfect — and save your critique for questions that explicitly ask for it.
- **Flagging symptoms as separate defects.** In error-checking questions, one broken row or step often causes several visible inconsistencies. Count the minimal set of defects, not every downstream symptom, unless the question asks otherwise.
- **Confusing "no deadline" with "no urgency".** Senior stakeholders often send undated requests that carry real strategic weight. Prioritisation questions punish both extremes: dropping everything for seniority, and mechanically sorting by date.
- **Leaping from outlier to diagnosis.** An anomalous number is an invitation to investigate, not a verdict. Options that prescribe action ("rewrite it", "cut it") before inquiry are usually wrong.
- **Anchoring on the headline event.** In dependency puzzles, candidates anchor on the final event (go-live, migration) and misplace the activities tied to it. Work forwards from the first constraint, not backwards from the last.
- **Rushing table questions.** Tables reward systematic row-by-rule checking. Skimming a four-row table to save 20 seconds routinely costs the question.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures your ability to interpret, manipulate, and draw sound conclusions from numerical information of the kind your role actually produces and consumes. For a content strategist, that is not accountancy: it is content performance data, audit statistics, publishing throughput, survey results, readability metrics, and the resourcing arithmetic behind team plans. Questions present a table, chart description, or short data narrative, then ask you to calculate or infer.

Typical format: an online, timed test lasting 20 to 35 minutes with 15 to 25 questions. Most platforms allow a calculator and rough paper, and many are adaptive. What is scored is not raw arithmetic speed but the ability to select the right operation, keep track of denominators and units, and resist plausible-looking wrong answers. Employers use numeric reasoning for senior content roles because content strategy is increasingly evidence-led: you are expected to direct evaluation, justify consolidation decisions with data, and challenge weak numerical claims made by others.

At your level, the emphasis shifts from computing figures to interrogating them. You direct the evaluation of strategies, which means you are the person who catches the percentage applied to the wrong base, the average that hides a bimodal distribution, and the trend that is really a definition change. The questions below reflect that emphasis while still requiring you to do the arithmetic accurately — every worked explanation shows the calculation step by step.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Percentages, ratios and rates** map to **User-centred content design**: ensuring content is regularly reviewed and evaluated means working with review coverage rates, failure-demand percentages, and satisfaction scores — and knowing exactly what each denominator is.
- **Averages and distributions** map to **Agile working**: measuring and evaluating outcomes across sprints means understanding when a mean is informative and when it conceals variation that changes the decision.
- **Trend interpretation** maps to **Strategic thinking** and **User focus**: bringing insight about how user needs have changed over time depends on reading time series correctly — separating seasonality, level shifts, and definitional changes from genuine trends.
- **Resource and capacity arithmetic** maps to your duty to **work with lead content designers to organise and manage teams**: pages per designer per sprint, review backlogs against capacity, and the cost of governance overhead are all planning numbers you must get right.
- **Data-based challenge** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**: influencing and negotiating with senior stakeholders often turns on redoing their arithmetic politely but correctly.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Review coverage percentage**

Your content estate holds 1,250 pages. Your governance standard requires every page to be reviewed within the last 12 months. The audit shows 950 pages meet the standard. What percentage of the estate is out of compliance?

- A) 76%
- B) 24%
- C) 30%
- D) 32%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Out-of-compliance pages = 1,250 − 950 = 300. As a percentage of the estate: 300 ÷ 1,250 = 0.24 = 24%. Option A (76%) is the compliance rate — the classic complement error, reading the question as "in compliance". Option C comes from using 1,000 as a convenient but wrong denominator; option D from 300 ÷ 950, dividing by the compliant pages rather than the whole estate. Always fix the denominator first: "percentage of the estate" means divide by 1,250. In governance reporting, quoting 24% versus 76% to a board changes the entire tone of the conversation, so state which way round your figure runs.

**Question 2 (easy) — Publishing throughput**

A content team publishes the following numbers of pages over four sprints: 18, 22, 17, 23. What is the mean number of pages published per sprint?

- A) 19
- B) 20
- C) 21
- D) 22

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Sum the sprints: 18 + 22 = 40; 40 + 17 = 57; 57 + 23 = 80. Divide by the number of sprints: 80 ÷ 4 = 20. A quick sanity check: the values straddle 20 symmetrically (−2, +2, −3, +3), so the mean must be exactly 20. Sanity checks like this are worth cultivating — under time pressure they catch keying errors instantly. In sprint planning with a lead content designer, this mean becomes your baseline velocity; but note for later questions that a mean alone says nothing about consistency, which is why you would also glance at the spread before committing the team to a forecast.

**Question 3 (easy) — Ratio of content types**

After a consolidation exercise, your estate contains guidance pages and transactional pages in the ratio 5:2. There are 350 pages in total. How many are transactional?

- A) 100
- B) 140
- C) 250
- D) 70

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The ratio 5:2 divides the estate into 5 + 2 = 7 parts. Each part is 350 ÷ 7 = 50 pages. Transactional pages take 2 parts: 2 × 50 = 100. Option C (250) is the guidance count (5 × 50) — the read-the-wrong-half error. Option B comes from taking 2/5 of 350 (confusing the part-to-part ratio with a part-to-whole fraction), and option D from halving that again. Ratios appear constantly in estate reporting — guidance to transactional, published to archived, reviewed to unreviewed — and the reliable method is always: total the parts, size one part, multiply.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Percentage change in failure demand**

Contact centre data shows 4,800 calls last quarter were classified as content failure demand (calls caused by users not finding or understanding online content). This quarter the figure is 4,080. Meanwhile total calls fell from 32,000 to 30,000. Which statement is correct?

- A) Failure demand fell 15% in absolute terms, and from 15% to 13.6% as a share of all calls.
- B) Failure demand fell 15% in absolute terms, and its share of all calls was unchanged.
- C) Failure demand fell 7.2% in absolute terms, and from 15% to 13.6% as a share of all calls.
- D) Failure demand rose as a share of calls because total calls fell.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Absolute change: (4,800 − 4,080) ÷ 4,800 = 720 ÷ 4,800 = 0.15 = 15% fall. Share last quarter: 4,800 ÷ 32,000 = 0.15 = 15%. Share this quarter: 4,080 ÷ 30,000 = 0.136 = 13.6%. So both the absolute number and the share fell — option A. Option B misses that the denominator (total calls) also changed, so the share must be recomputed, not assumed. Option D has the direction of the logic right in general (a shrinking denominator can raise a share) but the arithmetic wrong in this case: failure demand fell faster (15%) than total calls (6.25%), so its share still dropped. This two-lens check — absolute and share — is exactly how you should present content-improvement results to senior stakeholders, because either lens alone can mislead.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Weighted average satisfaction**

You aggregate user satisfaction for three journeys you oversee. Journey X: 82% satisfied from 3,000 responses. Journey Y: 76% satisfied from 1,500 responses. Journey Z: 90% satisfied from 500 responses. What is the overall satisfaction rate across all responses, to the nearest percentage point?

- A) 83%
- B) 80%
- C) 81%
- D) 79%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Weight each rate by its response count — never average the percentages directly. Satisfied users: X: 0.82 × 3,000 = 2,460; Y: 0.76 × 1,500 = 1,140; Z: 0.90 × 500 = 450. Total satisfied = 2,460 + 1,140 + 450 = 4,050. Total responses = 3,000 + 1,500 + 500 = 5,000. Overall rate = 4,050 ÷ 5,000 = 0.81 = 81%. Option A (83%) is the trap: the unweighted mean (82 + 76 + 90) ÷ 3 = 82.7 ≈ 83% treats a 500-response journey as equal in weight to a 3,000-response journey, letting the small, high-scoring Journey Z drag the figure upward. Notice the pattern: the weighted answer sits closer to Journey X's 82% than the unweighted one does, because X contributes 60% of all responses. When you aggregate satisfaction, completion, or comprehension rates across the journeys you oversee — a routine step in directing content evaluation — weighting by volume is what keeps a board-level figure honest, and it is the first thing to check when a stakeholder's roll-up looks surprisingly rosy.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Reading a trend with a definition change**

Monthly page views for your guidance hub: January 210,000; February 205,000; March 208,000; April 265,000; May 262,000; June 267,000. In April, the analytics package was reconfigured to include mobile app webviews, previously untracked. A stakeholder says: "Views jumped 27% in April — the March content redesign worked." What is the soundest response?

- A) Agree — the April rise coincides with the redesign.
- B) Point out that the measurement definition changed in April, so the jump cannot be attributed to the redesign without separating webview traffic; the flat pattern before and after the step suggests a level shift, not growth.
- C) Argue the redesign failed because May fell slightly.
- D) Average the six months and report 236,000 views as the stable monthly figure.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Compute the shape first: January to March hover around 205,000-210,000 (flat); April to June hover around 262,000-267,000 (flat). The series shows a one-off step of roughly 55,000-60,000 (about 27% on the old base: 57,000 ÷ 208,000 ≈ 0.27) exactly when the definition changed, with no ongoing growth on either side of the step. That pattern is the signature of a measurement change, not a content effect — a genuine redesign benefit would more plausibly show growth continuing or building after launch, and would need the new-traffic source excluded to test. Option A commits the coincidence-equals-cause error; option C over-reads a 3,000-view wobble (about 1%) as failure; option D averages across a definitional break, producing a number that describes no real month. Bringing insight about how user needs change over time — a named element of your User focus skill — depends on refusing to read definition changes as behaviour changes.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Capacity versus backlog**

Your review backlog stands at 300 pages. Each of your 4 content designers can review 15 pages per two-week sprint alongside other work. New pages needing review arrive at 20 per sprint. How many sprints until the backlog is cleared?

- A) 5
- B) 6
- C) 7.5
- D) 15

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Net clearance per sprint = capacity − arrivals = (4 × 15) − 20 = 60 − 20 = 40 pages per sprint. Sprints to clear 300 pages = 300 ÷ 40 = 7.5 sprints. Option A (5) ignores the incoming flow (300 ÷ 60); option D (15) uses one designer's capacity; option B is a rounding guess. The strategic insight embedded in the arithmetic: a backlog is a flow problem, not a stock problem. If arrivals rose to 60 per sprint, net clearance would be zero and the backlog would never clear at any starting size — which is why, when you design governance, you set review capacity against expected arrival rates, not against the current backlog alone. Presenting the "7.5 sprints, provided arrivals stay at 20" formulation to a lead content designer turns a vague worry into a plannable commitment.

**Question 8 (challenging) — Consolidation business case arithmetic**

A consolidation proposal will merge 400 duplicate pages into 120. Maintaining a page costs an average of 1.5 hours of designer time per quarter. Designer time is costed at £45 per hour. The consolidation project itself will take 350 hours of designer time as a one-off. After how many complete quarters does the cumulative saving exceed the one-off cost?

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Pages eliminated = 400 − 120 = 280. Quarterly time saving = 280 × 1.5 = 420 hours. Quarterly cost saving = 420 × £45 = £18,900. One-off cost = 350 × £45 = £15,750. After 1 quarter, cumulative saving = £18,900, which already exceeds £15,750. So the answer is 1 quarter. You can also stay in hours and skip the money entirely: 420 hours saved per quarter versus 350 hours invested — the saving exceeds the cost within the first quarter (350 ÷ 420 ≈ 0.83 of a quarter), so the first complete quarter clears it. Choosing the unit that cancels (hours here, since £45 multiplies both sides) is a professional's shortcut worth practising. Options B-D come from misreading the merge as saving only 120 pages' costs, or adding rather than netting. Payback arithmetic of this kind is how a content strategist turns "we should tidy the estate" into a fundable proposal.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Sample sizes and confident claims**

Two user research rounds tested comprehension of a rewritten eligibility page. Round 1 (old page): 6 of 10 participants completed the comprehension task. Round 2 (new page): 8 of 10 completed it. A stakeholder drafts a blog post: "The rewrite improved comprehension by 33%." Which critique is most important?

- A) The improvement is 20 percentage points, not 33%, so the claim is arithmetically wrong.
- B) With samples of 10, the difference between 6/10 and 8/10 is well within the range of chance variation, so the data cannot support a confident causal percentage claim at all — though it is encouraging and worth testing at scale.
- C) The claim should say 25% because 8 ÷ 6 is not a valid calculation.
- D) Comprehension tasks are qualitative, so no numbers should ever be reported.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** First check the arithmetic the stakeholder used: (8 − 6) ÷ 6 = 2 ÷ 6 ≈ 33% relative improvement — so the calculation behind "33%" is actually defensible as a relative change, and the 20-percentage-point figure in option A is the absolute change; both are legitimate framings. The deeper problem is inferential: with n = 10 per round, two additional successes are easily produced by sampling variation alone (small usability samples routinely swing by two or three participants), so no precise improvement percentage — 33% or 20 points — should be asserted as a finding. Option D overcorrects: small-sample task completion counts are legitimately reported as counts ("8 of 10"), with appropriate uncertainty. The professional response, and the one option B captures, is to report the direction honestly, withhold the confident percentage, and recommend a larger or repeated test. When you assure the quality of content claims made on behalf of your teams — in blog posts, show-and-tells, or business cases — this is among the most common corrections you will make.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Interpreting a two-way table**

An audit cross-tabulates your estate's 1,200 pages by review status and traffic band:

| | High traffic | Low traffic | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reviewed in date | 380 | 460 | 840 |
| Overdue review | 120 | 240 | 360 |
| Total | 500 | 700 | 1,200 |

Which statement is supported by the table?

- A) Most overdue pages are high traffic.
- B) A high-traffic page is more likely to be in date (76%) than a low-traffic page (about 66%), and overdue high-traffic pages, though a minority, number 120.
- C) Low-traffic pages are reviewed more reliably than high-traffic pages.
- D) 30% of high-traffic pages are overdue.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Work the conditional percentages by column. High traffic in date: 380 ÷ 500 = 0.76 = 76%; high traffic overdue: 120 ÷ 500 = 24%. Low traffic in date: 460 ÷ 700 ≈ 0.657 ≈ 66%; low traffic overdue: 240 ÷ 700 ≈ 34%. So option B's figures check out exactly. Option A fails: overdue pages split 120 high versus 240 low, so most overdue pages are low traffic. Option C reverses the true comparison (66% < 76%). Option D says 30%, but the true figure is 24% — likely computed from 360 ÷ 1,200, which is the overall overdue rate, not the high-traffic rate; conditioning on the wrong total is the standard two-way-table trap. Strategically, the table tells a familiar story: teams keep high-traffic pages fresher, but 120 overdue high-traffic pages are where user harm concentrates — that is your prioritisation target, and the number you would put in front of a lead content designer this week.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Compound change over multiple periods**

Organic search traffic to a key journey fell 10% in the first quarter after a site migration, then rose 15% in the second quarter, then fell 5% in the third. A stakeholder claims traffic is now level with pre-migration volumes because −10 + 15 − 5 = 0. Taking pre-migration traffic as 100,000 sessions per quarter, what is the actual third-quarter volume, and is the stakeholder right?

- A) 100,000 — the stakeholder is right.
- B) 98,325 — the stakeholder is wrong; traffic is about 1.7% below pre-migration levels.
- C) 101,750 — traffic is above pre-migration levels.
- D) 95,000 — traffic is 5% down.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Percentage changes compound multiplicatively, not additively. Step by step from 100,000: after −10%: 100,000 × 0.90 = 90,000. After +15%: 90,000 × 1.15 = 103,500. After −5%: 103,500 × 0.95 = 98,325. So traffic sits at 98,325 — about 1.7% below the pre-migration baseline — and the stakeholder's additive shortcut overstates recovery. The error is small here but grows fast with bigger swings (a 50% fall followed by a 50% rise leaves you at 75%, not 100%). When you evaluate whether a strategy is meeting business requirements — a named element of your Strategic thinking skill — compounding errors in recovery narratives are worth catching early, because "we're back to normal" and "we're still 1,700 sessions per quarter down" lead to different decisions about further investment.

### Preparation tips

- **Rehearse the denominator question.** Before computing any percentage, say to yourself: "percentage of what?" Most numeric-reasoning errors at senior level are not arithmetic slips but denominator choices — share of estate versus share of compliant pages, share of callers versus share of visitors.
- **Keep a two-lens habit.** For any change, compute both the absolute figure and the percentage, and for any share, note whether the denominator also moved. Presenting both lenses is also better stakeholder communication.
- **Practise mental benchmarks.** Know instantly that 1/7 ≈ 14%, 1/6 ≈ 17%, 1/3 ≈ 33%, and that a 10% fall then 10% rise lands at 99%. Benchmarks let you sanity-check options before computing precisely.
- **Use the units that cancel.** As in the payback question, working in hours (or pages, or responses) before converting to money often halves the arithmetic and the error surface.
- **Recreate your real dashboards on paper.** Take a table from your own analytics or audit reporting and set yourself three questions about it: a percentage, a weighted average, a trend interpretation. Data you recognise builds fluency faster than abstract drills.
- **Under time pressure, estimate first.** Round the numbers, get an approximate answer, then compute exactly only if two options survive the estimate. This is how experienced test-takers bank easy marks and protect time for hard items.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Adding percentage changes across periods.** Sequential changes compound; −10% then +15% then −5% is not zero. Multiply the factors.
- **Averaging percentages without weighting.** Satisfaction rates, completion rates, and compliance rates from groups of different sizes must be weighted by group size before combining.
- **Reading the wrong cell or the wrong total.** Two-way tables punish haste: always confirm whether a percentage should condition on the row total, the column total, or the grand total.
- **Treating definition changes as behaviour changes.** A step change in a metric that coincides with a tracking or definition change is measurement, not user behaviour, until proven otherwise.
- **Confusing relative and absolute change.** "Improved by 33%" (relative) and "improved by 20 percentage points" (absolute) describe the same data differently. Know which the question asks for, and which your stakeholder's claim uses.
- **Trusting small samples with precise claims.** Counts from usability rounds of 5-10 participants support directions, not decimal-point percentages. Challenge precision that outruns the data.
