## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the everyday thinking skills your role demands, using practical workplace scenarios rather than abstract puzzles. Instead of asking you to rotate shapes or complete number squares, it presents you with the kinds of material you see every shift on the service desk — ticket queues, escalation rules, asset records, knowledge base steps — and asks you to reason quickly and accurately about them.

The typical format is an online, timed test lasting between 15 and 30 minutes, with somewhere between 15 and 30 questions. Questions cover pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective: your answers are compared against a norm group of people at a similar level. Many modern platforms are adaptive, which means the questions get slightly harder when you answer correctly and slightly easier when you do not — so do not panic if the test suddenly feels difficult; that often means you are doing well. Employers usually see a balance of speed and accuracy rather than a single mark, so working carefully matters just as much as working quickly. You will almost always be offered a few ungraded practice questions before the real test starts, so you can get used to the screen layout without any pressure.

For a service desk analyst, cognitive ability matters because your day is a stream of small, fast decisions. Which call do you take first? Does this fault match a pattern you have seen before? Is the asset record you are looking at consistent with what the user is telling you? Should this issue go to second-line support now, or can you resolve it yourself? A well-designed cognitive assessment simulates exactly those moments in miniature, which is why it predicts success in first-line support roles.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The assessment dimensions map directly onto the named skills in your role summary:

- **Pattern recognition** maps to your **Service focus** skill: showing an awareness of different products and services means noticing when several tickets describe the same underlying fault, or when a spike of calls follows a change to a service.
- **Logical deduction** maps to your duty to know when to escalate issues to your manager: escalation rules are conditional statements ("if priority 1 and unresolved after 30 minutes, then escalate"), and applying them correctly is pure deduction.
- **Error checking** maps to your **Asset and configuration management** skill: tracking, logging, and correcting information to protect assets and components depends on spotting mismatches between records and reality.
- **Prioritisation** maps to your **Customer service management** skill: resolving user requests to a minimum of the agreed service level agreement means ranking tickets by urgency and impact, not just by arrival order.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to your **Ownership** skill and your core task of taking calls and trying to resolve queries: you own an issue until it is resolved, mitigated, or handed to a new owner, and that requires methodical fault-finding.
- **Attention to user detail** maps to your **User focus** skill: understanding user stories and user needs starts with reading what the user actually said, carefully and completely.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern recognition in ticket references**

Your department's ticket system uses references in the format INC-YYYY-NNNNN for incidents (for example INC-2026-00417) and REQ-YYYY-NNNNN for service requests (for example REQ-2026-00098). While tidying your queue, you see these five references:

1. INC-2026-00521
2. REQ-2026-00104
3. INC-00522-2026
4. INC-2026-00523
5. REQ-2026-00105

Which reference breaks the standard pattern?

- A) Reference 1
- B) Reference 2
- C) Reference 3
- D) Reference 5

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The pattern is always prefix, then the four-digit year, then the five-digit number. Reference 3 swaps the year and the number, reading INC-00522-2026 instead of INC-2026-00522. All the others follow the rule exactly. This kind of check might look trivial, but reference conventions are what let you and your colleagues find tickets quickly, link related incidents, and report accurately. Spotting a malformed reference early — often caused by manual typing instead of system generation — protects the quality of the records that your whole team relies on. A good technique is to check one element at a time: prefix first, year second, number third, rather than trying to judge the whole reference at a glance.

**Question 2 (easy) — Logical deduction from an escalation rule**

Your service desk procedure says: "Any priority 1 incident that has not been resolved within 30 minutes must be escalated to the duty manager." You are handling a priority 1 incident that you resolved after 25 minutes. A colleague asks whether you should have escalated it. Which one of the following is definitely true?

- A) You broke the procedure by not escalating.
- B) You followed the procedure, because the incident was resolved within 30 minutes.
- C) You should have escalated it anyway, to be safe.
- D) The incident should have been priority 2.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The rule only requires escalation when two things are both true: the incident is priority 1, and it is unresolved after 30 minutes. Your incident was priority 1, but it was resolved at 25 minutes, so the escalation condition was never triggered. Option A is wrong because no rule was broken. Option C describes a personal preference, not what the procedure requires — and escalating everything "to be safe" would flood your manager with noise. Option D invents information: nothing in the scenario tells us the priority was wrong. Assessments reward this discipline of concluding only what the given rule guarantees, and so does your job: knowing when to escalate — and when not to — is listed explicitly in your role.

**Question 3 (easy) — Error checking an asset record**

A user calls to report a broken laptop. You look up the asset record, which reads: Asset tag LT-30412; Model: Vantage 14; Assigned to: J. Osei; Location: Leeds office; Status: In repair. The caller says: "Hi, this is Jide Osei from the Leeds office. My Vantage 14 laptop, tag LT-30412, has a cracked screen — this is the first time I've reported it." Which field in the record looks wrong?

- A) Asset tag
- B) Assigned to
- C) Location
- D) Status

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Compare each field of the record with what the caller says. The tag (LT-30412), the assignee (J. Osei), and the location (Leeds) all match. But the status says "In repair", while the caller says this is the first time the fault has been reported — so the laptop cannot already be in repair. Either the status was set on the wrong asset, or an old repair was never closed off. Your asset and configuration management skill is exactly this: tracking, logging, and correcting information to protect assets. In real life, you would resolve the discrepancy before booking the repair, because acting on a wrong record can send an engineer to the wrong device or lose track of a machine entirely. Field-by-field comparison beats reading the whole record in one go.

**Question 4 (easy) — Applying a knowledge base article**

A knowledge base article for printer faults says: "Step 1: Check the printer is powered on. Step 2: If powered on, check the network cable is connected. Step 3: If connected, restart the printer. Step 4: If the fault continues after restart, log a ticket for second-line support." A user tells you their multi-function device is powered on and the network cable is connected, and they have already restarted it twice, but it still will not print. What should you do next according to the article?

- A) Ask them to restart it a third time.
- B) Ask them to check the power again.
- C) Log a ticket for second-line support.
- D) Tell them the article does not cover their situation.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Walk the user's situation through the steps in order. Step 1 is satisfied (powered on). Step 2 is satisfied (cable connected). Step 3 is satisfied (restarted — twice, in fact). Step 4 says that if the fault continues after restart, you log a ticket for second-line support. That is exactly the situation, so C is correct. Option A adds a step the article never asks for; option B goes backwards; option D is wrong because the article covers this case precisely. Following a documented sequence faithfully — without skipping ahead or looping back unnecessarily — is a core first-line skill. It also shows good ownership: you have done everything within your remit, and now you pass the issue on through the correct route rather than leaving the user stuck.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Prioritising a ticket queue**

It is 09:05 on a Tuesday. Four items are waiting for you:

1. A priority 2 incident: a whole floor in the Cardiff office has lost access to the case management application (logged 08:55).
2. A priority 4 service request: a user wants a second monitor (logged yesterday, SLA target is five working days).
3. A priority 3 incident: one user cannot print to the multi-function device but can print to another printer nearby (logged 09:00).
4. A voicemail from a user who says their password reset "didn't work" and they cannot log in at all (not yet logged).

Which order of attention is most defensible?

- A) 2, 3, 4, 1 — first in, first out
- B) 1, 4, 3, 2 — highest impact first, then the unlogged caller, then the workaround case, then the routine request
- C) 4, 1, 3, 2 — always deal with phone messages before tickets
- D) 1, 3, 4, 2 — handle everything already in the system before logging new work

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Prioritisation questions ask you to weigh impact and urgency together. Item 1 affects a whole floor and blocks casework — highest impact, so it comes first (even if your immediate action is to confirm it is being escalated and communicated). Item 4 comes next: the user is completely unable to work, and the issue is not even logged yet, so nothing will happen until you capture it — an unlogged issue is an invisible issue. Item 3 has a workaround (a nearby printer), which genuinely lowers its urgency. Item 2 is routine, inside a five-day SLA. Option A confuses arrival order with importance; option C elevates the contact channel above the impact; option D delays a completely blocked user just because their issue arrived by voicemail. Notice the pattern: rank by impact-weighted urgency, and never let unlogged work stay unlogged for long.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Pattern recognition across incoming calls**

During Monday morning, you notice the following calls between 09:00 and 09:40: five users cannot open email; two users cannot open email and also cannot open the intranet; one user reports a slow laptop. All seven email callers are based in the Newcastle office; the slow-laptop caller is in Bristol. What is the most reasonable working hypothesis?

- A) Seven users coincidentally forgot their email passwords.
- B) There is a site-level issue affecting Newcastle, possibly network-related, since some users have lost more than one service.
- C) The slow laptop in Bristol is causing the email problems in Newcastle.
- D) The email service is down nationally.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Look for what the affected calls share and what they do not. All seven email issues come from one site — Newcastle — which points towards something local to that site rather than a national outage (option D would predict calls from many sites). Two callers have lost email and the intranet, which suggests the problem may sit below the applications, at the network level. Option A stretches coincidence past breaking point, and option C connects two unrelated facts — one slow laptop in a different city cannot cause a site-wide issue. Forming a sensible working hypothesis matters because it changes your next action: instead of troubleshooting seven mailboxes one at a time, you would flag a possible site issue to your manager, link the tickets together, and check whether a major incident should be raised. That is service focus in action — seeing the service behind the symptoms.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Deduction from categorisation rules**

Your desk's routing rules say: (i) hardware faults on multi-function devices go to the print vendor; (ii) all account lockouts go to the identity team unless caused by a known application fault; (iii) application faults in the HR system go to the HR applications team. A user is locked out of the HR system. The known-issues list shows a current HR system fault that is locking out some users. Where should the ticket go?

- A) The print vendor
- B) The identity team
- C) The HR applications team
- D) Nowhere — the user should wait until the known issue is fixed

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Apply the rules in order and mind the exception. Rule (i) is irrelevant — no printer involved. Rule (ii) would normally send a lockout to the identity team, but it contains an explicit exception: "unless caused by a known application fault". The known-issues list shows exactly such a fault in the HR system, so the exception applies and rule (ii) steps aside. Rule (iii) then routes an HR application fault to the HR applications team. Option D fails your ownership duty: even when a known issue exists, the ticket still gets logged, linked to the problem record, and routed, so the user is counted, informed, and helped if a workaround exists. Exception clauses ("unless...") are where deduction questions — and real routing mistakes — most often trip people up, so slow down whenever you see one.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Error checking a handover note**

At shift handover you read this note from a colleague: "INC-2026-00610: user Priya Shah, Swansea, cannot access shared drive. Checked permissions — correct. Escalated to second line at 16:40. User called back at 16:20 asking for an update; told her escalation had already been done." What is logically wrong with the note?

- A) Permissions cannot be correct if the user has no access.
- B) The user is said to have been told about the escalation at 16:20, but the escalation happened at 16:40.
- C) Shared drive issues should never be escalated.
- D) The ticket reference has the wrong format.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Check the timeline. The note claims the escalation happened at 16:40, yet also claims that at 16:20 — twenty minutes earlier — the user was told the escalation "had already been done". Both statements cannot be true: either one of the times is mistyped, or the user was told something inaccurate. Option A is wrong because permissions can be correctly configured while access fails for other reasons (network, drive mapping, server fault). Option C is false — escalating an unresolved access issue is proper procedure. Option D is wrong: INC-2026-00610 matches the standard format. Spotting timeline contradictions matters on a real desk because handover notes drive the next shift's actions and what the user is told; a contradictory record damages both the fix and the user's trust. When checking any note, extract the timestamps and read them in order.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Applied problem solving on a fault**

A user reports that their laptop cannot reach any websites. You establish these facts: (i) their email client is sending and receiving normally; (ii) a colleague sitting next to them, on the same office network, browses the web without difficulty; (iii) the user's browser shows a proxy error message; (iv) the user installed "a free PDF tool" yesterday. What is the most likely area of the fault?

- A) The office network is down.
- B) The website the user wants is down.
- C) Something on the user's own laptop — most plausibly a browser or proxy setting changed around the time the new software was installed.
- D) The user's network cable is unplugged.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Use elimination, which is the analyst's core diagnostic method. Fact (ii) rules out option A: the same network serves the neighbouring colleague perfectly. The user cannot reach any website, which rules out option B — one site being down would not block them all. Fact (i) rules out option D: email is flowing, so the laptop is connected to the network. What remains is the laptop itself, and two clues point the same way: the browser reports a proxy error, and new third-party software was installed yesterday — free tools sometimes alter proxy settings. C is the only option consistent with every fact. Notice the technique: each fact eliminates a whole class of causes, narrowing from network to machine to a specific setting. That is faster and more reliable than trying fixes at random, and it gives you a clear story for the ticket record.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Multi-constraint scheduling**

You must visit three users for desk-side support this week — Aled (fault on specialised IT equipment), Bea (new starter setup), and Chris (recurring laptop crash) — one visit per day at most, Monday to Friday. Constraints: (i) Aled's specialised equipment requires a vendor engineer who is on site only on Thursday; (ii) Bea starts work on Wednesday, and her setup must be completed before her first day; (iii) Chris's crash logs are captured by an overnight tool that finishes on Wednesday morning, and his visit must happen after the logs are available; (iv) you are on the phones all day Monday and cannot make any visit. What is the only workable plan?

- A) Aled Tuesday, Bea Wednesday, Chris Thursday
- B) Bea Tuesday, Aled Thursday, Chris Friday
- C) Aled Thursday, Bea Monday, Chris Friday
- D) Chris Tuesday, Bea Wednesday, Aled Thursday

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Work through the constraints one at a time and eliminate. Constraint (iv) removes Monday entirely, which eliminates option C (Bea on Monday). Constraint (iii) says Chris's visit must come after Wednesday morning, so Chris on Tuesday is impossible — eliminating option D. Constraint (i) pins Aled to Thursday, the vendor engineer's only day on site — eliminating option A, which puts Aled on Tuesday. That leaves option B, and checking it confirms every constraint is met: Bea's setup happens on Tuesday, which is before her Wednesday start (constraint ii); Aled is seen on Thursday with the vendor engineer present (constraint i); Chris is seen on Friday, after his logs finished on Wednesday morning (constraint iii); and no visit falls on Monday (constraint iv). The reliable technique is to find the most restrictive constraint first — here, the vendor's single available day — and let it anchor the plan, exactly as you would when juggling desk-side visits, vendor engineers, and phone cover in a real week. Options that fail even one constraint fail entirely: scheduling is pass/fail, not a matter of averages.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Spotting a pattern across time**

Your desk logs the number of "cannot log in" incidents each day for two weeks. Week 1: Mon 41, Tue 12, Wed 10, Thu 11, Fri 9. Week 2: Mon 44, Tue 13, Wed 11, Thu 40, Fri 10. A change to the identity service was deployed on the evening of the Wednesday of week 2. Which statement best fits the data?

- A) The Thursday spike in week 2 is normal — Thursdays are always busy.
- B) Mondays show a regular spike (probably expired passwords after the weekend), and the unusual Thursday spike in week 2 immediately follows the identity service change, so the change is a credible suspect worth reporting.
- C) The identity change caused the Monday spikes.
- D) There is no pattern in the data.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Separate the recurring pattern from the anomaly. Mondays are high in both weeks (41 and 44) while Tuesday to Friday normally sit around 9-13 — a classic weekly rhythm, commonly caused by passwords expiring over the weekend. That baseline makes the week 2 Thursday value of 40 stand out sharply: it is roughly four times the normal weekday level, and it occurs the morning after the identity service change. Correlation is not proof, but a four-fold spike immediately after a related change is exactly the kind of pattern you should report to your manager and note against the change record. Option A is contradicted by week 1's Thursday (11). Option C fails because the Monday spikes existed before the change. Option D ignores two clear patterns. Your role summary says analysts should know when to escalate — and "unusual pattern, plausibly linked to a change" is a textbook trigger.

### Preparation tips

- Practise with your real environment in mind. When you next handle a ticket, notice the thinking you are doing — matching a pattern, applying a rule, checking a record — and name it. The assessment tests the same moves with the serial numbers filed off.
- Do a little, often. Three 20-minute practice sessions beat one exhausting evening. Cognitive speed improves with rested, repeated practice, not cramming.
- Learn the elimination habit. On every multiple-choice question, cross out options that any single fact contradicts. Even when unsure, eliminating two options doubles your odds.
- Read the question stem twice, especially conditional words: "unless", "only", "must", "may". Most wrong answers come from a skimmed condition, exactly as most misrouted tickets do.
- Time yourself gently. Start untimed, then introduce a timer once your accuracy is steady. Speed built on accuracy lasts; speed built on guessing collapses.
- Sleep, hydrate, and set up a quiet space for the real test — the same care you would take before a busy Monday morning shift.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Answering from experience instead of from the text.** You may know a cleverer fix than the knowledge base article describes, but the question asks what the article says. On the desk, too, documented procedure is the baseline you deviate from knowingly, not accidentally.
- **First-in, first-out prioritisation.** Arrival order feels fair, but assessments (and SLAs) reward impact-weighted urgency. A whole-floor outage beats an older monitor request.
- **Missing the exception clause.** Rules with "unless" or "except" are the most commonly misapplied, in tests and in ticket routing alike. Slow down when you see one.
- **Confusing correlation with proof.** A spike after a change is a suspect, not a verdict. The strong answer reports and investigates; the weak answer asserts.
- **Panicking when questions get harder.** On adaptive tests, harder questions usually mean you are performing well. Keep your rhythm.
- **Leaving questions blank.** Unless the instructions say wrong answers are penalised, an eliminated-down guess is always better than a blank.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures how confidently and accurately you work with the kinds of numbers your role genuinely produces: ticket volumes, SLA percentages, call handling times, first-contact resolution rates, asset counts, and simple trends over days and weeks. It is not a maths exam. There is no algebra, no trigonometry, and nothing you did not meet at school — the core operations are percentages, averages, ratios, differences, and reading tables and charts correctly. What the assessment really tests is whether you can extract the right numbers from realistic workplace data and reason with them carefully under mild time pressure.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 20 to 30 minutes with 15 to 20 questions. Each question usually presents a small table or chart — for example, a week of ticket statistics — followed by one or more multiple-choice questions. An on-screen calculator is often allowed, and rough paper almost always is. Scoring compares your accuracy and speed against a norm group of people at a similar level, and some platforms adapt the difficulty as you go. As with the cognitive test, employers see how careful you are, not just how fast.

For a service desk analyst, numeric confidence matters more than people sometimes expect. Your role summary asks you to produce relevant reports, interpret a data set, and communicate it to others — that is the IT service reporting skill — and to resolve requests to a minimum of the agreed service level agreement, which is measured in percentages and clock-times. When your manager says "we hit 92% against the priority 3 SLA last week", you should be able to picture what that means, notice when a figure looks wrong, and explain a simple trend to a stakeholder without anxiety. Practising the questions below builds exactly that comfort.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Percentages and SLA arithmetic** map to your **Customer service management** skill: resolving user requests "to a minimum of the agreed service level agreement" means understanding what 90% within 8 working hours actually requires, and whether a given week's figures meet it.
- **Reading tables and interpreting data sets** maps directly to your **IT service reporting** skill: producing reports in an agreed format and timeframe, interpreting a data set, and communicating it to others all start with accurate table-reading.
- **Averages and rates** map to your daily work of taking calls: average handling time, calls per hour, and first-contact resolution rate are the numbers by which desk performance is described.
- **Counts, differences, and reconciliation** map to your **Asset and configuration management** skill: tracking and correcting information about assets often comes down to making counts agree — what the register says versus what the stock check found.
- **Trend awareness** maps to your **Service focus** and **User focus** skills: noticing that a metric is drifting week on week is often the first quantitative sign of a user-experience problem worth raising.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Percentage of tickets resolved**

On Monday your desk received 80 incident tickets and resolved 60 of them the same day. What percentage of Monday's tickets were resolved the same day?

- A) 60%
- B) 70%
- C) 75%
- D) 80%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Percentage resolved = resolved ÷ received × 100. Step by step: 60 ÷ 80 = 0.75, and 0.75 × 100 = 75%. A quick sense-check: 60 out of 80 is the same as 6 out of 8, which is 3 out of 4 — three quarters — and three quarters is 75%. Option A is a trap for people who read "60 resolved" and echo the raw number as a percentage; the raw count and the percentage only coincide when the total is exactly 100. Same-day resolution percentage is one of the most common figures in desk reporting, so being able to produce it instantly — and sense-check it with simple fractions — is genuinely useful.

**Question 2 (easy) — Average handling time**

You handled five calls this morning, lasting 4, 6, 5, 9, and 6 minutes. What was your average (mean) handling time?

- A) 5 minutes
- B) 6 minutes
- C) 7 minutes
- D) 9 minutes

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The mean is the total time divided by the number of calls. Step 1: add the durations: 4 + 6 = 10; 10 + 5 = 15; 15 + 9 = 24; 24 + 6 = 30 minutes. Step 2: divide by the number of calls: 30 ÷ 5 = 6 minutes. Option D is the longest single call, not the average — a common mix-up. Notice that one long call (9 minutes) pulls the average above the most common value (6 appears twice); in desk reporting, a handful of long, difficult calls can raise a whole team's average, which is worth remembering before anyone reads too much into a single day's figure.

**Question 3 (easy) — Reading a simple table**

Your weekly report shows tickets logged by category:

| Category | Tickets logged |
|---|---|
| Password and account | 45 |
| Hardware | 20 |
| Printing and MFDs | 15 |
| Applications | 30 |
| Other | 10 |

How many tickets were logged in total, and which category was largest?

- A) 110 total; Applications largest
- B) 120 total; Password and account largest
- C) 120 total; Applications largest
- D) 110 total; Password and account largest

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Add the column: 45 + 20 = 65; 65 + 15 = 80; 80 + 30 = 110; 110 + 10 = 120 tickets in total. The largest single value in the column is 45, for Password and account. Both parts of the answer must be right, which is why careful adding matters: stopping one row early gives 110 and steers you to options A or D. Reading a categorised table like this is the first step of your IT service reporting skill — and noticing that password issues make up 45 out of 120 tickets (37.5%) is exactly the kind of observation that leads a desk to suggest self-service password reset.

**Question 4 (easy) — SLA target check**

The SLA for priority 3 incidents says 90% must be resolved within 8 working hours. Last week your desk resolved 63 of its 70 priority 3 incidents within 8 working hours. Did the desk meet the SLA?

- A) Yes — exactly 90% were resolved in time.
- B) Yes — 93% were resolved in time.
- C) No — only 63% were resolved in time.
- D) No — 88% were resolved in time.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Work out the percentage resolved in time: 63 ÷ 70 = 0.9, and 0.9 × 100 = 90%. The target is 90%, and 90% meets a "minimum of 90%" requirement exactly, so the answer is A. A tidy way to see it without a calculator: 10% of 70 is 7, so 90% of 70 is 70 − 7 = 63 — precisely the number resolved. Option C is the classic error of reading the raw count (63) as a percentage. This calculation is the literal meaning of your customer service management skill statement — "resolve user requests to a minimum of the agreed service level agreement" — so it is worth being able to do it both forwards (what percentage did we achieve?) and backwards (how many tickets can we afford to miss and still hit 90%?).

**Question 5 (moderate) — Percentage change in call volume**

Your desk took 500 calls in March and 575 calls in April. By what percentage did call volume increase from March to April?

- A) 7.5%
- B) 13%
- C) 15%
- D) 75%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Percentage change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100. Step 1: find the increase: 575 − 500 = 75 calls. Step 2: divide by the original (March) figure: 75 ÷ 500 = 0.15. Step 3: convert to a percentage: 0.15 × 100 = 15%. Option D comes from stopping after step 1 and reading the raw increase as a percentage. Option B (roughly 75 ÷ 575) divides by the new month instead of the old one — percentage change is always measured against the starting value. A 15% month-on-month rise is the kind of figure your manager would want flagged with a suggested cause (a big software rollout? seasonal joiners?), which is how interpreting a data set turns into communicating it to others.

**Question 6 (moderate) — First-contact resolution rate**

"First-contact resolution" (FCR) means the ticket was resolved during the user's first call, with no follow-up needed. Yesterday the desk handled 140 calls; 98 were resolved at first contact. Your desk's FCR target is 65%. Which statement is correct?

- A) FCR was 70%, which beats the target by 5 percentage points.
- B) FCR was 65%, exactly on target.
- C) FCR was 70%, which beats the target by 5%.
- D) FCR was 42%, below target.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Step 1: FCR = 98 ÷ 140 = 0.7 = 70%. (Sense-check: 10% of 140 is 14, so 70% is 14 × 7 = 98 — matches.) Step 2: compare with the 65% target: 70% − 65% = 5 percentage points above target. The subtle part is the wording: the difference between two percentages is measured in percentage points, not percent. Option C says "beats the target by 5%", but 5% of 65% would be a much smaller amount (3.25 points); the desk actually beat the target by 5 points, which is about 7.7% above it in relative terms. Assessments — and well-written service reports — reward this precision, because "up 5 points" and "up 5 per cent" describe genuinely different improvements.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Ratio of analysts to calls**

Your desk expects 360 calls today and each analyst can handle about 45 calls per day. Rota planning uses the ratio of one analyst per 45 expected calls. How many analysts are needed today, and if only 6 are available, roughly how many calls per analyst does that imply?

- A) 8 needed; 60 calls each if only 6 available
- B) 8 needed; 45 calls each if only 6 available
- C) 7 needed; 51 calls each if only 6 available
- D) 9 needed; 40 calls each if only 6 available

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Step 1: analysts needed = expected calls ÷ calls per analyst = 360 ÷ 45 = 8. Step 2: if only 6 analysts are in, calls per analyst = 360 ÷ 6 = 60. So the desk is two analysts short, and each person present would carry about 60 calls instead of 45 — a third more than the comfortable load (60 ÷ 45 = 1.33). This is exactly the arithmetic behind availability conversations on a real desk: it turns "we're short-staffed" into "each of us will take about 15 extra calls", which is concrete, checkable, and far more persuasive when your senior analyst or manager is deciding whether to move cover around.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Reading a two-way table**

The monthly report breaks tickets down by site and by whether the SLA was met:

| Site | SLA met | SLA missed |
|---|---|---|
| Leeds | 180 | 20 |
| Cardiff | 135 | 15 |
| Newcastle | 88 | 22 |

Which site had the worst SLA performance in percentage terms?

- A) Leeds
- B) Cardiff
- C) Newcastle
- D) They all performed equally.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Work out each site's percentage met. Leeds: total = 180 + 20 = 200; met = 180 ÷ 200 = 90%. Cardiff: total = 135 + 15 = 150; met = 135 ÷ 150 = 90%. Newcastle: total = 88 + 22 = 110; met = 88 ÷ 110 = 0.8 = 80%. Newcastle is worst at 80%, ten points behind the other two sites. The trap is comparing raw missed counts: Leeds missed 20 tickets and Newcastle missed 22 — close in absolute terms — but Leeds handled almost twice the volume, so its miss rate is half Newcastle's. Converting to percentages before comparing sites of different sizes is one of the most important habits in service reporting, and it is exactly what "interpret a data set and communicate this to others" means in practice.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Working backwards from an SLA**

Next week your desk expects 240 priority 3 incidents. The SLA requires 90% resolved within 8 working hours. What is the maximum number of those incidents the desk can miss (fail to resolve within 8 hours) while still meeting the SLA?

- A) 12
- B) 24
- C) 36
- D) 90

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** If 90% must be resolved in time, then at most 10% may miss. Step 1: 10% of 240 = 240 × 0.10 = 24. Step 2: sense-check the other way: meeting the SLA requires at least 90% of 240 = 216 resolved in time, and 240 − 216 = 24 misses. So the desk can miss up to 24 and still be exactly at 90%; the 25th miss drops performance to 215 ÷ 240 ≈ 89.6%, which fails. Option A halves the allowance; option C uses 15%; option D confuses the target percentage with a count. Working backwards from a target to a "budget" of allowable misses is genuinely how desks manage a live week — by mid-week, if 15 of the 24 are already spent, everyone knows the remaining margin without recalculating from scratch.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Combining averages**

Your desk reports average call handling time separately for the morning and afternoon. This Tuesday: morning — 60 calls at an average of 5 minutes; afternoon — 40 calls at an average of 8 minutes. What was the overall average handling time for the day?

- A) 6.5 minutes
- B) 6.2 minutes
- C) 6.8 minutes
- D) 13 minutes

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** You cannot simply average the two averages, because the morning had more calls and must count for more. Step 1: total morning minutes = 60 × 5 = 300. Step 2: total afternoon minutes = 40 × 8 = 320. Step 3: total minutes for the day = 300 + 320 = 620. Step 4: total calls = 60 + 40 = 100. Step 5: overall average = 620 ÷ 100 = 6.2 minutes. Option A (6.5) is the unweighted average of 5 and 8 — the classic mistake. Option D adds the averages, which has no meaning. The weighted average matters whenever you combine figures from groups of different sizes: sites, shifts, or weeks. If your desk merged two teams' statistics and the result looked odd, this is the first calculation to re-check.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Trend and projection from a chart description**

The number of open tickets at the end of each week has been: week 1: 120; week 2: 132; week 3: 145; week 4: 160. Your manager asks: "If this keeps growing at roughly the same rate, about how many open tickets should we expect at the end of week 6?" Which is the best estimate?

- A) About 175
- B) About 190
- C) About 210
- D) About 320

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** First measure the pattern. Week-on-week increases: 132 − 120 = 12; 145 − 132 = 13; 160 − 145 = 15. The increase itself is creeping up — roughly 12 to 15 per week, so "the same rate" sensibly means about 15 or slightly more per week from here. Step 1: week 5 ≈ 160 + 15 = 175. Step 2: week 6 ≈ 175 + 16 ≈ 191, so "about 190" is the best estimate. Option A stops at week 5. Option C would need the growth to nearly double each week; option D doubles the current backlog for no reason. Two lessons carry into real reporting: measure the differences before projecting, and present projections as rounded estimates ("about 190") rather than false precision — a backlog trending upward at 15 a week is a message about demand outstripping capacity, and that message, communicated clearly, is the real point of the report.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Asset stock reconciliation**

The asset register says the Leeds store room holds 84 laptops. A physical stock check counts 78. Separately, the register shows 5 laptops issued to new starters this week that the stock team says were taken from the Leeds store but never recorded as removed from store stock. After correcting the register for those 5, how many laptops remain unaccounted for?

- A) 0
- B) 1
- C) 5
- D) 6

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Step 1: the raw discrepancy is 84 (register) − 78 (counted) = 6 laptops apparently missing. Step 2: the 5 new-starter laptops explain part of the gap — they genuinely left the store room but the register still counts them as in store. Correcting the register: 84 − 5 = 79 laptops that should be on the shelf. Step 3: compare with the count: 79 − 78 = 1 laptop still unaccounted for. So the honest position after correction is one missing device, which needs investigating — perhaps a repair loan or an unrecorded disposal. Option A assumes the 5 explain everything (they explain only 5 of the 6); option D forgets to apply the correction at all. This is your asset and configuration management skill in numeric form: reconciliation is subtraction done carefully, one explained difference at a time, until what remains is either zero or a genuine exception worth escalating.

### Preparation tips

- Rebuild comfort with percentages first — they are the backbone of SLA and desk reporting. Practise the three directions: find the percentage (63 of 70), find the part (90% of 240), and find the change (500 to 575).
- Use the 10% trick for quick sense-checks: 10% of any number is one decimal shift, and most SLA arithmetic (90%, 80%, 95%) is a short step from there.
- Always write down the two numbers you are using and which is the denominator. Most numeric errors on the desk and in tests are "right method, wrong number".
- When combining groups (shifts, sites, weeks), remember weights: totals first, then divide. Never average averages from different-sized groups.
- Practise reading tables aloud: "180 met out of 200 total at Leeds". Saying the denominator out loud prevents the raw-count-as-percentage trap.
- Use rough paper even for "easy" questions, and use the on-screen calculator for division — accuracy under time pressure is the skill being measured.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Reading a count as a percentage.** "63 resolved" is not 63% unless the total is 100. Always divide by the total first.
- **Dividing by the wrong base.** Percentage change is measured against the old value; SLA performance against total tickets, not tickets met.
- **Averaging averages.** Two shifts of different sizes must be combined by totals, not by averaging their averages.
- **Confusing percentage points with percent.** Moving from 65% to 70% is a 5-point rise, which is about a 7.7% relative rise. Reports that mix these up mislead managers.
- **Projecting without measuring.** Look at the week-on-week differences before extending a trend; do not eyeball the last number and guess.
- **Rushing the "easy" additions.** Most table-reading errors are simple mis-additions made at speed. Add twice, in a different order the second time.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately you read, understand, and draw conclusions from written material of the kind your role actually uses: IT policies, service notices, knowledge base articles, emails from users, and short reports. The most common format presents a passage of 100 to 250 words followed by statements you must judge as **True** (the passage supports it), **False** (the passage contradicts it), or **Cannot say** (the passage gives you too little information to decide either way). Other question types ask you to pick the best summary of a passage, identify what a user's email is actually asking for, or choose the clearest rewrite of a clumsy sentence.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 15 to 25 minutes with 20 to 30 statements spread across several passages. The golden rule is that you must answer only from the passage — not from your own experience, however relevant. This feels strange at first for experienced staff, because you know things the passage does not say; practising the discipline is most of the preparation.

For a service desk analyst, verbal reasoning is a daily working skill in light disguise. Every call begins with listening and reading: the user's description of their problem, the history in the ticket, the steps in the article. Misread one line — "restart the router" versus "restart the laptop" — and you send the fix in the wrong direction. Your role also asks you to communicate outwards: writing ticket updates the next shift can act on, explaining an outage notice to a worried caller in plain words, and producing reports others can trust. Employers test verbal reasoning for first-line roles because accurate reading and clear, faithful restatement are the raw materials of good support.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Reading comprehension of procedures and notices** maps to your **Customer service management** skill: resolving requests within the SLA depends on correctly understanding what the SLA, the procedure, and the article actually say.
- **True/False/Cannot-say discipline** maps to your **Asset and configuration management** skill: recording only what is known to be true — and flagging what is unknown — is how records stay trustworthy.
- **Identifying what a user is really asking** maps to your **User focus** skill: understanding user stories and user needs starts with reading a user's message for its need, not just its surface words.
- **Faithful restatement and summary** maps to your **IT service reporting** skill: interpreting a data set or an incident update and "communicating this to others" without adding or losing meaning is a verbal skill.
- **Clear-writing judgement** maps to your **Ownership** skill: when you hand an issue to a new owner, the written handover is the baton — if it is unclear, the ownership chain breaks.

### Practice questions

**Passage A (for Questions 1-3):** "From Monday 2 March, the department's remote access service will require multi-factor authentication (MFA) for all users. Users who have not enrolled a second factor by Friday 27 February will be unable to connect remotely until they enrol. Enrolment takes about ten minutes and can be completed from the office network or by calling the service desk. Desk analysts may enrol users over the phone only after completing identity verification. The change does not affect access from departmental offices."

**Question 1 (easy) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "After 2 March, a user who has not enrolled a second factor will be unable to log in from a departmental office."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage says explicitly: "The change does not affect access from departmental offices." The statement claims office access will be blocked for unenrolled users, which directly contradicts that sentence, so the answer is False. The trap is the emotional pull of the earlier sentence about users being "unable to connect" — but that sentence is about connecting remotely, not from the office. Verbal questions often place the contradicting sentence at the end of the passage, after the alarming one; reading to the end before judging is the discipline. On a real desk, this exact distinction — remote access versus office access — is what you would need to explain calmly to a worried caller on 3 March.

**Question 2 (easy) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "A service desk analyst may enrol a user over the phone without verifying their identity, provided the user is calling from an office number."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage states that analysts "may enrol users over the phone only after completing identity verification". The word "only" makes identity verification an absolute condition — there are no stated exceptions, including calls from office numbers. The statement claims an exception exists, so it contradicts the passage: False. Notice the technique: find the sentence that governs the statement, and check the strength of its qualifying words. "Only after" is strict; "normally after" would have made the answer Cannot say, because an unstated exception might then exist. This mirrors real security procedure, where "only" means only — and where analysts are the first line of defence against callers who try to talk their way around verification.

**Question 3 (easy) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "Most users had already enrolled a second factor by 27 February."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Search the passage for any information about how many users enrolled by the deadline. There is none: the passage sets the deadline and describes the consequences of missing it, but says nothing about how many people actually met it. The statement is perfectly plausible — and plausibility is exactly the trap. "Cannot say" is the correct answer whenever the passage neither supports nor contradicts the statement, no matter how likely the statement sounds from your general experience. This is the single most important habit in verbal reasoning tests, and it has a direct workplace parallel: a ticket note should never say "user probably didn't enrol" when what you actually know is "enrolment status not yet checked".

**Passage B (for Questions 4-6):** "Service desk analysts must log every user contact as a ticket, including contacts resolved during the call. Where a contact reports a fault already covered by an open major incident, the analyst should link the new ticket to the major incident record rather than troubleshooting individually. Users should be given the major incident reference and the current estimated restoration time, where one has been published. Analysts should not give personal estimates of restoration times. Tickets linked to a major incident are closed automatically when the major incident is resolved."

**Question 4 (easy) — Identifying the main obligation**

Which of the following best summarises the passage's core instruction to analysts?

- A) Analysts should avoid logging tickets during major incidents to save time.
- B) Every contact is logged; contacts about a known major incident are linked to it, and users are told the reference and any published restoration estimate.
- C) Analysts should troubleshoot each major-incident-related call individually to be thorough.
- D) Analysts should estimate restoration times from their own experience so users get an answer.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B faithfully compresses all four instructions: log everything, link rather than troubleshoot when a major incident is open, share the reference and any published estimate, and (implicitly) stay within published information. Option A contradicts the first sentence — every contact is logged, even during major incidents. Option C contradicts the explicit "rather than troubleshooting individually". Option D contradicts "Analysts should not give personal estimates". Summary questions reward the answer that keeps every obligation and adds nothing; the wrong options each break exactly one sentence of the passage, which is how such questions are usually constructed. In real work, this passage is also a model of why linking matters: it keeps the user counted, keeps the picture of impact accurate, and closes their ticket automatically at resolution.

**Question 5 (moderate) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "If no restoration time has been published, the analyst should tell the user their own best guess."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Two sentences govern this. First: users should be given "the current estimated restoration time, where one has been published" — so the sharing duty applies only to published estimates. Second, and decisively: "Analysts should not give personal estimates of restoration times." A "best guess" from the analyst is precisely a personal estimate, so the statement contradicts the passage: False. The workplace logic is worth absorbing: a guessed time that passes without restoration produces a second, angrier call and erodes trust in every future estimate the desk gives. The honest alternative — "no estimate has been published yet; here is your reference; updates will follow" — is both compliant with the passage and kinder to the user over the whole life of the incident.

**Question 6 (moderate) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "A ticket linked to a major incident may need to be reopened by the analyst if the user's fault continues after the major incident is resolved."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage says linked tickets "are closed automatically when the major incident is resolved". It says nothing about what happens if a user's individual fault persists afterwards — no reopening procedure is described, and none is ruled out. The statement is operationally sensible, and many real desks do exactly this, but the passage neither supports nor contradicts it, so the disciplined answer is Cannot say. This question is a deliberate test of the experienced analyst's restraint: the more you know about real service desks, the more tempted you are to answer True. Answer from the text alone. The same restraint keeps real records honest — writing what the procedure says, not what you assume it must intend.

**Passage C (for Questions 7-9):** An email from a user: "Hi, sorry to bother you. My laptop has been slow all week, but that's not why I'm writing. This morning I got an email saying my mailbox is nearly full and I'll stop receiving messages tomorrow unless I clear space. I'm in hearings all day and can't sort this out myself. I've also noticed the email came from an address I don't recognise — is it even genuine? Please can someone help before tomorrow? Thanks, Sam (Casework, Cardiff)."

**Question 7 (moderate) — What is the user actually asking for?**

Which of the following best captures Sam's need?

- A) A fix for a slow laptop this week.
- B) Urgent help with the mailbox-full warning before tomorrow, including a check of whether the warning email is genuine.
- C) Training on how to archive email.
- D) A new laptop, since the current one is slow.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Read for the stated need, its deadline, and its conditions. Sam explicitly sets the slow laptop aside ("that's not why I'm writing"), which eliminates options A and D as the primary need — though a good analyst would log it as a secondary issue. The core request is help with the mailbox warning before tomorrow, and Sam adds a second, connected question: whether the email is genuine at all. Option B is the only answer that captures both the urgency and the authenticity concern. Option C invents a need Sam never expressed — Sam is in hearings and cannot act personally, so training would not help by tomorrow anyway. This is your user focus skill in miniature: user need first ("keep my email working while I'm unavailable"), including the parts the user flags as doubts, not just the parts that look like standard requests. And the authenticity question matters doubly: a "mailbox full" email from an unrecognised address is a classic phishing pattern, so verifying it comes before acting on it.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Choosing the best written reply**

Which reply to Sam is clearest and most appropriate?

- A) "Hi Sam — your mailbox quota issue has been remediated via retention policy adjustment pending infrastructure-side archival execution."
- B) "Hi Sam — thanks for flagging this. First, please don't click anything in that email: we'll check whether it's genuine and confirm. Meanwhile we've raised ticket INC-2026-00712 and will make sure your mailbox keeps receiving messages — no action needed from you today. We've also logged your slow laptop as a separate ticket and will follow up after your hearings."
- C) "Hi Sam — this is probably fine, these warnings usually sort themselves out. Ignore it."
- D) "Hi Sam — please free up 500MB by deleting old attachments before 9am tomorrow, per policy EM-14."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Judge each reply against the user's situation: unavailable all day, worried about authenticity, facing a deadline. Option B addresses the possible phishing risk first (do not click), takes ownership with a ticket reference, removes the burden from the user ("no action needed from you today"), and captures the secondary issue without letting it distract. Option A may be accurate but is written in system jargon a caseworker should not have to decode — clear writing means the reader's language, not the desk's. Option C is dangerously casual: it dismisses a possible phishing email and promises nothing. Option D ignores everything Sam said about being unavailable and pushes the work back onto the user. Clear-writing questions in verbal tests reward the answer that is accurate, plain, and shaped around the reader — which is also exactly what your ticket updates and user emails should look like.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Inference from a service report extract**

A monthly report states: "Complaints about the service desk fell from 30 in June to 18 in July. The desk answered 12% fewer calls in July, as the new online self-service portal absorbed simple password resets. Average call duration rose from 5.1 to 6.4 minutes." Which inference is best supported?

- A) The desk gave worse service in July, because calls took longer.
- B) The remaining calls in July were likely more complex on average, since simple password resets — typically quick calls — moved to the portal.
- C) The portal caused the complaints to fall.
- D) Desk staff worked more slowly in July.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Connect the facts without overreaching. The portal absorbed simple password resets; password resets are typically short calls; removing short calls from the mix raises the average duration of the calls that remain, even if analysts work exactly as before. Option B follows from this composition effect and is the best-supported inference. Options A and D both misread the rising average as worse or slower service — the classic mistake of ignoring a change in the mix of work. Option C overreaches: complaints fell in the same period, but the passage never links the two, and Cannot-say discipline applies to causes just as it does to facts. This is the inference skill your reporting duty depends on: when a metric moves, ask first "did the work change?" before concluding "did the people change?" — and write the report so readers draw the supported conclusion, not the tempting one.

**Passage D (for Questions 10-11):** "The department will replace multi-function devices (MFDs) at all sites during the autumn. Each site will lose printing for one working day during its swap. Site swap dates will be announced at least ten working days in advance. Analysts should direct users' questions about swap dates to the published schedule rather than answering from memory. During a site's swap day, urgent printing can be done at the nearest alternative site, and the service desk can redirect print queues on request. Specialised IT equipment, including large-format scanners, is out of scope for this programme."

**Question 10 (challenging) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "A user at a site whose swap is underway has no way to print urgently that day."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage offers two explicit routes for urgent printing on a swap day: printing at the nearest alternative site, and asking the service desk to redirect print queues. The statement claims there is "no way", which the passage directly contradicts, so the answer is False. The pattern to notice: absolute statements ("no way", "never", "all", "none") are contradicted by a single counter-example in the passage, so hunt for one before considering Cannot say. In real life, this is also the difference between a frustrating call and a helpful one — the analyst who knows the passage can turn "printing is down today" into "here are your two options right now", which is service focus and customer service in the same breath.

**Question 11 (challenging) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "Large-format scanners at each site will be replaced after the MFD programme finishes."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage says large-format scanners are "out of scope for this programme" — meaning this programme will not replace them. It says nothing about what happens after the programme: a later replacement might be planned, or might not. The statement asserts a future replacement the passage neither promises nor denies, so the answer is Cannot say. Be careful of the half-match trap: the statement mentions something the passage genuinely discusses (scanners, scope), which creates a feeling of familiarity — but familiarity is not support. Check what is actually asserted (future replacement) against what is actually written (excluded from this programme). On the desk, the same care stops you telling a user "your scanner will be replaced later" when all you truly know is "not in this programme" — a small overstatement that becomes a broken promise in the user's memory.

### Preparation tips

- Practise the True/False/Cannot-say discipline daily: read a short work notice, invent three statements about it, and judge each strictly from the text. Cannot-say will feel uncomfortable at first; that discomfort is the skill forming.
- Read the whole passage before judging any statement — contradicting sentences are often placed at the end.
- Circle qualifying words in both passage and statement: "only", "unless", "all", "may", "must", "where published". Most answers turn on one such word.
- For "best summary" and "best reply" questions, eliminate options that break any single sentence of the passage; the right answer breaks none.
- When a statement sounds plausible from your own desk experience, treat that as a warning sign, not evidence. The test asks what the passage supports.
- Build the plain-writing habit: after reading any technical notice, say it back in one sentence a non-technical colleague would understand. Your calls, tickets, and test answers will all improve together.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Answering from experience, not from the passage.** Your desk knowledge is an asset at work and a liability in this test. Text only.
- **Stopping at the first relevant sentence.** A later sentence may qualify or reverse it. Read to the end.
- **Treating plausible as true.** "Probably true in real life" is exactly what Cannot say exists for.
- **Missing absolutes.** Statements with "no way", "never", or "all" are usually False if the passage contains even one exception — find it.
- **Choosing summaries that add helpful detail.** A summary that includes anything the passage does not say is wrong, however sensible the addition.
- **Rushing user emails.** In tests and on the desk alike, users often bury the real request mid-message ("that's not why I'm writing"). Read the whole message before deciding what is being asked.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement test (SJT) presents realistic workplace dilemmas and asks you to judge possible responses. Unlike the other assessments in this guide, there is often no single "right fact" — instead, the test measures your professional judgement: how you balance the user's needs, your team, procedure, honesty, and knowing your own limits. Common formats include choosing the most effective response, choosing the most and least effective from four options, ranking all the options, or rating each response's effectiveness on a scale.

The typical format is an online test of 20 to 40 minutes with 10 to 20 scenarios. It is usually untimed or generously timed, because it measures judgement rather than speed. Scoring compares your choices against a key agreed by experienced professionals and calibrated to the organisation's values — in UK government contexts, that means behaviours like user focus, taking ownership, working openly with colleagues, escalating honestly, and treating everyone with respect.

For a service desk analyst, the SJT is arguably the most job-relevant assessment of all, because your role lives in exactly these moments: a caller is upset, a queue is building, you are not sure of the answer, a colleague is struggling, or a rule points one way and kindness seems to point another. Your role summary is explicit about the judgement it expects — empathise with the end user, own an issue until it is resolved or handed over, and know when to escalate to your manager. The scenarios below practise those exact muscles. A tip before you start: answer as your best professional self on a normal day — not an idealised hero, and not your most tired Friday afternoon. Consistency and honesty produce the truest score.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Handling difficult calls with care** maps to your **Customer service management** skill: "empathise with the end user" and "use customer-focused metrics to achieve the desired outcome" describe the behaviour SJTs score most heavily for first-line roles.
- **Knowing when to escalate** maps to your role-level duty: "know when to escalate issues to your manager" — SJT scenarios repeatedly probe whether you escalate too early (avoiding ownership), too late (guarding pride), or at the right moment for the user.
- **Staying with an issue** maps to your **Ownership** skill: "own an issue until a new owner has been found or the problem has been mitigated or resolved" — the SJT tests whether you hand over cleanly or drop the baton.
- **Accurate records and honesty** maps to your **Asset and configuration management** and **IT service reporting** skills: several scenarios test whether you keep records truthful under pressure.
- **Seeing the user behind the ticket** maps to your **User focus** and **Service focus** skills: choosing responses that serve the user's real need, and noticing when many users share one underlying problem.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — The angry caller**

A caller is angry: their laptop failed this morning, they have a deadline at noon, and this is their second call because the first ticket "went nowhere". They raise their voice and say the desk is useless. What is the most effective first response?

- A) Tell them calmly that you cannot help while they are shouting, and ask them to call back later.
- B) Listen without interrupting, acknowledge the frustration and the deadline, apologise for the poor experience so far, then confirm the ticket details and set out what you will do right now.
- C) Explain that the first analyst followed procedure and the delay is not the desk's fault.
- D) Transfer the call straight to your manager, since the caller is upset.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B does the three things effective first-line responses always do with an upset user: let them be heard, acknowledge the impact honestly (frustration plus a real deadline), and move immediately to concrete action. This is "empathise with the end user" made practical — empathy followed by a plan, not empathy instead of a plan. Option A escalates the conflict and abandons the user at their moment of greatest need; a boundary on genuinely abusive language may be appropriate later, but this caller is frustrated, not abusive. Option C defends the desk instead of helping the user — even if factually right, it wins the argument and loses the user. Option D passes the emotion upwards without attempting the recovery that is squarely within your role; escalation is for issues beyond your remit, not for feelings you would rather avoid. Note the general SJT pattern: acknowledge, take ownership, act.

**Question 2 (easy) — Unsure of the fix**

A caller has a fault you do not recognise, on an application you rarely support. The knowledge base article is unclear, and you are not confident the steps it suggests are safe. The caller says "just try something". What is the most effective response?

- A) Try a few likely fixes — you will probably stumble on the right one.
- B) Tell the caller their application is not supported and close the ticket.
- C) Be honest that you want to check before acting, place them briefly on hold or arrange a call-back, and confirm the correct approach with a senior analyst or the article's owning team.
- D) Read the unclear article steps to the caller and let them decide whether to try them.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C is honest about the limits of your knowledge, protects the user from harm, and uses the escalation route your role explicitly gives you — knowing when to ask is a listed duty, not a weakness. Option A risks making things worse on a live system to avoid a moment of awkwardness; "just try something" from a frustrated caller does not transfer the risk to them. Option B is false (the desk supports departmental applications) and abandons ownership. Option D dresses up option A in the user's clothing: the caller cannot judge the safety of steps you yourself doubt, so passing the decision to them is not respect — it is risk transfer. SJT keys consistently reward calibrated confidence: act within your competence, verify at its edge, escalate beyond it, and be transparent throughout.

**Question 3 (easy) — The queue and the colleague**

Mid-morning, calls are queuing. You notice the new analyst next to you has been on one call for 25 minutes, looks flustered, and is repeating the same steps. You are between calls. What is the most effective action?

- A) Stay focused on taking the next queued call — the queue is the priority.
- B) Send them a quick message offering help, and if they accept, either take over the call warmly or suggest the next diagnostic step — then take your next queued call.
- C) Interrupt their call and take over, announcing that you will handle it from here.
- D) Mention it to your manager at the end of the week so they can arrange training.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B helps two users at once: the caller stuck in a 25-minute loop, and your colleague, who learns a next step instead of a humiliation. It costs perhaps two minutes against the queue — a good trade, because one call circling for 25 minutes hurts service more than a brief pause before your next pick-up. Option A optimises your own statistics while a caller and a colleague both struggle in plain sight; team service is measured at the desk level, not the individual level. Option C solves the call but damages the colleague's confidence and their standing with the caller — how you help matters as much as helping. Option D defers to the right person at the wrong speed: training feedback is valuable this week, useless for the caller right now, and stronger SJT answers act on the immediate problem and feed the learning back afterwards.

**Question 4 (easy) — Logging the awkward ticket**

Just before your shift ends, you resolve a quick call — a user's monitor cable was loose. Logging it properly will take three minutes, and your lift home is waiting. A colleague says: "It was nothing — don't bother logging it." What do you do?

- A) Skip logging this once; it genuinely was trivial.
- B) Log it tomorrow morning from memory.
- C) Log it now, briefly but accurately, before leaving.
- D) Ask the colleague to log it for you tomorrow.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C is the disciplined choice, and the reasoning matters more than the three minutes. Complete logging is not bureaucracy: desk statistics drive staffing and SLA reporting, and "trivial" contacts are data — if loose cables happen weekly at one site, the pattern only becomes visible if the contacts are logged. Your reporting skill depends on the data being complete, and your asset and configuration skill depends on records reflecting reality. Option B fades: memory-based logging tomorrow produces vaguer, less accurate records, and busy mornings routinely delete good intentions. Option D transfers your duty to someone with even less knowledge of the call. Option A starts a slope: every unlogged contact makes the next one easier to skip, and understates the desk's real workload — which ultimately argues against the staffing your team needs. Small integrity, consistently applied, is what SJT keys reward.

**Question 5 (moderate) — The security-shaped request**

A caller says they are a private secretary calling on behalf of a director who is locked out and about to join a ministerial call. They ask you to reset the director's password and read it out over the phone "to save time", but they cannot answer the identity verification questions for the director's account. What is the most effective response?

- A) Reset the password and share it — the situation is clearly urgent and senior.
- B) Refuse and end the call.
- C) Explain warmly that you cannot bypass identity verification for any account, and offer the fastest compliant route — for example, contacting the director directly through a verified channel, or engaging your manager immediately given the urgency.
- D) Ask the caller more questions until you feel comfortable, then reset it.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** This scenario is a classic social-engineering pattern: urgency plus seniority plus a request to bypass procedure. Option C holds the security line without abandoning the user's genuine need — it pairs a firm "no" to the bypass with an energetic "yes" to the fastest legitimate path, and it brings your manager in because the stakes are high and the situation unusual: exactly what "know when to escalate" means. Option A could hand an account — possibly a director's account — to an impostor; no deadline outweighs that. Option B protects security but fails the user completely if the request is genuine; the desk's job is to be both safe and helpful. Option D substitutes your comfort for the verification procedure, which exists precisely because comfort can be manufactured by a skilled caller. SJT keys in government settings score security-versus-service dilemmas heavily: the strong answer never trades verification away, and never stops helping either.

**Question 6 (moderate) — The pattern in the queue**

During one hour you personally take four calls from different users, all describing slow logins in the Swansea office. Each fault is mild, and each user is content with the workaround you give. Your shift is busy. What is the most effective action beyond resolving each call?

- A) Nothing further — each user was helped and each ticket is closed.
- B) Link the tickets, note the common site and symptom, and flag the pattern to your senior analyst or manager as a possible underlying problem.
- C) Email all Swansea users warning of major problems ahead.
- D) Start investigating the Swansea network yourself between calls.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B is the behaviour that turns a good analyst into a valuable one: seeing the service behind the symptoms. Four similar reports from one site in one hour — from just the calls that happened to reach you — suggests a shared cause that individual workarounds will never fix, and possibly a fault that is still developing. Linking tickets and flagging the pattern is precisely your role's shape: you spot and report; senior colleagues investigate and coordinate. Option A treats the desk as four separate transactions and lets an emerging problem grow unrecorded — it fails the "be aware of different products and services" spirit of your service focus skill. Option C overreaches your knowledge (you do not know it will worsen) and your remit (site-wide communications are coordinated, not improvised). Option D overreaches your role and would pull you off the phones your team needs you on. Escalating a well-described pattern is ownership, not avoidance.

**Question 7 (moderate) — The tempting shortcut on records**

While updating an asset record after a call, you notice the previous analyst recorded a laptop as returned to store, but the user has just told you they still have it. Correcting the record means reopening a closed task and possibly flagging your colleague's error. What do you do?

- A) Leave the record — it is the previous analyst's responsibility.
- B) Correct the record with a factual note of what the user told you, and mention it to the colleague or your senior analyst so any similar errors can be caught.
- C) Correct the record but keep it quiet to protect your colleague.
- D) Tell your manager the colleague is making errors, without touching the record.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B does both halves of the job: it fixes the data and addresses the cause, factually and without blame. Your asset and configuration management skill is literally "track, log and correct information to protect assets" — a laptop recorded as in store while actually with a user is exactly the kind of error that loses equipment and corrupts stock checks (as the numeric section's reconciliation question showed). Option A walks past a known-wrong record; ownership does not stop at "not mine". Option C fixes today's record but hides the signal — if the colleague misunderstands the return process, the error will repeat, and quiet corrections mean nobody learns. Option D inverts C: it raises the person without fixing the fact, and framing it as "making errors" turns a process signal into an accusation. The professional pattern is: correct the record, state the facts, share the learning through the lightest appropriate channel.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Promising what you cannot promise**

A caller with an ongoing fault asks: "Can you promise this will be fixed by tomorrow? I'll tell my team it will be." Second-line support owns the ticket; you genuinely do not know the timescale. The caller is pleasant and clearly under pressure. What is the most effective response?

- A) Promise tomorrow — it will probably be fine, and the caller needs reassurance.
- B) Say it is out of your hands and they should contact second line themselves.
- C) Be honest that you cannot promise a time because another team is working on it, commit to what you can control — chasing an update today and calling them back with whatever you learn — and log their deadline pressure on the ticket.
- D) Say nothing is ever certain in IT and they should plan for the worst.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C separates what you control from what you do not, and promises only within the first set — the single most important honesty habit in service work. Chasing the update, calling back, and recording the user's deadline are all genuinely yours to promise, and they give the caller something real to tell their team. Option A buys thirty seconds of relief at the cost of tomorrow's broken promise, a second angrier call, and the desk's credibility — and the caller will repeat your promise to their team, multiplying the damage. Option B breaks the ownership chain your role defines: you own the relationship until a new owner is genuinely in place, and "contact them yourself" is the desk washing its hands. Option D is honest in the least helpful way available — vague pessimism serves nobody. Notice that C also feeds useful information forward: logging the deadline gives second line what it needs to prioritise humanely.

**Question 9 (challenging) — The colleague under pressure and the metric**

Your desk publishes each analyst's daily resolved-call count. A colleague quietly tells you they have started closing tickets as "resolved" after giving users a workaround, then advising users to "call back and open a new ticket if it recurs" — it keeps their numbers high, and they suggest you do the same, adding "everyone does it". What is the most effective response?

- A) Adopt the practice — if everyone does it, refusing just makes your numbers look worse.
- B) Decline for yourself but say nothing further; their tickets are their business.
- C) Decline, explain briefly why it misleads the desk's data and harms users, and if the practice continues or really is widespread, raise the pattern with your senior analyst or manager — focusing on the metric's design as well as the behaviour.
- D) Announce at the team meeting that the colleague is gaming the statistics.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** This is the hardest kind of SJT scenario: integrity versus loyalty versus self-interest, with a system pressure underneath. Option C gets all three layers right. Personally, you decline — closing unresolved issues misstates the truth, and users who must call back and start again are paying for the metric. Interpersonally, you tell the colleague why, giving them the chance to stop before anything escalates. Systemically, if it continues or is genuinely widespread, that is a signal the metric itself is driving bad behaviour — leadership needs to know, and framing it as "our measure may be encouraging early closure" raises the issue honestly without turning it into a trial of one colleague. Option A makes you part of a practice that quietly breaks your desk's reporting and your users' trust. Option B protects your own hands while the data and the users keep suffering — clean hands are not the same as ownership. Option D chooses the maximum-humiliation channel, which converts a fixable behaviour into a public conflict. The professional sequence is: decline, explain, then escalate the pattern — not the person — through the right channel.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Most and least effective**

At 16:45, fifteen minutes before your desk closes, a major application goes down. Calls flood in. Your manager is in a meeting and unreachable for a few minutes. The desk procedure says a suspected major incident should be reported to the duty manager immediately. Rank-relevant options:

1. Follow the major incident procedure: alert the duty manager through the standing route, keep logging and linking the incoming calls, and tell callers honestly that the issue is being escalated.
2. Wait for your own manager to leave the meeting, since they know the application best.
3. Keep answering calls individually and troubleshooting each one until closing time.
4. Post in the team channel that the application "seems broken" and carry on as normal.

Which is the MOST effective and which is the LEAST effective response?

- A) Most: 1; Least: 3
- B) Most: 1; Least: 4
- C) Most: 2; Least: 3
- D) Most: 3; Least: 2

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Option 1 is clearly most effective: the procedure exists precisely for this moment, the duty manager route is designed to work when your own manager is unreachable, and linking calls while giving callers honest status is textbook major-incident hygiene — it protects users, data, and the desk's credibility simultaneously, right up against closing time. For least effective, compare 2, 3, and 4. Option 4 is weak — vague, informal, no procedure — but it does at least create a visible signal that others might act on. Option 2 delays the alarm but only by minutes, and includes an intention to escalate. Option 3 is the worst: it actively absorbs the desk's remaining capacity in individually troubleshooting symptoms of a single cause, raises no alarm at all through any channel, and runs the clock out so the incident crosses into the unstaffed evening unreported. It fails escalation, ownership, and users all at once while feeling busy and diligent from the inside — which is exactly why SJTs test it. The lesson: in a suspected major incident, the first duty is to make the right people aware through the designed route; heroic individual effort that delays the alarm is not diligence but risk.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Inclusion on the phones**

A caller with a stammer is struggling to describe their fault. Calls are queuing, and your average handling time is monitored. Partway through, a colleague messages you: "Wrap it up, queue's building." What is the most effective response?

- A) Politely hurry the caller along, offering to guess the rest of their fault description.
- B) Give the caller the time they need, use patient techniques — letting them finish, confirming understanding, offering to switch to email or chat if they would prefer — and briefly reply to your colleague that you are on a call that needs proper time.
- C) Ask the caller to email the desk instead, and take the next queued call.
- D) Transfer the caller to a quieter colleague.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B treats the caller as the point of the desk, not an obstacle to its metrics. Letting the caller finish their own sentences (guessing is widely experienced as disrespectful — which also weakens option A), confirming understanding, and offering an alternative channel as a choice rather than a redirection are standard good practice for supporting callers with speech differences. The brief message back to your colleague matters too: it calmly asserts that handling time serves service, not the reverse — a point your customer-focused metrics skill statement makes explicitly, since metrics exist "to achieve the desired outcome", and the desired outcome is a helped user. Option C converts the caller's difficulty into an exclusion: it may suit some users, but imposed rather than offered, it says "your call is too slow for us". Option D exports the discomfort and makes the caller start again — worse service dressed as consideration, unless the caller themselves requests a different channel or person. Public service means every user gets support that works for them; the strong answer adapts the service to the user, never the user to the service.

### Preparation tips

- Before the test, reread your organisation's values and your role's behaviours: user focus, ownership, honesty, escalation, inclusion. SJT keys are built from these, so refreshing them tunes your judgement.
- For every scenario, identify who is affected — the user, the team, the data, the wider service — and prefer responses that serve all of them rather than trading one off silently.
- Watch for the recurring shapes: honesty versus comfort (never promise what you cannot control), security versus urgency (never trade verification), ownership versus handover (pass the baton, never drop it), and individual versus pattern (link and flag repeats).
- In "most and least effective" formats, judge each option independently before comparing; the least effective is often the busy-looking one, not the lazy-looking one.
- Answer as your consistent professional self, not an imagined hero. Keys are calibrated to realistic good practice, and inconsistency between answers reads as noise.
- Talk through a few scenarios with a trusted senior colleague. Hearing how experienced people weigh the same dilemma is the fastest calibration available.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Choosing the "toughest" or "kindest" answer every time.** Good judgement is situational; the key rewards the response that fits this scenario, not a fixed persona.
- **Escalating to avoid ownership, or owning to avoid escalation.** Both extremes score poorly. The strong answer does what you can and involves others at the designed moment.
- **Trading honesty for short-term comfort.** Promised times, quietly closed tickets, and skipped logs all buy seconds now and cost trust later; keys punish all three.
- **Punishing colleagues publicly.** Responses that fix problems through humiliation score badly even when the underlying concern is valid. Address facts first, through the lightest channel.
- **Forgetting the data.** Records, links, and logs appear in many scenarios as a quiet secondary duty; answers that help the caller but corrupt or omit the record are weaker than they look.
- **Ignoring the security pattern.** Urgency plus seniority plus "skip the check just this once" is always a red flag, in tests and in real calls alike.
