## Introduction

Welcome. This guide is written for you as a principal developer in the management track of the UK Government Digital and Data profession. You lead and plan development across large or multiple teams, you are an expert across many technical areas or a deep specialist in one, and you use that expertise alongside related disciplines to keep multiple teams working effectively. Your role summary also names developing capability through internal mobility, career paths and recruitment, and identifying, testing and championing emerging technologies.

The management track adds responsibilities that change the centre of the work: setting the strategy and managing resource allocation for solution development programmes, and working with client functions to establish business requirements. Alongside these sit leading and developing teams of experts, analysing and optimising processes across the organisation, and setting policy and standards.

That combination is unusual and it is what makes the role difficult. You are expected to hold deep technical judgement and to spend most of your time on things that are not technical: where the people go, what the programme commits to, what the business actually needs as distinct from what it has asked for, and which of several defensible strategies the organisation will follow. The technical depth is not decoration — a resource allocation made by someone who cannot judge technical difficulty is guesswork, and a requirement negotiated by someone who cannot see the implementation cost is a promise the teams will have to break.

You may be preparing for a formal assessment, or you may want to re-examine judgement that has become fluent. Fluency is the risk at this level, because the feedback loops on strategy and resourcing decisions are years long and heavily confounded, so a wrong pattern can persist for a very long time without correction.

Three things shape what follows. First, resource allocation is a zero-sum activity where every yes is an unstated no, and the discipline is making the no explicit. Second, business requirements as stated are usually solutions in disguise, and the useful work is recovering the need underneath. Third, strategy at your level is mostly about what you will not do, which is the part organisations resist.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the reasoning your role demands, presented through role-realistic material. On the management track at principal level that means programme strategy, resource allocation, requirements analysis, organisational process design and capability planning.

The format is typically fifteen to thirty minutes online, covering deduction, pattern recognition, error checking, prioritisation and applied problem solving, scored against a norm group of comparable seniority.

Three things distinguish this section at your level.

The first is that stated requirements are usually solutions. "We need a dashboard" is an answer somebody has already reached, and the question it answers — usually about a decision they need to make faster — often has a cheaper answer.

The second is that allocation decisions are comparative. There is no such thing as a good allocation in isolation; there is only the best available use of a person or a period, and evaluating one option without its alternatives is not evaluation.

The third is that organisational effects lag and confound. A change you make now shows up in eighteen months, mixed in with four other changes, which means you must reason about mechanisms rather than waiting for outcomes.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Requirements analysis** maps to working with client functions to establish business requirements.
- **Allocation and prioritisation** map to managing resource allocation for solution development programmes.
- **Strategy reasoning** maps to setting the strategy for those programmes.
- **Process diagnosis** maps to **Development process optimisation**.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Deduction from an allocation rule**

Your policy states: "A team may be allocated additional developers only where its backlog exceeds two sprints of capacity, unless the allocation is to cover planned absence." A team received additional developers. Which must be true?

- A) Its backlog exceeded two sprints.
- B) The allocation covered planned absence.
- C) Either its backlog exceeded two sprints, or the allocation covered planned absence, or the policy was not followed.
- D) The allocation was justified.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A rule with an exception, plus a fact silent on both branches, licenses only the disjunction — and note that the third limb, the policy not being followed, must be included, since the facts do not establish that the policy was applied at all. Options A and B each pick a branch. Option D assumes compliance and equates compliance with justification, which are different claims.

**Question 2 (easy) — A requirement that is a solution**

A client function tells you they need a real-time dashboard showing case volumes. What is the strongest first step?

- A) Scope the dashboard and estimate it.
- B) Ask what decision they would make differently with the information and how quickly — because "real-time dashboard" is a solution, and if the underlying need is a weekly capacity decision then a daily emailed figure meets it at a fraction of the cost.
- C) Explain that real-time is expensive.
- D) Offer a daily report instead.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** "Real-time" is the most expensive word in requirements and it is almost never needed. The question in option B recovers the actual need, and the two parts matter jointly: what decision, and how quickly — because a decision made weekly cannot benefit from real-time data no matter how much people would like to see it. Option D reaches a plausible answer without establishing it, which means you may be wrong and you have also taught the client that you substitute your judgement for their request. Option C leads with cost, which sounds obstructive. Option A prices the solution rather than meeting the need.

**Question 3 (moderate) — Allocation with an unstated cost**

A programme director asks you to move two developers onto a high-profile project for six weeks. What is the strongest response?

- A) Move them; the director has asked and the project is high-profile.
- B) Say what the move costs — specifically what those two people would otherwise have delivered and what slips as a result — and let the director decide with both sides visible, because a resource request considered without its opportunity cost is always granted.
- C) Decline; the existing work is committed.
- D) Move one developer as a compromise.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Every allocation is a comparison, and the request as posed presents only one side of it. Option B supplies the other side and leaves the decision where it belongs — the director may well decide the high-profile project is worth the slip, and that is a legitimate call made properly rather than by default. The sentence "a resource request considered without its opportunity cost is always granted" is the mechanism, and it is why allocation decisions drift towards whatever is most visible. Option D splits the difference without evaluating either side and delivers two half-served pieces of work. Option C makes the decision unilaterally on your side.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Diagnosing a programme in trouble**

A programme is six months behind. The programme manager attributes it to unclear requirements. Teams attribute it to changing priorities. What is the strongest reading?

- A) Requirements are unclear; the programme manager has the fullest view.
- B) The two accounts are likely the same phenomenon seen from different positions — priorities changing and requirements being unclear are both what it looks like when the business need has not been settled — so the useful question is who is empowered to decide the scope and whether they have.
- C) The teams are closer to the work and more likely correct.
- D) Both are symptoms of poor programme management.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The two explanations sound contradictory and describe one situation. When nobody has authority to settle scope, the teams experience it as priorities changing and the programme manager experiences it as requirements never becoming clear. Option B identifies the single underlying question — who decides, and have they — which is answerable and actionable, whereas adjudicating between the two accounts is neither. Option D names a category rather than a mechanism. Options A and C pick a side on the basis of position rather than evidence.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Strategy as exclusion**

You are asked to set a technical strategy for solution development across the organisation. What makes it useful?

- A) A clear statement of the technologies and approaches the organisation will use.
- B) A clear statement of what the organisation will not do — which platforms will not be supported, which patterns are out of scope, which needs will not be met centrally — because a strategy that only says yes provides no basis for declining anything and therefore constrains nothing.
- C) Alignment with the wider government technology strategy.
- D) Endorsement from senior leadership.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A strategy is a set of decisions made in advance so that individual cases do not have to be argued, and it can only do that if it excludes. A document listing approved technologies without saying what happens to the unapproved ones generates the same argument every time. Option B is the substance. Options C and D are both genuinely necessary — a strategy without leadership endorsement is a document, and one misaligned with wider government direction will be overridden — but they are conditions for a strategy to have effect rather than what makes it useful. Option A is the form most strategies take and the reason most have no effect.

**Question 6 (moderate) — A process improvement with a distributed cost**

You are considering mandating a design review step before any significant build starts. What is the strongest analysis?

- A) Adopt it; design review catches expensive mistakes early.
- B) Price the delay across the volume — if forty significant builds a year each wait an average of five days for a review slot, that is 200 team-days of delay annually, which the review must beat in avoided rework — and design the mechanism so the waiting time is small, since the value is in the review and the cost is nearly all in the queue.
- C) Adopt it for high-risk builds only.
- D) Make it advisory rather than mandatory.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Design review is usually worthwhile and the reason such steps fail is almost always the queue rather than the review. Option B quantifies the delay across the annual volume, which converts a plausible-sounding step into a comparison, and then identifies the design constraint — availability of review slots — that determines whether it works. Option C is a sensible refinement that needs a definition of high-risk. Option D avoids the mandate and thereby avoids the benefit, since advisory reviews are sought by the teams who need them least. Option A adopts a control without pricing it.

**Question 7 (harder) — Competing bids for the same capacity**

Three programmes each need the same specialist team next quarter. Their stated business cases claim benefits of £2m, £1.4m and £900k. What is the strongest basis for deciding?

- A) The £2m programme; it has the largest claimed benefit.
- B) Interrogate the claims before comparing them — benefits in business cases are self-reported by the party requesting resource, are stated on different bases and over different periods, and frequently include benefits that would arrive anyway — and then decide on comparable figures, weighted by what is lost if each waits a quarter.
- C) Split the team across all three.
- D) Decide on strategic alignment rather than benefits.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Self-reported benefits from parties competing for a resource are the least reliable numbers in an organisation, and comparing them as stated rewards optimistic authorship. Option B normalises them and adds the criterion the business cases omit, which is what a quarter's delay actually costs each — a programme whose benefit is undiminished by waiting has a much weaker claim than one where delay forfeits the benefit entirely. Option C is the fairness instinct and serves all three badly. Option D substitutes one soft criterion for another. Option A treats the largest claim as the largest benefit.

**Question 8 (harder) — A capability decision**

Two teams have an expert each in a critical technology. One expert is leaving. What is the strongest response?

- A) Recruit a replacement.
- B) Treat this as revealing a single point of failure that existed before the resignation — one expert per team is not resilience — so the response is to spread the knowledge deliberately, through pairing, documentation and rotation, and recruitment is one input to that rather than the whole answer.
- C) Move the remaining expert to cover both teams.
- D) Reduce dependence on the technology.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The resignation is the trigger and not the problem. Two teams with one expert each was already fragile — either departure, illness or holiday leaves a team without cover — and replacing the leaver restores the fragile state rather than fixing it. Option B addresses the structural position and keeps recruitment as one contributor. Option C creates a single point of failure across both teams, which is strictly worse. Option D may be right over a longer horizon and does nothing about the next three months. The general habit worth having is asking what a triggering event revealed rather than only what it caused.

**Question 9 (harder) — Reading a request from a client function**

A client function asks for a system that replicates their current manual process exactly, including several steps they acknowledge are workarounds for a limitation in an old system being retired. What is the strongest response?

- A) Build what they asked for; they know their process.
- B) Point out specifically which steps exist only because of the system being retired, and propose the process without them — because automating a workaround makes it permanent, and the point at which the old system goes is the only realistic opportunity to remove those steps.
- C) Build it as specified and improve it later.
- D) Ask them to redesign their process first.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The client function has already told you the workarounds are workarounds, which means the conversation is available. Automating them encodes a retired system's limitations into a new one indefinitely, and option C's "improve it later" reliably does not happen once the process is in use and people have been trained on it. Option B is specific — naming which steps and why — which is what makes it a proposal rather than a criticism of their process. Option D hands them a large piece of work with no support and delays everything. Option A is respectful of their expertise and ignores what they have told you.

**Question 10 (harder) — An organisational pattern**

Over two years, four separate programmes have each built their own notification capability. Each was aware of the others. What is the strongest analysis?

- A) The teams should collaborate more.
- B) Four teams independently choosing to build rather than reuse, while knowing about each other, indicates that reuse was more expensive than building — most likely because the existing capabilities were not discoverable, not usable without their owning team's help, or came with a dependency on that team's roadmap — so the fix is in what makes shared capabilities usable, not in encouraging collaboration.
- C) A central notification service should be mandated.
- D) The programmes were under time pressure.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Four informed teams making the same choice is a rational response to incentives, not a failure of cooperation. Option B identifies the realistic mechanisms, and each implies a different remedy: discoverability needs a catalogue, usability needs self-service onboarding, and roadmap dependency needs governance that stops a consuming team being blocked by a providing team's priorities. That last one is the commonest and the least discussed. Option C mandates reuse without making it cheaper, which produces compliance theatre and shadow implementations. Option A prescribes a behaviour without addressing why the behaviour is absent. Option D is probably true and does not explain a repeated pattern.

**Question 11 (harder) — A strategy that is being ignored**

Eighteen months after you set a technical strategy, most teams' choices are inconsistent with it. What is the strongest first step?

- A) Enforce it through the design authority.
- B) Establish why — whether teams do not know it, do not find it applicable to their situation, or find compliance more expensive than the alternative — because those have entirely different remedies, and a strategy widely ignored by capable people is more often unusable than defied.
- C) Rewrite the strategy to match what teams are doing.
- D) Escalate the non-compliance.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Widespread non-compliance by competent teams is evidence about the strategy. Option B names the three realistic causes and they diverge sharply: an unknown strategy needs communication, an inapplicable one needs scope or exceptions, and an expensive one needs the cost removing — most often by supplying the tooling or platform that makes the compliant path the easy path. Option A enforces before diagnosing, and enforcement against a strategy that is genuinely unusable produces malicious compliance. Option C abandons the strategic intent. Option D treats a systemic signal as a discipline matter.

**Question 12 (harder) — Deciding between two defensible strategies**

Two credible technical strategies are proposed for the next three years. Both are defensible; your senior colleagues are split. What is the strongest approach?

- A) Choose the technically stronger one.
- B) Ask which is more recoverable if wrong, and which the organisation can actually execute given its skills and delivery capacity — because with two defensible options the differentiator is rarely technical merit, and a strategy the organisation cannot execute is worse than a slightly weaker one it can.
- C) Take the decision to leadership.
- D) Adopt elements of both.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** When two options are both defensible, the technical comparison has already failed to separate them, and continuing to argue it produces a decision by stamina rather than by reasoning. Option B introduces the two criteria that do separate them: reversibility, and executability given the organisation you actually have. Option D is the consensus-seeking response and is usually the worst available, since a hybrid strategy typically acquires the costs of both and the coherence of neither — and coherence is most of what a strategy provides. Option C defers a decision that is yours to make and asks leadership to adjudicate a technical split they cannot evaluate. Option A restates the criterion that has already tied.

### Preparation tips

- **Ask what decision a requested capability would change, and how quickly.**
- **State the opportunity cost of every resource request.**
- **Define a strategy by what it excludes.**
- **Price a new process step across the annual volume, and design out the queue.**
- **Normalise self-reported benefits before comparing them.**
- **Ask what a triggering event revealed, not only what it caused.**
- **Read widespread non-compliance as evidence about the rule.**
- **Separate two defensible strategies by reversibility and executability.**

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Estimating the solution somebody asked for instead of finding the need.**
- **Granting a resource request without stating what it displaces.**
- **Adjudicating between two accounts that describe the same cause.**
- **Writing a strategy that only says yes.**
- **Comparing business cases as written.**
- **Replacing a departing single point of failure with another one.**
- **Automating a workaround for a system being retired.**
- **Adopting elements of both strategies.**

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment tests whether you can reach a defensible conclusion from data at speed. On the management track at principal level the data is programme finance, resource models, benefits cases, portfolio-level delivery metrics and capacity plans.

The format is typically fifteen to twenty-five minutes with a calculator, mixing computation with interpretation. Interpretation items dominate at your level, because the numbers you deal with are mostly produced by other people who want a particular answer.

Three habits are worth naming.

The first is that you are usually assessing someone else's arithmetic, and the errors are rarely in the sums. They are in the base, the period, the assumption of linearity, or a benefit that would have arrived anyway.

The second is that people costs dominate software programmes, and any analysis that treats them as fixed while varying the technology is measuring the smaller half.

The third is that portfolio-level figures conceal enormous variation, and a mean across programmes is generally the least informative statistic available.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Programme finance** maps to setting strategy and managing resource allocation.
- **Benefits analysis** maps to working with client functions to establish business requirements.
- **Resource modelling** maps to managing resource allocation across programmes.
- **Process metrics at scale** map to **Development process optimisation**.
- **Capacity and availability** map to **Availability and capacity management**.
- **Risk quantification** maps to **Information security** and **Systems design**.

*Table 1 — Four development programmes, current year*

| Programme | People | Annual cost (£000) | Features delivered | Live incidents |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Atlas | 24 | 2,640 | 61 | 14 |
| Beacon | 15 | 1,725 | 48 | 4 |
| Cirrus | 32 | 3,840 | 44 | 27 |
| Dovetail | 9 | 945 | 31 | 3 |

**Question 1 (easy) — Cost per person**

Which programme has the highest annual cost per person?

- A) Atlas
- B) Beacon
- C) Cirrus
- D) Dovetail

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Atlas £2,640k/24 = £110k; Beacon £1,725k/15 = £115k; Cirrus £3,840k/32 = £120k; Dovetail £945k/9 = £105k. Cirrus is highest at £120k per person. The spread is modest — £105k to £120k — which is worth noting because it means cost differences between these programmes are driven almost entirely by headcount rather than by rate, and any cost conversation is therefore a headcount conversation.

**Question 2 (easy) — Total portfolio**

What is the approximate total annual cost of the four programmes?

- A) £7.5m
- B) £9.2m
- C) £11.4m
- D) £8.1m

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 2,640 + 1,725 + 3,840 + 945 = £9,150k, so about £9.2m across 80 people. Establishing the denominator early is worth the ten seconds, because every subsequent percentage and every proposal for a portfolio-level change is measured against it — a tooling investment of £200k is 2% of the portfolio, which is a very different conversation from £200k in the abstract.

**Question 3 (moderate) — Cost per feature**

Which programme has the highest cost per feature delivered?

- A) Atlas
- B) Beacon
- C) Cirrus
- D) Dovetail

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Atlas £2,640k/61 = £43k; Beacon £1,725k/48 = £36k; Cirrus £3,840k/44 = £87k; Dovetail £945k/31 = £30k. Cirrus is highest at roughly £87k per feature, nearly three times Dovetail. Before drawing any conclusion, note that "features" is not a standardised unit — a programme delivering large features scores badly on this measure while delivering more — so the metric identifies where to look rather than what is true.

**Question 4 (moderate) — What the data supports**

What is the strongest conclusion about Cirrus from Table 1?

- A) Cirrus is inefficient and should be restructured.
- B) Cirrus is the outlier on every derived measure — highest cost per person, highest cost per feature, and by far the most incidents — which makes it the clearest candidate for investigation, though the table contains nothing about what the programmes are building and a high-complexity or high-risk programme would produce exactly this profile.
- C) Cirrus has too many people.
- D) Cirrus's incidents are caused by its size.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The consistency across three independent measures is a real signal and the confound is decisive: the table has no column for what each programme does. A programme replacing a critical legacy system carrying decades of accumulated business rules will have expensive features and frequent incidents regardless of how well it is run. Option B states the finding and the limit, which is the honest position and also the one that leads to a useful next step. Option A converts a signal into a verdict about people and structure. Option C infers overstaffing from output. Option D asserts a causal mechanism the data does not distinguish.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Incidents normalised**

Which programme has the highest incident rate per feature delivered?

- A) Atlas
- B) Beacon
- C) Cirrus
- D) Dovetail

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Atlas 14/61 = 0.23; Beacon 4/48 = 0.08; Cirrus 27/44 = 0.61; Dovetail 3/31 = 0.10. Cirrus at 0.61 incidents per feature is more than seven times Beacon. Normalising by delivery volume is the right correction to make before comparing, since a programme shipping more will naturally cause more incidents — and having made it, the gap widens rather than narrows, which strengthens the case that something specific to Cirrus warrants a look.

**Question 6 (moderate) — A benefits claim**

A client function proposes a system claiming £1.8m annual savings from removing 30 full-time equivalent roles. Fully loaded cost per role is £42,000. Is the claim internally consistent?

- A) Yes; 30 × £42,000 = £1.26m, close enough.
- B) No — 30 roles at £42,000 is £1.26m, not £1.8m, so the claim overstates by about £540,000 or 43%, and the difference needs explaining before the case can be relied on.
- C) Yes; other savings would make up the difference.
- D) There is not enough information.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 30 × £42,000 = £1,260,000 against a claim of £1,800,000, an overstatement of £540,000. The discrepancy might have an explanation — accommodation costs, licences, or a different loading assumption — and it needs stating rather than assuming. Checking the internal arithmetic of a business case before engaging with its conclusions is one of the highest-value things you can do in a review, and it takes under a minute. Option A waves through a 43% gap. Option C supplies the explanation on the author's behalf.

**Question 7 (harder) — Benefits that would arrive anyway**

The same case notes that 12 of the 30 roles are currently vacant and were due to be removed in a planned restructure. What is the strongest assessment?

- A) The saving is unaffected; the roles still go.
- B) Those 12 roles would be removed regardless, so they are not a benefit attributable to this system — the attributable saving is 18 roles at £42,000, about £756,000, which is 42% of the claim.
- C) The 12 vacancies should be excluded but the restructure roles counted.
- D) The whole case should be rejected.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A benefit that would arrive without the investment is not a benefit of the investment, and double-counting savings already committed elsewhere is the commonest inflation in a business case. 18 × £42,000 = £756,000, against a claimed £1.8m. Note this is not an accusation of bad faith — attribution errors of this kind are usually genuine, because the person writing the case sees the roles going and counts them. Option C draws the distinction in the wrong place. Option D rejects a case that may still be worth doing at £756,000, depending on cost.

*Table 2 — Resource plan, next four quarters*

| Quarter | Available developer-quarters | Committed | Requested (uncommitted) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Q1 | 80 | 74 | 12 |
| Q2 | 80 | 68 | 22 |
| Q3 | 84 | 51 | 40 |
| Q4 | 84 | 44 | 48 |

**Question 8 (moderate) — Reading the plan**

In which quarter does committed plus requested demand most exceed availability?

- A) Q1
- B) Q2
- C) Q3
- D) Q4

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Q1: 74 + 12 = 86 against 80, over by 6. Q2: 68 + 22 = 90 against 80, over by 10. Q3: 51 + 40 = 91 against 84, over by 7. Q4: 44 + 48 = 92 against 84, over by 8. Q2 has the largest excess at 10. Note that the largest total demand is Q4 at 92, which is the figure the eye goes to and the wrong one, because availability also rises in the second half of the year — the comparison has to be against each quarter's own capacity. The more important observation is that every quarter is oversubscribed, by between 7% and 13%.

**Question 9 (harder) — What the pattern shows**

What is the strongest interpretation of the trend in Table 2 from Q1 to Q4?

- A) Demand is increasing.
- B) Committed work falls from 74 to 44 while requested work rises from 12 to 48, which is the normal shape of a plan where near-term work is firm and later work is aspiration — so the later quarters are not really oversubscribed yet, they are unallocated, and the decision point is when requests convert to commitments.
- C) The organisation is running out of capacity.
- D) More developers are needed in Q4.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The two columns move in opposite directions in the way plans always do: commitments are made close in, and distant quarters hold requests that have not yet been decided. Reading Q4's apparent oversubscription as a capacity crisis misunderstands what the number is — it is a queue of asks, most of which will be declined, deferred or dropped. The genuine finding is that Q4 has 40 developer-quarters uncommitted, which is where your allocation discretion actually lives. Options A, C and D each treat unconverted requests as real demand, which is how organisations end up hiring for work that never materialises.

**Question 10 (harder) — Cost of a portfolio-wide change**

You are considering a change requiring two days of training for every developer across the four programmes. Using Table 1 headcounts and a working year of 220 days, what is the approximate cost in delivery capacity?

- A) About 0.9%
- B) About 2.2%
- C) About 4.4%
- D) About 0.4%

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Total headcount is 24 + 15 + 32 + 9 = 80. Training days: 80 × 2 = 160 developer-days. Total annual capacity: 80 × 220 = 17,600 developer-days. 160/17,600 = 0.91%. Just under one percent of annual capacity, or about £83,000 at the portfolio's £9.15m cost. That is a real number and a modest one, and being able to produce it converts "we can't spare two days" into a proposition that can be weighed. The general habit is to express any portfolio-wide intervention as a percentage of total capacity before anyone argues about it.

**Question 11 (harder) — A trend across a portfolio**

Portfolio-wide feature delivery per quarter over two years: 41, 44, 39, 47, 43, 46, 42, 48. A stakeholder asks whether delivery is improving. What is the strongest answer?

- A) Yes; the trend is upward from 41 to 48.
- B) The series oscillates between 39 and 48 with a very slight upward drift — the first four average 42.75 and the last four 44.75, a difference of about 5% that sits well within the quarter-to-quarter variation — so the honest answer is that delivery is broadly flat and the data cannot support a claim of improvement.
- C) No; delivery is flat.
- D) There is insufficient data.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** First-to-last comparison suggests a 17% rise; comparing the two halves gives about 5%; the range within each half is larger than the difference between them. Option B characterises the variation and states what it does and does not support, which is what the stakeholder needs. Option C is closer to right than A and still overclaims, since "flat" is itself a conclusion the noise barely supports. Option A picks the two endpoints that tell the preferred story, which is the single most common misuse of a time series in a management report. Option D declines to characterise data that can be characterised.

**Question 12 (harder) — Marginal resourcing**

A programme with 24 people is 8 weeks behind. Adding 6 people is proposed to recover the schedule. What is the strongest assessment?

- A) Six people is a 25% increase and should recover roughly 8 weeks over a quarter.
- B) Adding a quarter more people to a late programme will slow it in the near term — the new people need onboarding from the existing team, and coordination overhead rises — so the realistic effect is a further delay before any gain, and the recovery options that work on this timescale are scope reduction or sequencing changes rather than headcount.
- C) Adding people never helps a late programme.
- D) Add three people instead.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This is Brooks's observation and it holds for the reason option B gives: onboarding six people costs the existing twenty-four real time, and the coordination paths grow faster than the headcount. Over a longer horizon extra capacity does help, which is why option C overstates — the point is about the recovery timescale, not about hiring in general. The practical value of knowing this is in option B's last clause: when a programme is late and the deadline is fixed, the levers that work quickly are scope and sequence, and proposing them early is more useful than agreeing to a headcount increase everyone will later describe as having failed.

### Preparation tips

- **Check a business case's internal arithmetic before engaging with its conclusions.**
- **Strip out benefits that would arrive without the investment.**
- **Normalise counts by delivery volume before comparing programmes.**
- **Distinguish committed demand from requested demand in a resource plan.**
- **Express portfolio-wide interventions as a percentage of total capacity.**
- **Compare halves of a time series rather than endpoints.**
- **Treat people costs as the dominant variable in a programme comparison.**
- **Reject linear recovery-by-headcount arguments on short timescales.**

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Reading programme metrics as evidence about people rather than about what they build.**
- **Accepting a claimed saving without dividing it by the unit cost.**
- **Counting savings already committed to another initiative.**
- **Treating uncommitted requests as real demand.**
- **Reporting first-to-last change in a noisy series.**
- **Agreeing to headcount as a schedule recovery plan.**
- **Comparing cost per feature without asking what a feature is.**

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment tests whether you can determine exactly what written material establishes. On the management track at principal level the material is programme governance, business cases, funding conditions, employment and resourcing guidance, and agreements with client functions.

The commonest format gives a passage and asks you to mark statements True, False or Cannot Say. True means the passage establishes it, False means it contradicts it, and Cannot Say means neither. Your experience is the main hazard, because you will know how these arrangements usually work and the passage describes this one.

Three specifics matter at your level.

The first is that funding and governance documents attach conditions, and a condition attached to one thing does not travel to another. Reading a condition as general when it is specific, or as specific when it is general, changes what you are permitted to do.

The second is that business cases and strategies are written persuasively, which means separating what is asserted from what is established is a routine requirement rather than an academic exercise.

The third is that employment and resourcing guidance uses precise modal language, and the difference between "may", "should" and "must" determines whether you have discretion.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Reading governance and funding conditions** maps to managing resource allocation for programmes.
- **Reading business cases** maps to working with client functions to establish requirements.
- **Reading employment guidance** maps to leading and developing teams of experts.
- **Reading strategy** maps to setting strategy for solution development programmes.
- **Reading standards** maps to setting policy and standards.
- **Precision in written advice** maps to your role in advising senior stakeholders.

### Practice questions

*Passage A — for Questions 1 to 4*

"Funding for the programme is approved in two tranches. The first tranche is released on approval of the outline business case. The second tranche is released on approval of the full business case, and is conditional on the programme demonstrating that the benefits identified in the outline case remain achievable. Where the full business case identifies benefits lower than those in the outline case, the second tranche may be reduced proportionately at the discretion of the investment committee. Funding not drawn within the financial year in which it is released lapses and must be re-approved. The investment committee meets quarterly and may, in exceptional circumstances, approve funding out of cycle."

**Question 1 (easy)**

Statement: The second tranche of funding is released on approval of the full business case.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Stated directly, with a further condition attached about benefits remaining achievable. The release trigger is what the statement asserts and the passage establishes it.

**Question 2 (moderate)**

Statement: If the full business case identifies lower benefits, the second tranche will be reduced.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage says the tranche "may be reduced proportionately at the discretion of the investment committee". "May" plus "at the discretion of" makes reduction a possibility rather than a consequence, and the statement's "will" asserts an automatic outcome the passage contradicts. Two separate signals of discretion appear in one clause, which is a drafter telling you emphatically that this is a judgement.

**Question 3 (moderate)**

Statement: Funding released in one financial year and unspent must be re-approved before it can be used.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Stated directly: funding not drawn within the financial year in which it is released "lapses and must be re-approved". Note the trigger is the year of release rather than the year of approval, which is a distinction that matters in practice — a tranche approved in March and released in April has the following twelve months, not one.

**Question 4 (harder)**

Statement: The programme cannot receive funding between quarterly investment committee meetings.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage contradicts this: the committee "may, in exceptional circumstances, approve funding out of cycle". The statement's "cannot" is defeated by the existence of the out-of-cycle route, even though that route is discretionary and conditional. Note the asymmetry with Question 2: there, "may" defeated a statement asserting that something *will* happen; here, "may" defeats a statement asserting that something *cannot*. A discretionary power establishes possibility and not certainty, so it refutes claims in both directions.

*Passage B — for Questions 5 to 8*

"The service agreement between the digital function and the client function provides that the client function is responsible for defining business requirements and for prioritising the delivery backlog. The digital function is responsible for technical decisions, including choice of technology, architecture and delivery approach. Where a business requirement can only be met at disproportionate technical cost, the digital function must set out the cost and propose alternatives, and the client function decides whether to proceed. Changes to the agreement require the consent of both functions. The agreement was last revised at the start of the current financial year."

**Question 5 (easy)**

Statement: The client function prioritises the delivery backlog.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Stated directly as one of two responsibilities assigned to the client function. Straightforward, and worth reading against what follows, which qualifies how that priority interacts with technical cost.

**Question 6 (moderate)**

Statement: The digital function can decline to build a requirement that would cost a disproportionate amount.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage contradicts this. In the disproportionate-cost case the digital function "must set out the cost and propose alternatives", and "the client function decides whether to proceed". The obligation is to inform and propose; the decision is allocated elsewhere. Reading a duty to raise cost as a power to refuse is the natural error and an important one, since it describes exactly the boundary of your authority in this arrangement.

**Question 7 (moderate)**

Statement: The client function can require a particular technology to be used.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Technical decisions "including choice of technology" are allocated to the digital function. The passage does not merely omit a client power over technology; it assigns the decision elsewhere, which contradicts the statement. This is the mirror image of Question 6, and the two together define a clean split: what to build is theirs, how to build it is yours, and disproportionate cost is escalated rather than vetoed.

**Question 8 (harder)**

Statement: The agreement has been revised within the last twelve months.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The agreement "was last revised at the start of the current financial year", and the passage does not say how far into the financial year we are. If it is month two, the revision was two months ago; if it is month twelve, it was almost twelve months ago and could be marginally over depending on the exact dates. The statement is probably true and not established, because the passage supplies one end of the interval and not the other. Items that give a relative time reference without an anchor are a reliable Cannot Say pattern.

*Passage C — for Questions 9 to 12*

"Where a post has been advertised twice without a suitable appointment, the recruiting manager may propose a revised specification, an increased pay band subject to approval by the pay committee, or engagement of an interim resource for a period not exceeding twelve months. Interim engagements beyond twelve months require the approval of the director of resources. An interim engagement should not be used where the underlying need is permanent, unless a plan to establish permanent capability accompanies the request. Recruiting managers should consult the capability lead before advertising a post requiring specialist skills."

**Question 9 (moderate)**

Statement: A recruiting manager can increase the pay band for a post advertised twice without success.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage says the manager "may propose" an increased pay band "subject to approval by the pay committee". Proposing is not deciding, and the approval sits elsewhere. The statement asserts a power the passage allocates to another body, which makes it False rather than Cannot Say. Note that the same sentence gives the manager a genuine unilateral option — revising the specification — so the drafting deliberately distinguishes which of the three routes needs approval.

**Question 10 (moderate)**

Statement: An interim engagement can never be used where the underlying need is permanent.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage contradicts "never": an interim engagement "should not be used where the underlying need is permanent, unless a plan to establish permanent capability accompanies the request". The exception provides a route. Note also that the base obligation is "should not" rather than "must not", which independently softens it. Two reasons the statement fails, either of which is sufficient.

**Question 11 (moderate)**

Statement: A recruiting manager must consult the capability lead before advertising a specialist post.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage uses "should consult", not "must". In a passage that uses "require the approval of" and "subject to approval by" elsewhere, the choice of "should" for this obligation is deliberate and marks it as a strong expectation rather than a requirement. The statement's "must" overstates it. This is the kind of distinction that matters when you are the person deciding whether a step can be skipped under time pressure.

**Question 12 (harder)**

Statement: An interim engagement of eighteen months would require the approval of the director of resources and a plan to establish permanent capability.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The first half is established: engagements beyond twelve months require the director of resources' approval, and eighteen months exceeds twelve. The second half is not, because the capability plan is required only where the underlying need is permanent, and the statement says nothing about whether it is — an eighteen-month interim engagement could cover a temporary programme. A compound statement is only True if every part is established, so one unestablished conjunct makes the whole thing Cannot Say. Checking each limb of a compound statement separately is the discipline this item rewards.

### Preparation tips

- **Distinguish proposing from deciding.**
- **Read "may" as defeating both "will" and "cannot".**
- **Check whether a duty to inform is being read as a power to refuse.**
- **Note when a power is allocated elsewhere — that makes a statement False, not Cannot Say.**
- **Look for the anchor before accepting a relative time reference.**
- **Read "should" against "must" within the same passage.**
- **Check each limb of a compound statement separately.**
- **Notice which of several listed routes carries an approval condition.**

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Reading a discretionary reduction as an automatic one.**
- **Treating an obligation to set out costs as a right of veto.**
- **Assuming a client function can direct technology choices.**
- **Marking a probably-true time claim as established.**
- **Reading "should consult" as a requirement.**
- **Missing an "unless" clause that supplies a route.**
- **Marking a compound statement True when only one limb is established.**

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment presents realistic scenarios and asks you to identify effective and ineffective responses. On the management track at principal level the scenarios involve allocation decisions that disappoint people, relationships with client functions, disagreement with senior colleagues, and the persistent gap between what a programme has promised and what it can deliver.

The commonest formats ask for the most and least effective response, or for ratings. Scoring is against a key derived from experienced practitioners at comparable seniority.

Three patterns run through the strong answers.

The first is that you make decisions that disappoint capable people regularly, and how you do it determines whether they keep bringing you their problems. The reasoning being visible matters more than the decision going their way.

The second is that your relationship with client functions is the mechanism through which requirements get established honestly. A client function that has learned to inflate its case because you discount it has stopped giving you information.

The third is that you are far enough from delivery that your picture is second-hand, and the strong responses consistently include a step to check the picture rather than acting on the version that reached you.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Resource allocation** maps to managing resource allocation for solution development programmes.
- **Client relationships** maps to working with client functions to establish business requirements.
- **Strategy leadership** maps to setting programme strategy.
- **Capability development** maps to mobility, career paths and recruitment.
- **Process and standards** map to **Development process optimisation** and setting policy.
- **Technical judgement in a management context** maps to your expert-level technical skills.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Declining a request**

A programme manager makes a well-argued case for two more developers. You have decided against it. What is the most effective response?

- A) Tell them the answer is no.
- B) Tell them the answer is no, what it was weighed against, and what would change it — because a decision with visible reasoning can be planned around and argued with on the merits, while a bare no invites them to try again through a different route.
- C) Tell them there is no capacity available.
- D) Defer the decision to next quarter.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The decision is the same in all four; what differs is what the programme manager can do next. Option B lets them plan, and the "what would change it" clause is the part that turns a refusal into information — if the answer would be different at a higher backlog or after another programme finishes, they can watch for that. Option C is the standard formulation and is usually not quite true, since capacity exists and has been allocated elsewhere, and people can tell. Option D avoids the conversation and costs a quarter. Option A is honest and unusable.

**Question 2 (easy) — A client function's stated requirement**

A client function insists a system must produce a particular report in a specific format. It would be expensive to build. What is the most effective response?

- A) Build it; the client function defines requirements.
- B) Ask what the report is used for and by whom, because expensive format requirements usually come from an existing downstream process or an external obligation — and if it is an internal habit there may be a cheaper answer, while if it is a statutory return the format is genuinely fixed and the conversation is over.
- C) Propose a cheaper alternative format.
- D) Explain the cost and ask them to reconsider.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The question distinguishes the two cases that look identical from outside, and they have opposite answers. A statutory return or an external system's input format is immovable and arguing about it wastes everyone's time; an internal convention someone has been maintaining for years is negotiable and often nobody has been asked. Option B finds out in one question. Option C proposes before understanding, which occasionally proposes something illegal. Option D puts the cost on them without helping them evaluate it. Option A treats the stated form as the requirement.

**Question 3 (moderate) — A programme that will miss its commitment**

You conclude that a programme will miss a publicly committed date by around four months. The programme's leadership believes it can recover. What is the most effective response?

- A) Accept their assessment; they are closer to it.
- B) Establish what their recovery plan depends on and test those assumptions specifically — recovery plans usually rest on one or two optimistic premises — and if the assumptions do not hold, put the revised position to whoever owns the public commitment, since the cost of a late disclosure rises steeply the closer it gets to the date.
- C) Report your assessment upward immediately.
- D) Wait a month to see whether recovery materialises.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** You may be wrong and the way to find out is to examine the recovery plan's assumptions rather than to compare overall judgements. Option B does that and then acts, which respects the programme's position without deferring to it indefinitely. The timing argument is the substance: a four-month slip disclosed now is a planning problem, and the same slip disclosed in three months is a crisis with no options left. Option C goes over the programme's head before checking. Option D is the natural instinct and spends the month in which the disclosure would have been cheapest. Option A defers to people whose judgement is not independent of their interest.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Two strong internal candidates**

Two strong developers apply for the same lead role. One is appointed. What is the most effective response to the other?

- A) Tell them it was close and encourage them to apply again.
- B) Tell them specifically what the difference was and what would make the difference next time, and follow it with something concrete — an opportunity, a piece of scope, a route into the next vacancy — because "it was close, try again" without specifics is the message that precedes a resignation.
- C) Explain that the decision was difficult.
- D) Offer them a different role.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** An unsuccessful strong internal candidate is at their most likely to leave in the following two months, and generic encouragement is what convinces them there is no path. Option B gives them the two things that keep people: a specific answer, and a concrete next step. The specificity is uncomfortable to deliver and it is the entire value — vague feedback is indistinguishable from having no reason. Option A is the standard response and is heard as a polite refusal. Option C says nothing. Option D may be part of the answer and is not a substitute for the reason.

**Question 5 (moderate) — A client function that has learned to inflate**

You notice a client function consistently overstates urgency and benefits, apparently because they expect you to discount their case. What is the most effective response?

- A) Continue discounting; it works.
- B) Name the dynamic with them directly and change your side of it — commit to taking their stated position at face value and to explaining any decision that goes against them — because the inflation is a rational response to being discounted, and only you can break the loop.
- C) Ask them to be more realistic.
- D) Apply a consistent discount factor openly.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This is an equilibrium each side maintains by responding to the other, and it degrades the information you have to allocate on. Option B is the only response that changes the incentive, and it requires you to move first, which is the uncomfortable part. Option D is honest and formalises the arms race, giving them a known factor to inflate against. Option C asks them to unilaterally disarm while you keep discounting. Option A preserves a working arrangement in which neither party knows what the other means.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Reallocating from a struggling programme**

A programme is behind and consuming a large share of your capacity. Another programme with a clearer benefit case is waiting. What is the most effective response?

- A) Move capacity to the programme with the clearer benefit case.
- B) Establish first whether the struggling programme is behind because it is under-resourced or because it is poorly scoped — removing capacity from an under-resourced programme guarantees failure while removing it from an unscoped one changes nothing — and be clear that a decision to reduce it is a decision about what it will no longer deliver.
- C) Split the difference and move some capacity.
- D) Leave it; the struggling programme needs support.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The same observable state — behind and expensive — arises from causes with opposite implications for what you should do. Option B distinguishes them and adds the honest framing, which is that reducing a programme's resource is a scope decision whether or not anyone writes it down as one. That framing is what stops the programme being asked to deliver the same thing with less. Option C is the fairness reflex applied to an analytical question. Option A acts on the benefit case alone. Option D declines the allocation decision that is your job.

**Question 7 (harder) — Disagreement with a director**

A director wants a programme structured in a way you believe will fail, for reasons that are organisational rather than technical. What is the most effective response?

- A) Set out your technical objections.
- B) Engage with the organisational reasoning on its own terms — understand what the structure is meant to achieve politically or operationally, and look for a structure that achieves the same thing without the failure mode — because a technical objection to an organisationally-motivated decision does not connect, and will be heard as not understanding the constraint.
- C) Comply; the structure is their decision.
- D) Escalate above the director.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The mismatch is the point. If the structure is chosen to give a particular group visible ownership, or to fit a funding boundary, then a technical argument against it addresses nothing the director cares about and confirms that you do not see the problem they are solving. Option B takes the constraint seriously and looks for a design satisfying both, which is usually available once the actual requirement is understood. Option A is the natural move and the one that fails. Option D escalates a disagreement you have not yet had properly. Option C abandons a judgement you were hired for.

**Question 8 (harder) — Evidence your strategy is not working**

Two years into a technical strategy you set, the intended consolidation has not happened and teams are diverging further. What is the most effective response?

- A) Strengthen enforcement.
- B) Establish why the strategy is not being followed before doing anything else, since two years of divergence by capable teams usually means the compliant path is harder than the alternative — and if that is the case the remedy is to make the compliant path easier, through platform, tooling or funded migration, rather than to enforce harder.
- C) Revise the strategy to reflect current practice.
- D) Accept that consolidation is not achievable.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Two years is long enough that the explanation is structural, and the most common structure is that consolidating costs a team real time now for a benefit accruing to the organisation later. No amount of enforcement changes that arithmetic for an individual team; funding the migration does. Option B looks for the mechanism and points at the remedy. Option A escalates a losing approach and produces nominal compliance. Option C mistakes documenting failure for resolving it. Option D concedes before diagnosing.

**Question 9 (harder) — A capable person in the wrong role**

An excellent developer you promoted into a lead role is struggling and appears unhappy. What is the most effective response?

- A) Give them more support and time.
- B) Have the conversation directly, including that returning to a senior technical role is a legitimate option rather than a failure — because the most common reason people stay in a management role that does not suit them is that stepping back looks like defeat, and you are the person who can make it not look like that.
- C) Return them to their previous role.
- D) Arrange management training.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Both of the plausible situations — needing support, or being in the wrong role — are addressed by the conversation, and neither is addressed by acting without it. What makes option B strong is the second half: the option of stepping back exists in principle everywhere and is unusable in practice unless someone with authority frames it as a legitimate choice. Doing that visibly also makes future promotions less risky for everyone, because the exit exists. Option C decides for them. Option D assumes the cause is skill. Option A is right if the cause is a learning curve, and you do not yet know that it is.

**Question 10 (harder) — Pressure to commit to a scope**

A client function asks you to commit to a scope for a twelve-month programme. Your teams' estimates carry wide uncertainty. What is the most effective response?

- A) Commit to the scope; a programme needs a defined scope.
- B) Commit to the outcomes and the earliest deliverables precisely, and to the later scope in ranges with the uncertainty stated — and propose a review point at which the later scope firms up — because a precise twelve-month commitment made on uncertain estimates is a commitment you will break, and breaking it costs more than the discomfort of saying so now.
- C) Refuse to commit until the estimates improve.
- D) Commit with a contingency allowance.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B gives the client function the certainty that is genuinely available — near-term deliverables and overall outcomes — without manufacturing certainty that is not. The review point is what makes it acceptable, because it tells them when they will know. Option D is the conventional answer and fails because a contingency inside a committed scope is invisible to the client and gets consumed early. Option C declines to help with a decision they need to make now, and estimates for month eleven will not improve materially before month six. Option A buys twelve months of goodwill and spends it all at once.

**Question 11 (harder) — A supplier relationship**

A supplier delivering a significant component is underperforming. Your programme depends on them and switching would be costly. What is the most effective response?

- A) Escalate formally with the supplier's management.
- B) Establish specifically what is failing and whether it is capability, resourcing or a misaligned incentive in the contract — and address whichever it is, since a supplier resourcing your work with junior staff because the contract pays for time is a contract problem, not a performance problem, and escalation will not fix it.
- C) Begin planning to switch suppliers.
- D) Add your own people to cover the shortfall.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The three causes need different remedies, and the contractual one is both the most common and the one escalation cannot touch — a supplier responding rationally to how the contract pays will keep doing so through any number of escalation meetings. Option B diagnoses before acting. Option D is the instinct that gets programmes through the quarter and quietly transfers the supplier's obligation to your teams while they continue to be paid. Option A is a reasonable step once you know what you are escalating about. Option C is the last resort the scenario has already priced.

**Question 12 (harder) — A decision you made that has gone wrong**

An allocation decision you made six months ago contributed to a programme's difficulties. Nobody has attributed it to you. What is the most effective response?

- A) Note it privately and allocate differently in future.
- B) Say it, specifically, in whatever review is happening — because the organisation is currently learning the wrong lesson about why the programme struggled, and an allocation error that goes unnamed will be repeated by whoever allocates next.
- C) Ensure the programme gets the resource it needs now.
- D) Raise it privately with your line manager.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The argument for option B is organisational rather than moral: a review reaching the wrong conclusion about cause will produce the wrong remedy, and the person who knows the actual cause is you. Allocation decisions are made repeatedly by several people, so the error is highly likely to recur unless it is named. Option C is necessary and does nothing about the lesson. Option D contains the information where it cannot inform the review. Option A is the comfortable choice and guarantees the repetition, since a private resolution to do better does not transfer to anyone else.

### Preparation tips

- **Give reasoning and a change condition when declining a request.**
- **Ask what a format requirement is for before pricing or challenging it.**
- **Test a recovery plan's assumptions rather than comparing judgements.**
- **Give an unsuccessful internal candidate a specific reason and a concrete next step.**
- **Break a discounting equilibrium by moving first.**
- **Distinguish under-resourced from poorly scoped before reallocating.**
- **Engage with organisational reasoning on its own terms.**
- **Name your own contribution to a failure while a review is still forming.**

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Saying "there is no capacity" when capacity has been allocated elsewhere.**
- **Answering a technical objection to an organisationally-motivated decision.**
- **Splitting the difference on an allocation question.**
- **Telling a strong unsuccessful candidate it was close.**
- **Formalising a discount factor against an inflating client function.**
- **Enforcing a strategy harder when the compliant path is more expensive.**
- **Committing precisely to twelve-month scope with a contingency.**
- **Covering a supplier's shortfall with your own people.**

## Conclusion

You have worked through four assessments pitched at principal developer on the management track, and the threads are worth pulling out.

The cognitive section kept returning to the gap between what is asked for and what is needed. "We need a real-time dashboard" is a solution somebody reached before speaking to you. A resource request without its opportunity cost is always granted. A strategy that only says yes constrains nothing. And four teams knowingly building the same capability is a rational response to reuse being expensive, not a failure of collaboration — which means the fix is in discoverability, self-service and roadmap independence rather than in encouraging people to talk more.

The numeric section was about assessing other people's arithmetic. Check the internal consistency of a business case before engaging with its conclusions; strip out benefits that would have arrived anyway; distinguish committed demand from a queue of requests; compare halves of a time series rather than endpoints; and know that adding a quarter more people to a late programme slows it before it helps.

The verbal section was about the documents defining your authority and your discretion. Proposing is not deciding. A duty to set out costs is not a power to refuse. "May" defeats both "will" and "cannot". And a compound statement needs every limb established before it is True.

The situational judgement section was about the fact that most of your decisions disappoint someone capable. The strong answers made reasoning visible, distinguished causes that produce identical symptoms, engaged with organisational constraints rather than arguing past them, and — in the last question — named an error of your own so the organisation would learn the right lesson from it.

If one thing is worth carrying, it is that pattern in the client function question about inflated cases. A great deal of what makes this role work is the quality of information reaching you, and that quality is set by how you respond to it. People bring accurate estimates, early bad news and honest requests to someone who has shown that accuracy is not punished. Almost everything else in the role is easier when they do.

Good luck. The feedback loops here are long and the judgement is hard to check, which is exactly why deliberate practice like this is worth the time.
