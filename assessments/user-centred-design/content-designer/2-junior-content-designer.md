## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the thinking processes your role actually demands, using practical workplace scenarios rather than abstract shapes and sequences. Instead of asking you to rotate triangles in your head, it presents materials a junior content designer really handles — style guide rules, draft page content, publishing checklists, content inventories, and user feedback — and asks you to reason about them quickly and accurately.

The typical format is an online, timed test lasting 15 to 30 minutes. Questions cover pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective: your answers are compared against a norm group of people at a similar level. Many modern platforms are adaptive, which means the questions get slightly harder when you answer correctly and slightly easier when you do not — so do not panic if the test suddenly feels difficult, because that usually means you are doing well. Employers normally see a balance of speed and accuracy rather than a single score, so working carefully matters just as much as working quickly. Most platforms offer a few ungraded practice questions before the real test starts, so you can get comfortable with the interface without any pressure.

For a junior content designer, cognitive assessment is especially relevant because so much of your value comes from noticing things. You notice when a draft breaks the style guide. You notice when two pages contradict each other. You notice when a publishing checklist step has been missed. You notice patterns in user feedback that suggest a page is confusing. A well-designed cognitive assessment simulates precisely those noticing-and-reasoning tasks in miniature, which is why practising them genuinely improves your day-to-day craft as well as your test performance.

One more reassurance before the questions begin: at your level, employers are looking for aptitude and potential, not encyclopaedic knowledge. The questions below never require you to have memorised a real style guide. Every rule you need is stated in the question itself. Your job is simply to apply the stated rules carefully — which is exactly what you do at work when a senior colleague gives you direction and you carry it out independently.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The assessment dimensions map directly to the named skills in your role summary:

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **User-centred content design**: demonstrating basic but decent editorial standards and showing an awareness of style standards means recognising when content follows an established pattern — and, more importantly, when it quietly breaks one.
- **Error checking** also maps to **User-centred content design** and to your duty to support content team administration and publishing processes: before content is published, someone must spot the typo, the broken convention, the missed checklist step. At your level, that someone is often you.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **User focus (content design)**: seeing the purpose of user stories and focusing on user needs means reasoning correctly from evidence — "users said X, therefore the page should do Y" — without jumping to conclusions the evidence does not support.
- **Prioritisation** maps to **Agile working (content design)**: working in an Agile team means constantly deciding what to do first — responding to feedback, adapting your plan, and staying transparent about what you are working on and why.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Content concepts and prototyping** and to your duty to independently identify user issues and needs: when something about a page is not working, you break the problem down, form a simple hypothesis, and work out a sensible next step — often collaboratively, through pair working.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern recognition in a style guide**

Your team's style guide says page titles must: start with a verb, use sentence case (only the first word capitalised, except proper nouns), and contain no full stop at the end. While reviewing a content inventory, you see these four titles:

1. Apply for a fishing licence
2. Renew your passport
3. Check your State Pension
4. How to report a lost passport.

Which title breaks the most style guide rules?

- A) Title 1
- B) Title 2
- C) Title 3
- D) Title 4

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Work through each rule for each title. Titles 1 and 2 start with verbs ("Apply", "Renew"), use sentence case, and have no full stop — fully compliant. Title 3 starts with a verb ("Check") and the capitals in "State Pension" are allowed because it is a proper noun, so it is compliant too. Title 4 breaks two rules at once: it starts with "How" rather than a verb, and it ends with a full stop. This is exactly the kind of check you perform when supporting publishing processes: you compare each item against every rule, one at a time, rather than skimming. In a timed test, resist the urge to stop at the first broken rule you spot — the question asks which title breaks the most rules, so you need the full count for each option.

**Question 2 (easy) — Logical deduction from a publishing rule**

Your team's process states: "A page may only be published if it has been second-checked by another content designer, unless it is a minor fix approved by the lead content designer." A colleague tells you: "This page was published this morning, and it was not second-checked." Which one of the following conclusions must be true?

- A) The team broke the publishing process.
- B) The page was a minor fix approved by the lead content designer.
- C) Either the page was an approved minor fix, or the process was broken.
- D) The page contained an error.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The rule is a conditional with one permitted exception. From the facts given, you cannot tell whether the exception applies. Option A assumes the process was broken, but the page might have been an approved minor fix. Option B assumes the exception applies, but you have no evidence of that either. Option D introduces information that appears nowhere in the question — an unchecked page is not automatically a wrong page. The only conclusion guaranteed in every possible case is C: one of the two possibilities must hold. Assessments reward this discipline of deducing only what the information guarantees. It matters at work too: before you flag a colleague for skipping a check, you would quietly confirm whether the lead approved it — which protects team relationships as well as your logic.

**Question 3 (easy) — Error checking a draft against instructions**

A senior content designer asks you to make exactly three changes to a draft: (i) change "commence" to "start", (ii) change "in order to" to "to", and (iii) add a bullet list of the three required documents. You receive the updated draft back from a peer and check it. The draft now says "start" instead of "commence", says "to" instead of "in order to", includes a bullet list of the three documents, and the page title has also been changed from "Apply for a permit" to "Permit applications". How many of the changes made to the draft were requested?

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Count carefully. Four changes were made in total, but the question asks how many were requested. Changes (i), (ii) and (iii) were all requested and all completed — that is three. The title change was not on the list, so it is an extra, unrequested change (and one worth querying, because the new title no longer starts with a verb). The answer is 3. This mirrors a real and important junior-level habit: when you work independently after being given direction, you deliver exactly what was agreed, and you flag anything extra rather than letting it slip through. Unrequested changes are how errors and inconsistencies creep into published content.

**Question 4 (easy) — Prioritisation of a morning's tasks**

It is 9:30 am. Four things need your attention:

1. A factual error has been reported on a live page that tells users the wrong deadline for a benefit application — users may act on it today.
2. Your stand-up is at 10:00 am and you have not yet updated your ticket on the team board.
3. A colleague has asked you to pair on a draft this afternoon and wants you to read it first.
4. The weekly content community newsletter arrived in your inbox.

What should you do first?

- A) Update your ticket so the board is accurate for stand-up.
- B) Start fixing, or escalate, the factual error on the live page.
- C) Read the colleague's draft while it is fresh in your mind.
- D) Read the newsletter quickly before the day gets busy.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Prioritisation questions ask you to weigh impact and urgency together. Item 1 is both urgent and high impact: a wrong deadline on a live page can cause real users to miss a benefit application today. That harm outranks everything else on the list. As a junior, you may not fix it alone — the right first action might be to alert your senior content designer immediately — but either way, it comes first. Item 2 is genuinely useful and takes two minutes, so it fits before stand-up but after you have raised the live error. Item 3 is for the afternoon. Item 4 supports your engagement with the cross-government content community, which matters, but it is the least urgent item here. The pattern to remember: harm to users outranks internal tidiness, and internal tidiness outranks nice-to-haves.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Pattern recognition across user feedback**

Your team collects a one-question feedback survey on four pages of the same service. Over the last month, the percentage of users answering "No" to "Is this page useful?" was: Page A 8%, Page B 9%, Page C 31%, Page D 7%. All four pages have similar traffic and cover similar steps of the journey. As a junior content designer, what is the most reasonable first interpretation of Page C's figure?

- A) Page C is badly written and should be rewritten immediately.
- B) Page C is an outlier worth investigating — the cause could be the content, the placement in the journey, or something else entirely.
- C) Pages A, B and D must be unusually good, so Page C is probably fine.
- D) The survey is unreliable and should be ignored.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Three pages cluster at 7–9% and one sits at 31% — more than three times the cluster. Recognising the outlier is pattern recognition; deciding what it means requires restraint. An outlier is a prompt for inquiry, not a conclusion. Page C might be confusing, but the cause might also be that users arrive there expecting something the service does not offer, or that a recent policy change made the page out of date, or that a link is broken. Option A jumps to a solution before understanding the problem — the opposite of the user focus skill, which asks you to formulate hypotheses and gain insights from data before making decisions. Options C and D both dodge the signal instead of exploring it. At your level, the ideal real-world move is to note the pattern, form a hypothesis, and raise it with your senior content designer or user researcher — which is exactly what "independently identify user issues and needs" looks like in practice.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Logical deduction about content formats**

Your team's guidance states: (i) if content includes step-by-step instructions, it must use a numbered list; (ii) if content is a numbered list, each step must start with a verb; (iii) content about eligibility must never use step-by-step instructions. You are reviewing a page about eligibility for a travel document. Based only on the rules, which of the following must be true about this page?

- A) It must use a numbered list.
- B) It must not contain step-by-step instructions.
- C) Each of its list items must start with a verb.
- D) It must not contain any lists at all.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Follow the chain carefully. The page is about eligibility, so rule (iii) applies directly: it must never use step-by-step instructions. That is option B, and it follows immediately. Option A is wrong — numbered lists are required only when there are step-by-step instructions, which this page must not have. Option C only applies to numbered lists, which this page is not required (or likely) to have. Option D overreaches: the rules ban step-by-step instructions on eligibility pages, but a bulleted list of eligibility criteria breaks no stated rule. The skill here is applying rules exactly as written, without adding restrictions that are not there — the same discipline you use when applying style standards to a draft. Assessments often include one option that is "too strong" (like D); reject any conclusion that goes beyond what the rules say.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Error checking a content inventory**

You are helping with a content audit. The rules for the inventory spreadsheet are: every row must have a unique page ID; the "Last reviewed" date must not be in the future; the "Owner" column must name a team, not a person. You check this extract (today is 1 June):

| Page ID | Title | Last reviewed | Owner |
|---|---|---|---|
| 101 | Apply for a licence | 12 May | Licensing team |
| 102 | Renew a licence | 3 July | Licensing team |
| 103 | Pay a licence fee | 28 April | Sarah Okafor |
| 102 | Report a lost licence | 15 May | Licensing team |

How many rows contain at least one error?

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Check each row against each rule. Row 1 (ID 101): unique ID, past date, team owner — clean. Row 2 (ID 102): the date 3 July is after today (1 June), so the "Last reviewed" date is in the future — error. Row 3 (ID 103): "Sarah Okafor" is a person, not a team — error. Row 4 (ID 102): this ID duplicates row 2's ID, breaking the unique-ID rule — error. Note that the duplicate affects rows 2 and 4 as a pair, but row 2 already counts as an error row because of its future date, and row 4 counts because of the duplicate. Three rows contain at least one error, so the answer is 3. Supporting content team administration often means exactly this kind of systematic check: rule by rule, row by row, without letting an error you have already found distract you from finding the next one.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Prioritising feedback during iteration**

You published a draft for review and received four pieces of feedback: (i) a user researcher says test participants could not find the "continue" action; (ii) a policy colleague says a legal term has been simplified in a way that changes its meaning; (iii) a fellow content designer suggests a shorter synonym for one word; (iv) a stakeholder says they would prefer the page to "sound more impressive". Your senior content designer asks you to propose an order for addressing the feedback. Which order is most defensible?

- A) iv, iii, ii, i — handle the stakeholder first to keep them happy
- B) ii, i, iii, iv — fix accuracy, then findability, then polish, then discuss tone
- C) i, ii, iii, iv — user research always comes first, no matter what
- D) iii, i, ii, iv — start with the quickest fix to build momentum

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The strongest reasoning weighs consequence, not convenience or hierarchy. Feedback (ii) means the page may currently be legally inaccurate — content that misstates a legal meaning can cause users real harm and must be corrected before anything else. Feedback (i) is next: if users cannot find the "continue" action, they cannot complete the task at all, which defeats the page's purpose. Feedback (iii) is worthwhile polish. Feedback (iv) is not a fix but a conversation: "sounding impressive" usually conflicts with plain language, so the right response is to discuss the evidence for plain language with the stakeholder — calmly and last. Option C sounds virtuous but is a rigid rule rather than reasoning: user research findings are vital, yet an accuracy error that changes legal meaning is the rare thing that outranks them. Options A and D prioritise comfort and momentum over user impact. Explaining an ordering like this out loud is exactly your role duty to explain content decisions.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Applied problem solving with a confusing page**

A page explaining how to appeal a decision has a high drop-out rate at one step. You look at the step and see it contains: a 62-word sentence, three legal terms with no explanation, and a link labelled "click here". You can make one change today and test it. Following your team's approach of iterating based on evidence, which single change is the best first experiment?

- A) Rewrite the entire page from scratch so all problems are fixed at once.
- B) Break the 62-word sentence into shorter sentences, since sentence length affects the whole step's readability.
- C) Change the link label from "click here" to a descriptive label, since the drop-out happens where users must follow the link.
- D) Add a glossary page defining all legal terms used across the service.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The question gives you a specific clue: the drop-out happens at a step where users must follow a link, and that link is labelled "click here" — a label that tells users nothing about where it goes and is poor for accessibility, because screen reader users often navigate by links alone. The most targeted, testable hypothesis is that users do not follow the link because they cannot tell what it does. Option A abandons iteration entirely: changing everything at once means you will never know which change worked. Option B is a genuinely good improvement, but it is a general readability fix rather than one aimed at the observed failure point. Option D creates a new artefact to maintain and sends users away from their task. The Agile habit being tested is small, evidence-led steps: one focused change, then measure. If the link fix does not move the numbers, the long sentence is a strong next candidate.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Multi-rule deduction for content placement**

Your service has four page types with placement rules: (i) "Start" pages must link to exactly one "Apply" page; (ii) "Apply" pages must not link back to "Start" pages; (iii) "Guidance" pages may link to any page type, including other guidance pages; (iv) every page must be reachable from the "Start" page by following links. You review a map showing: Start → Apply; Apply → Confirmation; Guidance A → Apply; Guidance B → Guidance A. Which rule is broken?

- A) Rule (i)
- B) Rule (ii)
- C) Rule (iii)
- D) Rule (iv)

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Test each rule against the map. Rule (i): the Start page links to exactly one Apply page — satisfied. Rule (ii): the Apply page links only to Confirmation, never back to Start — satisfied. Rule (iii) permits guidance pages to link anywhere, and both guidance links (to Apply, and to Guidance A) are permitted — satisfied. Rule (iv) requires every page to be reachable from Start by following links. Trace it: from Start you can reach Apply, then Confirmation. But nothing links to Guidance A except Guidance B, and nothing links to Guidance B at all — so neither guidance page is reachable from Start. Rule (iv) is broken. The trap is that rule (iii) mentions guidance pages, tempting you to look for a guidance-related violation in the links they send out, when the real problem is the links they never receive. Orphaned content — pages users cannot reach — is a classic real-world finding in content audits, and spotting it requires exactly this kind of systematic tracing.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Reconciling contradictory evidence**

You are reviewing a page that explains two ways to submit a form: online or by post. You have three pieces of evidence: (i) analytics show 85% of users choose online; (ii) a user researcher reports that in testing, several participants with low digital confidence could not find the postal option, which is mentioned only in the final paragraph; (iii) a stakeholder wants the postal option removed from the page entirely to encourage digital uptake. Your senior asks you to recommend a next step with a reason. Which recommendation is strongest?

- A) Remove the postal option from the page, because 85% of users choose online anyway.
- B) Move the postal option higher on the page, because users who need it cannot find it, and the page must work for everyone including those with low digital confidence.
- C) Leave the page as it is, because the evidence is contradictory so no change is safe.
- D) Create a separate page for the postal option, because separating channels keeps each page simpler.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Work through what each piece of evidence actually supports. The 85% figure tells you most users choose online — it does not tell you the remaining 15% do not matter; in a government service, that minority may be exactly the users with the greatest need and the fewest alternatives. The research finding is direct evidence of a failure: people who need the postal route cannot find it. The stakeholder's wish is a preference, not evidence, and it conflicts with the government principle that services must work for everyone, including people who cannot or do not use digital channels. Option A treats a majority statistic as permission to exclude a minority. Option C mistakes tension between evidence and opinion for genuine contradiction — the two pieces of evidence are actually consistent. Option D might occasionally be right, but it adds a navigation step for the very users who already struggle to find things. Option B addresses the observed failure, is testable, and gives you an evidence-based reason you can explain respectfully to the stakeholder — which is precisely how stakeholder relationship management and user focus work together at your level.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Scheduling a content task under constraints**

You have four tasks for the week (Monday to Friday), each taking one day: draft a new page (D), get it factchecked by a policy expert (F), revise it after factcheck (R), and support the team's fortnightly publishing run (P). Constraints: (i) F cannot start until D is complete; (ii) R cannot start until F is complete; (iii) the policy expert is only available Tuesday and Thursday; (iv) the publishing run is fixed on Wednesday; (v) you can do at most one task per day. If you want to finish R as early in the week as possible, on which day should you do D?

- A) Monday
- B) Tuesday
- C) Wednesday
- D) Thursday

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Work forwards through the dependencies. F needs the policy expert, who is available only Tuesday or Thursday. For F to happen on Tuesday, D must be complete by Monday evening — so D on Monday enables F on Tuesday. Wednesday is blocked by the publishing run (P). R can then happen on Thursday. Check the alternative: if D happens any later than Monday, the Tuesday factcheck slot is missed, F slips to Thursday, and R cannot happen until Friday. So doing D on Monday finishes R on Thursday — one day earlier. The full schedule is: Monday D, Tuesday F, Wednesday P, Thursday R. This kind of dependency reasoning is quietly common in content work: publishing runs, factcheck availability and review steps all constrain each other, and Agile working means seeing those constraints early and planning transparently around them rather than discovering them mid-week.

### Preparation tips

- **Practise with real artefacts.** Open a style guide — your team's, or the GOV.UK style guide — and spend ten minutes checking a real page against it. This is exactly the pattern-checking muscle the assessment measures.
- **Slow down to speed up.** Junior-level candidates often lose marks by rushing. Read each rule in the question twice before applying it. Careful reading is faster than re-reading after a wrong turn.
- **Use paper.** For deduction questions, jot the rules as short symbols (e.g. "steps → numbered list"). Getting rules out of your head and onto paper frees your attention for reasoning.
- **Check every option.** Multiple-choice questions at this level often include one "nearly right" option. Confirm your answer by quickly testing why the other three fail.
- **Do a timed mini-drill.** Once you feel comfortable, set a timer for ten minutes and attempt four questions. The aim is calm familiarity with time pressure, not speed records.
- **Sleep and settle.** On the day, treat it like publishing day: prepare the night before, arrive (or log in) early, and do the platform's practice questions to settle your nerves.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Adding rules that are not there.** If the question states three style rules, apply those three — not the rules you remember from your own team. Assessments test your application of given information.
- **Stopping at the first error you find.** Error-checking questions usually ask "how many" — keep scanning systematically to the end of the table or list.
- **Confusing "could be true" with "must be true".** Deduction questions reward only conclusions guaranteed by the facts. If you can imagine any scenario where an option fails, it is not the answer to a "must be true" question.
- **Letting seniority sway prioritisation.** A director's undated request does not outrank a live error harming users. Rank by impact and urgency, not by who is asking.
- **Panicking when questions get harder.** On adaptive tests, harder questions mean you are performing well. Keep breathing, keep reasoning.
- **Skipping the worked check.** Before you submit, spend five seconds confirming your chosen option against the question's exact wording — "breaks the most rules" is not the same as "breaks a rule".

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A numeric reasoning assessment measures how comfortably you work with numbers in everyday job situations. Please do not worry: this is not an advanced maths exam. For a junior content designer, the numbers involved are the ones content teams genuinely see — page views, task completion rates, feedback survey percentages, research participant counts, readability scores, and publishing statistics. The mathematics never goes beyond percentages, averages, ratios, simple differences, and reading tables and charts accurately. If you can work out a discount in a shop or split a bill with friends, you already have the raw skills; the assessment simply checks you can apply them calmly to workplace data.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 20 to 30 minutes with 15 to 20 questions. Each question presents a small table, chart or short scenario with numbers, then asks a multiple-choice question. A basic on-screen calculator is often allowed — check the instructions on the day. Scoring compares your accuracy and speed against a norm group at a similar level. Employers use numeric assessments for content roles because modern content design is evidence-led: your role summary says junior content designers independently identify user issues and needs, and in practice many of those issues first appear as numbers — a completion rate that dips, a feedback score that drifts, a page view pattern that looks odd. A designer who can read those numbers correctly spots real problems earlier and avoids being misled by noisy data.

A gentle word about calculators and workings: even when a calculator is allowed, the biggest risk is not arithmetic but setup — dividing the wrong number by the wrong total, or misreading a row label. The practice questions below therefore show every step of the arithmetic, so you can learn the setups as reusable patterns.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Percentages and proportions** map to **User focus (content design)**: understanding user experience analysis means interpreting completion rates, drop-out percentages and feedback scores without misreading them.
- **Averages and comparisons** map to **User-centred content design**: using evidence to review content — comparing before-and-after readability figures, or one page's performance against similar pages — is how you show your work is improving things.
- **Reading tables and charts** maps to your duty to **support content team administration and publishing processes**: publishing schedules, content audits and performance dashboards all arrive as tables, and accurate reading prevents accurate-looking mistakes.
- **Simple rates and trends** map to **Agile working (content design)**: teams review metrics each sprint or each week; spotting whether a number is genuinely rising, falling or just wobbling helps the team adapt and respond to feedback.
- **Sense-checking figures** maps to **Strategic thinking (content design)**: contributing to improving processes and guidelines includes noticing when a reported number cannot be right — a completion rate over 100%, a total that does not add up — before anyone acts on it.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Reading a feedback table**

Your team's dashboard shows last week's "Was this page useful?" responses:

| Page | Yes | No |
|---|---|---|
| Apply for a permit | 320 | 80 |
| Renew a permit | 150 | 50 |
| Replace a permit | 90 | 10 |

What percentage of responses for "Apply for a permit" said Yes?

- A) 75%
- B) 80%
- C) 84%
- D) 90%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Step 1: find the total responses for that page: 320 + 80 = 400. Step 2: divide the Yes responses by the total: 320 ÷ 400 = 0.8. Step 3: convert to a percentage: 0.8 × 100 = 80%. The most common error is dividing Yes by No (320 ÷ 80 = 4, which is meaningless here) or using the wrong row. Always build the correct total first — percentage questions are almost always "part ÷ whole × 100", and the whole must include everyone who responded. Reading feedback percentages correctly is a weekly task when you support your team's reporting.

**Question 2 (easy) — Comparing two completion rates**

In April, 1,200 users started an online form and 900 completed it. In May, 1,500 users started the form and 1,200 completed it. By how many percentage points did the completion rate improve from April to May?

- A) 3 percentage points
- B) 5 percentage points
- C) 10 percentage points
- D) 25 percentage points

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Step 1: April's completion rate = 900 ÷ 1,200 = 0.75 = 75%. Step 2: May's completion rate = 1,200 ÷ 1,500 = 0.80 = 80%. Step 3: the difference = 80% − 75% = 5 percentage points. Notice the language: "percentage points" measures the simple difference between two percentages. Option D (25%) is a trap for people who notice completions rose from 900 to 1,200 — a 33% rise in raw completions, not the rate change asked for; option A tempts those who subtract starts from completions in some muddled way. In content work, the completion rate is usually the fairer measure than raw counts, because traffic changes month to month — exactly why your team tracks rates, not just totals.

**Question 3 (easy) — Averaging research session lengths**

You are supporting a user researcher by noting how long each usability session lasted: 28 minutes, 32 minutes, 35 minutes, 25 minutes, and 30 minutes. What was the average (mean) session length?

- A) 28 minutes
- B) 29 minutes
- C) 30 minutes
- D) 32 minutes

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Step 1: add the values: 28 + 32 + 35 + 25 + 30 = 150 minutes. Step 2: divide by the number of sessions: 150 ÷ 5 = 30 minutes. A quick sense-check: the answer should sit between the smallest (25) and largest (35) values, and 30 does. Sense-checking like this catches keying errors instantly. Averages appear constantly in content work — average time on page, average feedback score, average words per sentence — and the mean is the version you will meet most often. When you help administer research logistics, small calculations like this help the team plan how many sessions fit in a day.

**Question 4 (easy) — Publishing workload ratio**

This week your team must publish 24 content updates. The team agrees to split them between two junior content designers in the ratio 3:1, with you taking the larger share because your colleague is on training for part of the week. How many updates will you publish?

- A) 6
- B) 12
- C) 16
- D) 18

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Step 1: add the ratio parts: 3 + 1 = 4 parts. Step 2: find the size of one part: 24 ÷ 4 = 6 updates. Step 3: your share is 3 parts: 3 × 6 = 18 updates. Your colleague takes 1 part = 6 updates, and a check confirms 18 + 6 = 24. The common error is dividing 24 by 3 to get 8, or taking three-quarters of the wrong number. Ratios follow the same three steps every time: total the parts, size one part, multiply. Workload splits, page-per-week planning and even style decisions ("one example for every three rules") all use this pattern.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Percentage change in page views**

A page you rewrote had 4,500 views in the four weeks before your changes and 5,850 views in the four weeks after. What was the percentage increase in views?

- A) 13%
- B) 23%
- C) 30%
- D) 35%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Step 1: find the increase: 5,850 − 4,500 = 1,350. Step 2: divide the increase by the original value: 1,350 ÷ 4,500 = 0.3. Step 3: convert to a percentage: 0.3 × 100 = 30%. The classic error is dividing by the new value (1,350 ÷ 5,850 ≈ 23%, which is option B, deliberately placed as a trap). Percentage change is always measured against the starting value. One further thought your role will teach you: more views is not automatically success — for some pages, a rewrite that helps users succeed faster reduces repeat visits. In an assessment, answer the arithmetic asked; at work, add the interpretation.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Reading a two-way table**

A content audit categorises 200 pages by status and by age:

| | Under 1 year old | Over 1 year old | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Up to date | 70 | 50 | 120 |
| Needs review | 30 | 50 | 80 |
| Total | 100 | 100 | 200 |

What percentage of the pages that are over 1 year old need review?

- A) 25%
- B) 40%
- C) 50%
- D) 62.5%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The question asks about a specific group: pages over 1 year old. Step 1: find that group's total: 100 pages. Step 2: find how many of them need review: 50. Step 3: 50 ÷ 100 = 0.5 = 50%. The traps: option A (25%) comes from dividing 50 by the grand total of 200 — that answers a different question ("what percentage of all pages are old and need review?"); option B (40%) is the share of all pages needing review (80 ÷ 200); option D comes from dividing 50 by 80 (the share of needs-review pages that are old). Two-way tables reward one habit above all: identify the exact group the question names, and use that group's total as your denominator. Content audits produce tables exactly like this, and the difference between the right and wrong denominator can change what your team decides to fix first.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Readability scores before and after

Your team uses a readability tool that scores pages from 0 (very hard) to 100 (very easy), and aims for 60 or above. You edit four pages, with these scores:

| Page | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 48 | 63 |
| 2 | 55 | 61 |
| 3 | 42 | 58 |
| 4 | 51 | 66 |

What was the average improvement in score, and how many pages now meet the target?

- A) Average improvement 13; 3 pages meet the target
- B) Average improvement 13; 4 pages meet the target
- C) Average improvement 15; 3 pages meet the target
- D) Average improvement 12; 2 pages meet the target

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Step 1: find each page's improvement: Page 1: 63 − 48 = 15. Page 2: 61 − 55 = 6. Page 3: 58 − 42 = 16. Page 4: 66 − 51 = 15. Step 2: average them: (15 + 6 + 16 + 15) = 52; 52 ÷ 4 = 13. Step 3: count pages scoring 60 or above after editing: Pages 1 (63), 2 (61) and 4 (66) — that is 3 pages; Page 3 (58) falls just short. So the answer is an average improvement of 13, with 3 pages meeting the target. Two-part questions like this reward tidy working: compute one part fully, write it down, then compute the other. Note also the working insight for your craft: Page 3 improved the most (16 points) yet still misses the target — improvement and attainment are different measures, and reporting both gives your team the honest picture.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Estimating a publishing backlog rate**

Your team's publishing queue currently holds 36 items. Each week the team publishes 12 items, but 8 new items join the queue. If these rates stay steady, how many weeks will it take to clear the queue completely?

- A) 3 weeks
- B) 6 weeks
- C) 9 weeks
- D) The queue will never clear

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Step 1: find the net reduction per week: 12 published − 8 added = 4 items cleared per week. Step 2: divide the backlog by the net rate: 36 ÷ 4 = 9 weeks. Option A comes from dividing 36 by 12 and ignoring the new arrivals — the most common real-world planning mistake, and exactly why backlogs get underestimated. Option D would be right only if arrivals equalled or exceeded publications. This net-rate pattern appears whenever work flows in while you work it down: publishing queues, feedback inboxes, review backlogs. When your team plans sprint capacity in Agile ceremonies, this simple calculation helps you give honest estimates instead of hopeful ones.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Survey sample sense-check**

A stakeholder tells you: "Only 5 users out of the 10 we tested completed the task — but our page gets 40,000 visits a month, so 20,000 users a month must be failing." Which statement best describes the numerical reasoning here?

- A) The arithmetic and the conclusion are both sound.
- B) The arithmetic (50% of 40,000 = 20,000) is correct, but projecting from 10 test participants to 40,000 visitors states false precision — the small sample suggests a problem worth investigating, not an exact failure count.
- C) The stakeholder should have multiplied by 10, not divided by 2.
- D) Ten participants is too few to conclude anything at all, so the test result should be ignored.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Check the arithmetic first: 5 out of 10 is 50%, and 50% of 40,000 is indeed 20,000 — the multiplication is fine. The reasoning is the problem. A sample of 10 gives a rough signal, not a precise rate: with so few participants, the true failure rate could plausibly be much lower or higher than 50%. So the projection "20,000 users must be failing" claims a precision the data cannot support. But option D swings too far the other way: usability testing with small numbers is genuinely valuable for identifying that a problem exists and what it looks like — five people failing the same step is a strong signal that something is wrong. The balanced reading is B: real signal, false precision. This distinction sits at the heart of your user focus skill — using quantitative and qualitative data appropriately — and being able to explain it gently to a stakeholder is stakeholder relationship management in action.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Weighted average feedback score**

Three related pages collect star ratings (1 to 5). Last month:

| Page | Average rating | Number of ratings |
|---|---|---|
| Guidance overview | 4.0 | 300 |
| How to apply | 3.0 | 100 |
| After you apply | 4.5 | 100 |

What was the overall average rating across all three pages combined?

- A) 3.83
- B) 3.90
- C) 3.95
- D) 4.00

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** You cannot simply average the three averages ((4.0 + 3.0 + 4.5) ÷ 3 ≈ 3.83 — option A, the trap), because the pages received different numbers of ratings. A combined average must be weighted by group size. Step 1: total the rating points for each page: Guidance overview: 4.0 × 300 = 1,200. How to apply: 3.0 × 100 = 300. After you apply: 4.5 × 100 = 450. Step 2: add them: 1,200 + 300 + 450 = 1,950 total rating points. Step 3: total the number of ratings: 300 + 100 + 100 = 500. Step 4: divide: 1,950 ÷ 500 = 3.9. So the overall average is 3.90. Notice how close options B and C sit — when a test offers near-neighbour options, that is a hint the writer expects small arithmetic slips, so recompute once before committing. The workplace relevance is direct: when your team reports "average satisfaction" across a service, weighting by the number of responses is the difference between an accurate figure and a misleading one, because a busy page's score should count for more than a quiet page's.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Interpreting a trend with a seasonal dip**

Monthly views of a page about school admissions were: March 10,000; April 9,500; May 9,000; June 8,500; July 4,000; August 4,200. A colleague says: "Views have collapsed — the July redesign must have failed." Which is the strongest numerical objection to this claim?

- A) Views fell every month from March, so the decline started long before July's redesign; and a school admissions page would be expected to dip sharply during the summer holidays regardless.
- B) The redesign clearly worked, because views rose from 4,000 in July to 4,200 in August.
- C) You cannot compare months with different numbers of days.
- D) 4,000 is 40% of 10,000, which is not a collapse.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Look at the shape of the data before accepting any story about it. From March to June, views fell steadily by 500 a month — a gentle decline that began well before the redesign. The sharp drop is between June (8,500) and July (4,000), which coincides with the redesign but also with the school summer holidays — and for a school admissions page, demand naturally falls when the admissions cycle quietens. Two candidate explanations coincide, so the redesign cannot be blamed on this evidence alone; you would compare against July of the previous year to separate seasonality from the redesign's effect. Option B over-reads a tiny 200-view rise (5%) as vindication — the same leap-to-conclusions error in the opposite direction. Options C and D are technically-flavoured but trivial: day-count differences cannot explain a 53% drop, and D's arithmetic observation does not engage with the cause. The habit being tested — asking "what else could explain this number?" — is central to using evidence honestly, and it will serve you in every content review you ever do.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Checking a stakeholder's summary against source data**

A stakeholder's slide says: "Our content improvements increased task completion by 20%, and user complaints halved." The source dashboard shows: task completion rose from 60% to 72%; complaints fell from 48 per month to 30 per month. Which parts of the slide are accurate?

- A) Both claims are accurate.
- B) The completion claim is accurate; the complaints claim is not.
- C) The complaints claim is accurate; the completion claim is not.
- D) Neither claim is accurate.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Take each claim in turn. Completion: it rose from 60% to 72%. As a relative change, that is an increase of 12 percentage points on a base of 60: 12 ÷ 60 = 0.20 = 20%. So "increased by 20%" is defensible as a relative increase (though a careful writer would say "by 12 percentage points, a 20% relative increase" to avoid ambiguity). Complaints: halving 48 would give 24, but complaints fell to 30. The actual fall is 18 complaints: 18 ÷ 48 = 0.375 = 37.5% — a substantial improvement, but not "halved". So the completion claim stands and the complaints claim overstates. The assessment skill is verifying each claim independently against source data, showing your arithmetic for both. The workplace skill is the same, with added diplomacy: when you spot an overstated figure in a stakeholder's slide, you flag it early and kindly — an inflated claim that reaches a senior audience embarrasses everyone and undermines trust in the genuinely good result sitting right next to it.

### Preparation tips

- **Rebuild the basics gently.** Spend a little time each day on percentages, averages and ratios until the three core setups feel automatic: part ÷ whole × 100; total ÷ count; total the ratio parts first.
- **Always write the setup before calculating.** Even with a calculator, jot "increase ÷ original" or "row total, not grand total" first. Most numeric errors are setup errors, not arithmetic errors.
- **Practise on your team's real dashboards.** Ask yourself one question a day about a real table: "What percentage is that? Is it rising or is that noise?" Real data builds real fluency.
- **Sense-check every answer.** Averages must sit between the smallest and largest values; percentages of a group cannot exceed 100%; a queue that shrinks by 4 a week cannot clear 36 items in 3 weeks. A five-second sanity check catches most slips.
- **Learn the vocabulary.** "Percentage points" versus "per cent", "average" versus "total", "of starts" versus "of all users" — assessment questions choose these words deliberately, and so should you.
- **Time yourself late, not early.** Master the setups untimed first; add time pressure only in your final practice sessions so speed builds on accuracy.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Using the wrong denominator.** The single most common numeric error: dividing by the grand total when the question names a subgroup, or by the new value when percentage change needs the original. Circle the group the question names.
- **Averaging averages.** When groups differ in size, combine them with a weighted average. Unweighted averages of averages look plausible and are wrong.
- **Ignoring flows in and out.** Backlog and queue questions almost always involve a net rate. Subtract arrivals from clearances before dividing.
- **Reading charts and tables too fast.** Check the units, the row and column labels, and whether figures are counts or percentages before touching the numbers.
- **Trusting small samples too precisely.** Five out of ten users failing signals a real problem; it does not mean exactly 50% of all users fail. Report signals as signals.
- **Abandoning a question after one slip.** If your answer matches no option, do not guess wildly — re-check your setup first. Test-writers place traps at the answers produced by common errors, so a near-miss option is a clue, not a coincidence.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately you read, understand and draw conclusions from written information. For most roles this is one useful skill among several; for a content designer it is close to the heart of the job itself. Every day you read policy notes, style guidance, research summaries, stakeholder emails and existing pages — and you must extract exactly what they say, no more and no less, before you can turn them into content users understand.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 15 to 25 minutes. You are shown a short passage — often 100 to 250 words — followed by one or more statements or questions. The most common question type asks you to judge each statement as **True** (the passage supports it), **False** (the passage contradicts it) or **Cannot say** (the passage gives too little information to decide). Other question types test comprehension ("what is the main point?"), inference ("what follows from this?"), and clear-writing judgement ("which rewrite best conveys the meaning?"). Scoring is objective and usually norm-referenced, and — one very important rule — you must judge every statement using only the passage, not your outside knowledge. Even if you happen to know a fact about government services, if the passage does not state or imply it, the answer is "Cannot say".

Employers use verbal reasoning for content roles because the risks of misreading are real. A junior content designer who misreads a policy note might publish guidance that misleads thousands of users; one who misreads a stakeholder email might deliver the wrong change. The assessment simulates those moments safely: short passages, careful questions, and the discipline of separating what is written from what you assume.

Take heart: as someone who works with words all day, you are practising in your home territory. The skills below will sharpen quickly.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Precise comprehension** maps to **User-centred content design**: demonstrating decent editorial standards begins with reading source material accurately — you cannot simplify a policy correctly if you have misunderstood it.
- **True/false/cannot-say discipline** maps to **User focus (content design)**: focusing on user needs and the purpose of user stories means distinguishing what evidence actually says from what you hope or assume it says.
- **Inference** maps to **Strategic thinking (content design)**: understanding how activities meet strategic goals requires reading guidance and strategy documents and drawing only the conclusions they genuinely support.
- **Clear-writing judgement** maps to **User-centred content design** and your duty to **explain content decisions**: choosing the clearest rewrite, and explaining why it is clearest, is your daily craft made into a question format.
- **Reading stakeholder communications accurately** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management (content design)**: understanding who your stakeholders are and what evidence matters to them starts with reading their emails and comments for what they actually request.

### Practice questions

For questions 1 to 4, read this passage — an extract from an internal style guidance note:

> "Our style guide requires plain language on all public-facing pages. Aim for short sentences, and prefer common words over formal ones — for example, use 'help' rather than 'assistance'. Technical terms may be used where the audience is specialist, but each technical term must be explained at first use on a page. Legal terms that have a precise meaning must not be replaced with everyday synonyms; instead, explain them in a following sentence. This guidance applies to new content immediately, and to existing content as pages come up for review."

**Question 1 (easy) — True/false/cannot say**

Statement: "The style guide allows technical terms on pages written for a specialist audience."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True only for legal content

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Find the sentence that addresses the claim: "Technical terms may be used where the audience is specialist, but each technical term must be explained at first use." The statement says the guide "allows" technical terms for specialist audiences — and the passage says exactly that, with a condition attached (explain at first use). A condition does not turn permission into prohibition, so the statement is True. Option D invents a distinction the passage does not make. The technique: locate the governing sentence, compare the statement's claim to it word by word, and do not let an attached condition confuse you about whether the core permission exists.

**Question 2 (easy) — True/false/cannot say**

Statement: "Legal terms should be swapped for simpler everyday words."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True for new content only

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage states the opposite: "Legal terms that have a precise meaning must not be replaced with everyday synonyms; instead, explain them in a following sentence." The statement directly contradicts this, so it is False. Notice how plausible the statement sounds — swapping hard words for easy ones is the general spirit of plain language, and a hasty reader pattern-matches on that spirit and answers True. Verbal reasoning tests deliberately include statements that fit the theme but contradict the letter. This mirrors a real content design subtlety you will meet often: plain language means explaining precise terms, not deleting their precision — "you may be evicted" and "you may be asked to leave" are not legally the same thing.

**Question 3 (moderate) — True/false/cannot say**

Statement: "All existing pages must be rewritten in plain language within twelve months."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, because the guidance applies immediately

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage says the guidance applies "to existing content as pages come up for review" — but it never states how often reviews happen or sets any deadline. Twelve months appears nowhere. Could all pages come up for review within twelve months? Possibly. Does the passage guarantee it? No. Does it contradict it? Also no. When a passage neither supports nor contradicts a statement, the answer is Cannot say. Option D misapplies "immediately", which the passage attaches only to new content. This is the judgement that most distinguishes strong candidates: resisting the pull to answer True or False when the honest answer is that the passage is silent. The same discipline protects you at work when a colleague asks "does the guidance say we have to?" — sometimes the accurate answer is "it doesn't say", and that answer is a professional one.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Main point comprehension**

Which option best summarises the passage's overall position on word choice?

- A) Always use the simplest word available, without exception.
- B) Prefer common words, but preserve technical and legal terms where precision matters — supported by explanation.
- C) Avoid technical terms entirely on public-facing pages.
- D) Formal words are acceptable if sentences are kept short.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Summary questions ask you to weigh the whole passage, not one sentence. The passage prefers common words ("use 'help' rather than 'assistance'"), permits technical terms for specialist audiences with explanation, and protects legal terms from inaccurate substitution. Option B captures all three moves: a preference for plainness plus principled exceptions. Option A overstates — "without exception" ignores the legal-terms rule. Option C contradicts the technical-terms permission. Option D invents a trade-off (formality for brevity) the passage never offers. In your work, producing a faithful one-sentence summary of a guidance note is a genuine task — for example, when you explain a content decision to a stakeholder — and the skill is the same: include the qualifications, because the qualifications are where the meaning lives.

For questions 5 to 7, read this passage — an email from a policy stakeholder:

> "Thanks for the draft. Legal have confirmed the eligibility rules changed on 6 April: applicants must now have lived in the UK for 3 years, not 5. Please update the eligibility page before Friday, as the old figure is still live. Separately, the team would like the application page to mention our new helpline, but the helpline number is not yet confirmed — do not publish it until I send confirmation. I'll be on leave next week, so direct any questions to Priya."

**Question 5 (easy) — Identifying the required action**

Which action is the stakeholder asking you to complete by Friday?

- A) Publish the new helpline number.
- B) Update the residence requirement on the eligibility page from 5 years to 3 years.
- C) Redirect all questions to Priya.
- D) Update both the eligibility page and the application page.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The email contains two requests with different conditions, and the Friday deadline attaches only to the first: updating the eligibility page because "the old figure is still live". The helpline mention is explicitly conditional — "do not publish it until I send confirmation" — so option A is the one action you must not take yet, and option D fails because it bundles the blocked task with the required one. Option C describes next week's arrangement, not a Friday deliverable. Reading emails this precisely is a daily junior-level duty: stakeholder emails routinely mix an urgent request, a conditional request, and housekeeping, and acting on the wrong one — especially publishing something explicitly embargoed — damages trust. When you extract actions from an email, note each action with its own deadline and its own conditions.

**Question 6 (moderate) — True/false/cannot say**

Statement: "The eligibility page currently tells users the wrong residence requirement."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True only after Friday

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Chain two facts from the email: the rules changed on 6 April so the requirement is now 3 years, and "the old figure is still live" — the old figure being 5 years. If the live page shows 5 years and the true requirement is 3 years, the live page is telling users the wrong requirement. The statement is True by direct inference from what the passage states. This is a fair inference, not an assumption: both links in the chain are in the text. Compare that with question 3, where the missing link (a review timetable) was not in the text — learning to feel that difference is the core of true/false/cannot-say technique. At work, this exact inference is what makes you treat the fix as urgent: users reading the page today may wrongly believe they are ineligible.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Inference about communication**

You update the eligibility page on Thursday, and on the following Tuesday a question arises about the helpline wording. Based only on the email, what should you do?

- A) Publish the helpline number, since a week has passed.
- B) Email the stakeholder and wait for their return.
- C) Direct the question to Priya.
- D) Ask Legal, since they confirmed the eligibility rules.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The email anticipates exactly this situation: "I'll be on leave next week, so direct any questions to Priya." Tuesday falls in that week, so the stated route is Priya. Option A is dangerous: the embargo lifts when confirmation is sent, not when time passes — no confirmation, no publication. Option B ignores the instruction and delays the work. Option D confuses roles: Legal confirmed the eligibility change, but the helpline is the policy team's matter, and the passage gives you no basis for routing it to Legal. The question tests whether you register contingency instructions on first reading — the sentences that seem like small talk ("I'll be on leave…") but actually assign a path for problems. Good junior content designers file those details the moment they read them.

For questions 8 to 10, read this passage — an extract from a user research summary:

> "We tested the draft 'Report a repair' page with eight social housing tenants. Six completed the task without help. Two participants, both using screen readers, could not identify where to enter the property reference number, because the field label was read out as 'reference' with no further context. Participants generally described the page as 'clear' and 'quick', though three said they were unsure whether they would receive a confirmation message after submitting. We recommend clarifying the field label and adding a sentence about what happens after submission. We did not test the page on mobile devices."

**Question 8 (easy) — True/false/cannot say**

Statement: "All participants completed the task without help."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True for screen reader users

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage says six of eight completed without help, and two "could not identify where to enter the property reference number". Six is not all; the statement is contradicted, so it is False. This looks easy on the page, but under time pressure candidates skim "completed the task without help", match it to the statement, and click True without registering the "Six" that precedes it. The protection is simple: for any statement containing "all", "none", "always" or "never", hunt for the exception before answering — one counter-example makes the statement False. The same reflex serves you in research readouts at work: the two participants who struggled are usually the most important finding, not a footnote.

**Question 9 (moderate) — True/false/cannot say**

Statement: "The page works well on mobile devices."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) False, because screen reader users struggled

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage's final sentence states plainly: "We did not test the page on mobile devices." No mobile evidence exists in either direction, so the passage can neither support nor contradict the statement — Cannot say. Option D commits a subtle error worth studying: it borrows evidence about screen readers (an assistive technology finding) and applies it to mobile devices (a different dimension entirely); the passage links the two nowhere. Beware, too, the opposite temptation: "participants described the page as clear, so it probably works fine on mobile" — that is your optimism talking, not the text. The report writer included the "not tested" sentence precisely so readers would not over-extend the findings; honouring such boundaries is part of using research evidence with integrity, a core element of your user focus skill.

**Question 10 (moderate) — Drawing the right conclusion from findings**

Based only on the passage, which conclusion is best supported?

- A) The page should be rebuilt from scratch because it fails accessibility standards.
- B) The label change should be prioritised because it blocked task completion for assistive technology users, and a post-submission sentence should be added to address the confirmation uncertainty.
- C) No changes are needed because most participants succeeded and described the page as clear.
- D) The confirmation message issue is more serious than the label issue because three participants raised it versus two.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Match each conclusion to the evidence's weight. The label problem completely blocked two screen reader users from completing the task — a failure of the page's core purpose for those users, and government content must work with common assistive technologies. The confirmation uncertainty caused doubt but not failure — all three who raised it still completed the task. So the label fix is the priority, and the added sentence handles the secondary issue: exactly option B, which also mirrors the researchers' own recommendation. Option A overshoots the evidence — two specific findings do not condemn a page most users praised. Option C commits the majority fallacy: "most succeeded" does not excuse a barrier that excluded users with access needs. Option D counts heads while ignoring severity: three mildly unsure participants do not outweigh two fully blocked ones. Severity-weighted reading of research findings is exactly how you translate user stories and insights into content decisions.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Clear-writing judgement**

A draft sentence reads: "In the event that the applicant is in receipt of Universal Credit, the applicant may be eligible for a reduction in respect of the fee payable." Which rewrite best preserves the meaning while making it plain?

- A) "If you get Universal Credit, you may pay a smaller fee."
- B) "If you get Universal Credit, you will not pay the fee."
- C) "Universal Credit recipients always receive fee reductions."
- D) "You might pay less, depending on your benefits."

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Plain-language rewriting must simplify the words without changing the meaning, and the meaning here has two load-bearing parts: the condition (getting Universal Credit) and the uncertainty ("may be eligible" — a possibility, not a guarantee). Option A keeps both: "If you get Universal Credit" preserves the condition; "may pay a smaller fee" preserves the possibility; and it converts the stiff constructions ("in the event that", "in receipt of", "in respect of") into everyday English, while addressing the reader as "you" — better for user-centred content than the distancing "the applicant". Option B upgrades a possibility into a promise of no fee at all — factually wrong twice over. Option C converts "may" into "always" and loses the direct address. Option D keeps the uncertainty but blurs the condition: "depending on your benefits" no longer tells the reader which benefit matters, forcing them to guess. This is the discipline behind your editorial standards: plain language is a precision exercise, and the test of a rewrite is that a lawyer and a user would both accept it.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Reconciling two documents**

Guidance note A (dated January) says: "Users must verify their identity before viewing their application status." Guidance note B (dated March) says: "Following the service update, users can view their application status without signing in; identity verification is now required only when users wish to change their details." A colleague drafting the status page asks you what the current rule is. Based only on the two notes, what should you tell them?

- A) Users must verify their identity to view their status, as note A states.
- B) The notes conflict, so no rule can be stated until someone decides.
- C) Note B is more recent and describes the position "following the service update": viewing status requires no sign-in, and verification is needed only to change details — though it would be sensible to confirm nothing has changed since March.
- D) Users never need to verify their identity.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** When two authoritative documents disagree, check whether one supersedes the other. Note B is dated later and explicitly frames itself as describing the position "following the service update" — it does not merely differ from note A; it accounts for why note A is out of date. So the current rule, on the evidence given, is note B's: no sign-in to view, verification to change. Option A clings to the superseded note. Option B overstates the conflict: dated documents with an explicit update reference are not an unresolvable contradiction. Option D over-extends note B, which still requires verification for changes — "only when" is a limitation, not an abolition. The answer's final clause matters too: recommending a quick confirmation reflects healthy verbal-reasoning humility (your evidence ends in March) and real workplace practice — you would confirm with the service team before publishing. Handling versioned guidance is everyday work in government content design, where policies genuinely do change between January and March.

### Preparation tips

- **Use only the passage.** The golden rule of verbal reasoning: your outside knowledge, however good, is switched off. Practise answering as if the passage is the only document in the world.
- **Read the statement before re-reading the passage.** Skim the passage once for structure, read the statement carefully, then return to the passage to hunt for the governing sentence. This is faster than memorising the passage.
- **Learn the trigger words.** "All", "never", "must", "only", "immediately" — these small words decide most answers. Underline them (mentally or on paper) in both passage and statement.
- **Practise "Cannot say" until it feels comfortable.** Most wrong answers come from forcing True or False onto a silent passage. If you cannot point to the supporting or contradicting sentence, the answer is Cannot say.
- **Read your team's real documents actively.** Once a day, take one paragraph from a policy note or research summary and ask: "What does this actually commit to? What does it stay silent on?" That is free verbal reasoning training.
- **Keep calm about dense passages.** Government prose can be heavy. You untangle heavy prose for a living — treat each passage as a page you are about to make plain, and the structure will reveal itself.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Importing outside knowledge.** Knowing the real GOV.UK rule is a hazard here: answer from the passage, even when the passage differs from real life.
- **Pattern-matching on theme instead of letter.** A statement can sound like the passage's spirit and still contradict its words (remember the legal-terms question). Match claims to sentences, not vibes to vibes.
- **Missing conditions and exceptions.** "May be used, provided that…" is permission with a condition. Statements that drop the condition, or drop the permission, are testing whether you noticed both halves.
- **Treating inference as guessing.** A valid inference chains only facts stated in the passage. If your chain needs one outside link — a review timetable, a mobile test — the answer is Cannot say.
- **Head-counting instead of severity-weighting.** Three mildly confused users do not outweigh two blocked users. Read findings for consequence, not just frequency.
- **Rushing the final options.** Clear-writing questions often place the best rewrite beside a slightly-too-strong one ("may" versus "will"). Compare the shortlisted options word by word before choosing.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment presents realistic workplace scenarios and asks you to judge how effective different responses would be. There are no trick sums and no passages to parse — instead, the test measures your professional instincts: how you balance user needs against stakeholder pressure, when you ask for help, how you handle mistakes, and how you behave in a team.

The typical format is an online test of 20 to 40 minutes, usually untimed or generously timed, with 10 to 20 scenarios. Question styles vary: some ask you to pick the single most effective response, some the least effective, some ask you to rank all the responses, and some ask you to rate each response independently on an effectiveness scale. There are rarely perfect answers — the test-writers design options along a spectrum from clearly strong to clearly weak, with plausible-but-flawed options in between. Scoring compares your judgements with those of experienced professionals and subject-matter experts.

For a junior content designer, situational judgement matters enormously, and here is the encouraging truth: it is the assessment where your attitude counts more than your experience. At your level, employers are not looking for someone who already knows everything. They are looking for someone who puts users first, communicates honestly, asks for direction at the right moments, owns their mistakes quickly, collaborates generously, and treats everyone — colleagues, stakeholders, and users of every background and ability — with respect. Those behaviours map directly onto your role duties: explaining content decisions, working collaboratively, working independently after being given direction, and engaging with the content community.

One preparation note before you begin: answer as your best professional self, honestly. Do not try to game the test by guessing what sounds impressive — the strongest answers are usually the ones that feel calm, honest and user-focused. If you find yourself torn between two options, ask: "Which response best serves users, keeps my team informed, and matches the level of responsibility I actually hold?" That question resolves most dilemmas at your level.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **User-first judgement** maps to **User focus (content design)**: scenarios test whether you keep the user's need in view when a stakeholder, a deadline, or your own convenience pulls the other way.
- **Collaboration and openness** map to **Agile working (content design)** and **Content concepts and prototyping**: responding well to feedback, working in the open, pair working, and keeping the team's board honest are all situational behaviours.
- **Knowing when to escalate** maps to your level's defining balance: you **work independently after being given direction by more senior content designers** — so scenarios probe whether you know which decisions are yours and which need a senior's input.
- **Handling stakeholders respectfully** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management (content design)**: understanding what evidence matters to stakeholders, and disagreeing with them constructively, using evidence rather than opinion.
- **Ownership and honesty** map to **User-centred content design** and your publishing duties: owning errors quickly, protecting quality under time pressure, and following process even when nobody is watching.
- **Community contribution** maps to your duty to **engage with and contribute to the cross-government content community**: several scenarios test generosity with knowledge and willingness to learn from peers.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — A stakeholder wants jargon restored**

You simplified a page in line with your team's style guide, changing "remuneration" to "pay". A policy stakeholder emails: "Please change it back — 'remuneration' is the correct term and 'pay' sounds unprofessional." What is the most effective response?

- A) Change it back immediately — stakeholders outrank style guides.
- B) Reply explaining the evidence for plain language, note that the style guide requires common words, offer to discuss, and let your senior content designer know about the conversation.
- C) Ignore the email — the style guide settles the matter.
- D) Change it back, but keep "pay" in brackets afterwards as a compromise.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This scenario tests whether you can hold a user-centred line kindly. Option B does everything right for your level: it engages with the stakeholder respectfully, explains the content decision with evidence (a named duty of your role), keeps the door open, and — crucially — loops in your senior, because a stakeholder disagreement about standards is exactly the kind of situation where a junior should have backup rather than fight alone. Option A abandons users and the style guide at the first sign of pressure, and teaches stakeholders that pushing works. Option C wins the battle and loses the relationship: silence reads as arrogance and poisons future collaboration — the opposite of managing stakeholder relationships. Option D creates a worse page than either version: "remuneration (pay)" doubles the reading load and signals that the team cannot make a decision. The professional behaviours rewarded here are user focus, evidence-based explanation, and sensible escalation.

**Question 2 (easy) — You spot your own published mistake**

Ten minutes after publishing an update, you notice you introduced an error: the page now says applications close on "31 June" — a date that does not exist. Nobody else has noticed. What is the most effective response?

- A) Fix it quietly — no harm done, no need to mention it.
- B) Fix it (or start the fix immediately), tell your senior content designer what happened, and check whether the correct date is 30 June or something else before republishing.
- C) Leave it until the next scheduled publishing run to avoid making extra work.
- D) Report it to your senior and wait for them to tell you what to do.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Two things matter here: fixing the user-facing problem fast, and being honest about it. Option B does both — and adds the step juniors sometimes miss: verifying the correct fact before republishing, because guessing "30 June" and being wrong again would double the damage. Why tell your senior about a mistake you could hide? Because openness is how teams stay safe: if the error was caused by a process gap (say, no second check on date changes), your honesty lets the team fix the process, and your credibility grows rather than shrinks — people trust colleagues who surface their own errors. Option A fixes the page but hides the learning. Option C leaves a nonexistent date live in front of users for no defensible reason. Option D is the over-escalation mirror of A: you do not need permission to correct your own fresh error, only to be transparent about it. Ownership plus honesty plus verification is the pattern.

**Question 3 (easy) — Unclear direction from a senior**

Your senior content designer asks you to "tidy up the fees page before Thursday" and then goes into back-to-back meetings. Reading the page, you find it needs far more than tidying: the structure is confusing and some content may be out of date. What is the most effective response?

- A) Do a light tidy only — that is what was literally asked.
- B) Rewrite the whole page thoroughly — they will be pleased you took initiative.
- C) Send the senior a short message: you have started the tidy-up, you think the page has deeper problems (with one or two examples), and you ask whether they want a bigger rework now or the tidy first — then continue with the tidy while awaiting a reply.
- D) Wait until Thursday to ask, since they are busy.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Your level is defined by working independently after being given direction — which includes recognising when the direction no longer fits what you found, and saying so early. Option C threads the needle: it makes progress on the agreed task (so Thursday is safe either way), surfaces the new information with concrete examples, and asks a precise, answerable question — all in a message the senior can handle between meetings. Option A delivers the letter of the request while knowingly leaving a confusing, possibly inaccurate page in front of users — the found problems go unmentioned, which is the real failure. Option B is the seductive one: initiative sounds admirable, but silently expanding scope risks duplicating planned work, missing Thursday, or making changes that need policy checks you do not know about — initiative at your level means raising, not unilaterally deciding. Option D sits on important information for days. The rewarded behaviours: transparency, momentum, and asking good questions at the right time.

**Question 4 (easy) — Pairing invitation when you feel behind**

Your team encourages pair working. A fellow junior invites you to pair on their draft this afternoon, but you are behind on your own ticket, which is due tomorrow. What is the most effective response?

- A) Decline without explanation and work on your ticket.
- B) Accept and pair all afternoon — collaboration always comes first.
- C) Tell your colleague you are behind on a ticket due tomorrow, offer a shorter slot (say, 45 minutes) or tomorrow afternoon, and flag your workload at stand-up if the ticket is at risk.
- D) Accept, but secretly work on your own ticket during the session.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Healthy Agile teams run on honest, visible trade-offs. Option C is honest about your constraint, still offers real collaboration (a focused 45 minutes of pairing is genuinely useful), and uses the team's own mechanism — stand-up — to make workload pressure visible so the team can help. That last part matters: in Agile working, quietly drowning is not diligence; the framework exists so teams can rebalance. Option A protects your ticket but wastes a chance to collaborate and leaves your colleague with an unexplained no — small withdrawals from the relationship account. Option B sacrifices a commitment the team is counting on; "collaboration first" is not a rule that overrides all planning. Option D is the clearly worst behaviour: it fakes attention, shortchanges both tasks, and if noticed (it usually is) damages trust more than an honest no ever would. The rewarded behaviours: honesty, proportionate generosity, and using team ceremonies for their real purpose.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Pressure to skip the second check**

It is 4:40 pm. A stakeholder urgently needs a correction published today, and your team's process requires a second check by another content designer before publishing. The only available checker left at 4:30. Your senior is reachable by phone. What is the most effective response?

- A) Publish without the check — it is a small change and the stakeholder said it is urgent.
- B) Tell the stakeholder it will be published tomorrow morning, without exploring alternatives.
- C) Call your senior, explain the situation, and ask whether they can second-check remotely or approve an exception — then follow whatever the process allows.
- D) Ask the stakeholder to confirm in writing that they take responsibility, then publish unchecked.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Publishing processes exist because unchecked "small changes" are how wrong dates, broken links and legal inaccuracies reach users — and your role explicitly includes supporting publishing processes, not bypassing them. But rigid refusal (option B) is not the ideal either, because processes usually contain legitimate flexibility: a remote second check, or a lead-approved exception. Option C finds the legitimate path: it treats the urgency seriously, uses the escalation route that exists precisely for edge cases, and keeps the decision with someone whose role carries that authority. Option A takes a risk that is not yours to take. Option D misunderstands responsibility: a stakeholder's email cannot transfer accountability for content quality — the check exists to catch errors, and paperwork does not catch errors. If your senior is unreachable, option B becomes the right fallback: a few hours' delay is almost always cheaper than an unchecked mistake. Rewarded behaviours: respect for process, resourceful escalation, and holding urgency and quality together instead of trading one away instantly.

**Question 6 (moderate) — A research finding you disagree with**

User research shows participants struggled with a page structure you designed and were proud of. The researcher presents the findings at a team meeting and recommends restructuring. Privately, you think two of the five participants misunderstood the task. What is the most effective response?

- A) Accept the recommendation without mentioning your concern — challenging research looks defensive.
- B) In the meeting or shortly after, thank the researcher, raise your observation about the two participants as a question, and offer to work with them on what a follow-up test or revision should look like.
- C) Explain to the team why the research is flawed and the structure should stay.
- D) Say nothing, but quietly keep the structure unchanged.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This scenario probes two behaviours at once: how you respond to feedback about your own work, and how you treat evidence. Option B models both well — it engages with the findings rather than defending territory, raises a legitimate methodological question as a question rather than a verdict, and moves toward collaboration (a follow-up test) rather than a standoff. Note the emotional intelligence in sequencing: thank first, question second — researchers are colleagues, not opponents, and your role says you start from discovery and work closely with user researchers. Option A fails differently than it seems: suppressing a genuine observation is not humility, it is withholding information the team needs to interpret its evidence — and it breeds quiet resentment. Option C converts a two-participant doubt into a wholesale dismissal of the research; even if you were right about those two, three participants still struggled. Option D is the worst: it ignores evidence, the team's decision-making process, and honesty all at once. Rewarded behaviours: openness to feedback, constructive challenge, and letting evidence — not ego — decide.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Accessibility versus a launch deadline**

Two days before a page goes live, you notice its infographic contains essential eligibility information as text within the image, with no text alternative — screen reader users would miss the information entirely. The designer who made the infographic is busy, and the product manager says: "We can fix accessibility after launch." What is the most effective response?

- A) Accept the product manager's call — they own the timeline.
- B) Explain that the missing information is essential and launching would exclude screen reader users; propose a fast fix (adding the eligibility information as body text below the image before launch) and involve your senior if the deadline pressure continues.
- C) Refuse to be involved with the launch and tell the team it is illegal.
- D) Add a note to the backlog so the problem is fixed in a future sprint.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Government services must be accessible to everyone, and content must work with common assistive technologies — this is a core element of user-centred content design, not a nice-to-have. The key insight in option B is that this conflict is largely false: the fix (duplicating the essential information as real text under the image) takes minutes, not days, so you can protect users and the deadline. Strong situational judgement often looks like this — refusing the either/or and offering a third path. Option B also escalates proportionately: you explain first, propose a solution, and bring in your senior only if pressure continues, which matches your level. Option A treats accessibility as a scheduling preference the product manager can waive; excluding disabled users from essential eligibility information is a harm and, for public sector websites, a legal compliance issue — not a timeline trade-off. Option C has the right concern with the wrong manner: dramatic refusal and legal threats damage the collaboration you need, when a constructive fix was available. Option D knowingly ships exclusion. Rewarded behaviours: inclusion, solution-focused challenge, and calibrated escalation.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Credit for your work**

In a show-and-tell, a senior colleague presents the improved sign-up journey. The before/after content comparisons — which you wrote — are shown without your name, and afterwards a stakeholder emails the senior colleague praising "your excellent rewrite". What is the most effective response?

- A) Reply-all to the email correcting the record.
- B) Say nothing — juniors should not expect credit.
- C) Mention it privately to the senior colleague: you were glad the work landed well, and you would appreciate being named when your work is shown, since visibility matters for your development.
- D) Raise it as a formal grievance with HR.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** This is a workplace maturity question, and the test-writers are looking for calibrated assertiveness: neither swallowing the issue nor detonating it. Option C addresses the right person, privately, with a constructive frame — most cases like this are oversight rather than theft, and a private conversation lets the colleague fix it gracefully (many will reply to the stakeholder crediting you unprompted). It also names a legitimate professional interest: at junior level, visible work is how you build the track record for your next step. Option B teaches you to be invisible and lets a small unfairness calcify into a pattern; self-advocacy is a professional skill, not arrogance. Option A makes a public correction for what is probably a private-scale problem — it embarrasses the colleague and makes the stakeholder's inbox the venue for team dynamics. Option D leaps past every informal step; formal routes exist for patterns and serious cases, not first instances of missed credit. Rewarded behaviours: direct communication, proportionality, and assuming good intent first.

**Question 9 (moderate) — A community request during a busy week**

You are part of the cross-government content community. A content designer in another department posts: "Has anyone written content for reporting a lost document? We're starting from scratch." You wrote exactly that content last quarter, but you are having a busy week. What is the most effective response?

- A) Ignore the post — your own team's work comes first this week.
- B) Post a short reply linking to your published pages and offering a 20-minute call next week, and mention anything you learned the hard way.
- C) Spend half a day writing them a detailed guide.
- D) Reply saying you did this work, but that they should figure it out themselves since your version may not fit their context.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Engaging with and contributing to the cross-government content community is a named duty at your level — and this scenario tests whether you contribute proportionately when it is inconvenient. Option B is generous and bounded: linking existing published work costs minutes, the offer of a short call defers deeper help to a week you can afford, and sharing lessons learned ("what I'd do differently") is often the most valuable part for the other team. This is precisely how the community creates value: reuse instead of reinvention across government. Option A hoards knowledge that cost the taxpayer once already and would now be paid for twice. Option C is generous but disproportionate — sacrificing half a day of committed team time for an unrequested guide overshoots what the situation needs; it also does the other designer's thinking for them. Option D wraps a refusal in a truism: of course contexts differ, which is exactly why a conversation helps — mentioning the difference while withholding the help is the worst of both. Rewarded behaviours: generosity, proportionality, and community citizenship.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Conflicting instructions from two seniors**

Your senior content designer told you on Monday to restructure a guidance page into three shorter pages. On Wednesday, while your senior is away at a conference, the service's delivery manager tells you: "Don't split that page — we need it as one page for the launch next week. Please put it back." The delivery manager seems certain, and the launch matters. What is the most effective response?

- A) Follow the delivery manager's instruction — they are present and the launch is imminent.
- B) Follow your senior's original instruction — content decisions belong to content designers.
- C) Explain that your senior directed the split, ask the delivery manager for the reason behind the single-page need, and contact your senior (message or call) to resolve the conflict before undoing the work — while making sure the launch deadline is flagged in that message.
- D) Do neither and work on other tickets until your senior returns.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Conflicting instructions from people with different kinds of authority is one of the classic junior-level dilemmas, and the tested behaviour is: do not silently pick a side — surface the conflict to the people who own it, fast. Option C does the three necessary things: it is transparent with the delivery manager (no secret non-compliance), it gathers the missing information (there may be a real launch constraint your senior does not know about — or the delivery manager may not know why the split serves users), and it puts the decision back where it belongs, urgently enough to respect the launch. A short message — "DM asks me to revert the split for launch; deadline is next week; how do you want me to proceed?" — usually resolves this within hours. Option A undoes directed work on one person's say-so and leaves your senior ambushed on return. Option B wins a turf war instead of solving a coordination failure, and ignores that the delivery manager might hold genuinely new information. Option D is the quiet catastrophe: paralysis, with the conflict undiscovered until the week of launch. Rewarded behaviours: transparency, escalation with deadlines attached, and curiosity about the other side's reasons before judging them.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Least effective: a tempting shortcut with data**

Your team lead asks you to prepare a short summary of how the new guidance pages performed, for a stakeholder meeting tomorrow. Checking the dashboard, you find the data is mixed: feedback scores improved, but task completion is flat and one page's drop-out rate worsened. Which response is LEAST effective?

- A) Present all three findings, with a sentence on what the team plans to investigate about the worsened page.
- B) Present the improved feedback scores prominently and omit the flat completion and worsened drop-out, since the meeting needs a positive story.
- C) Ask your team lead whether they want a one-slide summary or a fuller picture, and mention now that the data is mixed so they are not surprised tomorrow.
- D) Present the findings and note honestly that four weeks of data may be too little to judge some of the changes.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Note the reversed question format — you are hunting the worst response, and careless reading loses easy marks here. Option B is the clearly least effective: selectively reporting only favourable metrics is a small act of dishonesty with a long tail. The omitted numbers are on a dashboard anyone can open; when a stakeholder finds them later, the cost is not one awkward meeting but durable doubt about everything your team reports — the exact opposite of using evidence to build stakeholder trust. It also deprives the team of the chance to act on the worsened page early. Every other option is defensible: A is the honest core; C adds smart expectation-management, warning the lead before the meeting rather than during it; D adds appropriate statistical humility about a short measurement window. In least-effective questions, do not overthink the ranking of the good options — identify the one that breaches a professional principle (here, honesty with evidence) and select it. Rewarded behaviour, by inversion: report the data you have, not the data you wish you had.

**Question 12 (challenging) — A user complaint that crosses teams**

A member of the public emails the general feedback inbox, which you help monitor: "Your website told me I could apply by phone, but the phone line says applications are online only. I've wasted two weeks." You check: your team's page does say "you can apply by phone", but the phone service is run by a different directorate, which changed its policy without telling your team. What is the most effective response?

- A) Reply to the user explaining the other directorate caused the error, and forward the complaint to them.
- B) Update or flag the incorrect page urgently, tell your senior what you found, make sure someone sends the user an apology with the correct application route, and suggest the team establish a way to be notified when the phone service's policies change.
- C) Update the page and consider the matter closed.
- D) Log the complaint in the backlog for prioritisation at the next sprint planning.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This scenario layers four duties, and option B handles each in the right order. First, stop the harm: the page is actively misdirecting users today, so fixing or flagging it is urgent — under your team's process that may mean a senior publishes the fix, which is why telling your senior comes packaged with it. Second, repair the user's experience: someone must reply with an apology and the correct route; a person wasted two weeks because of the site, and closing the loop with them is basic service ownership. Third, address the cause: the deep problem is not the sentence but the missing communication channel between directorates — suggesting a notification arrangement is exactly the "contribute to iterating and improving processes" behaviour in your strategic thinking skill, and it is what stops the same failure recurring on other pages. Option A gets the sequence exactly backwards: it prioritises blame over fixing, exposes internal boundaries to a member of the public (who rightly experiences government as one service), and leaves the wrong content live. Option C fixes today's symptom and abandons both the user and the cause. Option D treats a live, harm-causing inaccuracy as a routine backlog item — sprint cadence is for planned work, not for stopping ongoing harm. Rewarded behaviours: user focus, urgency calibration, honest escalation, and systems thinking about causes.

### Preparation tips

- **Anchor on the user.** When torn between options, ask which response best serves the people using the service. In government content roles, that anchor rarely misleads.
- **Learn your escalation lines.** Before any assessment (and in real life), be clear about what you decide alone, what you decide-then-inform, and what needs a senior first. Most junior-level scenarios are testing exactly this boundary.
- **Practise the "third option" habit.** Many scenarios present a false either/or (deadline versus quality, stakeholder versus user). The strongest answers often protect both — a fast fix, a remote check, a short pairing slot.
- **Read the question stem twice.** "Most effective" and "least effective" look identical at speed. Check which one you are answering before you click.
- **Reflect on real moments.** Think back over your recent weeks: a piece of feedback, a mistake, a conflict. What did you do? What would the best version of you have done? Honest reflection is the best situational judgement training there is.
- **Be yourself, calibrated.** Answer as your genuine professional self on a good day — honest, calm, user-focused. Trying to impersonate an imagined perfect employee produces inconsistent answers that scoring algorithms notice.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Over-escalating everything.** Asking a senior before fixing your own typo signals dependence, not diligence. Escalate decisions that carry risk or authority beyond your level — handle the rest and stay transparent.
- **Under-escalating the big ones.** The mirror error: quietly absorbing stakeholder conflicts, accessibility risks or conflicting instructions. If two people with authority disagree, surfacing the conflict is your job; resolving it is theirs.
- **Choosing the "heroic" option.** Rewriting the whole page unasked, spending half a day on someone else's request, refusing to launch — dramatic responses usually overshoot. Proportionality scores better than heroism.
- **Choosing the conflict-avoidant option.** Saying nothing to keep the peace — about credit, about flawed research, about a stakeholder's jargon — reads as weak judgement, not politeness. Constructive voice beats silence.
- **Sacrificing honesty for a good story.** Selective reporting, hidden mistakes and quiet non-compliance are heavily penalised in scoring, because they are the behaviours that most damage real teams.
- **Forgetting the relationship after the decision.** The best options usually include a human step — thanking the researcher, apologising to the user, looping back to the stakeholder. If two options differ only in that step, choose the one that includes it.
