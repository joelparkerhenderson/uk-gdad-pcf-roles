## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the mental processes your role actually uses — pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving — through practical scenarios rather than abstract puzzles. Instead of number sequences and rotated shapes, a job-specific version hands you a swimlane diagram with a flaw in it, a set of business rules to reason from, or a backlog with dependencies to untangle, and asks you to think quickly and accurately.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 15 to 30 minutes with roughly 15 to 30 multiple-choice questions. Many platforms are adaptive: answer correctly and the questions get harder, which means a test that feels increasingly difficult is often going well. Scoring is objective and usually norm-referenced — your performance is compared against a large group of similar candidates — and employers often see accuracy and speed separately, so working carefully matters as much as working fast. Almost every platform offers a short set of unscored practice questions before the timer starts; always take them, because familiarity with the interface is free marks.

Why do employers use cognitive assessments for junior business analysts? Because your core output is structured thinking made visible. When you support the representation of a business situation within a defined task, you are doing pattern recognition and logical modelling. When you contribute to evaluating a process, you are error checking. When you juggle the specific tasks you are responsible for against the direction you receive, you are prioritising. A well-designed cognitive test compresses a week of those demands into twenty minutes — and practising for it strengthens the same muscles you use at your desk.

### How this assessment maps to your role

Each cognitive dimension connects to a named skill in your junior business analyst role:

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **Business modelling** and **Business process improvement**: visualising business processes and supporting the representation of business situations means noticing recurring structures — repeated process shapes, consistent notation, duplicated steps — and spotting where a model breaks its own conventions.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Context, problem and option analysis** and **Adapting to delivery methodologies**: contributing to option assessment under supervision, and explaining how your approach changes between scrum and kanban, both require reasoning validly from rules and facts to conclusions.
- **Error checking** maps to **Requirements definition and management** and **Testing (business analysis)**: supporting the capture and validation of requirements, and helping trace requirements through to tested functionality, is systematic error detection — finding the requirement that contradicts a rule, the criterion that drifted out of scope, the model that disagrees with the procedure.
- **Prioritisation** maps to your role level: being **responsible for the output of specific tasks** while receiving direction means constantly sequencing competing demands sensibly, and knowing when a clash needs a conversation rather than a guess.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Systems analysis** and **User experience analysis**: supporting the specification of system requirements, and supporting the analysis and prioritisation of user experience needs, both mean decomposing a fuzzy situation into parts you can examine one at a time.

### Practice questions

The questions progress from easy to hard. Write your answer down before reading the explanation.

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern recognition in a process model**

Your team's process modelling convention says: every process step is named with a verb followed by a noun ("Check application", "Send letter"), and every decision point is phrased as a yes/no question ending in a question mark ("Application complete?"). Reviewing a draft model, you see these five labels:

1. Check application
2. Application complete?
3. Sending of the letter
4. Record decision
5. Escalation needed?

Which label breaks the convention?

- A) Label 2
- B) Label 3
- C) Label 4
- D) Label 5

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Test each label against the two rules. Labels 1 and 4 are verb-plus-noun ("Check application", "Record decision") — correct for steps. Labels 2 and 5 are yes/no questions with question marks — correct for decisions. Label 3, "Sending of the letter", is a noun phrase, not a verb followed by a noun; the convention requires "Send letter". This looks pedantic, but naming conventions are load-bearing: consistent verb-noun labels keep models scannable, make it obvious what happens at each step, and let colleagues search and compare models reliably. When you support the representation of business situations, running a quick convention check before circulating a model is a habit that makes your output — the output you are responsible for at this level — consistently trustworthy.

**Question 2 (easy) — Logical deduction from a business rule**

A benefits service has this rule: "An application is fast-tracked if the applicant is terminally ill or the application is a resubmission following an administrative error." You are told that Priya's application was not fast-tracked. Which one of the following must be true?

- A) Priya's application contained an error.
- B) Priya is not terminally ill and her application is not a resubmission following an administrative error.
- C) Priya's application will be rejected.
- D) Priya did not meet the financial eligibility criteria.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The rule says either condition is sufficient for fast-tracking: terminal illness, or a qualifying resubmission. Priya's application was not fast-tracked — so neither sufficient condition can have applied, because if either had, the application would have been fast-tracked. Logicians call this reasoning pattern denying the consequent: from "if A or B, then C" and "not C", you can validly conclude "not A and not B". Option A confuses things entirely (an administrative error would push towards fast-tracking on resubmission, not away). Options C and D introduce topics — rejection, financial criteria — the rule says nothing about. Business rules like this fill government service design, and reasoning about what they do and do not imply is exactly what you do when you support the analysis and validation of requirements against policy constraints.

**Question 3 (easy) — Prioritising your task list**

You are responsible for four pieces of work this week:

1. A process model your senior business analyst needs by Wednesday for a stakeholder workshop.
2. Reviewing a colleague's user stories — they asked for comments "by Friday, ideally sooner".
3. Your monthly timesheet, due Friday, taking ten minutes.
4. Drafting survey questions for a piece of user research that starts next Monday.

It is Monday morning. Which order of focus is most defensible?

- A) 3, 2, 1, 4 — clear the quick task, then help others, then your own work
- B) 1, 2, 4, 3 — workshop-critical model first, then the review, then the survey draft, with the timesheet slotted in before Friday
- C) 4, 1, 2, 3 — next week's work first because research is important
- D) 2, 1, 3, 4 — helping colleagues always comes first

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Sequence by deadline and impact together. The process model (1) has the earliest deadline and feeds a stakeholder workshop — if it slips, other people's time is wasted, so it anchors Monday and Tuesday. The story review (2) is due Friday but "ideally sooner", and a colleague is waiting; it comes next. The survey draft (4) must be ready for Monday, so it needs meaningful time this week — ahead of the timesheet in focus terms, though the ten-minute timesheet (3) can be slotted into any gap before Friday. Option A promotes a trivial task to first place; "quick wins first" feels productive but delays the highest-impact work. Option C works on next week before this week's hard deadline. Option D makes a rule of always helping first, which ignores the workshop dependency. Note the realistic nuance: the right answer is not a rigid sequence but a defensible focus order with small tasks slotted into gaps — exactly how you will actually manage the outputs you are responsible for.

**Question 4 (easy) — Error checking a swimlane handoff**

A swimlane diagram shows a grant application process across three lanes: Applicant, Caseworker, Finance team. The written procedure says: "The caseworker approves the application and passes it to the finance team, who make the payment and notify the applicant." The diagram shows: Applicant submits → Caseworker approves → Caseworker makes payment → Caseworker notifies applicant. What is wrong with the diagram?

- A) The notification step is missing.
- B) The payment and notification steps are both in the wrong lane — the procedure assigns them to the finance team.
- C) The approval step should be in the Finance lane.
- D) Nothing — the diagram matches the procedure.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Compare each step's lane against the procedure, one step at a time. Submission belongs to the applicant — the diagram agrees. Approval belongs to the caseworker — the diagram agrees, which rules out option C. Now the procedure's second half: "passes it to the finance team, who make the payment and notify the applicant." The "who" is the finance team, so both payment and notification belong in the Finance lane. The diagram instead keeps both in the Caseworker lane — two steps, both misplaced, exactly as option B says. Option A is false: notification is present, just in the wrong lane — and "present but misplaced" versus "missing" is a distinction worth being precise about, because the fixes are different. Option D would wave through a diagram that contradicts the procedure on who controls public money, and lane errors in financial processes are not cosmetic: swimlanes encode accountability, and a diagram showing caseworkers making payments misstates a segregation-of-duties control. The reliable checking technique: read each step aloud together with its lane — "the caseworker... makes the payment?" — and the contradiction with the procedure announces itself. That habit will serve every model you review while supporting the representation of business situations.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Deduction about iteration**

Your team is deciding how to deliver a new reporting feature. Two facts are agreed: (i) the users' actual reporting needs are poorly understood; (ii) the underlying data is well understood and stable. A senior colleague says: "This is exactly the situation where iteration earns its keep." Which statement best explains why iteration is especially valuable here?

- A) Iteration is always the fastest way to deliver any feature.
- B) Because user needs are uncertain, releasing small versions and learning from real use will correct misunderstandings early, before much has been built on them.
- C) Because the data is stable, the team can build the whole feature in one pass without user feedback.
- D) Iteration lets the team skip requirements work entirely.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Match the reasoning to the facts. The risk in this situation is concentrated in one place: fact (i) says user needs are poorly understood, so any big up-front design is likely to be wrong in ways nobody can predict from a meeting room. Iteration attacks exactly that risk — build a small slice, put it in front of real users, learn, adjust — so misunderstandings are corrected while they are still cheap. That is option B, and it is the substance of your named skill "explain why iteration is important". Option A overclaims: iteration is not always fastest, it is risk-reducing under uncertainty. Option C draws the wrong conclusion from fact (ii) — stable data reduces one risk but does nothing about the user-needs risk, which is the binding one. Option D confuses iterating with skipping analysis; iterative teams still capture and validate needs, just in smaller, testable increments. Being able to say why iteration fits this situation — rather than reciting that iteration is good — is what separates an analyst who understands methodology from one who follows it.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Pattern recognition in user research findings**

You are supporting the analysis of usability test results. Five participants attempted the same three tasks. Completion results (✓ = completed, ✗ = failed):

| Participant | Task 1: Find form | Task 2: Complete form | Task 3: Check status |
|---|---|---|---|
| P1 | ✓ | ✗ | ✓ |
| P2 | ✓ | ✗ | ✓ |
| P3 | ✓ | ✓ | ✗ |
| P4 | ✓ | ✗ | ✓ |
| P5 | ✓ | ✗ | ✓ |

What is the most useful pattern to report to the team?

- A) P3 is an unusually skilled participant.
- B) Task 2 failed for four of five participants — the form completion step has a systematic problem worth investigating; Task 3's single failure is worth a note.
- C) The service works well because Task 1 always succeeded.
- D) The sample is too small to say anything at all.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Read the table by columns, not rows — in usability data, tasks are usually the unit of interest, because a failing task points at a fixable design problem. Task 1: five of five completed. Task 2: one of five completed — a striking, consistent failure pattern across four different people, which is the signature of a systematic design problem rather than individual error. Task 3: four of five, one failure worth noting but not alarming alone. Option B reports exactly this, with proportionate emphasis. Option A reads by rows and personalises the data — but P3's difference (passing Task 2, failing Task 3) is more likely noise or an interesting edge case than "skill", and usability testing is about the design, not grading participants. Option C cherry-picks the good news. Option D misunderstands small-sample research: five participants cannot give you percentages you can generalise, but four-of-five failing the same task is a strong qualitative signal — small samples reliably surface big problems. This is the heart of your **user experience analysis** skill: supporting data-informed decisions means finding the pattern that points at an action.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Error checking requirements against a policy constraint**

A policy constraint states: "Applicants must be able to complete the entire process without providing an email address." You are checking a set of draft requirements:

1. The system must send an application receipt to the applicant's email address.
2. The system must display an on-screen reference number when an application is submitted.
3. The system must allow status checks using the reference number and the applicant's date of birth.
4. The system must offer email notifications to applicants who choose to provide an email address.

Which requirement conflicts with the policy constraint?

- A) Requirement 1
- B) Requirement 2
- C) Requirement 3
- D) Requirement 4

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The constraint protects a complete no-email journey. Requirement 1 says the system "must send" a receipt to "the applicant's email address" — phrased as unconditional, it presumes every applicant has and provides an email address, which breaks the constraint. Requirement 2 provides an on-screen receipt that needs no email — it actively supports the constraint. Requirement 3 builds status checking on reference number and date of birth, no email involved — also supportive. Requirement 4 is the give-away contrast: it makes email explicitly optional ("who choose to provide"), which is exactly how requirement 1 should have been phrased. The fix is not to delete requirement 1 but to recondition it ("where an applicant has provided an email address, the system must send..."). Spotting unconditional "must" phrasing that smuggles in an assumption is a core requirements-validation skill — and policy constraints about digital inclusion, like this one, are common and consequential in government services, where applicants without email access have a legal right to complete their journey.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Applied problem solving with WIP and blockers**

Your kanban team has columns To do / Analysis (WIP limit 2) / Build (WIP limit 3) / Test (WIP limit 2) / Done. Current state: Analysis holds items A and B; Build holds C, D, E; Test holds F and G. Item F is blocked awaiting a security review that will take three more days; G finishes testing today. You have finished analysing item A. What happens to the flow, and what is the most useful action?

- A) A moves to Build immediately — Build's limit is not reached.
- B) A cannot move to Build (full at 3), so raise it at stand-up; when G completes today, one Build item can move to Test, then A can enter Build. Meanwhile, F's three-day blockage deserves attention since it holds a Test slot.
- C) A moves straight to Test, skipping Build, because Test will have a free slot today.
- D) Nothing can move until F is unblocked.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Walk the board. Build holds C, D, E — three items against a limit of 3, so it is full and A cannot enter yet; option A miscounts. When G completes today, Test drops to one item (the blocked F), opening a Test slot; one of C, D or E can then advance to Test, freeing a Build slot for A. So the flow unblocks naturally today — but only if the team notices and moves things, which is why raising it at stand-up matters. The deeper observation in option B is F: a blocked item sitting in Test for three more days occupies half of Test's capacity, throttling the whole downstream flow — exactly the kind of systemic issue WIP limits are designed to make visible. Escalating or expediting the security review may be worth more than any local shuffling. Option C invents a rule — items do not skip Build because Test has space. Option D is too pessimistic, as the walk-through shows. Reading a board this way, as a system of constraints rather than a to-do list, is what "adapting how you work based on your team's delivery methodology" looks like in practice.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Spotting the flaw in a process improvement claim**

A team lead argues: "We piloted the new triage checklist in the Croydon office last month. Croydon's error rate fell from 12% to 9%. The other offices did not use the checklist and their average error rate stayed at 12%. So the checklist works, and rolling it out nationally will cut the national error rate by a quarter." What is the strongest criticism of the final conclusion?

- A) The error rates are probably measured wrongly.
- B) Croydon's fall shows nothing because error rates never change for real reasons.
- C) The comparison is encouraging, but one office over one month is a small basis — Croydon may differ from other offices, other local changes may explain the fall, and a national rollout may not reproduce pilot conditions.
- D) A fall from 12% to 9% is a fifth, not a quarter.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** First credit what the argument does well: it has a comparison group (other offices at a stable 12%), which makes it far stronger than a before-and-after story alone. The weakness is in the leap from one encouraging pilot to a confident national forecast. One office in one month is a small sample in both space and time; Croydon may differ from other offices in caseload mix, staffing, or morale; the pilot itself may have changed behaviour simply through attention and novelty (people often perform differently when they know they are being observed); and rollouts rarely reproduce the enthusiasm and support of a pilot. Option C names this cluster of concerns without dismissing the evidence — the analyst's balanced posture. Option A speculates with no basis. Option B is nihilism, not criticism. Option D is worth checking — 12% to 9% is a fall of 3 points on 12, and 3 ÷ 12 = 25%, so it genuinely is a quarter; D's arithmetic complaint is wrong. Contributing to the evaluation of process improvements means exactly this: welcoming evidence, sizing it honestly, and separating "promising pilot" from "proven national result".

**Question 10 (hard) — Multi-constraint scheduling**

You must complete four analysis activities for a discovery: stakeholder interviews (I), a process observation visit (O), a data-quality review (D), and a findings workshop (W). Constraints: (i) W must come after all three other activities; (ii) O must happen on a Tuesday or Thursday, when the operations centre runs a full service; (iii) D cannot start until the data extract arrives, which is promised for Wednesday morning; (iv) each activity takes one full day, and you can do at most one per day. The week runs Monday to Friday. Which schedule satisfies every constraint?

- A) I Monday, O Tuesday, D Wednesday, W Thursday
- B) O Monday, I Tuesday, D Wednesday, W Friday
- C) I Monday, D Tuesday, O Thursday, W Friday
- D) I Wednesday, O Thursday, D Friday, W Friday

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Check each option against each constraint, eliminating on the first breach. Option B: O on Monday breaks constraint (ii) — eliminated. Option C: D on Tuesday breaks constraint (iii), because the extract arrives Wednesday — eliminated. Option D: two activities share Friday, breaking (iv) — and W would not be after D anyway — eliminated. Option A: I Monday (no constraint on I), O Tuesday (satisfies ii), D Wednesday (extract arrives Wednesday morning, and D occupies that day — satisfies iii), W Thursday (after I, O and D — satisfies i). Everything holds. Notice the efficient technique: you never needed to construct the schedule from scratch, only to test the offered ones — elimination against constraints is faster and less error-prone under time pressure. The same discipline runs your real planning: when someone proposes a discovery schedule, walking it against the hard constraints (site days, data availability, sequencing) is how you catch the plan that looked fine in someone's head.

**Question 11 (hard) — Deduction with an exception clause**

A department's data-sharing rule states: "Case data may be shared with another government department only if a data-sharing agreement is in place, unless the request is from law enforcement with a signed urgency certificate, in which case data may be shared and the agreement completed retrospectively within ten working days." You learn that case data was shared yesterday with another department, and no data-sharing agreement was in place at the time. Which conclusion must be true if the sharing complied with the rule?

- A) The rule was breached.
- B) The request came from law enforcement with a signed urgency certificate, and an agreement must be completed within ten working days of the sharing.
- C) A data-sharing agreement is not needed for this kind of request.
- D) The ten-working-day clock has already expired.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Structure the rule before reasoning: the default path requires an agreement in place before sharing; the exception path (law enforcement plus signed urgency certificate) permits sharing first with a retrospective agreement within ten working days. The facts: sharing happened with no agreement in place. The question adds a crucial premise — "if the sharing complied with the rule". Under that premise, the default path is unavailable (no agreement existed), so the sharing can only have travelled the exception path: law enforcement request, signed certificate, and a live obligation to complete the agreement within ten working days. That is option B, in full. Option A contradicts the stated premise of compliance. Option C misreads the exception as abolishing the agreement — the exception defers it, retrospectively required, not waived. Option D invents a fact: the sharing was yesterday, so the ten-day clock has barely started. Exception clauses like this are everywhere in government business rules, and the analyst's discipline is always the same: map the default path and the exception path separately, then locate the facts on one of them. This is precisely the reading you do when assessing how policy constraints shape system requirements.

**Question 12 (hard) — Choosing the right model for the job**

Your senior business analyst asks you to help a stakeholder group understand a problem: applicants are abandoning a licence application partway through, and nobody agrees where or why. Four modelling approaches are available. Which is the most appropriate first step?

- A) A detailed data model of the licensing database, showing every entity and relationship.
- B) An organisation chart of the licensing directorate.
- C) A process model of the applicant's journey from start to submission, annotated with the drop-off numbers at each step.
- D) A full target operating model for the future licensing service.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Choose the model that answers the question being asked. The problem is located in a journey — people start, and somewhere along the way they stop — and the disagreement is about where and why. A process model of the applicant's journey makes the "where" visible step by step, and annotating it with drop-off numbers turns argument into evidence: the step where abandonment spikes is where investigation should focus. That is option C, and it directly exercises your **business modelling** skill of visualising business processes to represent a situation. Option A models the wrong layer: the database's structure says nothing about where humans give up. Option B models the organisation, but applicants do not experience the org chart. Option D models a future state before the current problem is even understood — target operating models are valuable, but designing the destination before diagnosing the breakdown inverts the analysis. The principle generalises across your career: model selection is itself an analytical decision, and the best model is the simplest one that makes the disputed question visible. When colleagues disagree about facts, the analyst's job is to build the picture in which the facts can be seen.

### Preparation tips

- **Practise in short, regular sessions.** Twenty minutes several times a week builds pattern-recognition and logic fluency far better than an occasional marathon. Spacing lets your brain consolidate.
- **Always take the platform's unscored practice questions.** Interface familiarity — where the timer sits, how to flag and move on — is free performance on test day.
- **Slow down on rules, speed up on checking.** Most errors come from misreading the rule or constraint, not from faulty logic afterwards. Ten careful seconds on "what exactly does the convention say?" repays itself.
- **Use elimination on constraint questions.** Testing each option against each constraint until one breaches is usually faster than constructing the answer yourself — and it mirrors how you would validate a real plan.
- **Read tables by the unit of interest.** Ask "is the signal in the rows or the columns?" before diving in — usability results by task, error rates by office, requirements by policy area. Choosing the right axis is half the answer.
- **Connect each practice session to your craft.** After practising, find one real artefact — a model, a backlog, a findings table — and apply the same check to it. This doubles the value and quietly improves your actual deliverables.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Answering the question you expected.** Adaptive tests mix question types; a prioritisation question can follow three deduction questions. Read the actual ask — most, least, must be true, best first step — every single time.
- **Adding facts the question never gave you.** Deduction questions punish plausible assumptions. If the rule does not say it and the facts do not state it, it is not available to you.
- **Miscounting against limits.** "WIP limit 3" with three items means full, not one-more-allowed. "At least", "at most", "within", and "by" each have exact meanings — translate them deliberately.
- **Personalising data patterns.** When a table shows one row behaving differently, check whether the story is really about a column (a systematic cause) before concluding it is about a person or a place.
- **Spending too long on one hard question.** Ninety seconds of no progress means eliminate what you can, choose, and move on. One question rarely matters; a lost five minutes always does.
- **Treating "no perfect option" as a broken question.** Occasionally every option has a flaw; pick the one that best captures the genuine issue. Tests (and workplaces) sometimes offer only least-wrong choices.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures how accurately and confidently you handle the numbers your role genuinely produces: percentages and percentage changes, averages and weighted averages, ratios, rates, and data presented in tables and charts. It is emphatically not an advanced maths test — there is nothing beyond everyday arithmetic — but it is a precision test: it checks whether you select the right numbers, apply the right operation, and interpret the result sensibly, all under moderate time pressure.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 20 to 30 minutes with 15 to 20 questions. Each question presents data — a table of completion rates, a chart of weekly volumes, a short paragraph with figures — followed by a multiple-choice question with four or five options. A calculator is usually permitted (always check your test's instructions), rough paper is invaluable, and many platforms adapt question difficulty to your performance. Employers typically receive accuracy and speed measures separately, so a careful, methodical approach is rewarded.

Why does numeric reasoning matter for a junior business analyst? Because at your level, numbers stop being someone else's job. When you contribute to evaluating a business process, you compute the before-and-after. When you support the prioritisation of user experience needs, you summarise research data. When you help assess options, you compare costs and volumes. Your outputs feed decisions, and a wrong percentage in a summary can steer a team wrong for weeks. The distracting fear — "I'm not a maths person" — is worth retiring now: workplace numeracy is a small set of methods applied carefully, and every question below shows its full working so you can adopt the method, not just admire the answer.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Percentages and percentage change** map to **Business process improvement**: contributing to the analysis and evaluation of processes almost always means quantifying change — error rates, completion rates, processing times — and knowing the difference between a percentage and a percentage-point change.
- **Averages and weighted averages** map to **User experience analysis** and **Context, problem and option analysis**: survey scores, task times, and satisfaction ratings arrive as averages, and combining groups of different sizes correctly is the difference between a true summary and a subtly false one.
- **Ratios and rates** map to **Adapting to delivery methodologies**: velocity, throughput, and capacity are rates; reading them correctly helps you contribute usefully to sprint planning and flow discussions.
- **Reading tables and charts** maps to **Requirements definition and management** and **Testing (business analysis)**: coverage tables, defect summaries, and traceability matrices are tabular data, and extracting the right cells — then computing the right derived figure — is a daily micro-skill.
- **Sense-checking and interpretation** map to **Business modelling**: understanding how processes, systems and data interact includes noticing when a figure is implausible, which is often the first clue that a model or an extract is wrong.

### Practice questions

Use a calculator and rough paper, as you would in a real test. Every explanation shows the arithmetic step by step.

**Question 1 (easy) — Percentage of stories completed**

Your team planned 24 user stories for a programme increment and completed 18 of them. What percentage of the planned stories were completed?

- A) 66%
- B) 70%
- C) 75%
- D) 80%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Percentage = part ÷ whole × 100. Step 1: part = 18 completed, whole = 24 planned. Step 2: 18 ÷ 24 = 0.75. Step 3: 0.75 × 100 = 75%. Sense-check with fractions: 18/24 simplifies to 3/4 (divide both by 6), and three-quarters is 75% — confirmed. A quick habit worth building: simplify the fraction before dividing when the numbers share an obvious factor; 3 ÷ 4 is harder to fumble than 18 ÷ 24. When you report a figure like this in a sprint review, pairing it with the raw numbers ("75% — 18 of 24") lets colleagues verify at a glance and protects you if anyone misheard the base.

**Question 2 (easy) — Average handling time**

During a process observation you time six phone enquiries. They take 4, 7, 5, 9, 6, and 5 minutes. What is the mean handling time?

- A) 5 minutes
- B) 5.5 minutes
- C) 6 minutes
- D) 6.5 minutes

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Mean = total ÷ count. Step 1: total = 4 + 7 = 11; 11 + 5 = 16; 16 + 9 = 25; 25 + 6 = 31; 31 + 5 = 36 minutes. Step 2: count = 6 enquiries. Step 3: 36 ÷ 6 = 6 minutes. Sense-check: the values range from 4 to 9, and 6 sits inside that range, slightly below the middle of the extremes (6.5) because more values cluster low — plausible. Worth noticing for your observation write-ups: the mean alone hides the 9-minute call, and in process analysis the long calls often matter most (they may indicate complex cases or system slowness). Report the mean with the range — "average 6 minutes, ranging 4 to 9" — and your summary tells the truer story.

**Question 3 (easy) — Reading a defect summary table**

The latest test cycle produced this defect summary:

| Severity | Raised | Fixed |
|---|---|---|
| Critical | 4 | 4 |
| Major | 15 | 9 |
| Minor | 36 | 18 |

What fraction of all raised defects have been fixed?

- A) Just over a half
- B) Exactly a half
- C) Just under two-thirds
- D) Three-quarters

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Step 1: total raised = 4 + 15 + 36 = 55. Step 2: total fixed = 4 + 9 + 18 = 31. Step 3: fraction fixed = 31 ÷ 55 ≈ 0.564, or about 56%. Half of 55 is 27.5, and 31 exceeds that comfortably but falls well short of two-thirds (which would be about 36.7), so "just over a half" is the right description — option A. The table also rewards a severity-aware read: all four critical defects are fixed (100%), majors are at 9/15 = 60%, minors at 18/36 = 50%. That is a healthy pattern — fixing effort is concentrated where severity is highest — and it is exactly the kind of interpretive sentence that turns a table into a useful test summary when you support test outcome reporting.

**Question 4 (easy) — Percentage decrease in processing time**

After a process improvement, the average time to process a claim fell from 20 days to 16 days. What is the percentage decrease?

- A) 16%
- B) 20%
- C) 25%
- D) 4%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Percentage change = (change ÷ original) × 100. Step 1: change = 20 − 16 = 4 days. Step 2: divide by the original value: 4 ÷ 20 = 0.20. Step 3: × 100 = 20% decrease. Option D is the raw change in days dressed up as a percentage; option C comes from dividing by the new value (4 ÷ 16 = 25%) — the single most common percentage-change error. Remember the direction rule: change is always measured against where you started. And a curiosity worth filing away: a 20% decrease from 20 days is not undone by a 20% increase from 16 days (that would give 19.2 days) — percentage changes are not symmetric, which occasionally matters when someone claims a process has "recovered" to its old level.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Weighted average satisfaction score**

You are summarising user satisfaction for a service across two channels. The online channel had 300 respondents with a mean score of 4.2 out of 5. The phone channel had 100 respondents with a mean score of 3.4. What is the overall mean satisfaction score across all respondents?

- A) 3.8
- B) 4.0
- C) 4.1
- D) 3.9

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Do not average the averages — the groups differ in size, so weight them. Step 1: online total score = 300 × 4.2 = 1,260. Step 2: phone total = 100 × 3.4 = 340. Step 3: combined total = 1,260 + 340 = 1,600 across 300 + 100 = 400 respondents. Step 4: overall mean = 1,600 ÷ 400 = 4.0. The unweighted trap answer is (4.2 + 3.4) ÷ 2 = 3.8 — option A — which understates the true figure because it ignores that three-quarters of respondents used the online channel. Sense-check: the overall mean must sit between the two group means, closer to the bigger group's 4.2 — and 4.0 does. One more analyst's observation for your write-up: the overall 4.0 conceals a channel gap of 0.8 points, and the phone users' notably lower experience may be the actionable finding. Averages summarise; gaps motivate.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Sprint velocity and forecasting**

Your scrum team's last four sprints delivered 21, 19, 24, and 20 story points. The remaining backlog for the current release is 63 points. Using the four-sprint average velocity, how many more sprints should you forecast to finish the release?

- A) 2 sprints
- B) 3 sprints
- C) 4 sprints
- D) 5 sprints

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Step 1: average velocity = (21 + 19 + 24 + 20) ÷ 4. Total: 21 + 19 = 40; 40 + 24 = 64; 64 + 20 = 84. Average: 84 ÷ 4 = 21 points per sprint. Step 2: sprints needed = 63 ÷ 21 = 3 exactly. Forecast three more sprints — option B. Two cautions belong with the arithmetic. First, if the division had produced 3.2, the answer would be 4 sprints, not 3 — partial sprints round up, because the release is not done until the work is done. Second, the forecast inherits the assumption that future sprints resemble past ones; holidays, team changes, and unusually risky stories all bend velocity. In a planning conversation, the strong move is to give the calculation and its assumption together: "about three sprints at current velocity, if nothing changes the team's capacity". That sentence is your adapting-to-delivery-methodologies skill speaking in numbers.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Percentage points versus percentages**

A digital service's take-up rose from 40% of applicants to 50% of applicants after an awareness campaign. Which statement is accurate?

- A) Take-up rose by 10 percentage points, which is a 25% increase in take-up.
- B) Take-up rose by 10%, which is the same as 10 percentage points.
- C) Take-up rose by 25 percentage points.
- D) Take-up rose by 10%, so the campaign made little difference.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Two different measures hide in "rose from 40% to 50%". The percentage-point change is the simple difference: 50 − 40 = 10 percentage points. The percentage change treats the starting level as the base: 10 ÷ 40 = 0.25 = a 25% increase. Option A states both correctly. Option B calls the change "10%", conflating points with percent — the most common confusion in workplace reporting, and one that materially misleads: a rise of 10% of 40% would be only 4 points (to 44%). Option C swaps the two numbers. Option D compounds B's error with a judgement the data does not support — a quarter more take-up is a substantial shift. As the analyst in the room, using "percentage points" precisely, and gently translating when others do not, prevents a whole class of misunderstandings in benefits discussions and performance reviews.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Ratios in a research sample**

A user researcher plans 24 usability sessions and wants the sample split between "digitally confident" and "digitally less confident" participants in the ratio 1:2 respectively. How many digitally less confident participants should be recruited?

- A) 8
- B) 12
- C) 16
- D) 18

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A ratio of 1:2 means the sample divides into 1 + 2 = 3 equal shares. Step 1: one share = 24 ÷ 3 = 8 participants. Step 2: digitally confident = 1 share = 8; digitally less confident = 2 shares = 2 × 8 = 16. Option C. Option A answers the wrong group (the confident one); option B assumes an even split, ignoring the ratio; option D would require a 1:3 split. Always anchor which side of the ratio belongs to which group — the word "respectively" pins "1" to confident and "2" to less confident, and skimming past it reverses the recruitment. The scenario is realistic: services must work for people with lower digital confidence, so research deliberately over-samples them — and when you support user experience analysis, checking that the recruited sample matches the intended ratio is exactly the kind of quiet quality control that keeps findings credible.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Reading a two-way table**

Applications received last month, by channel and outcome:

| | Approved | Rejected | Incomplete |
|---|---|---|---|
| Online | 640 | 80 | 80 |
| Post | 120 | 40 | 40 |

Which statement is supported by the table?

- A) Online applications had a higher approval rate than postal applications.
- B) Postal applications had a higher approval rate than online ones.
- C) Approval rates were identical across channels.
- D) Most rejections came from postal applications.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Compute each channel's approval rate from its own row total. Online total = 640 + 80 + 80 = 800; approval rate = 640 ÷ 800 = 0.80 = 80%. Post total = 120 + 40 + 40 = 200; approval rate = 120 ÷ 200 = 0.60 = 60%. Online's 80% beats post's 60%, so option A is supported and B and C fall. Check D separately: rejections were 80 online versus 40 postal, so most rejections came from the online channel — D confuses the bigger raw count's location (online, simply because online volume is four times larger) with rates anyway, and still gets the count comparison backwards. The core skill here is denominator discipline: every rate needs its own row's total, and comparing raw counts across groups of very different sizes almost always misleads. Two-way tables like this are everywhere in service data, and reading them rate-first is what makes your process-improvement observations sound.

**Question 10 (hard) — Two-step target calculation**

A service receives 3,200 applications per month, and 15% currently arrive by post. A digital take-up plan aims to cut postal applications by 30% over the next year, with total volume unchanged. If the plan succeeds, how many applications per month will still arrive by post, and what percentage of all applications will that be?

- A) 336 applications; 10.5%
- B) 480 applications; 15%
- C) 144 applications; 4.5%
- D) 336 applications; 12%

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Two steps, and the base changes between them. Step 1: current postal volume = 15% of 3,200 = 0.15 × 3,200 = 480 per month. Step 2: the plan cuts this by 30%: reduction = 0.30 × 480 = 144, so remaining postal = 480 − 144 = 336 per month. Step 3: as a share of the unchanged total: 336 ÷ 3,200 = 0.105 = 10.5%. Option A carries all three steps correctly. Option B is the current state with no reduction applied; option C mistakes the reduction (144) for the remainder, then divides that; option D computes the volume correctly but fumbles the final percentage (336 ÷ 2,800, perhaps, from wrongly shrinking the total). The recurring trap in multi-step problems is base drift — the 30% applies to postal volume (480), not to total volume (3,200), and the final percentage divides by the total, not the postal figure. Write intermediate results down; the two seconds spent writing "480" is what makes the rest of the chain safe. Benefit projections in option analyses are built exactly like this question, so the method transfers directly.

**Question 11 (hard) — Cost comparison across options**

You are supporting an options assessment for clearing a scanning backlog of 9,000 documents. Option 1: the in-house team scans 150 documents per day at a marginal cost of £1.50 per document. Option 2: an external supplier charges a flat £11,700 and completes the whole backlog in 30 days. Option 3: a hybrid — the supplier takes 6,000 documents for £8,000, delivering them over 40 days, while in parallel the in-house team scans the remaining 3,000 at its usual per-document cost and daily rate. Which option is cheapest, and which is fastest?

- A) Cheapest: Option 2; fastest: Option 2
- B) Cheapest: Option 3; fastest: Option 3
- C) Cheapest: Option 1; fastest: Option 2
- D) Cheapest: Option 3; fastest: Option 2

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Work each option fully — cost, then time — and only then compare. Option 1: cost = 9,000 × £1.50 = £13,500; time = 9,000 ÷ 150 = 60 days. Option 2: cost = £11,700; time = 30 days. Option 3: cost = supplier £8,000 plus in-house 3,000 × £1.50 = £4,500, totalling £12,500; time = the slower of the two parallel streams — in-house needs 3,000 ÷ 150 = 20 days, but the supplier's portion takes 40 days, so the backlog is fully cleared only after 40 days. Now compare. Costs: £13,500 / £11,700 / £12,500 — Option 2 is cheapest. Times: 60 / 30 / 40 days — Option 2 is fastest too. So Option 2 wins on both dimensions: option A. The distractors reward partial work: option D is what you get if you compare only the headline supplier fees (£8,000 looks cheaper than £11,700) and forget to add the in-house £4,500; option B additionally assumes a hybrid must be fastest because two teams work at once — but parallel streams finish when the slowest finishes, and 40 days loses to 30; option C misjudges the in-house cost as lowest without computing it. Three habits from this question transfer straight into real options assessments: always total all cost components of a hybrid, always take the maximum (not the minimum) of parallel durations, and never compare options until every cell of your little cost/time table is filled in.

**Question 12 (hard) — Interpreting a trend with a definition change**

Monthly complaint volumes for a service were: January 210, February 205, March 200, April 320, May 318, June 322. A note under the chart says: "From April, the complaints definition was widened to include informal complaints raised by phone, which were previously logged separately; roughly 110 informal phone complaints were logged per month under the old system." A stakeholder says: "Complaints jumped 60% in April — the service has deteriorated badly." What is the fairest assessment?

- A) The stakeholder is right: a 60% rise is a serious deterioration.
- B) Adjusting for the definition change (about 110 informal complaints per month now included), like-for-like volumes are roughly flat — around 210 before and about 210 after — so the data shows no evidence of deterioration.
- C) The service improved, because informal complaints fell.
- D) The data proves nothing because definitions can always change.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** First verify the stakeholder's arithmetic: March 200 to April 320 is a rise of 120; 120 ÷ 200 = 60% — the calculation is right. But the note changes what the numbers mean: from April, the series includes roughly 110 informal phone complaints per month that were previously logged elsewhere. Make the comparison like-for-like by subtracting the newly included category from the later months: April 320 − 110 = 210; May 318 − 110 = 208; June 322 − 110 = 212. Set those against January-to-March's 210, 205, 200 and the underlying series is essentially flat — no evidence of deterioration, exactly as option B concludes. Option A does correct arithmetic on incomparable numbers, the classic broken-time-series error. Option C invents a finding about informal complaints that the data cannot show. Option D overcorrects into nihilism — the definition change is documented and quantified, so adjustment is possible and the data remains useful. This pattern deserves a permanent place in your toolkit: before interpreting any step-change in a time series, ask "did the measurement change?" Definition changes, system migrations, and counting-rule tweaks produce more dramatic "trends" in government data than reality usually does — and the analyst who checks the footnote before the headline protects the whole team from a false alarm.

### Preparation tips

- **Master the core methods until they are reflexes:** percentage = part ÷ whole × 100; percentage change = change ÷ original × 100; weighted average = sum of (group size × group mean) ÷ total size; rate problems = quantity ÷ time, then scale. Nearly every question above is one of these, sometimes chained.
- **Name the base aloud before every percentage.** "Thirty percent of what?" is the question that catches base drift in multi-step problems — the single biggest source of wrong answers.
- **Write intermediate results down.** In two-step problems, the number you hold in your head is the number you lose. Rough paper is a professional tool, not a crutch.
- **Sense-check against benchmarks:** halves, quarters, and tens. Does the weighted average sit between the group means, nearer the larger group? Does the answer fit inside the data's range? Two seconds of checking beats two marks lost.
- **Practise the percentage-points distinction** until you can explain it to a colleague in one sentence — teaching it is the surest sign you own it.
- **Rehearse with real work artefacts.** Take an actual defect table, survey summary, or velocity history and compute one derived figure from it each week. Real data has the messiness tests imitate, and the habit visibly improves your deliverables.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Averaging averages across different-sized groups.** If group sizes appear anywhere in the question, they belong in your calculation — compute totals first, then divide.
- **Dividing by the new value in percentage change.** The original value is always the base. "From 20 to 16" means 4 ÷ 20, never 4 ÷ 16.
- **Confusing percentages with percentage points.** "From 40% to 50%" is 10 points and 25 percent — if a question offers both as options, it is testing exactly this.
- **Comparing raw counts when rates are needed.** Bigger groups produce bigger counts of everything; divide by each group's own total before comparing.
- **Ignoring footnotes and definition changes.** A step-change in a chart with a note underneath is usually about the note, not the world. Read everything around the data before calculating anything with it.
- **Rounding too early.** Carry full precision through intermediate steps and round only at the end; early rounding compounds, and adaptive tests punish near-miss answers with plausible-looking distractors.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment measures how precisely you read, understand, and draw conclusions from written material. Each question gives you a short passage — for your role, typically a policy extract, a governance note, a stakeholder email, a research summary, or a service standard — followed by statements to evaluate or questions to answer. The dominant format asks you to judge statements as **True** (supported by the passage), **False** (contradicted by the passage), or **Cannot say** (the passage gives insufficient information either way). Supporting formats test inference (what follows from the passage), comprehension (what the passage actually instructs), and clear-writing judgement (which wording expresses a rule most clearly and accurately).

The typical format is an online, timed test of 15 to 25 minutes with 15 to 30 questions, often organised as several statements per passage. The single most important rule — and the one that decides most marks — is this: answer only from the passage. Your workplace knowledge, however accurate, is inadmissible evidence for the duration of the test. If the passage does not establish a claim, the answer is "Cannot say" even when you happen to know the claim is true in real life.

Why do employers use verbal reasoning for junior business analysts? Because your role converts language into deliverables. You capture requirements from what stakeholders said — not what they roughly meant. You read policy constraints and identify what they do and do not require. You draft user stories, acceptance criteria, model annotations, and findings summaries that other people build from. In all of that work, the gap between "must" and "should", "all" and "most", "by Friday" and "within five working days" is where defects are born. Verbal reasoning practice trains the precise-reading reflex that closes those gaps — and it trains the equally valuable writing reflex of noticing when your own drafts are ambiguous.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Precise comprehension** maps to **Requirements definition and management**: supporting the identification, capture and validation of requirements depends on extracting exactly what a source document requires, permits, and stays silent about.
- **True/False/Cannot-say discipline** maps to **Context, problem and option analysis**: contributing to option assessment means separating what the evidence establishes from what is merely plausible — the same muscle as refusing to mark an unsupported statement "True".
- **Inference** maps to **Business modelling** and **Systems analysis**: reading a procedure or specification and working out its consequences ("if incomplete applications are returned, then an application without a signature never reaches a caseworker") is how you check that a model faithfully represents its source.
- **Reading stakeholder language** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**: your level explicitly includes supporting stakeholder relationship management, and that starts with reading stakeholders' messages attentively — including their qualifications, hedges, and the things they carefully do not promise.
- **Clear-writing judgement** maps to **Testing (business analysis)** and **User experience analysis**: acceptance criteria and findings summaries must be unambiguous and accessible, because vague wording becomes defects downstream and impenetrable wording excludes readers. Choosing the clearest sentence in a test rehearses choosing the clearest sentence in your artefacts.

### Practice questions

Read each passage carefully, then answer using only what it says.

**Passage 1 (for Questions 1–3)**

"The department's accessibility policy requires all new internal digital tools to meet WCAG 2.2 level AA before launch. Tools launched before this policy took effect must be assessed within twelve months of the policy's start date, and any that fail assessment must have a remediation plan agreed within eight weeks of the assessment. The policy does not apply to third-party tools procured through the central framework, which are covered by separate contractual accessibility clauses."

**Question 1 (easy) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "A new internal digital tool may launch before meeting WCAG 2.2 level AA, provided a remediation plan is agreed."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Locate the rule that governs new tools: they must meet WCAG 2.2 level AA "before launch". The remediation-plan route belongs to a different population — tools launched before the policy took effect that then fail their retrospective assessment. The statement grafts the remediation mechanism onto new tools, creating a hybrid the passage never permits, so the passage contradicts it: False. This is a common verbal-reasoning trap structure — two rules for two different groups, and a statement that quietly swaps the groups. It is also a common real-world misreading: teams sometimes assume a remediation plan is a general licence to launch inaccessible tools, when the policy reserves it for legacy cases. When you validate requirements against policy, your first question should always be: which population does this clause actually govern?

**Question 2 (easy) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "Third-party tools procured through the central framework have no accessibility obligations."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage says the policy "does not apply" to those third-party tools — but immediately adds that they "are covered by separate contractual accessibility clauses". So obligations exist; they simply live in contracts rather than in this policy. The statement claims no obligations at all, which the final clause contradicts: False. The trap is anchoring on "does not apply" and stopping reading — the sentence's second half reverses the impression its first half creates. Sentences of the form "X is exempt from A, but covered by B" appear constantly in policy and governance documents, and the analyst who reports only the exemption gives stakeholders a dangerously incomplete picture. Read every sentence to its end before extracting a rule from it.

**Question 3 (easy) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "Most of the department's existing internal tools will fail their accessibility assessment."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage establishes a process — legacy tools must be assessed within twelve months, and failures trigger remediation plans — but says nothing about how many tools exist, how many have been assessed, or what the pass rate is or will be. A prediction about "most" tools failing is neither supported nor contradicted by a passage that is silent on outcomes: Cannot say. Note the pull of cynical plausibility: anyone who has worked with legacy tools might privately expect a high failure rate, and that expectation is precisely the kind of outside knowledge the test forbids. The professional parallel is exact — when summarising a policy for your team, the moment you find yourself writing what will probably happen rather than what the document says, you have crossed from analysis into speculation, and the summary should say so explicitly.

**Passage 2 (for Questions 4–6)**

"From: Devi (Product Manager). Subject: Re: options for the renewals notification. Thanks for the options summary — really clear. I've discussed with the service owner and we're leaning towards option 2 (email with SMS fallback), but we haven't decided. Two asks before Thursday's session: first, we need indicative costs for options 2 and 3 only — don't spend time costing option 1, as printing costs rule it out. Second, the service owner asked a good question: does option 2 work for users who have neither email nor mobile? If the answer is no, we need to know what proportion of the user base that is before we commit. Can you dig into both? Shout if Thursday is unrealistic."

**Question 4 (easy) — Comprehension**

Which pieces of work does Devi ask you to do before Thursday?

- A) Cost all three options and confirm the decision for option 2.
- B) Provide indicative costs for options 2 and 3, and investigate whether option 2 covers users with neither email nor mobile — including what proportion of users that is, if it does not.
- C) Cost option 1 and research printing suppliers.
- D) Arrange a meeting between the service owner and users without email.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The email contains exactly two asks, both explicitly flagged ("Two asks before Thursday's session"). First: indicative costs for options 2 and 3 only, with option 1 explicitly excluded ("don't spend time costing option 1"). Second: establish whether option 2 works for users with neither email nor mobile, and — conditionally — quantify that group if the answer is no. Option B captures both asks, including the conditional structure of the second. Option A invents a decision that the email explicitly withholds ("we haven't decided") and re-includes the excluded costing. Option C does the one piece of work the email ruled out. Option D fabricates an action never mentioned. The practical habit this rehearses: when a stakeholder email contains numbered or signposted asks, extract them into a checklist verbatim before starting, and preserve any conditional logic ("if no, then quantify") — flattening conditionals into unconditional tasks is how effort gets wasted.

**Question 5 (moderate) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "The decision to adopt option 2 has been made."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Devi writes: "we're leaning towards option 2... but we haven't decided." The statement claims the decision has been made; the email explicitly states it has not: False. This looks easy laid out on the page, yet it is among the most consequential misreadings in real delivery work. "Leaning towards", "minded to", "the preferred option", and "the current front-runner" all signal provisional status — and analysts who report them as decisions set teams building the wrong certainty. Notice too the evidential structure: this is False rather than Cannot say because the email directly addresses the question and answers it negatively. Reserve Cannot say for genuine silence; when the passage speaks to the point and contradicts the statement, the answer is False. Precision about decision status is part of supporting stakeholder relationship management — stakeholders trust analysts who represent their positions exactly, hedges included.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Inference**

Which of the following is the best inference from Devi's email as a whole?

- A) Cost is not a factor in this decision.
- B) The choice between options is being tested against both affordability and whether the notification reaches all users, before any commitment is made.
- C) Option 3 has already been rejected.
- D) The service owner opposes option 2.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Assemble the signals. Devi asks for costs (affordability is live), excludes option 1 on cost grounds (cost can be decisive), and relays the service owner's question about users with neither email nor mobile — with the explicit condition "before we commit" (coverage matters and commitment awaits evidence). Together these support B: the decision is being tested on affordability and reach before commitment. Option A is contradicted twice — costs are requested, and cost already eliminated option 1. Option C fails because option 3 is still being costed, which signals it remains in play. Option D over-reads the service owner's question: asking whether option 2 covers everyone is due diligence, not opposition — indeed the service owner is part of the "we" leaning towards option 2. Distinguishing a probing question from a hostile position is a stakeholder-reading skill of real value: analysts who treat every challenge as resistance misread the room, and the misreading shows in their stakeholder summaries.

**Passage 3 (for Questions 7–9)**

"Requirements sign-off procedure: A requirements baseline may be signed off only when every requirement in scope has been reviewed by the business owner and the lead developer. Requirements marked 'deferred' are excluded from the baseline and do not require review before sign-off. After sign-off, any change to a baselined requirement must go through change control; changes to deferred requirements may be made informally until they are brought into scope. Bringing a deferred requirement into scope requires review by both reviewers and re-issue of the baseline."

**Question 7 (moderate) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "A requirements baseline can be signed off even though some requirements associated with the project have not been reviewed."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** At first glance the procedure seems to demand total review — "every requirement in scope has been reviewed" — which tempts a False. But track the scope words: the review obligation covers requirements *in scope*, and deferred requirements "are excluded from the baseline and do not require review before sign-off". So a project can carry deferred requirements that remain unreviewed while the baseline is validly signed off — which is precisely what the statement describes. The passage supports it: True. The two-step reasoning here — a general rule plus an exclusion that creates a permitted case — is the everyday logic of governance documents. Analysts who read only the headline rule over-report obligations; analysts who track scope qualifiers report the rule as it actually operates. When you support requirements management within a defined scope, "in scope" is never filler — it is the boundary of every obligation in the sentence.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Applying the procedure to a scenario**

After sign-off, the business owner wants two changes: (1) reword baselined requirement R-12; (2) update deferred requirement R-30, which stays deferred. According to the procedure, what is required?

- A) Both changes must go through change control.
- B) Change 1 must go through change control; change 2 may be made informally.
- C) Both changes may be made informally since the business owner requested them.
- D) Change 2 requires review by both reviewers and re-issue of the baseline.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Route each change through the correct clause. R-12 is baselined, and "any change to a baselined requirement must go through change control" — change 1 takes the formal route. R-30 is deferred and stays deferred, and "changes to deferred requirements may be made informally until they are brought into scope" — change 2 takes the informal route. That is option B. Option A over-applies change control to a population the procedure explicitly relieves. Option C invents a requester-based exemption — the procedure attaches formality to the requirement's status, never to who asks. Option D misfires by applying the bringing-into-scope clause: that heavier process (dual review plus baseline re-issue) triggers only when a deferred requirement enters scope, and the stem says R-30 stays deferred. Questions like this rehearse a precise professional skill: procedures are routing tables, and applying them means classifying the case first, then following that case's rule — not applying the heaviest rule everywhere to be safe, which wastes effort and erodes teams' patience with governance.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Clear-writing judgement**

You are adding a note to the team wiki summarising when informal changes are allowed. Which wording is clearest and most accurate?

- A) "Informal changes are fine for anything not yet built."
- B) "You can change deferred requirements informally, but only while they stay deferred — once one comes into scope, it needs review by the business owner and lead developer and a re-issued baseline, and changes to baselined requirements always go through change control."
- C) "Modifications of a non-baselined nature may, in certain deferred circumstances, potentially be progressed absent formal change governance, subject to applicable procedural provisions."
- D) "All changes need change control."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B preserves every load-bearing element in plain words: which requirements can change informally (deferred ones), the boundary of that freedom (while they stay deferred), what ends it (coming into scope, with dual review and baseline re-issue), and the contrasting rule for baselined requirements. A colleague can act on it without reading the source. Option A substitutes a different test — "not yet built" — for the procedure's actual test of deferred status; build state and baseline state are independent, so the summary would authorise informal changes to baselined-but-unbuilt requirements, which the procedure forbids. Option C buries the meaning under hedged officialese ("potentially", "subject to applicable procedural provisions") that transmits no usable rule — the style plain-language guidance exists to eliminate. Option D is simple, memorable, and wrong: it erases the informal route the procedure deliberately provides, and over-strict summaries cause real harm by making governance look heavier than it is. Accuracy first, then plainness — clear writing is the shortest faithful statement of the rule, not the shortest statement.

**Passage 4 (for Questions 10–12)**

"Discovery findings summary (extract): We interviewed 14 small-business owners about the licence renewal process. Eleven said the renewal reminder arrives too close to the deadline; of these, six had paid a late fee in the past two years. Nine interviewees renewed online, three by phone, and two by post. Several online renewers described the payment step as confusing, and two abandoned their first attempt before completing it. Interviewees who renewed by post did not report payment difficulties. We did not ask about the application process for new licences, and these findings should not be read as covering it. Sample size and recruitment mean findings are indicative rather than statistically representative."

**Question 10 (moderate) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "A majority of the interviewees said the renewal reminder arrives too close to the deadline."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** A small calculation inside a verbal question — work it on paper. The sample is 14 interviewees; 11 said the reminder arrives too close to the deadline. A majority of 14 requires at least 8 (since 14 ÷ 2 = 7, a majority means more than 7). Eleven exceeds seven comfortably — 11 ÷ 14 ≈ 79% — so the statement is supported: True. The care point is the word "majority": it is a precise threshold claim, not a vibe, and checking it takes one line of arithmetic. Notice what you may not do: extend the finding beyond the room. Eleven of fourteen interviewees is strong qualitative signal about this sample — and the passage itself warns that findings are "indicative rather than statistically representative", so "a majority of interviewees" is supportable while "a majority of small-business owners" would be Cannot say. Reporting research within its stated limits is exactly the discipline your user experience analysis skill calls for: supporting data-informed decisions means presenting findings at the strength the data actually earns.

**Question 11 (hard) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "The late fees paid by interviewees were caused by the reminder arriving too close to the deadline."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage reports an association: eleven interviewees said the reminder arrives late, and six of those eleven had paid a late fee within two years. It nowhere asserts that the late reminder caused the fees — and mechanism-wise, late fees can arise from forgetting, cash-flow choices, disputes, or postal delays regardless of reminder timing. Nor does the passage contradict the causal claim; a late reminder plausibly contributes. Asserted by neither side, the statement is Cannot say. This is the correlation-causation boundary wearing verbal-reasoning clothes, and it is the single most common overreach in findings summaries: the slide that says "late reminders are causing late fees" makes a causal promise the interviews cannot keep. The defensible phrasing — "six of the eleven who found reminders late had also paid late fees" — keeps the association visible while letting the team investigate causation properly. Guarding that line in your own write-ups is what makes your research summaries trustworthy inputs to prioritisation rather than advocacy.

**Question 12 (hard) — Inference within stated limits**

The product manager asks: "Based on this extract, what should we look at first?" Which response best reflects the findings and their stated limits?

- A) "Redesign the postal renewal route — it is the least used channel."
- B) "The strongest signals are reminder timing and the online payment step: most interviewees found reminders too late, and the payment step confused several online renewers, two of whom abandoned. Both warrant investigation, though with 14 interviews these are indicative findings, not proof."
- C) "Fix the new licence application process, since renewals and applications are similar."
- D) "No action can be considered until we run a statistically representative survey."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B does three things the extract licenses. It ranks by signal strength: reminder timing troubled 11 of 14 — the dominant finding — and the payment step produced both described confusion and two abandoned attempts, which is behavioural evidence, stronger than opinion alone. It proposes proportionate next steps ("warrant investigation") rather than leaping to solutions. And it carries the passage's own caveat forward — indicative, not representative — without letting the caveat paralyse action. Option A misreads channel volume as a problem signal; postal renewers were few but reported no payment difficulties, and low usage of a channel is not evidence it is broken. Option C walks directly past the passage's explicit fence: "We did not ask about the application process... these findings should not be read as covering it." Option D weaponises the caveat into inaction — small-sample qualitative research is exactly how discovery legitimately identifies what to examine next, and demanding statistical proof before any investigation inverts the discovery process. The professional pattern in B is worth memorising: strongest signals first, behavioural evidence weighted above stated opinion, limits stated, action proportionate. That is a findings summary a team can steer by.

### Preparation tips

- **Read the passage once, properly, before touching the statements.** A single careful read that builds a mental map beats keyword-hunting, which is how swapped-population and reversed-clause traps catch people.
- **Track the scope words:** in scope, deferred, new, existing, before the policy, procured through the framework. Most hard True/False/Cannot-say items turn on which population a rule governs, not on what the rule says.
- **Read compound sentences to the end.** "Exempt from X, but covered by Y" reverses direction mid-sentence; extract rules only from complete sentences, never from their first halves.
- **Calibrate claim strength.** "Leaning towards" is not "decided"; "not captured properly" is not "missed"; "associated" is not "caused". Match the strength of your verdict to the strength of the words — in tests and in your own summaries.
- **Do embedded arithmetic on paper.** Majority thresholds, fractions of samples, and working-day counts hide inside verbal questions; treat them with numeric-test care rather than eyeballing.
- **Practise on live documents.** Once a week, take a real policy extract or stakeholder email and write three statements about it — one True, one False, one Cannot say. Composing the statements teaches the distinctions faster than answering them.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Marking plausible statements True.** Plausibility plus silence equals Cannot say. Your workplace experience is precisely the knowledge the test asks you to set aside.
- **Confusing False with Cannot say.** False requires the passage to contradict the statement; silence is never contradiction. Ask "does the passage speak to this at all?" before choosing between them.
- **Swapping populations.** When a passage contains rules for two groups (new and legacy, in-scope and deferred), expect at least one statement that applies group A's rule to group B. Check which group the statement is actually about.
- **Over-reading questions as positions.** A stakeholder asking "does this work for everyone?" is probing, not opposing. Inference questions reward reading people as carefully as reading rules.
- **Letting caveats swallow findings — or findings escape caveats.** "Indicative, not representative" limits how far conclusions travel; it does not delete them. The best answers carry both the finding and its boundary.
- **Spending statement-time re-reading the whole passage.** Read once well, then verify each statement against the specific sentence it depends on. Budget time per passage, and flag rather than stall.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment (SJT) presents realistic workplace scenarios and asks you to evaluate possible responses. The material is human rather than technical: a stakeholder pushes for scope, a deadline collides with quality, a discrepancy surfaces in someone's slide deck, a colleague quietly struggles. Your task takes one of a few forms: choose the **most effective** response, choose the **least effective**, rank all responses from best to worst, or rate each response's effectiveness independently.

The typical format is an online assessment of 20 to 40 minutes covering 10 to 20 scenarios, often untimed or generously timed because it measures judgement rather than speed. There is rarely a flawless option; responses are scored against a profile agreed by experienced practitioners — for your role, senior business analysts and others who know how UK government digital teams actually work. The scoring consistently rewards a recognisable set of professional behaviours: user focus, open collaboration, ownership of your outputs, honesty about problems and mistakes, proportionate escalation, and inclusion.

Why do employers use SJTs for junior business analysts? Because your level is defined by a particular balance: you receive direction from senior colleagues, yet you are responsible for the output of specific tasks, and you support stakeholder relationship management. That balance generates daily judgement calls that no technique can settle — when to resolve something yourself versus when to raise it, how to be helpful to a stakeholder without committing your team, what to do when the process and the deadline pull in opposite directions. Analysts with sound judgement at these moments become trusted with progressively bigger tasks; the SJT is a compressed rehearsal of exactly those moments. And unlike cognitive tests, SJT practice has a direct workplace payoff even when no assessment is looming: every scenario you think through carefully now is a situation you will handle faster and better when a version of it happens on a Tuesday afternoon.

One note on mindset: when your chosen answer differs from the keyed one, treat the explanation as a colleague's reasoning to compare against your own, not a verdict. Judgement grows through exactly that comparison.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Judging when to act and when to raise** maps to your role level: being **responsible for the output of specific tasks** while **receiving direction from more senior business analysts** means constantly locating the line between your call and their call — the central axis of most SJT scenarios.
- **Handling stakeholder pressure** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**: your level explicitly supports stakeholder relationship management, and scenarios test whether you can be responsive and respectful without conceding scope, misrepresenting decisions, or bypassing your team's prioritisation.
- **Protecting evidence and honesty in analysis** maps to **Requirements definition and management**, **User experience analysis** and **Testing (business analysis)**: captured requirements, research findings, and test records are only valuable while they are truthful, and several scenarios test whether you defend that truthfulness under social pressure.
- **Team behaviours in delivery** maps to **Adapting to delivery methodologies**: ceremonies, WIP limits, and iteration only work when people behave as the method assumes; scenarios about stand-ups, sprint pressure, and shortcuts test whether you understand the behavioural half of methodology.
- **Inclusion and user focus** map to **Business process improvement** and the profession's purpose: services and processes must work for everyone, and scenarios probe whether accessibility, assisted-digital users, and quiet voices stay visible when time is short.

### Practice questions

Decide your answer before reading each explanation. All options are things a real person might plausibly do — the differences lie in their consequences.

**Question 1 (easy) — The scope-expanding stakeholder**

You are responsible for capturing requirements for a small improvement to a grants system. In a review session, a stakeholder from the finance team says: "While you're at it, add a requirement for a full audit-trail export — it's basically the same work." You suspect it is substantial extra work, and it was not in the agreed scope. What is the most effective response?

- A) Add the requirement — keeping finance stakeholders happy matters most.
- B) Tell the stakeholder that scope is fixed and the request cannot be considered.
- C) Capture the request accurately as a candidate requirement, note it as outside the current agreed scope, and take it to your senior business analyst and the prioritisation discussion — telling the stakeholder that is what you are doing.
- D) Quietly investigate the export requirement for a few days before mentioning it to anyone.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C is the full professional loop: the stakeholder's need is captured faithfully (respect and good analysis), its scope status is made explicit (honesty), it is routed to the people who own prioritisation (correct authority), and the stakeholder is told the route (transparency, which protects the relationship). Option A converts a hallway remark into committed scope — the phrase "basically the same work" is how scope creep introduces itself, and accepting it silently commits colleagues who were not in the room. Option B protects scope but needlessly bruises the relationship and, worse, loses the request entirely; today's out-of-scope need is often next quarter's priority, and a junior analyst who keeps such needs visible adds real value. Option D spends your task time on unprioritised work while hiding a live request from your team — the worst of both worlds. The pattern to internalise: capture everything, commit nothing beyond your authority, route visibly. It appears in many disguises across SJTs and careers alike.

**Question 2 (easy) — Your model is challenged in public**

You produced a process model for a workshop. Mid-session, an operations team leader says, in front of everyone: "This is wrong — step four hasn't worked like that since last year." You believe you modelled it from the current procedure document. What is the most effective response?

- A) Defend the model firmly — you followed the documented procedure, so the model is correct.
- B) Thank them, say the model reflects the documented procedure, and suggest capturing how step four works in practice right now — since the gap between document and reality is itself a valuable finding.
- C) Apologise, agree the model is wrong, and offer to redo it after the workshop.
- D) Move the workshop on quickly to avoid conflict.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B reframes the moment from contest to discovery, which is precisely what it is. If the documented procedure and operational reality have diverged, that divergence is one of the most useful things a workshop can surface — arguably more useful than the model being "right". B credits the challenger's operational knowledge, stands accurately behind your own work (you modelled the document faithfully), and converts the disagreement into captured analysis on the spot. Option A defends your correctness at the cost of the finding: "the model matches the document" can be true while the model still fails to represent the business. Option C swings the other way, conceding error you have no evidence of — your model may be a faithful picture of an outdated document, which is not the same as your mistake, and reflexive apology miscalibrates the record. Option D loses the finding entirely and teaches the room that challenges are unwelcome. The underlying discipline comes straight from your business modelling skill: a model is a claim about reality, sources can be wrong, and the analyst's loyalty is to accuracy, not to their artefact.

**Question 3 (easy) — A mistake discovered after distribution**

Two days ago you circulated a requirements summary to the delivery team and two stakeholder groups. Today you realise one requirement's priority is wrong: you typed "must have" where the workshop agreed "could have". Development planning happens tomorrow. What is the most effective response?

- A) Correct the document today, reissue it to everyone with a short note saying what changed and why, and tell your senior business analyst.
- B) Say nothing — one priority among many is unlikely to matter.
- C) Mention it verbally at tomorrow's planning session if the requirement comes up.
- D) Correct the master document quietly so future readers see the right priority.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Work backwards from the consequence: a "could have" recorded as "must have" directly distorts tomorrow's planning — teams sequence and staff work from exactly these labels, so the error has a countdown attached. Option A neutralises it completely: the artefact is corrected, every recipient learns of the change before they rely on the wrong version, the note's "what and why" makes the correction auditable, and your senior colleague is never surprised by it later. Option B gambles the team's plan on "probably fine" — and priority labels are precisely the field where wrongness is expensive. Option C leaves it to chance ("if it comes up") and wastes the day of margin you currently hold. Option D fixes the future while abandoning everyone who already downloaded, printed, or copied the wrong version — silent corrections routinely produce two versions of the truth circulating simultaneously. Note what A costs: one slightly uncomfortable email. Errors handled fast, openly, and completely are how junior analysts build reputations for reliability; the analysts teams worry about are not the ones who err, but the ones who go quiet about it.

**Question 4 (easy) — The struggling ceremony**

Your team's sprint retrospectives have become a formality: the same two people talk, actions are rarely recorded, and nothing changes. You find them unhelpful, and you suspect others do too. As a junior business analyst — not the facilitator — what is the most effective response?

- A) Stop attending; retrospectives are optional value, and yours is clearly zero.
- B) Tell the delivery manager privately that retrospectives are being run badly.
- C) At the next retrospective, raise the pattern itself as an improvement item — for example, suggesting rotating facilitation, a different format, and actually tracking actions — and offer to help.
- D) Wait until someone more senior notices; process quality is not a junior analyst's concern.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C uses the ceremony to repair the ceremony — which is exactly what retrospectives are for, and a route open to every team member regardless of seniority. It converts a private frustration into a shared, actionable observation; the concrete suggestions (rotating facilitation, format change, tracked actions) give the team something to adopt rather than just a complaint; and the offer to help signals ownership, not criticism. Option B is not wrong — the delivery manager should know — but as a first move it routes around the team and casts the observation as a complaint about the facilitator rather than a pattern the whole team owns; it becomes the right second move if C changes nothing. Option A abandons a team commitment unilaterally and silently, modelling exactly the disengagement that is hollowing the ceremony. Option D misreads your role: understanding how multidisciplinary teams and their methodologies work is a named skill at your level, and iteration applies to ways of working just as much as to products. Teams improve because someone chooses to say so constructively in the room — and there is no seniority threshold on being that someone.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Research findings under pressure**

You supported a round of usability testing. The findings are mixed: users completed the new journey faster, but four of six participants failed the eligibility-check step. The product manager, preparing a show-and-tell for senior leaders, says: "Let's lead with the speed improvement and leave the eligibility thing for another day — it'll muddy the story." You drafted the findings summary. What is the most effective response?

- A) Agree — the product manager owns the narrative, and the eligibility finding will keep.
- B) Say the summary must reflect both findings; suggest framing the eligibility failure as a discovered risk with a plan ("journey is faster; eligibility step needs redesign — here's what we're doing"), and if the product manager still insists on dropping it, involve your senior business analyst before the summary is finalised.
- C) Present the full findings to the senior leaders yourself at the show-and-tell to make sure the truth comes out.
- D) Keep the eligibility finding out of the summary but mention it quietly to a developer so someone knows.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Hold on to what the artefact is: a findings summary that others will treat as the truthful record of what the research showed. Four of six participants failing a step is a material finding — arguably the material finding, since eligibility failures block real users from a service — and a summary omitting it misinforms every decision downstream. Option B defends that truthfulness while genuinely helping the product manager: the "risk with a plan" framing is both honest and a better leadership story than unblemished good news, because senior audiences trust teams that surface problems with responses attached. And B ends with the correctly scaled backstop — not a public confrontation, but your senior business analyst involved before the record ships. Option A treats narrative ownership as covering the evidence itself; the product manager owns emphasis and framing, but a junior analyst still should not sign a summary that hides what the data showed. Option C leaps to the most dramatic escalation available — hijacking a leadership meeting — before trying any collaborative route. Option D is the quiet-workaround anti-pattern: the record stays wrong while an informal whisper substitutes for the finding. Your user experience analysis skill says findings support data-informed decisions; data that gets edited for comfort stops informing anything.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Two seniors, two instructions**

Your senior business analyst asked you to spend this week detailing requirements for feature A. Today the product manager — who does not manage you but leads the product — asks you to switch immediately to analysing a data question about feature B, "because the board meeting moved to Thursday". Your senior business analyst is in workshops until tomorrow but reads messages at lunchtime. What is the most effective response?

- A) Switch to feature B — the product manager leads the product and the board meeting is a serious deadline.
- B) Continue with feature A — your senior business analyst's instruction predates the product manager's request.
- C) Tell the product manager about your current commitment, message your senior business analyst summarising the conflict and proposing a plan (for example, starting the feature B analysis now given the board deadline, resuming feature A on Friday), and proceed on the proposed plan if no objection comes by early afternoon.
- D) Split every day this week evenly between the two features without telling anyone.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The conflict is real — both instructions are legitimate, and only information can resolve them. Option C makes the conflict visible to both parties, proposes a concrete resolution rather than dumping the problem upwards, uses the known communication window (lunchtime messages), and includes a sensible default so a hard deadline is not hostage to silence. That combination — transparency, a proposal, a default with a deadline — is the mature form of managing upwards, and it is available at every seniority level. Option A resolves by rank and leaves your senior business analyst to discover on Thursday that their plan silently dissolved; option B resolves by timestamp and risks the team walking into a board meeting unprepared; both substitute a mechanical rule for judgement and both keep someone important in the dark. Option D is the worst of the set: it halves progress on both fronts, satisfies neither deadline fully, and tells nobody — invisible compromise is still concealment. Notice that C never asks you to adjudicate between seniors; it engineers the situation so the right people can adjudicate quickly. That is exactly what "responsible for the output of specific tasks" looks like when tasks collide.

**Question 7 (moderate) — The uncomfortable data point**

Preparing materials for a process improvement workshop, you find that the "average approval time: 3 days" figure the improvement case has been built on excludes cases referred for extra checks — which are a fifth of all cases and average 15 days. Including them, the true average is around 5.4 days. The improvement case was written by the head of the operations team, who is sponsoring the workshop, and the workshop is in three days. What is the most effective response?

- A) Use the 3-day figure — it is the sponsor's own number, and the workshop is about improvement anyway.
- B) Verify your calculation, then take it to your senior business analyst promptly, proposing how to raise it with the sponsor — for example, presenting both figures as "3 days for standard cases; 5.4 days overall including referred cases", which is arguably a stronger case for improvement.
- C) Present your corrected figure at the workshop without warning anyone, so the data speaks for itself.
- D) Email the sponsor's director about the misleading figure.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Sequence matters as much as substance here. First verify — exclusion of a case category is sometimes a documented, defensible definition ("standard-track approval time") rather than an error, and your recalculation could itself contain a slip; checking first is analytical humility. Then route through your senior business analyst, who knows the sponsor and the history, with a proposal rather than a problem: the both-figures framing respects the sponsor's number while completing it, and — the persuasive touch — a 5.4-day reality makes the case for improvement stronger, which gives the sponsor a reason to welcome the correction. Option A knowingly builds a workshop on a figure you believe materially misleading; once seen, that cannot be unseen, and your analysis rests on it. Option C is theatrically honest but relationally reckless: ambushing a sponsor with their own error in front of their workshop maximises defensiveness and burns the relationship the improvement work depends on. Option D escalates over two heads on an unverified discrepancy — maximal damage, minimal diligence. The principle, worth keeping for decades: never suppress a data problem, and never weaponise one. Verify, route through your line, and wherever possible hand the owner a graceful way to be more right.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Accessibility versus the deadline**

Sprint review is tomorrow, and the team wants to demonstrate a new document-upload feature. Testing it this afternoon, you notice it cannot be operated by keyboard alone — the upload button only responds to mouse clicks, which will exclude users who cannot use a mouse. The acceptance criteria for the story do not mention keyboard access, though your organisation's standards require WCAG compliance. The developers are keen to demo and say: "Log it as a nice-to-have for later." What is the most effective response?

- A) Agree it is a nice-to-have, since the acceptance criteria passed.
- B) Record the criteria as passed, but log the keyboard failure as a defect against the organisation's accessibility standard — not a nice-to-have — flag the gap in the acceptance criteria to your senior business analyst, and suggest the demo mention the known issue.
- C) Insist the demo is cancelled until keyboard access works.
- D) Say nothing now and raise keyboard access at the retrospective.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Two records must both stay truthful, and option B keeps them so. The acceptance criteria, as written, passed — recording otherwise corrupts the test record. But the feature genuinely violates the organisation's accessibility standard, and severity is set by the applicable standard and the users excluded, not by whether someone remembered to write a criterion: keyboard operability is a hard requirement (WCAG), and "nice-to-have" mislabels a compliance defect. B also closes the loop that caused the miss — the criteria gap goes to your senior business analyst so future stories include accessibility criteria — and the suggestion to mention the known issue at the demo keeps the team's showcase honest without cancelling it. Option A lets the paperwork's silence overrule the standard; criteria are the team's current best statement of "done", not a ceiling on your professional attention. Option C overshoots: a demo of work-in-progress with a disclosed known issue is normal and honest, and unilaterally demanding cancellation exceeds both the need and your role. Option D parks a compliance defect for the ceremony calendar. The transferable rule: pass the letter, log the truth, fix the gap — and never let "not in the criteria" become "not a problem".

**Question 9 (moderate) — The colleague who asks you to bend a record**

A developer you work well with asks you to mark two requirements as "validated with business owner" in the traceability matrix. "She's on leave until Monday, but she'll definitely agree — and if those rows aren't green, the release review on Friday will make us re-plan for nothing." You maintain the matrix. What is the most effective response?

- A) Mark them validated — the business owner almost certainly will agree, and blocking the release over paperwork helps nobody.
- B) Refuse and report the developer to the delivery manager for attempting to falsify records.
- C) Decline to mark them validated, but engage the real problem: check whether validation can genuinely happen before Friday (a deputy, a documented email approval, a brief call), and if not, tell your senior business analyst so the review hears an honest status with a plan rather than a red surprise.
- D) Mark them "validated" but keep a private note that they are not really.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Strip the situation to its core: you are being asked to record something that has not happened, in an artefact whose entire value is that it records what happened. Option C declines that cleanly — no false green — while taking the developer's underlying problem seriously, which deserves it: an unnecessary re-plan is a real cost, and the pressure is understandable even though the proposed shortcut is not. Checking for a legitimate fast path (deputies, documented approval, a short call with the owner if she is reachable) frequently dissolves these dilemmas entirely; and if not, routing an honest "two validations outstanding, plan attached" into the review through your senior colleague converts a falsification request into managed risk. Option A trades the matrix's integrity for schedule comfort — and "she'll definitely agree" is a prediction, not a validation; the day a prediction is wrong is the day the matrix's every green cell becomes questionable. Option B leaps to the most adversarial reading and torches a good working relationship over what was likely deadline panic, skipping the collegial correction that would fix it. Option D is the strangest failure: the official record lies while a private note preserves your conscience — records exist for the team, not for you. Traceability is only worth maintaining truthfully; that is the whole of your testing-support skill in one sentence.

**Question 10 (hard) — Least effective: the slipping deadline**

You are responsible for delivering a business process analysis by Friday to feed a procurement decision. On Tuesday you realise a data source you need is unavailable until Thursday, making Friday extremely tight and possibly impossible. Which response would be **least effective**?

- A) Tell your senior business analyst today about the data delay, with options: a Friday version flagged as provisional pending Thursday's data, a Monday delivery, or descoping one process area.
- B) Ask whether anyone knows an alternative source or extract for the same data that is available sooner.
- C) Work quietly and intensely, compress the analysis into Thursday night, and deliver something on Friday without mentioning the problem to anyone.
- D) Check with the procurement lead whether Friday is a hard gate or whether Monday with complete data would serve the decision better.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Three of these options are the textbook toolkit for a slipping deadline, which is what makes the question hard — you are hunting the odd one out. Option A is early, honest escalation with options attached, the gold standard. Option B attacks the constraint itself; alternative sources often exist and asking costs minutes. Option D tests whether the deadline is real — deadlines sometimes have more flex than the calendar suggests, and "complete on Monday versus provisional on Friday" is exactly the trade-off the decision owner should choose. Option C is least effective on every axis that matters: it conceals a known risk from everyone who could help or adjust (the opposite of ownership, which is accountability plus transparency, not silent heroics); it stakes a procurement decision on a single compressed overnight analysis — the conditions under which analytical errors thrive; and it forecloses every option that A, B and D would have opened, because nobody can help with a problem they learn about after delivery. If Thursday night goes wrong, the team discovers on Friday that they have neither the analysis nor the time to react. The reflex to build: the moment a deadline risk becomes visible, its existence stops being your private information.

**Question 11 (hard) — Ranking responses: the workshop that excludes**

You are co-facilitating a requirements workshop for a service used heavily by people with limited digital access. Reviewing the invite list the day before, you notice every attendee is a policy or digital professional — no operational staff who serve users face-to-face, and no user representation of any kind. The workshop is tomorrow; your senior business analyst set the list. Rank these responses from most to least effective:

1. Raise it with your senior business analyst today: note the gap, and propose either a quick addition (an operational team member who could attend tomorrow) or an explicit plan to validate tomorrow's outputs with operational staff and user research afterwards.
2. Say nothing before the workshop, but during it, repeatedly challenge attendees' assumptions by asking "how do we know users want this?"
3. Add two operational staff to the invitation yourself this afternoon without consulting anyone.
4. Run the workshop as planned and never revisit the gap.

- A) 1, 2, 3, 4
- B) 1, 3, 2, 4
- C) 3, 1, 2, 4
- D) 1, 2, 4, 3

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Anchor the extremes first. Response 1 is clearly best: it surfaces the gap to the list's owner while there is still time, and offers two proportionate fixes — patch tomorrow, or validate afterwards — leaving the decision with the senior colleague who owns it. Response 4 is clearly worst... check it against response 3 before settling. Response 3 pursues the right value (representation) by overriding your senior colleague's decision in their own workshop without a word — it creates surprise attendees, possible facilitation problems, and a trust breach, though the workshop does gain operational voices. Response 4 delivers requirements for a limited-digital-access service built entirely on professionals' assumptions, unvalidated forever — the analytical harm is permanent and invisible, which is worse than 3's recoverable relational harm. So 4 ranks last, and 3 third. That leaves response 2 second: it is a weak half-measure — challenge-by-question during the session cannot substitute for absent knowledge, and springing it repeatedly may sour the room — but it keeps the gap visible, harms no relationship, and can seed the after-the-fact validation that response 1 would have planned properly. Order: 1, 2, 3, 4 — option A. Two lessons ride along: representation gaps are analysis defects, not just fairness concerns, because rooms full of similar professionals converge confidently on wrong requirements; and the response hierarchy runs raise-and-propose, then keep-visible, then unilateral action, then silence.

**Question 12 (hard) — Competing goods: mentoring versus delivery**

A trainee business analyst joined your team three weeks ago. Your senior business analyst asked you to "keep an eye on them" alongside your own full workload. Today the trainee brings you their first solo process model, visibly proud. It has real problems — inconsistent notation, a missing decision point, two steps in the wrong order — and they are due to present it to the operations manager tomorrow morning. You have a requirements deadline of your own tomorrow. What is the most effective response?

- A) Fix the model yourself tonight and send it back — fastest for everyone, and the presentation will go well.
- B) Tell them it needs work and list the problems by email, then return to your own deadline.
- C) Spend a focused half-hour with them now: walk through one or two problems together so they learn the checking method, let them fix the rest themselves this afternoon, offer a quick end-of-day review, and mention to your senior business analyst that you are balancing this against your deadline.
- D) Tell them it is fine as it is — confidence matters most at trainee level, and the operations manager will forgive a rough first model.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Two goods compete — the trainee's development and presentation tomorrow, your deadline tomorrow — and option C serves both with the scarce resource, your time, spent where it teaches most. Walking through one or two problems transfers the checking method (notation consistency, reading branches aloud, tracing sequence), which is worth more than any number of corrected diagrams; letting them fix the remainder converts your review into their skill; the end-of-day check protects tomorrow's presentation; and the note to your senior colleague keeps the workload trade-off visible — important, since "keep an eye on them" was a delegated responsibility, and its cost should not be invisible. Option A produces a good model and no growth: the trainee presents work they did not do and cannot defend under questioning — a small humiliation waiting to happen — and learns mainly that finished work gets taken away and fixed elsewhere. Option B is honest but cold arithmetic: a list of faults without method, landing on a proud three-week trainee the evening before their first stakeholder presentation, teaches anxiety rather than checking — and email is the wrong medium for a first hard feedback moment. Option D is kindness inverted: it protects this afternoon's feelings by spending tomorrow's credibility, theirs and the team's, in front of the operations manager. Developing others is not a distraction from a junior analyst's job; done as in C, it is the job, scaled to your level — and it is how you will one day supervise well.

### Preparation tips

- **Learn the scoring compass.** Government digital SJTs consistently reward user focus, truthful records, early honest escalation with options, proportionate responses, inclusion, and collaboration over heroics. Write these six down; after each practice question, identify which ones the best option served.
- **Locate the authority line in every scenario.** Ask "whose call is this?" before choosing. Most wrong answers either seize a decision above your level or abandon one that was genuinely yours.
- **Prefer options that engage the underlying problem.** The best responses usually decline the bad request and address the need behind it — the deadline, the fear, the missing validation. Refusal alone is second-rate; capitulation is worse.
- **In "least effective" questions, hunt the concealment.** The worst option is very often the one where information stays hidden — silent fixes, quiet workarounds, unmentioned risks — rather than the loudest or rudest one.
- **In ranking questions, anchor the extremes first,** then compare the middle pair by consequences: which harms are permanent, which recoverable? Analytical harms to decisions often outlast relational bruises.
- **Debrief real situations weekly.** Take one genuine workplace moment, list three responses you could have made, and rank them against the compass. SJT skill is judgement, and judgement compounds through honest reflection on your own Tuesdays.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Choosing by seniority instead of by information.** "Do what the most senior person says" fails whenever that person lacks context. The strong options make conflicts and evidence visible so the right person can decide well.
- **Confusing loyalty to colleagues with loyalty to records.** Helping a teammate never legitimately requires a false status, a hidden finding, or a bent matrix. The team is served by truthful artefacts, not by comfortable ones.
- **Over-escalating as a first move.** Jumping to directors, sponsors, or public forums before trying direct conversation and your own line management reads as poor judgement even when the concern is valid.
- **Under-escalating known risks.** The mirror error: sitting on a slipping deadline, a data discrepancy, or a compliance defect because raising it feels awkward. Awkward early beats catastrophic late, every time.
- **Solving people's problems instead of building their capability.** Doing the trainee's work, silently absorbing impossible workloads, and heroic overnight fixes score poorly because they hide problems and stunt growth — including yours.
- **Forgetting who the service is for.** When a scenario contains excluded users, missing voices, or accessibility gaps, the scoring almost certainly turns on whether your chosen option keeps them visible. In government, the user is the point.
