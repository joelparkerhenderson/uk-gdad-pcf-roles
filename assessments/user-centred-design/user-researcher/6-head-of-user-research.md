# Head of User Research - Psychometric Assessment Resources

## Introduction

Welcome. This guide is written for you as a head of user research in the UK Government Digital and Data profession — the person who leads user researchers in an organisation, attracts and builds talent, defines and assures best practice as an expert practitioner, influences organisational strategy and priorities, and collaborates with colleagues across government. It is a role in which most of what you do is exercised through other people, and in which your own hands-on research is now the smallest part of your contribution.

You may be approaching a formal assessment as part of a selection process, or you may be here because reasoning that has become second nature is worth periodically re-examining. Both are good reasons, and at your level the second may be the more valuable. The risk for someone with your experience is not gaps in technique. It is that technique has become automatic, automatic reasoning feels certain, and certainty is where the expensive errors live — particularly in a role where your judgement is rarely checked by anyone before it reaches a decision.

Four features of the role shape everything that follows. First, you are accountable for the quality of research produced by people you did not train, on services you have not seen, using evidence you will never inspect directly. That makes diagnostic reasoning — inferring the shape of a study from the traces it leaves in an output — a core competence rather than an occasional one. Second, you produce strategic insight by synthesising across programmes, which means holding several incomplete and partly contradictory pictures in mind long enough to notice what they jointly imply. Third, you negotiate with people who substantially outrank you, in forums where research is one input among many and rarely the loudest. Your influence rests almost entirely on your evidence being unassailable and your judgement being trusted. Fourth, you build capability: the profession you leave behind matters more than any study you assure.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the reasoning your role demands, presented through practical materials rather than abstract puzzles. At head-of-profession level the materials are organisational and the reasoning is almost entirely diagnostic and strategic. You will meet evidence assembled by other people for other purposes, standards applied unevenly across an organisation, findings that appear to conflict until you establish what each measured, and prioritisation problems where the units are programmes and capabilities rather than tasks.

The format is typically an online test of fifteen to thirty minutes covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective against a norm group of comparable seniority. Adaptive platforms raise difficulty after correct answers, and most employers receive an acuity report distinguishing speed from accuracy, which rewards precision over pace.

Three things characterise this section at your level.

The first is reasoning at a distance. You almost never see raw data. You see a report, a slide, a claim in a board paper, a summary of a summary — and you must judge from the visible traces whether the reasoning underneath holds. Reading an output for the shape of the study behind it is a specific and trainable skill, and several items below exercise it.

The second is that the interesting questions are usually about mechanisms rather than instances. When something goes wrong repeatedly, the informative feature is the distribution of the failures. Concentrated in one team, under one condition, among experienced people? That is a process telling you it cannot be complied with. Scattered randomly? That is something else entirely. Reading distributions diagnostically is what separates organisational leadership from very senior practice.

The third is that prioritisation is genuinely hard because nothing dominates. Programmes differ in time horizon, reversibility, population affected and political weight, and there is no formula. The discriminating factor is whether you reason from value of information — how much does a decision change as a result of what research says, and is there still time — rather than from importance, urgency or visibility.

You need to memorise nothing. What you need is the discipline not to let fluency substitute for examination.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Auditing against a standard**

Your organisational standard requires every research output to state: research questions, method, sample composition including access needs, analysis approach, limitations, and date. An output states the research questions, the method, the analysis approach and the date, describes its sample as "18 participants recruited through the central supplier", and has no limitations section. How many requirements are unmet?

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Four requirements are met: research questions, method, analysis approach and date. The sample description names a count and a source but says nothing about composition or access needs, so that requirement fails. Limitations are absent, so that fails too — two unmet, giving B. Option A usually comes from accepting the sample sentence, and that is the error worth dwelling on: "18 from the central supplier" describes provenance, not composition, and the standard exists precisely to prevent provenance being offered in place of it. At the scale you operate, note which requirements failed, because it is never random. Access needs and limitations are the two most commonly omitted fields in almost every organisation, which tells you where to point an assurance sample and what to fix in the template rather than in the individual report.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction from a governance rule**

Your standard states: "Research with participants who may be considered vulnerable requires an approved safeguarding protocol, unless the research is conducted entirely through an organisation covered by a standing partnership agreement." A directorate reports: "Our study with people experiencing homelessness ran last quarter without an approved protocol." Which must be true?

- A) The standard was breached.
- B) The study ran through a standing partnership organisation.
- C) Either the study ran entirely through a standing partnership organisation, or the standard was breached.
- D) Participants were exposed to risk.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A conditional with a stated exception, plus facts silent on the exception, licenses only the disjunction. Options A and B each assume a branch without evidence. Option D is the distractor built for someone with your responsibilities, because it is where professional instinct goes — but proceeding without a protocol is a governance failure, and whether anyone was actually exposed to risk is a separate empirical question these facts do not settle. Both matter and they are not the same, and conflating them in an assurance conversation weakens your position when the directorate can show that safeguards were in fact in place informally. Only C holds in every case. Note "entirely" in the exception: partial routing through a partnership does not qualify, and partial compliance is the assumption teams most often make.

**Question 3 (moderate) — Reading a study from its output**

A report states: "The service is trusted. All 22 participants said they were confident their data would be handled properly." The report notes recruitment from the departmental panel, sessions held in departmental offices, and the confidence question asked by the facilitator as the closing item. What is the most important observation?

- A) Twenty-two participants is too small a base for a trust measure.
- B) Panel recruitment, departmental setting and a direct closing question from the facilitator all bias in the same direction towards socially desirable responding, so unanimity is evidence about the conditions rather than about the service.
- C) Confidence about data handling is not the same construct as trust.
- D) A validated trust scale should have been used.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Unanimity is itself the alarm, and the strongest observation explains it. Each feature alone would warrant caution: a departmental panel selects for people already well disposed, a government building primes deference, and a direct question from the facilitator at the close invites the answer the participant believes is wanted. Because all three push the same way they compound rather than partially offset, and twenty-two out of twenty-two is close to what the conditions predict irrespective of the service. Option C is a legitimate secondary point and does not explain the inflation. Option A misdiagnoses validity as size — 220 participants under identical conditions would produce the same result. Option D leaves all three biases untouched. When this feeds back, name the compounding: fixing one factor changes nothing, and a directorate that swaps in a validated scale while keeping the panel and the setting will produce the same finding with better packaging.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Strategic prioritisation across programmes**

You can direct substantial additional research capacity to one of four programmes this quarter:

1. Programme A chooses a service model next month, committing five years of build.
2. Programme B runs a live service with a persistent 30% drop-off affecting 400,000 users annually.
3. Programme C launches in five weeks with designs already signed off.
4. Programme D is preparing a spending review submission due in eight months.

Which is strongest?

- A) B, because it affects the most users.
- B) A, because the decision is imminent, commits five years, and research is the only input that could still change it — giving it much the highest value of information.
- C) D, because spending review submissions shape long-term investment.
- D) C, because launching untested carries reputational risk.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Value of information is the right frame: how much does the decision change as a result of what research says, and is there time for it to? Programme A is highest on both counts — a service model choice is precisely where research shifts answers rather than decorating them, the window is open now, and five years of commitment makes a wrong choice enormously expensive. Programme B affects more users but has been stable for months and will remain so for three more; deferral costs comparatively little and analytics may be the better first instrument. Programme C is weakest despite sounding prudent: signed-off designs five weeks from launch means findings arrive with nowhere to go, which is research for reassurance. Programme D has eight months, and the profile trap at your level takes exactly this form — spending review work attracts attention, and attention is not decision leverage. Being able to say that to a director without sounding dismissive is the harder half.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Cross-programme synthesis**

Three unrelated items reach you in a month. A discovery in one programme finds people struggle to prove identity without a passport or driving licence. Analytics in a second show 45% drop-off at identity verification. The contact centre reports identity queries as its highest call driver across five services. What is the strongest synthesis?

- A) Three teams have found usability problems at their identity steps.
- B) The organisation has a cross-cutting identity assurance problem concentrated among people without common photographic documents — a strategic capability question for the roadmap, not five separate service fixes.
- C) Identity verification is hard and should be simplified.
- D) The three findings are unrelated and belong in separate reports.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The synthesis that adds value identifies a shared mechanism and reframes the unit of the problem. Five services showing difficulty at the same step, with a discovery pointing at document availability as the cause and contact centre volume confirming scale, indicates something structural rather than five coincidental interface issues. Option B also draws the consequence: this belongs on a roadmap as a capability question, not in five backlogs where each team builds its own partial workaround — which is both wasteful and, more importantly, leaves the excluded population excluded five times over. Option A describes without synthesising. Option C is true and strategically useless, since "simplify it" instructs nobody. Option D misses the pattern. Producing strategic insight from research programmes is central to your role, and this is its characteristic shape: several teams independently describing one thing.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Scope of a claim heading for a roadmap**

An organisational insight report drawing on fourteen studies contains the headline "users across the organisation's services want proactive notification". The supporting material comes from four studies, all of services with statutory deadlines. What is the most useful intervention?

- A) Remove the claim.
- B) Note that the evidence comes exclusively from deadline-driven services, so the claim over-reaches as written; narrow it to that service type, or test whether it holds where there is no deadline, before it informs investment.
- C) Commission more studies.
- D) Accept it; four studies is a reasonable base.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The evidence is real and the sentence built on it reaches further than the evidence goes — which is the single most common failure in organisational synthesis and the one with the largest financial consequences, because roadmap claims justify investment. Deadline-driven services supply an obvious and specific reason to want proactive notification: you are at risk of missing something. That motivation may not transfer to services used once, voluntarily, or where nothing can be missed. Option B preserves the finding, corrects the scope, and offers two proportionate routes. Option A discards something real. Option D lets an over-generalised claim justify spending across services it was never evidenced for. Option C reaches for the expensive remedy before the free one; narrowing the claim costs nothing.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Diagnosing a systemic problem**

Eight teams across your organisation consistently report recruitment producing digitally confident participants in work, despite services serving many who are neither. All eight use the central recruitment contract. What is the strongest diagnosis and response?

- A) Each team should supplement locally.
- B) The contract's supply model is structurally mismatched to the organisation's user base; document the pattern across all eight teams, specify what suitable supply would look like, take it to the contract owner, and enable partnership routes in the meantime.
- C) The teams need better screeners.
- D) Recruitment should be brought in-house.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Eight teams with the same problem and the same supplier is one supply problem, and diagnosing at the right level is the whole point of your role. Option B does the systemic work — evidence across teams is what moves a contract, and a specification is what an owner can act on — while unblocking teams immediately so eight studies are not held hostage to a procurement timeline. Option A accepts the structural failure and distributes its cost across every team indefinitely, which is how organisations end up with eight private workarounds and no institutional memory of why. Option C misdiagnoses: a screener filters the pool the supplier provides and cannot conjure a participant type the supply does not contain. Option D may eventually be right and is a large claim to make before specifying the requirement and testing whether the existing route can meet it. The generalisable pattern: when the same problem appears across many teams, the fix almost never belongs in the teams.

**Question 8 (harder) — Scoping a flaw**

A study you are assuring used a prototype whose save-and-return function was broken throughout. Four claims: (i) participants could not locate the eligibility criteria; (ii) participants abandoned when interrupted; (iii) sessions ran longer than expected; (iv) participants misread the declaration. Which are compromised?

- A) All four.
- B) None; the fault was incidental.
- C) Claims (ii) and (iii) are directly compromised, since inability to save and return plausibly produced both the abandonment on interruption and the extended durations, while (i) and (iv) concern comprehension of content and are largely unaffected.
- D) Only claim (ii).

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The skill is scoping precisely rather than treating a flaw as total or trivial. A broken save function makes interruption catastrophic rather than inconvenient, so claim (ii) is manufactured by the artefact; it also removes a natural pause, plausibly extending sessions, so (iii) is compromised too. Claims (i) and (iv) concern locating and understanding content, which the fault does not touch. Option A discards two sound findings and, at organisational scale, does real damage: a leadership that condemns wholesale teaches teams that any admitted fault invalidates everything, which is a direct incentive to conceal faults. Option B leaves two artefactual findings circulating. Option D catches the obvious compromise and misses the duration one. An audit that condemns everything is as useless as one that condemns nothing, because neither tells anyone what they can rely on.

**Question 9 (harder) — Apparent contradiction between programmes**

Two teams researching adjacent services reach opposite conclusions about the same user group: one reports a preference for completing in a single sitting, the other a preference for saving and returning. Both studies appear sound. What is strongest?

- A) Commission a third study.
- B) Establish what differs between the contexts before treating it as a contradiction — a preference expressed about a fifteen-minute task and one about a three-week process involving third-party documents are answers to different questions, and the synthesis is that preference depends on whether the task can be completed with information the user already holds.
- C) Report both and note the inconsistency.
- D) Treat the larger study as authoritative.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Apparent contradictions between sound studies are usually context differences wearing the costume of disagreement, and the diagnostic move is to ask what each participant was actually responding to. Option B reaches the synthesis, and note that the synthesis is more useful than either original finding — it gives every service in the organisation a rule for deciding which pattern applies to them, which neither study alone could. Option A spends money answering a question that dissolves on inspection. Option C abdicates precisely the synthesis your role exists to produce, and "note the inconsistency" is how genuine insight gets filed as a caveat. Option D picks a winner on an irrelevant criterion. This shape recurs constantly in cross-programme work: the insight is in the difference between contexts rather than in either result.

**Question 10 (harder) — Reading the distribution of failures**

Your standard requires ethics review for research with people who may be considered vulnerable. Across the year, fifteen of nineteen qualifying studies had review; the four that did not were all in one directorate, all under ministerial deadline pressure, all by experienced researchers. What is the strongest response?

- A) Report the breaches and require retrospective review.
- B) Treat the concentration as diagnostic: four failures in one directorate under the same pressure by people who know the requirement indicates a review process that cannot be complied with under deadline conditions — investigate what it demands and how long it takes, build a fast route, and address the specific breaches alongside.
- C) Remind all researchers of the requirement.
- D) Introduce a mandatory pre-fieldwork checkpoint for every study.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The distribution is the most informative feature in the data. Scattered breaches by different people would indicate conduct; four concentrated breaches under identical pressure by experienced people indicate a process that does not survive a deadline. Option B investigates the mechanism and fixes it, while the closing clause preserves the point that a systemic diagnosis is not a reason to leave individual failures unaddressed — participants may have been affected, and that requires its own answer. Option A treats the instances and guarantees recurrence. Option C is the weakest response available to any compliance failure: people who knew the rule and broke it under pressure will not be changed by hearing it again. Option D imposes friction on fifteen compliant studies to catch four, which is exactly how organisational burden accumulates and how the next workaround is invented — and at your level you are the person who will be asked, in two years, why research governance is so slow.

**Question 11 (harder) — Evidence for an investment decision**

The organisation is deciding whether to invest in shared identity capability. Available: qualitative findings from three programmes; analytics showing high identity drop-off across five services; a supplier claim of 94% first-time verification success; a contact centre cost model. What is your most rigorous contribution?

- A) Present the internal evidence supporting investment.
- B) Set out what each source establishes and its limits — including that the supplier's 94% requires interrogation of its denominator and population before it can sit alongside internal drop-off data — and name the one or two unknowns that would most change the decision.
- C) Recommend investment on the converging evidence.
- D) Recommend further research before deciding.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Your contribution at an investment decision is neither advocacy nor delay; it is making the evidence base legible so the decision is taken knowingly. Option B does three things. It states what each source supports, preventing the common failure in which converging evidence is treated as stronger than the sum of its parts — three sources sharing a blind spot converge on the same error. It flags the supplier figure specifically, because a vendor's 94% is very likely measured on users who presented acceptable documents, which excludes precisely the population driving your drop-off, and comparing the two directly would be a serious error with a large price tag. And it names the residual uncertainty, which tells a board what its decision actually rests on. Option A is advocacy dressed as evidence. Option C may reach the right answer on an unexamined basis. Option D defers a decision the evidence may already support, and heads of profession who reflexively call for more research find themselves excluded from decisions.

**Question 12 (harder) — A pattern in your own organisation**

Reviewing a year of outputs, you find that studies from two teams are consistently strong and studies from six are consistently weak. The two strong teams both contain researchers who came from the same previous employer. What is the most useful inference and action?

- A) The six weaker teams need training.
- B) Something transferable is likely operating in the two strong teams — a routine, a habit, a standard applied before circulation — and the highest-value action is to find out what it actually is by examining how they work, then build it into the organisation's standards and community of practice rather than attributing the difference to individual talent.
- C) Recruit more people from that employer.
- D) Increase assurance scrutiny on the six weaker teams.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The pattern is an opportunity to identify a practice, not to grade people. Researchers who bring consistently better outputs are almost always carrying a routine — a peer review habit, an analysis protocol, a rule about how claims are written, a check applied before anything circulates — and routines transfer in a way that ability does not. Option B goes and looks, which is the step almost everyone skips in favour of an assumption. Option A posits a knowledge deficit without diagnosing one, and training that does not target the real mechanism reliably fails to change practice. Option C is an unusable inference from a handful of people, and it also misattributes a practice to a pedigree. Option D catches more problems and creates no capacity to prevent them, while consuming your assurance capacity indefinitely. You lead a community of practice precisely so that discoveries like this can propagate — a naturally occurring comparison telling you what good looks like in your own context is the best raw material it will ever get.

### Preparation tips

- **Read outputs for the study behind them.** Practise inferring recruitment, setting and question order from what a report says and omits.
- **Scope flaws precisely.** Name which claims a fault compromises and which survive; wholesale condemnation incentivises concealment.
- **Ask who could not have appeared in this data.** The highest-yield question available against any evidence.
- **Read the distribution of failures.** Concentrated non-compliance diagnoses a process; scattered non-compliance diagnoses conduct.
- **Frame prioritisation as value of information.** Decision leverage and remaining time, never importance or profile.
- **Practise synthesis deliberately.** Take three unrelated pieces of evidence from this month and ask what single thing they might all describe.
- **Interrogate every vendor figure for population and denominator.** They are almost never comparable to internal metrics as stated.
- **Go and look before inferring a cause.** Especially when the inference flatters an assumption you already hold.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Accepting provenance as composition.** "Eighteen from the central supplier" says nothing about who they were.
- **Treating converging evidence as automatically stronger.** Shared blind spots converge too.
- **Reaching for training as the answer to a systemic problem.** Diagnose the mechanism first.
- **Reminding people of rules they knowingly broke.** The weakest available compliance response.
- **Adding friction for the compliant majority.** How burden accumulates and workarounds are born.
- **Picking a winner between contradictory studies.** The insight is usually in the difference between contexts.
- **Filing a contradiction as a caveat.** That is where organisational insight goes to die.
- **Letting fluency substitute for examination.** At your level the costly errors are the confident ones.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A numeric reasoning assessment measures how accurately you handle quantitative material. At your level the emphasis is almost wholly on interrogation. You will need to compute correctly, but the questions that matter ask whether a figure means what somebody claims — and in most rooms you enter, you are the only person who will ask.

The format is typically a timed online test of fifteen to thirty minutes, each item presenting a table, chart or paragraph of figures with a multiple-choice question. Calculators are usually provided and the arithmetic is deliberately tractable. Time per item is tight, so the operative skill is locating what matters and discarding what does not.

The professional stake is large. Quantitative evidence carries unearned authority: a number in a board paper is a fact, a qualitative finding is a view, regardless of the rigour behind either. The figures you encounter are increasingly consequential and increasingly detached from anyone who could explain them — business cases, vendor claims, benefits realisation figures, performance metrics that have been copied between documents for two years without anyone re-checking the definition. Being the person who asks what the denominator is, which population it represents and how the metric is defined is a substantial part of what your role contributes. Influencing senior stakeholders very often reduces to precisely that question, asked calmly, while a decision is moving.

Four habits carry most of the load. Establish what is being asked before looking at the data. Identify the denominator and the population it represents before calculating. Estimate, calculate, compare. And sanity-check against the world — a figure implying that 40% of users complete a form in under three seconds is measuring something other than what it claims.

Distractors come from a predictable catalogue: wrong denominator, percentage confused with percentage point, new value as the base in a percentage change, rates averaged without weighting, vendor metrics compared with internal metrics. Recognising your answer among the options proves nothing; knowing the catalogue lets you see the trap being set.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The numerical skills tested map onto your named skills:

- **Interrogating quantitative claims** maps to **Research management, leadership and assurance**, where you assure research quality across the organisation.
- **Aggregating across programmes** maps to synthesising findings from research programmes into strategic insight.
- **Comparing rates between user groups** maps to **Inclusive research**, where gaps between groups are the first visible trace of exclusion.
- **Judging sampling adequacy** maps to **User research methods**, where you help the organisation adopt and improve its methods.
- **Presenting figures at defensible strength** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**, where you influence and negotiate with senior stakeholders.
- **Detecting misleading metrics** maps to **User-centred practice and advocacy**, where you ensure senior discussions consider user needs — which requires the numbers in those discussions to mean what they appear to.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Rate from counts**

Across a programme, 45 of 60 participants completed the core task unaided. What percentage is that?

- A) 66%
- B) 72%
- C) 75%
- D) 80%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 45 ÷ 60 = 0.75, so 75%, and the answer is C. Estimate first: 45 of 60 is three quarters, since 30 would be half. Options A and B come from dividing by 68 and 62. Worth noting even here that aggregating 60 participants across a programme into one rate is meaningful only if the studies tested comparable tasks with comparable populations. If they did not, the figure is arithmetically correct and referentially empty — which is a distinction you will spend a good deal of your time enforcing in other people's work, and occasionally in your own summaries.

**Question 2 (easy) — Over-recruitment at scale**

Organisational no-show rate averages 20%. A programme needs 96 completed sessions. How many bookings?

- A) 112
- B) 115
- C) 120
- D) 124

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** If 20% do not attend, 80% do, so bookings must satisfy 0.8 × bookings = 96, giving 96 ÷ 0.8 = 120, and the answer is C. The common error adds 20% to 96, producing 115.2 and pointing at B — an underestimate, because the percentage applies to the larger booking figure. Verify: 20% of 120 is 24, and 120 − 24 = 96. The organisational consequence is worth stating: systematic under-recruitment does not distribute evenly across a sample, because the quotas that fail first are the hardest to fill — participants with access needs and from under-represented groups — which is exactly the composition your standards require and exactly the failure your standards were written to prevent.

**Question 3 (easy) — Disaggregated rates**

| Group | Participants | Completed |
|---|---|---|
| Digitally confident | 40 | 36 |
| Low digital confidence | 24 | 12 |
| Assistive technology users | 16 | 4 |

What is the overall rate, and the gap between highest and lowest group rates?

- A) 60% overall; 50 point gap
- B) 65% overall; 65 point gap
- C) 65% overall; 50 point gap
- D) 60% overall; 65 point gap

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Completions total 36 + 12 + 4 = 52, across 40 + 24 + 16 = 80 participants, so 52 ÷ 80 = 65%. Group rates are 90%, 50% and 25%, giving a gap of 65 points. Hence B. The substantive point sits behind the arithmetic and belongs in your standards rather than in a single report: the overall figure of 65% describes no group in the study, and reporting it alone conceals that assistive technology users fail three times in four. An organisational standard that does not require disaggregation by access need has a hole in it, and the hole is exactly the shape of the users least able to complain.

**Question 4 (easy) — Centre and spread**

Thirteen sessions produced task times in minutes: 5, 6, 7, 8, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 14, 16, 19, 84. Which summary is most defensible?

- A) Mean 16.1 minutes
- B) Median 10 minutes, range 5 to 84, with the 84-minute case described separately
- C) Median 10 minutes
- D) Mean 16.1 minutes, range 5 to 84

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The mean of about 16.1 exceeds eleven of the thirteen observations, so it represents almost nobody. The median is the seventh value, which is 10, and that describes the group well. But the median alone conceals a session lasting more than eight times the typical duration, and that session is very likely the most informative observation in the set — something went badly wrong for that person, and finding out what is the point of the research. Option B supplies centre, spread and explicit treatment of the outlier. Option D pairs the range with a misleading centre. Option C buries the extreme case. The rule to enforce organisationally: a central figure never travels alone, and an outlier is a lead, not noise.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Weighted aggregation**

Four studies: 15 participants at 40% completion; 25 at 60%; 10 at 70%; 30 at 80%. What is the overall rate across all 80 participants?

- A) 62%
- B) 65%
- C) 66%
- D) 68%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Convert each rate to counts before combining. Study 1: 40% of 15 = 6 completions. Study 2: 60% of 25 = 15. Study 3: 70% of 10 = 7. Study 4: 80% of 30 = 24. Total completions 6 + 15 + 7 + 24 = 52, across 80 participants, so 52 ÷ 80 = 0.65, which is 65% and the answer is B. Option A is the unweighted mean of 40, 60, 70 and 80, which is 62.5 and over-weights the two smallest studies at the expense of the 30-participant study that should dominate. Always return to counts. And build in a second habit alongside it, because at your level an aggregation error leaves your hands and travels on your authority: re-check the total before it goes anywhere. A plausible-looking figure attributed to the head of profession is the one nobody downstream will question.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Funnel denominators**

Funnel: 200,000 start, 150,000 pass identity, 90,000 upload evidence, 81,000 submit. What proportion of those who passed identity went on to submit?

- A) 40.5%
- B) 54%
- C) 60%
- D) 90%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The stem names users who passed identity, so the denominator is 150,000 and the part is 81,000: 81,000 ÷ 150,000 = 0.54, giving B. Option A uses 200,000, option C is the identity-to-upload rate, option D is the upload-to-submit rate. All are true of this funnel and one answers the question. Strategically the funnel also tells you where capacity belongs: 50,000 lost at identity and 60,000 between identity and upload, against 9,000 at submission. A directorate proposing to research the submission step would need a very good reason, and being able to see that in fifteen seconds is what lets you redirect a programme's research plan in the meeting rather than afterwards.

**Question 7 (moderate) — A benefits claim**

A benefits realisation paper states that a change raised completion from 60% to 72%, describing it as "a 20% improvement". Is that accurate?

- A) No; it is a 12% improvement.
- B) Yes as arithmetic — 12 ÷ 60 = 20% relative increase — but the expression is ambiguous, and the paper should state either "20% relative increase" or "12 percentage points".
- C) No; it is 12 percentage points and calling it 20% is wrong.
- D) No; it should be 16.7%.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The relative increase is 12 ÷ 60 = 0.2, so 20% is defensible arithmetic. The problem is labelling: "a 20% improvement" will be read by many readers as a rise from 60% to 80%. Option B captures both halves. Option C over-corrects into an error of its own, since relative change is a legitimate expression. Option A confuses the units and option D divides by the new value. At your level this is a governance matter rather than an arithmetic one: benefits papers routinely select the flattering framing, usually without intent to mislead, and an organisation in which nobody asks for both numbers will eventually publish one that cannot be defended. Asking for both, as a standing expectation rather than a challenge to any individual, is the cheapest control available.

**Question 8 (moderate) — A vendor claim**

A supplier states their identity product achieves "94% first-time verification success". Your organisation's rate, measured as verified users divided by users reaching the identity step, is 71%. What is the most important question before comparing?

- A) Whether the 94% is independently audited.
- B) What denominator the supplier uses — specifically whether it counts only users presenting acceptable documents, since excluding those without documents removes exactly the population driving your 29% failure.
- C) Whether the figure is current.
- D) Whether the supplier has other government clients.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Two rates are comparable only if their denominators describe the same population, and vendor metrics very frequently do not. If the supplier's 94% counts successes among users who submitted a valid passport or driving licence, the users you are losing have been excluded from the denominator entirely, and adopting the product could leave your failure rate essentially unchanged while the vendor's metric remains perfectly accurate. Option B identifies this. Options A, C and D are reasonable due diligence that does not touch comparability, and asking them instead produces the appearance of scrutiny without its substance — which is worse than asking nothing, because it satisfies the room. This single question is among the highest-value contributions available to you in a procurement discussion, and it is one almost nobody else in the room is positioned to ask.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Reconstructing an implied value**

A business case claims that a 5 percentage point completion improvement on 2 million annual starts is worth £12 million a year. What is the implied value per additional completed application?

- A) £6
- B) £60
- C) £120
- D) £240

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A 5 percentage point improvement on 2 million starts gives 0.05 × 2,000,000 = 100,000 additional completions. Dividing: £12,000,000 ÷ 100,000 = £120, so C. Option A divides by 2 million, valuing every start. Option B halves the improvement. The value of this calculation is that it takes thirty seconds and immediately tells you whether a business case is plausible — £120 per additional completed application may be entirely reasonable for a high-value transaction and wildly implausible for a simple information service. And the challenge it enables is far more effective than disputing a total: "so we are valuing each additional completion at £120 — is that the right order of magnitude?" is specific, answerable, and does not require you to accuse anyone of anything.

**Question 10 (harder) — A metric that conflates populations**

A board paper reports "average time to complete: 11 minutes" for a service your research shows most users taking over half an hour across multiple sittings. What is the likely explanation and the right intervention?

- A) The board paper is wrong and should be corrected.
- B) The metric almost certainly measures single sessions rather than end-to-end journeys, so multi-sitting completions are excluded or counted only for their final short visit, and abandoned sessions may be included — ask for the definition and the distribution, and propose an end-to-end measure alongside it.
- C) The research sample was atypical.
- D) The figures cannot be reconciled.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Session-based analytics and journey-based reality diverge systematically for any service completed over more than one sitting, and the divergence always flatters the service. Someone spending forty minutes across three visits typically appears as one short final session; abandoned sessions may drag the mean lower still. The figure is probably calculated correctly and defined in a way that cannot answer the question the board believes it answers. Option B names the mechanism and proposes a constructive intervention — asking for a definition is not an accusation, and offering an end-to-end measure gives the metric owner something to build rather than something to defend. Option A picks a fight over a number that is not wrong, which is how heads of profession acquire a reputation for obstruction. Option C blames the research. Option D forecloses a reconciliation that is both available and strategically useful.

**Question 11 (harder) — Sampling adequacy**

A directorate proposes to measure whether a design change improved completion by running 50 moderated sessions before and 50 after. Baseline is 62%; a 5 percentage point improvement is considered meaningful. What is the strongest response?

- A) Agree; 100 sessions is substantial.
- B) Explain that a 5 point difference on 50 per group is between two and three participants and well inside ordinary variation, so the comparison cannot detect it — recommend measuring the rate through live analytics across the full user base and spending the 100 sessions on mechanisms, which moderated research does uniquely well.
- C) Increase to 80 per group.
- D) Run it and report the observed difference with caveats.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Fifty per group cannot distinguish a five point difference from chance; reliable detection at that size requires several hundred per group. Option B says so and redirects, which is the substantive contribution: analytics measures rates across the whole population at negligible marginal cost, while moderated sessions are the only instrument that explains why a rate is what it is. Dividing the work along that line is the recommendation. Option A endorses a design that cannot answer its own question. Option C moves towards adequacy without reaching it while still misallocating scarce capacity. Option D is the most damaging at organisational level, because caveated findings from underpowered comparisons get cited without their caveats — and once your organisation establishes that fifty-session comparisons can measure rates, you will be arguing against that precedent for the rest of your tenure.

**Question 12 (harder) — A claim already in a board paper**

A board paper states: "Research confirms 93% of users can complete the service independently." You trace it to a study of 43 participants, 40 of whom completed, recruited through the departmental panel. The board meets in four days. What is most important?

- A) Ask for the figure to be removed.
- B) Go to the paper's author, explain that 43 panel-recruited participants cannot support a population rate, propose the accurate statement — 40 of 43 panel participants completed unaided — and offer what the organisation does have if the board needs a rate; then confirm the correction is made before the paper is tabled.
- C) Raise it at the board.
- D) Note it for the next assurance cycle.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A qualitative sample from a self-selected panel has become a population statistic in a document that will inform a decision, which is a category error rather than a rounding one. Option B does the four things that matter: it goes to the author first, names precisely what the evidence supports, offers an alternative source so the board is not simply left with a hole where a number was, and closes the loop by confirming the change lands. That last element is the one most often omitted, and the correction agreed but never made is a familiar organisational failure. Option A asks for removal without a replacement, which invites resistance and leaves the underlying question unanswered. Option C corrects the record in the most public and least recoverable setting, embarrasses the author, and converts your assurance role into an adversarial one. Option D allows a board decision to proceed on a claim you know to be unsupported, which is exactly what your role exists to prevent.

### Preparation tips

- **Ask who could not have appeared in this data.** The highest-yield question available against any figure.
- **Reconstruct implied unit values.** Thirty seconds of division establishes whether a business case is plausible.
- **Interrogate vendor denominators.** Vendor and internal metrics are almost never defined alike.
- **Ask for the definition and the distribution.** Most misleading metrics are correctly calculated and wrongly defined.
- **Return to counts before combining rates, and re-check the total.** An aggregation error leaving your hands travels on your authority.
- **Know what sample sizes can detect.** No power calculation needed to see that 50 per group cannot detect five points.
- **Trace three figures in current board papers back to source.** More instructive than any drill.
- **Ask for both framings as a standing expectation.** Percentage points and relative change, every time, as a norm rather than a challenge.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Averaging rates without weighting.** The commonest real-world error in aggregated reporting.
- **Comparing vendor rates to internal rates.** Different populations, same-looking numbers, large price tags.
- **Converting qualitative samples into population rates.** The error most likely to reach a board paper.
- **Objecting on sample size when the frame is the problem.** More of the same data is no better.
- **Reporting an aggregate that could be disaggregated.** A figure describing no group conceals the group that is failing.
- **Accepting a mean without a spread or a definition.** Session metrics systematically flatter multi-sitting services.
- **Endorsing an underpowered comparison "with caveats".** Caveats do not travel; the number does.
- **Correcting a board paper at the board.** Go to the author, and confirm the change lands.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately you read. At your level the documents that matter are governance frameworks, audit reports, cross-government standards, ministerial correspondence, board papers and contracts. A misread exception clause becomes an organisation-wide compliance failure; a misread audit finding becomes advice given confidently to a permanent secretary.

The classic format presents a short passage of dense workplace prose, typically three to six sentences, followed by statements to classify as True, False or Cannot Say. True means the statement follows necessarily. False means the passage contradicts it. Cannot Say means the passage neither establishes nor excludes it. Alongside these come comprehension items, main-point items, and questions asking which of four summaries is most accurate.

The governing rule is that you answer from the passage alone, and the part specific to you is this: expertise actively costs you marks here. The more governance prose you have read, the more readily you supply the clause you expect, complete the sentence the way such sentences usually end, and feel entirely confident doing it. Candidates at your level underperform on verbal reasoning at rates that surprise them, and the mechanism is nearly always this one. The counter-habit has to be mechanical: before marking anything True, put your finger on the words.

Cannot Say is the answer experienced leaders avoid most. Forming a view under incomplete information is what your job consists of, and the reflex is deeply worn in. Here it is a liability. If you are filling a gap with knowledge or inference, however reasonable, the answer is Cannot Say.

Small words carry the load. "All" is not "most". "May" is not "must". "Should" is not "will". "Recommends considering" is not "recommends". "Solely", "entirely", "unless", "provided that", "subject to" and "where practicable" govern everything around them. Wrong answers are constructed almost entirely from swaps of these terms, so a statement reading like a faithful paraphrase may still be False.

There is a direct professional payoff. Separating what a text states from what it implies is the discipline you enforce when assuring findings, and the one you need when a director tells you what a Cabinet Office standard requires.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The verbal skills tested map onto your named skills:

- **Reading precisely** maps to **Research management, leadership and assurance**, where you set and assure standards including ethics and safeguarding.
- **Separating statement from inference** maps to **Analysis and synthesis**, where you help the organisation generate clear and valuable findings.
- **Identifying the main point** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**, where you direct stakeholder strategy and communicate with senior stakeholders.
- **Handling qualified language** maps to **User-centred practice and advocacy**, where you ensure senior discussions consider user needs — which requires you to represent evidence at exactly its strength.
- **Reading for absence** maps to **Inclusive research**, where noticing that a framework never mentions access needs has an ethical consequence at organisational scale.
- **Reading cross-government prose accurately** maps to your role's requirement to collaborate with colleagues across government, where shared standards are the medium of collaboration.

### Practice questions

*Passage A — for Questions 1 to 4*

"Organisations must submit an annual research assurance return to the cross-government profession board, unless all research within the organisation has been conducted under a centrally managed programme. Returns must confirm that ethics review was completed where required, and must list any studies where it was not. Where an organisation identifies a study conducted without required ethics review, it must notify the board within ten working days of identifying it, separately from the annual return. Returns are published across government; notifications are not."

**Question 1 (easy)** — Statement: "Every organisation must submit an annual research assurance return." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The passage attaches an exception: organisations where all research was conducted under a centrally managed programme are exempt. The statement's "every" contradicts that, making it False. This is the most reliable pattern in the section — absolute term in the statement, exception in the passage. Note that the exception says "all research", not "most", which narrows it substantially and supplies Question 3.

**Question 2 (easy)** — Statement: "Notifications of missed ethics review are published across government." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The final sentence states that returns are published across government and notifications are not. The statement contradicts this, making it False. The item tests whether you read past the point where a sentence appeared to finish its work — the semicolon clause carries the operative distinction, and a reader who registered "returns are published across government" and moved on would generalise it to notifications. Governance prose places qualifications after semicolons with striking regularity, and slowing down there rather than speeding up is a habit worth building deliberately.

**Question 3 (moderate)** — Statement: "An organisation where nineteen of twenty studies ran under a centrally managed programme must submit a return." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The exemption applies only where *all* research ran under a centrally managed programme. Nineteen of twenty does not satisfy that, so the exemption does not apply and the requirement stands — the statement follows and is True. The item rewards carrying "all" from the first sentence to a scenario several lines later. It also reflects a real and recurring organisational error: partial satisfaction of an exception condition is non-compliance, and organisations routinely assume the reverse on the reasoning that they are substantially covered. Catching that assumption before a return is due, rather than after, is squarely your job.

**Question 4 (moderate)** — Statement: "A study conducted without required ethics review must be notified within ten working days of the study concluding." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The passage requires notification within ten working days of *identifying* the study, not of its conclusion. Those triggers can be many months apart — a study that concluded in February and was identified in a September assurance review must be notified in September, and would not be retrospectively late. The statement misstates the trigger, so it is False. Small distinction, large consequence: trigger events determine whether an organisation is compliant, and misreading one produces either unnecessary alarm or false comfort, both of which are expensive when reported to a cross-government board. Whenever a passage specifies a deadline, establish what starts the clock before anything else.

*Passage B — for Questions 5 to 8*

"The cross-government review of research assurance found considerable variation between departments. Five departments operate peer review before publication; the remainder rely on line management sign-off. Departments with peer review reported fewer instances of findings being challenged after publication, although the review did not examine whether those findings were more robust. The review recommends that the profession board consider whether a common assurance approach would be proportionate."

**Question 5 (easy)** — Statement: "Some departments do not operate peer review before publication." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The passage states that five departments operate peer review and the remainder rely on line management sign-off, establishing directly that some do not. The statement follows and is True. Answer quickly and bank the time. The only conceivable ambiguity — whether "the remainder" might be empty — is closed off by the passage describing what the remainder does.

**Question 6 (moderate)** — Statement: "Peer review produces more robust findings." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The passage reports fewer post-publication challenges in peer-reviewed departments and then states explicitly that robustness was not examined. Fewer challenges and greater robustness are different things: findings may be challenged less because they are better, or because they are blander, or because peer review sands off the phrasing that invites challenge. The passage flags its own silence, so the answer is Cannot Say. A candidate who still answers True has substituted professional intuition for what the text supports. The intuition may well be right, and it is not evidence — and at your level this is exactly the substitution that produces a cross-government recommendation resting on a finding the review declined to make.

**Question 7 (moderate)** — Statement: "The review recommends introducing a common assurance approach." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The review recommends that the profession board *consider whether* a common approach would be proportionate, which includes the possibility of concluding that it would not be. The statement asserts a recommendation to introduce, which the review did not make, so it is False rather than Cannot Say — the passage tells you what was recommended and it was something else. This distinction pervades review and audit prose, and misreading it produces a familiar sequence: a recommendation to consider becomes a commitment to deliver, the commitment is tracked, and eighteen months later a board is reporting delivery against something nobody ever decided to do.

**Question 8 (harder)** — Statement: "Line management sign-off is a weaker form of assurance than peer review." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The passage describes two approaches and reports one difference in outcome while explicitly declining to assess robustness. It makes no judgement about relative strength. A reader may reasonably infer that peer review is stronger, and the passage neither supports nor excludes that, so the answer is Cannot Say. What makes this harder than Question 6 is that the passage's structure leans towards peer review — mentioned first, associated with a favourable outcome — and structural lean is not content. Noticing when you are responding to a document's implied stance rather than its assertions is among the most transferable things this section trains, and it applies directly to audit reports, which routinely imply considerably more than they are prepared to state.

*Passage C — for Questions 9 to 12*

"Research findings must not be altered to reflect the preferences of stakeholders. Presentation may be adapted to the audience provided the substance is unchanged. Where a researcher is asked to alter findings, the matter must be raised with the head of profession, who is accountable for the integrity of research outputs. Where findings cannot reach decision-makers before a decision is taken, the reason must be recorded; records are reviewed annually by the profession board."

**Question 9 (easy)** — Statement: "Findings may be presented differently to different audiences." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The second sentence permits adapting presentation to the audience provided substance is unchanged, and the statement claims only that different presentation is permitted. It follows and is True. The statement is weaker than the passage's full provision, omitting the substance condition — but a weaker statement the passage supports is still True. Candidates occasionally mark such statements False for incompleteness, confusing "does not say everything" with "says something wrong".

**Question 10 (moderate)** — Statement: "A researcher asked to alter findings must refuse." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The passage establishes that findings must not be altered to reflect stakeholder preferences, and that a request to alter must be raised with the head of profession. Neither states what the individual researcher must personally do at the moment of the request. A duty to escalate is not a duty to refuse — arguably the escalation route exists so that an individual is not left to refuse alone, which is a substantive protection and, in your role, the protection you are supposed to provide. Since the passage neither imposes nor excludes a duty to refuse, the answer is Cannot Say. The item rewards noticing that "must not be altered" is a rule about outcomes while "must be raised" is a rule about conduct, and that neither entails the other.

**Question 11 (harder)** — Statement: "A decision taken before findings were available breaches the requirements unless the reason is recorded." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** Read the requirement precisely. The passage says that where findings cannot reach decision-makers in time, the reason must be recorded. It does not frame the timing failure as a breach that recording excuses; it treats late findings as an expected occurrence and requires honesty about them. The breach would consist in failing to record. The statement misdescribes the structure, so it is False. The distinction is fine and consequential: under the statement's reading, late findings are a compliance failure to be minimised, which produces concealment; under the passage's reading, they are a normal event to be documented, which produces data you can act on. Organisations get the behaviour their framing implies, and as the person who writes such frameworks, that is worth carrying away.

**Question 12 (harder) — Main point**

Which best captures the main point of Passage C?

- A) Research findings must never be changed.
- B) The substance of research must remain independent of stakeholder preference, with adaptation permitted in form, escalation provided where independence is pressured, accountability located with the head of profession, and honesty required where timing fails.
- C) The head of profession is accountable for research integrity.
- D) Reasons must be recorded when findings arrive late.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The main point must span the passage while adding nothing. Option A overstates: adaptation of presentation is expressly permitted, and the prohibition is specifically on altering substance to suit stakeholders. Options C and D are each accurate about one clause and silent on the rest, making them details rather than main points. Option B captures all four elements and the logic joining them — research is useful only if it is both independent and timely, so the passage protects independence with a rule, an escalation route and an accountable owner, and handles timing with a requirement of honesty rather than a prohibition. Main-point items reward the option broad enough to cover everything and disciplined enough to invent nothing, and three of these four are true details promoted beyond their scope.

### Preparation tips

- **Set expertise aside mechanically.** Before marking True, point at the words. This is the senior candidate's specific failure mode and it needs a specific counter-habit.
- **Treat Cannot Say as decisive.** The reflex to form a view is precisely what costs marks here.
- **Circle every qualifier.** All, every, entirely, solely, some, may, must, should, unless, provided that, subject to, where practicable, consider whether, proportionate.
- **Establish what starts the clock.** Deadlines hang on trigger events, and the trigger is often not the obvious one.
- **Separate "recommends considering X" from "recommends X".** Audit and review prose runs on this distinction.
- **Distinguish a failed condition from declared silence.** The first gives False; the second gives Cannot Say.
- **Notice structural lean.** Order of mention and favourable association are not assertions.
- **Practise on cross-government standards.** Write three statements about a page — one True, one False, one Cannot Say.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Answering from professional knowledge.** The dominant error at your level, by a wide margin.
- **Completing the clause you expected.** Governance prose has conventions, and passages are built to exploit them.
- **Missing "all", "entirely" and "solely".** Partial satisfaction of an exception is non-compliance.
- **Reading fewer challenges as greater robustness.** Passages routinely distinguish outcome proxies from the thing itself.
- **Confusing a rule about outcomes with a rule about conduct.** "Must not be altered" and "must be raised" are different obligations.
- **Marking a weaker-but-supported statement False.** Incomplete is not incorrect.
- **Responding to implied stance.** Tone and structure are not content.
- **Rushing after a semicolon.** The operative qualification lives there disproportionately often.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment presents realistic workplace scenarios and asks what you would do. There is no passage to parse and no arithmetic. You get a dilemma of the kind your role produces, four or five candidate responses, and a question asking which is most effective and often which is least.

This section measures whether your judgement matches what the profession considers good practice at your level — and level is the whole calibration. Your role description places you as the person who leads user researchers in an organisation, attracts and builds talent, defines and assures best practice, influences organisational strategy and priorities, and collaborates across government. The strongest answers involve acting on systems rather than instances, building capability rather than solving problems personally, absorbing pressure on behalf of the people you lead, and using influence in settings where you hold no formal authority.

Three features distinguish your level.

The first is that almost nothing you do is executed by you. An option that solves a problem through your personal intervention is usually the second-best answer, because it does not survive your absence and does not scale past your calendar. The reliable tell of a strong option at your level is that it changes a mechanism, a standard, a routine or a capability.

The second is that your authority is largely informal. You do not line-manage most of the people whose practice you assure, you cannot compel a directorate, and you influence stakeholders who substantially outrank you. Options that lead with instruction, mandate or escalation tend to score poorly — not because escalation is wrong, but because it is capital you spend rather than a tool you use freely.

The third is that you absorb pressure downwards. When a director leans on a researcher, the researcher should not be the one holding that line. Options that leave a junior colleague exposed to a power differential you are positioned to absorb are weak even when they preserve the finding.

Four principles carry most scenarios. Participants and users come first, including in research you did not commission. Protect the integrity of the evidence, and protect the people whose job it is to protect it. Build capability while solving the problem. Calibrate escalation to severity — physical risk goes up immediately and in parallel; practice problems go to the person first.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The judgement dimensions map onto your named skills:

- **Ethics and safeguarding at organisational scale** map to **Research management, leadership and assurance**, where you set and assure standards including ethics and safeguarding.
- **Assurance across boundaries** maps to assuring research quality across the organisation.
- **Talent and capability** map to your role level's requirement to attract and build talent, and to **User research methods**, where you lead a community of practice.
- **Influence and negotiation** map to **Stakeholder relationship management**, where you influence and negotiate with senior stakeholders to enable progress.
- **Organisational advocacy** maps to **User-centred practice and advocacy**, where you ensure discussions at senior levels or between organisations consider user needs.
- **Cross-government collaboration** maps to your role level's requirement to collaborate with colleagues across government.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — A researcher under pressure from a director**

A lead researcher tells you a director has asked them to remove an accessibility finding from a board summary because it will "muddy the message". They are unsure what to do. What is the most effective response?

- A) Tell them to leave it in and cite your instruction.
- B) Agree with them how the finding should be presented, including mitigation; offer to attend; and take the request itself up with the director yourself, since a director asking for findings to be removed is a matter at your level.
- C) Contact the director and instruct them not to make such requests.
- D) Raise it with the permanent secretary's office.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Two things must happen: the finding survives, and the researcher emerges more capable and less exposed. Option B does both. Agreeing the presentation together builds their judgement rather than substituting yours, and a constructive framing with mitigation gives them something to bring rather than a refusal. Offering to attend supplies backing without takeover. Most importantly, taking the request up with the director is your job — a power differential of that size is exactly what your role exists to absorb, and leaving a lead researcher to manage it alone is a failure of leadership even if the finding survives. Option A is least effective: it leaves them defending a position by proxy, teaches nothing, and reframes integrity as hierarchy. Option C treats a conversation as an instruction you are not positioned to give and will harden the position. Option D escalates far past the point, spending capital you will need for something that warrants it.

**Question 2 (easy) — A standard nobody follows**

Your organisation has a research standard that is widely ignored. Researchers describe it as unrealistic. What is the strongest approach?

- A) Reissue it with a message from you.
- B) Review recent outputs with researchers to find out which requirements are genuinely unworkable and which are merely unclear, revise it with them, and pair it with a lightweight routine — such as peer review — that gives compliance a moment of application and delivers something researchers want anyway.
- C) Introduce mandatory compliance checks.
- D) Run training on the standard.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A standard everybody ignores has already failed, and reissuing changes only the date. Option B treats the researchers' objection as data — some requirements probably are unworkable under real conditions, and the rest are usually unclear rather than unreasonable. Revising with them produces authorship and therefore defenders, which is the only mechanism by which a standard becomes self-sustaining rather than dependent on your attention. Pairing it with a routine matters because a standard without a moment of application is an aspiration; peer review supplies that moment and gives something back. Option A is least effective, being the purest form of appearing to act. Option C enforces a standard people have told you is unrealistic, producing compliance theatre and resentment. Option D is a useful component and insufficient alone, since training without a routine requiring the behaviour rarely changes practice.

**Question 3 (easy) — Building the pipeline**

Your organisation cannot recruit experienced user researchers. Posts sit vacant for months; teams are covered by junior staff working beyond their level. What is strongest?

- A) Keep advertising and escalate the recruitment difficulty.
- B) Treat it as capability design rather than recruitment: redeploy experienced researchers to lead across teams instead of embedding singly, invest in structured supervision for the junior staff you have, and build entry routes — apprenticeships, secondments, conversion from adjacent roles — while continuing to recruit.
- C) Lower the stated experience requirements.
- D) Fill gaps with agency researchers.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** Months of unfilled posts in a competitive market is not a problem more advertising solves, and treating it as one guarantees years of the same. Option B accepts the constraint and redesigns around it. Deploying experienced researchers across teams makes scarce expertise go further and gives junior staff the supervision they currently lack. Developing the people you have is the only supply route you fully control. Building entry routes addresses the pipeline rather than competing for the same small pool, which is the only intervention that changes the market position over a three-year horizon. Option C is least effective because lowering the requirement without changing the support structure reproduces the current situation while additionally setting people up to fail. Option D is a legitimate short-term measure and a poor strategy: agency capacity leaves when the contract ends and builds nothing. Attracting and building talent is named in your role, and it is a design problem far more often than a market one.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Assuring across a boundary**

A directorate you do not manage has published research whose central claim is unsupported, and it is being cited in a spending submission. What is most effective?

- A) Correct the record with the submission's author.
- B) Speak to the researcher and their lead first, set out specifically what the evidence supports, and agree how the correction is made and communicated to those already relying on it — with a clear next step if they decline.
- C) Raise it at the departmental board.
- D) Note it for the annual assurance return.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** The claim needs correcting and the relationship needs to survive, since you will be assuring this directorate for years. Option B goes to the people responsible first — they may hold context you lack, and if they do not, the correction lands far better coming from them — and it is not naive, since it carries a next step if they refuse. The element most often omitted is the one about communicating to those already relying on the claim, without which a corrected report sits quietly alongside an uncorrected submission. Option A bypasses the researcher, damages a relationship, and risks acting on your own misreading. Option C is disproportionate as a first move and converts assurance into confrontation. Option D is least effective by a distance: an annual return is a record, not an intervention, and a spending submission is moving now.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Ethics non-compliance concentrated in one place**

Studies in one directorate repeatedly proceeded without required ethics review, always under ministerial deadline pressure, always by experienced researchers. What is the strongest response?

- A) Report the breaches and require retrospective review.
- B) Address the specific breaches, and separately investigate the review process — what it demands, how long it takes, whether a fast route exists — on the reasoning that experienced researchers who know the requirement and breach it under pressure are telling you the process cannot be complied with when time is short.
- C) Remind all researchers of the requirement.
- D) Introduce a mandatory pre-fieldwork checkpoint for every study.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** The concentration is the most informative feature. Scattered breaches by different people would indicate conduct; repeated breaches by experienced people in one place under identical pressure indicate a process that does not survive a deadline. Option B handles both layers, and the clause about addressing the specific breaches matters — a systemic diagnosis is not a reason to leave individual failures unaddressed, particularly where participants may have been affected. Option A treats the instances and guarantees recurrence. Option C is least effective and is the weakest available response to any compliance failure: people who knew the rule and broke it under pressure will not be moved by hearing it again. Option D imposes friction on every compliant study to catch a localised failure, which is how organisational burden accumulates — and you are the person who will be asked in two years why research governance is so slow.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Competing directorates**

Two directors want your research capacity in the same quarter. One has a failing live service; the other a ministerially visible new programme. Both have raised it with your director general. What is most effective?

- A) Split capacity.
- B) Set out for each what research would deliver, what decision it informs, and the cost of deferral; recommend a sequence with reasoning; and put that recommendation to both directors and the director general together rather than negotiating separately.
- C) Prioritise the ministerial programme.
- D) Ask the director general to decide.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A prioritisation problem is being conducted as a political one, and the way through is to make the trade-off explicit and simultaneously visible. Option B does the analytical work and then handles the politics correctly: putting the recommendation to both at once prevents the parallel-negotiation dynamic in which each director hears something slightly different and trust collapses when they compare notes — which they will. Making a recommendation rather than presenting neutral options is essential; you are the expert on what research can deliver, and abdicating that wastes the role and the seat. Option A is least effective: splitting constrained capacity between two substantial needs reliably produces two studies too thin to inform either decision. Option C ranks by visibility. Option D discards your judgement, though the director general should certainly be in the room.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Evidence against a public commitment**

Research across programmes shows a publicly announced channel closure will substantially disadvantage users without reliable digital access. Colleagues propose framing it as "opportunities for future enhancement". What is most effective?

- A) Accept the framing; the commitment is public.
- B) Report the findings accurately and do the constructive work alongside — quantify who is affected, specify what assisted provision would mitigate it, and present finding and mitigation together so the organisation has a route that is neither reversal nor denial.
- C) Report starkly and let the organisation respond.
- D) Escalate to the director general before acting.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** The proposed framing protects the organisation from discomfort and delivers the worst available outcome: the disadvantage occurs anyway, nobody owns it, and the evidence that could have driven mitigation has been defused. Option B keeps the finding intact and makes it actionable. Quantifying who is affected converts an abstract concern into something a delivery plan can address, and arriving with mitigation means the finding is not simply a problem dropped on people who genuinely cannot reverse a ministerial announcement. Option A is least effective — softening findings to accommodate a commitment is the precise failure your standards exist to prevent, and it falls hardest on the users least able to absorb it. Option C is accurate and unhelpful, and at your level a finding delivered without a route forward will simply be managed around. Option D is worth doing in parallel and is not a substitute for the work.

**Question 8 (harder) — A serious safeguarding risk in another team's fieldwork**

You learn that a team is researching a domestic abuse support service, recruiting through the service's own contact list, running video sessions with no protocol for confirming a participant is alone. What should you do?

- A) Raise it with the researcher and offer to help design a safer approach.
- B) Intervene immediately: contact the researcher and their lead, set out the specific risks — that contact via the service's list may reach people whose devices are monitored, and that a video session without an aloneness protocol may expose a participant to danger during the call — press for fieldwork to pause until safeguards are in place, and inform the relevant director in parallel.
- C) Report it to the data protection officer.
- D) Raise it at the next community of practice.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** The potential harm is severe, immediate and physical. Contacting someone through a domestic abuse service's list can alert a perpetrator monitoring their device, and a video call where the researcher cannot establish whether the participant is alone can place a person in danger during the session itself. That severity determines everything: intervene now, press for a pause rather than an improvement plan, and inform in parallel rather than sequentially — because setting and assuring safeguarding standards is explicitly your accountability, and this is the situation the accountability exists for. Option A applies the right instinct at the wrong severity and leaves fieldwork running while you help. Option C addresses a real but secondary concern. Option D is least effective by a wide margin: deferring a live physical-safety risk to a scheduled forum is indefensible, and the community of practice is where the lesson goes afterwards, not where the intervention happens.

**Question 9 (harder) — An unowned problem**

Synthesising across programmes, you conclude that the organisation's largest user-facing problem is in the transitions between services — users falling between four services that each work adequately alone. No director owns this. What is most effective?

- A) Report it in your annual synthesis.
- B) Build the case with evidence from all four services, quantify the affected population using contact centre and analytics data, identify who would need to own it, and take it to a forum with cross-service authority — accepting that securing an owner for an unowned problem is itself months of work.
- C) Raise it with each of the four service owners separately.
- D) Propose research on the transitions.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Unowned problems are the characteristic failure of service-shaped organisations, and surfacing them is what cross-programme synthesis is for. Option B recognises that the hard part is not the insight but the ownership: evidence from all four services makes the problem undeniable, quantification makes it comparable to problems that do have owners and budgets, and a forum with cross-service authority is the only route by which anything unowned becomes owned. The closing clause is the realistic one — this is a campaign, not a communication. Option A is least effective: the insight is correct, and filed in an annual synthesis it will change nothing, which is the quiet fate of most organisational insight. Option C produces four conversations in which each owner reasonably concludes it is mostly someone else's problem. Option D proposes more evidence when the evidence is sufficient, which is a comfortable way of deferring a difficult conversation.

**Question 10 (harder) — Cross-government collaboration**

Three departments are each independently procuring user research recruitment services, all facing the same difficulty reaching digitally excluded participants. You have no authority over the other two. What is strongest?

- A) Focus on your own department's procurement.
- B) Convene the three departments' research leads, establish whether the requirement is genuinely common, and if so propose a shared specification or joint approach to market — accepting that you are building consensus rather than exercising authority, and that a well-evidenced shared requirement is more persuasive than three separate ones.
- C) Raise it with the cross-government profession board and ask them to mandate a common approach.
- D) Share your specification with the other two departments for information.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Three departments solving the same problem separately, badly, is exactly the waste that cross-government collaboration exists to prevent, and your role names that collaboration explicitly. Option B is calibrated to the authority you actually have: convening is available to you, mandating is not, and establishing whether the requirement is genuinely common before proposing a shared solution avoids the frequent failure in which a shared approach is agreed and then found to fit nobody. A joint approach to market also changes the supplier incentive, which is the underlying mechanism — three small contracts do not motivate a supplier to build reach into excluded populations, and one large one might. Option A is least effective: it accepts a solvable structural waste and leaves the other two departments to reproduce your difficulties. Option C reaches for a mandate before establishing the case, and boards asked to mandate without evidence usually decline. Option D shares information without creating anything.

**Question 11 (harder) — Assurance failure you signed off**

Nine months ago you assured a study whose screener excluded anyone who had contacted the support desk, systematically omitting users who had already struggled. Its findings shaped a live service change. What should you do?

- A) Add it to your assurance guidance as a lesson.
- B) Notify the team and decision-makers now, scope precisely which conclusions are compromised and which stand, commission targeted follow-up with the excluded group, and examine why the assurance process did not catch it — then change the process.
- C) Commission follow-up quietly and correct the record if findings differ.
- D) Ask your director general how to handle it.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** Four things need doing and option B does all of them. Notification now matters because a live change is running on the findings. Precise scoping matters because conclusions about users who never needed help may remain sound, and overstating the damage is as unhelpful as understating it. Targeted follow-up addresses the gap. And examining the assurance process is the element specific to your level: you signed this off, so the more consequential question is not what the study missed but why your check did not surface it, and what to add so the next one does. Option A converts a live problem into a training anecdote. Option C is least effective — concealing a known flaw while a service change proceeds is indefensible, and the concealment is what would end a career rather than the original error. Option D is worth doing alongside; routing your own error through someone else is slower and reads considerably worse than owning it.

**Question 12 (harder) — Setting a standard that will be resisted**

You intend to require that all research outputs disaggregate findings by access need. You anticipate resistance: teams will object that samples are too small to disaggregate meaningfully. What is strongest?

- A) Introduce the requirement and address objections as they arise.
- B) Engage the objection before introducing it, because it is partly correct: small samples cannot produce group rates. Frame the requirement as reporting who was in the sample and what each group experienced, rather than producing group statistics — and pair it with the recruitment support that makes including those participants feasible.
- C) Introduce it as guidance rather than a requirement.
- D) Require it only above a certain sample size.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** The anticipated objection is not obstruction; it is a real methodological point, and a standard that ignores it will be resisted and technically wrong at the same time. Option B resolves the tension by changing what is required: reporting composition and describing each group's experience is entirely feasible with eight participants, whereas reporting completion rates by group is not. That reframe makes the requirement workable while preserving what actually matters — that a report should never conceal whether disabled users were present or how they fared. The paired recruitment support is the other half, because requiring inclusion without enabling it produces either non-compliance or tokenism, and you will get the one that is easier to fake. Option A is least effective: introducing a requirement you know to be contestable and arguing it case by case spends enormous credibility for a worse outcome. Option C removes the force that makes it happen. Option D exempts exactly the small qualitative studies where the omission is most common and most damaging.

### Preparation tips

- **Ask what an option does to the system.** At your level, an intervention that depends on your personal attention has not solved anything.
- **Absorb pressure downwards.** If a power differential is above the level of the person facing it, it is yours.
- **Read the distribution of a problem.** Concentrated failures diagnose a process; scattered failures diagnose conduct.
- **Calibrate escalation to severity.** Physical risk goes up immediately and in parallel; practice problems go to the person first.
- **Engage the strongest objection before you launch.** A standard that has already answered its critics survives without you.
- **Make trade-offs visible to all parties simultaneously.** Parallel private negotiation collapses when the parties compare notes.
- **Bring mitigation with bad news.** A finding plus a route forward is actionable; a finding alone gets managed around.
- **Convene where you cannot mandate.** Most cross-government progress is consensus built on evidence, not authority exercised.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Reissuing a standard nobody follows.** The purest form of appearing to act.
- **Reminding people of rules they knowingly broke.** The weakest available compliance response.
- **Adding friction for the compliant majority.** How burden accumulates and workarounds are born.
- **Solving personally rather than changing the mechanism.** It does not survive your calendar.
- **Leaving a colleague to hold a line you should be holding.** A leadership failure even when the finding survives.
- **Filing a strategic insight rather than finding it an owner.** Unowned problems stay unowned unless someone campaigns.
- **Reaching for a mandate before building the case.** Boards asked to mandate without evidence decline, and you have spent the ask.
- **Softening findings to accommodate a commitment.** It falls hardest on users least able to absorb it.

## Conclusion

You have reached the end, and it is worth registering the scale: forty-eight questions across four assessment types, with the reasoning worked through on each. That is a substantial piece of professional preparation, and more than most people at your level undertake.

Look back at the ground. In the cognitive section you read studies from their outputs, scoped a flaw precisely enough to say which claims survived, synthesised three unrelated findings into a cross-cutting identity problem, diagnosed eight teams' recruitment failure as one supply problem, read the concentration of ethics breaches as a process diagnosis rather than a conduct one, and treated two unusually strong teams as a source of transferable practice rather than a story about talent. In the numeric section you weighted rather than averaged, interrogated a vendor's denominator, reconstructed an implied unit value, recognised a session metric masquerading as a journey measure, and declined to endorse a comparison that could not detect what it was built to find. In the verbal section you carried "all" across five lines, checked what started a deadline clock, separated fewer challenges from greater robustness, distinguished a rule about outcomes from a rule about conduct, and caught yourself reading structural lean rather than content. And in the situational judgement section you absorbed a director's pressure on a researcher's behalf, intervened on a live safety risk, convened three departments where you could not compel them, campaigned for an owner for an unowned problem, examined your own assurance process after your own sign-off failed, and designed a standard around its strongest objection rather than against it.

Two threads run through all of it. The first is whether you know the limits of the evidence and will say so — in rooms where saying so is unwelcome, about findings that support conclusions you would prefer, and about your own work. The second is whether you act on mechanisms rather than instances. Those two capabilities are, between them, most of what your role consists of. Organisations can generate opinions without you and read dashboards without you. What you supply is evidence that can be trusted at the strength stated, a practice around you that keeps producing it when you are not looking, and a profession that will still be producing it after you leave.

A word about the particular difficulty of your level. The errors that cost you most are no longer the ones you would notice. They are made fluently, in areas where your experience runs deep enough that checking feels unnecessary, and nobody in your organisation is positioned to catch them. The verbal reasoning section is the clearest demonstration — expertise measurably costs marks there — but the pattern generalises to every judgement you make alone. The heads of profession whose judgement holds up over a career are, with striking consistency, the ones who kept a habit of deliberate scepticism about their own reasoning long after it stopped feeling necessary, and who built structures that would catch them.

If you want to build on this, a few things repay effort more than rereading. Audit a study you personally assured and ask who could not have appeared in its sample. Trace three figures in current board papers back to source. Go and find out what your two strongest teams actually do differently, rather than assuming. Ask a head of profession in another department to critique your organisational standard as harshly as they would a stranger's, and return the favour honestly. And the next time you are about to reissue something, ask instead why the first issue did not take.

Finally, for the day itself. An assessment samples your reasoning on one morning under artificial conditions. It is not a verdict on your judgement or your career. Read the stem twice, set expertise aside in the verbal section, find the denominator, trust Cannot Say, and remember that at your level the strongest answer is almost always the one that is honest about uncertainty and acts on the mechanism rather than the symptom.

Good luck. The profession is stronger for leaders who prepare like this, and so — which is the point of all of it — are the people who depend on these services.
