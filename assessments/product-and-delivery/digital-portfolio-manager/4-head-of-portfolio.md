## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures reasoning — pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, applied problem solving — through scenarios built from the material your role actually governs: investment portfolios, control frameworks, capacity models, escalation chains, organisational metrics and assurance regimes. At head level, the questions concentrate on integration and design: reasoning across several artefacts and constraints simultaneously, detecting the flaw in a system rather than a datum, and distinguishing what evidence establishes from what it merely permits — under time pressure calibrated to executive workloads.

Typical format: online, timed, 15 to 30 minutes, 20 to 30 questions, frequently adaptive, normed against senior professional comparison groups, reported as a speed-accuracy profile. Take the ungraded warm-ups; even experienced executives benefit from two minutes of interface calibration.

The relevance to your role is structural. A head of portfolio is an organisation's instrument for seeing its change portfolio whole: you aggregate what programmes report, correct for the biases aggregation introduces, and provide leaders with insights into the portfolio's future — which is to say, your reasoning is a governance control. When your deduction is loose, the organisation's picture is loose; when your error-checking misses the artefact-level contradiction, boards decide on contradictions; when your prioritisation logic is captured by urgency or seniority, the portfolio's resources follow. There is also a leadership multiplier: you build and lead the team whose collective reasoning produces the portfolio's evidence base, and the disciplines this section drills — verify before certifying, quantify before ranking, design controls that survive their edge cases — are the ones your portfolio managers learn largely by watching you exercise them.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **Communicating analysis and insight** and your duty to provide leaders with insights into the portfolio's future: foresight is pattern-reading — cross-programme recurrences, leading indicators, the metric whose shape signals a reporting problem — surfaced early enough for leaders to act.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Governance and assurance**: ensuring the right controls are used means reasoning exactly about what each control mandates, permits and omits, and how controls interact — including the edge cases where well-intended rules contradict each other.
- **Error checking** maps to **Governance and assurance** and your duty to lead the tracking of digital projects: at your level the errors that matter are systemic — the aggregation that conceals, the measure that broke when the process changed, the register that disagrees with the pack a board will see tomorrow.
- **Prioritisation** maps to **Planning** and **Strategic thinking**: prioritising the most important or high-value work across a portfolio — under capacity constraints, with complex dependencies, against strategic criteria you yourself defined — is the role's core resource decision, repeated at every scale from your own diary to the investment pipeline.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Planning** and your duty to address risks and deal with or escalate issues: the issues that reach you are the ones without clean answers; solving them means constraint-reasoning with organisational, commercial and human variables included — and knowing which residue genuinely requires escalation above you.
- **Team-system reasoning** maps to your duty to build and lead a diverse, skilled and motivated team: workload allocation, capability coverage, and development sequencing across a team of portfolio managers are themselves constraint problems, and several questions below treat them as such.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern in escalation flows**

Reviewing a year of escalations reaching your desk, you count by category: supplier/commercial 14; inter-programme dependency 11; funding/approval timing 9; data or reporting quality 3; team capability 2. Your portfolio managers handle escalations below you; anything they cannot resolve rises. What does the distribution most plausibly indicate?

- A) Supplier management is the portfolio's weakest discipline.
- B) The categories reaching you in volume — commercial, dependency, funding — are those whose resolution needs authority or cross-boundary reach your portfolio managers lack, which is the escalation system working; the near-absence of data-quality and capability escalations, however, deserves a check: genuinely rare, or being absorbed below you?
- C) Your portfolio managers are escalating too much.
- D) The distribution is random and carries no information.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Read the distribution against what escalation volume actually measures — not problem frequency, but the boundary between what your team can resolve and what needs you. Commercial disputes, cross-programme dependency deadlocks and funding-timing collisions are precisely the categories requiring negotiating authority, cross-boundary convening power, or access to leadership that sits at your level: their dominance is the system routing correctly, not a discipline failing (option A confuses "reaches the head" with "is broken"). The analytically interesting signal is the near-zero categories. Data-quality and capability issues certainly occur — every portfolio has them — so their absence from your desk means either your portfolio managers resolve them well (good) or they are being absorbed: normalised, worked around, or withheld as embarrassing (bad, and invisible by construction). Option B both reads the volumes correctly and targets the check where the information isn't. Option C judges rate with no baseline — 39 escalations across a year and a portfolio may be high or low; the stem gives no comparator. The head-level habit this trains: your inbox is a sensor with known biases; what reaches you measures the escalation system as much as the portfolio, and auditing the silences is as important as triaging the arrivals. Leading the tracking of projects includes tracking what isn't tracked upward.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction across control rules**

Your control framework states: (i) any programme exceeding £5m lifetime cost requires a gate review at each phase transition; (ii) gate reviews must be chaired by someone independent of the programme; (iii) heads of portfolio may chair gate reviews except for programmes within their own portfolio. Programme Tay, in your portfolio, costs £8m and is approaching a phase transition. Which one of the following must be true?

- A) You will need to find a chair from outside your organisation.
- B) Tay requires a gate review, and you cannot chair it.
- C) Tay's review can be waived since you cannot chair it.
- D) Rule (iii) conflicts with rule (ii) and one must be repealed.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Chain the rules against the facts. Tay exceeds £5m and approaches a transition: rule (i) makes the gate review mandatory. Chairing: rule (iii) bars heads of portfolio from chairing reviews of programmes in their own portfolio — Tay is in yours, so you are barred; note rule (ii) would independently bar you anyway if your leadership of the portfolio compromises independence, but (iii) settles it explicitly. Both conclusions — review required, you excluded — follow necessarily: option B. Option A overreaches: the rules require independence from the programme, not from the organisation — another head of portfolio, or any qualified independent chair internally, satisfies the text; "outside your organisation" adds a constraint the framework doesn't contain. Option C inverts the logic: your ineligibility to chair is a constraint on the review's staffing, not a ground for waiving a mandatory control — a non sequitur that, in real governance, is exactly how controls erode ("we couldn't convene it, so we skipped it"). Option D misreads harmony as conflict: (iii) is a specific application of (ii)'s independence principle, not a contradiction. The head-level relevance is direct: you ensure the right controls are used, which includes the controls that constrain you — and modelling the distinction between "this rule is inconvenient for me" and "this rule is defective" is among the most-watched behaviours in your role.

**Question 3 (moderate) — Error checking an aggregate before the executive committee**

Your quarterly executive pack drafts: "Portfolio delivery confidence: 78% (up from 74%). All ten programmes reported. Method: unweighted average of programme confidence scores." Checking the underlying data: nine programmes cluster between 70% and 85%; the tenth — your largest, holding 40% of portfolio spend — reports 45%, down from 60% last quarter. What is the most important defect in the draft?

- A) The arithmetic is wrong; the average of the ten scores cannot be 78%.
- B) The headline conceals a materially deteriorating largest programme: unweighted averaging lets nine small improvements mask a 15-point fall in the programme holding 40% of the money — the pack's "up from 74%" tells leaders the opposite of what the spend-weighted picture shows.
- C) Ten programmes is too small a sample for averaging.
- D) Confidence percentages should never be averaged by any method.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The arithmetic may well be internally correct — nine scores in the 70–85 band with one 45 can average to 78 (option A is unverifiable from the stem and beside the point). The defect is representational and it is severe: an unweighted mean gives your largest programme — 40% of spend, confidence collapsing from 60 to 45 — exactly one-tenth of the headline's attention, while nine smaller improvements pull the average up. A spend-weighted view would show portfolio confidence falling, and falling because of the programme that matters most; the draft's cheerful trajectory ("up from 74%") is therefore not imprecise but directionally wrong for the question leaders actually ask: "how safe is our money?" This is the aggregation failure at its most consequential altitude — your pack is the instrument through which the executive sees the portfolio, and a mean that democratises unequal programmes is a lens that inverts the image. The competent fix, worth specifying because head-level review is about prescribing not just detecting: report spend-weighted alongside unweighted, decompose the movement ("nine programmes improved; the largest deteriorated 15 points — pack leads with that"), and never let a single average carry a mixed story. Options C and D retreat into methodological nihilism — averages mislead when misapplied, not always. Providing leaders with insights they can act on begins with refusing to sign the aggregate that hides the insight.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Prioritising the head's week**

Monday, 8:30. Competing for your week: (1) Thursday's investment committee — you present the annual prioritisation recommendation allocating £30m across fourteen bids; your team's analysis is complete but you have not yet stress-tested the top three rankings; (2) a portfolio manager you line-manage has requested an urgent conversation — she is visibly struggling and you suspect she may resign; (3) the director general's office wants your view by Wednesday on a machinery-of-government change that would move two programmes out of your portfolio; (4) a supplier's account director has requested a meeting about "a commercial matter best discussed early", unspecified; (5) your monthly portfolio community session — you coach twelve portfolio managers across the organisation — is Friday, currently unprepared. Which allocation is most defensible?

- A) 2 Monday morning; 1 stress-testing Monday–Tuesday; 3 drafted Tuesday, submitted Wednesday; 4 scheduled Wednesday with an agenda requested first; 5 prepared Thursday afternoon after the committee.
- B) 1 all day Monday and Tuesday; 3 Wednesday; 2 Thursday after the committee; 4 and 5 next week.
- C) 3 first — the director general outranks all else; then 1; 2 Friday; delegate 5; decline 4.
- D) 4 first, since unspecified commercial matters are usually serious; then 1, 3; 2 and 5 as time allows.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The discriminating item is (2), and it is placed first deliberately. A trusted portfolio manager signalling crisis is both time-critical — resignation decisions crystallise fast, and a person in difficulty who waits four days for their leader has learned what they rank — and high-stakes: your role's explicit duties include building and leading a motivated team and line management; losing a strong portfolio manager costs more portfolio capability than any single meeting this week creates. An hour Monday morning is the cheapest highest-return slot on the board (option B's Thursday slot for her is the classic executive failure: people problems deferred behind content problems until they convert to exit interviews). Item (1) then rightly dominates Monday–Tuesday: a £30m allocation whose top rankings are untested is not ready for a committee that will probe exactly there — stress-testing your own recommendation before others do is the difference between presenting analysis and defending hope. Item (3) needs a considered view by Wednesday: drafting Tuesday allows overnight distance before submission — machinery-of-government positions, once given, are quoted. Item (4): an unspecified "commercial matter" gets scheduled with an agenda requested — walking unprepared into a supplier conversation that may be a claim, a renegotiation opener or a fishing expedition surrenders the information advantage (option D's urgency-by-vagueness is how account directors set agendas). Item (5) is prepared Thursday after the committee — late but genuinely prepared, because coaching twelve portfolio managers on stale content wastes twelve people's Friday; note it is neither delegated (option C — the community session's value is you) nor dropped. The head-level pattern: people first when people are at risk, then the decision with the largest irreversible consequence, then positions that become commitments — and never let vagueness or rank jump the queue.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Pattern in the investment pipeline**

Analysing three years of investment decisions, you find: bids sponsored by directorates with a seat on the investment committee were approved at 71%; bids from directorates without a seat, 34% — with business-case quality scores (independently assessed) statistically similar across both groups. Committee members, shown the figures, respond: "We fund the strongest cases; seated directorates simply understand our standards better." What is the most defensible reading?

- A) The members' explanation is confirmed: familiarity with standards explains the gap.
- B) With case quality controlled and a 37-point approval gap remaining, the evidence indicates a structural advantage for represented directorates — advocacy in the room, informal pre-socialisation — that quality differences cannot explain; the members' account, offered without evidence, is a hypothesis the data already undermines.
- C) Unseated directorates should be told to write better cases.
- D) The gap is acceptable if the funded work delivers.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The stem's crucial clause is the control: independently assessed case quality is similar across groups, which is precisely the variable the members' explanation requires to differ. "They understand our standards better" predicts higher-quality submissions from seated directorates; the assessment says otherwise; so the offered account is not merely unevidenced but contradicted by the one measurement designed to test it. What remains to explain 71% versus 34% is what differs between the groups: presence — a sponsor in the room to advocate, clarify, and pre-socialise, and the relational capital of repeated committee membership. That is a structural bias in an allocation mechanism, and option B names it with the right epistemics (the data "indicates"; the mechanism is inferred). Option C prescribes for the disproven diagnosis — coaching better cases from directorates whose cases are already equivalent treats the symptom the data just excluded. Option D changes the question: downstream delivery success cannot validate an upstream allocation that systematically under-funds unrepresented bids — the unfunded counterfactuals never get to demonstrate anything. The head-level consequences are concrete and yours to own: allocation credibility is portfolio infrastructure, and a 37-point representation gap, once visible, is a finding you take to the committee with redesign options — blind first-round scoring, standing advocacy for unseated bids, rotating membership. The deeper discipline is hearing "we fund the strongest cases" for what it is when the controls say otherwise: the system explaining itself to itself. Evaluating whether current mechanisms meet business requirements is your strategic-thinking duty — and mechanisms rarely self-report their biases.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Deduction through a delegation design**

You are drafting delegation limits. Proposed rules: (i) portfolio managers may approve programme-level changes with cost impact up to £250k; (ii) changes above £250k, or any change affecting a statutory deadline regardless of cost, require head of portfolio approval; (iii) any single programme may receive at most three delegated approvals per quarter — the fourth and subsequent changes in a quarter, regardless of size, come to you. A programme submits, in one quarter: a £180k change (its fourth change that quarter, no statutory impact). Under the rules as drafted, who approves — and what behavioural risk does rule (iii) create that the drafting should anticipate?

- A) The portfolio manager approves — £180k is within delegation; rule (iii) is advisory.
- B) You approve — rule (iii) routes any fourth-in-quarter change to you regardless of size; and the rule predictably incentivises change-bundling: teams packaging several small changes into one submission to preserve delegation headroom, so the design needs a companion rule about bundled changes or aggregate quarterly cost.
- C) You approve, and the behavioural risk is that portfolio managers will stop approving anything.
- D) Nobody can approve it this quarter; the change must wait.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The mechanical half first: rule (iii) is drafted as mandatory routing ("come to you"), not guidance — a fourth-in-quarter change of any size escalates, so the £180k change is yours to approve despite sitting within the £250k limit; options A (demoting the rule to advisory) and D (inventing a prohibition where the rule creates a routing) both misread the text, and this much any careful portfolio manager should get. The head-level half is anticipating how the rule will reshape behaviour, because every threshold teaches the governed to manage against it: a frequency cap on approvals makes approval slots scarce, and scarce slots get economised — teams will bundle four genuine changes into one submission, preserving headroom while degrading the change record's granularity (one approval now covers four decisions, and the audit trail blurs exactly where rule (iii) sought scrutiny). Well-known cousins of this effect: invoice-splitting under spend thresholds — which is why procurement rules ban disaggregation explicitly — and its mirror image, aggregation, under frequency caps. The competent drafting response is a companion provision: bundled submissions count per constituent change, or a quarterly aggregate-cost trigger operates alongside the frequency trigger. Option C predicts paralysis, which frequency caps don't cause — they cause packaging. The discipline this trains is the designer's second question, the one that separates writing rules from designing controls: after "what does this rule require?", always "what will this rule teach people to do?" Ensuring the right controls are used means ensuring the controls survive contact with the incentives they create.

**Question 7 (hard) — Error checking a benefits claim across artefacts**

Three artefacts describe Programme Lune's benefits. Business case (approved two years ago): £6m annual benefits from month 30, comprising £4.2m staff-time savings and £1.8m reduced error costs. Latest programme report: "On track for full £6m benefits; staff-time savings validated by time-and-motion study." Finance's benefits ledger: £1.9m annualised benefits banked to date, all from error-cost reduction; staff-time savings "recognised in principle; not yet cash-releasing — headcount unchanged." The programme is at month 27. Which reading should govern your report to leadership?

- A) The programme's "on track for £6m" stands: the study validated the savings.
- B) The artefacts agree £1.9m is real; the contested £4.2m is time saved but not released — staff are doing other work, headcount and budgets unchanged — so the honest position is: £1.9m cash-releasing benefits banked (error costs, exceeding their £1.8m target), £4.2m of efficiency claims validated as time but not yet converted to cash or capacity value, and the conversion mechanism (role redesign, headcount plan, or redeployment accounting) is the open question leadership should press.
- C) Finance's ledger is authoritative; report benefits at £1.9m and the £4.2m as failed.
- D) Split the difference and report £4m as a prudent midpoint.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The three artefacts are not contradicting each other about facts — they are measuring different things, and the head-level skill is naming the seam. The time-and-motion study can be perfectly sound: staff genuinely save the hours. Finance's ledger can be simultaneously right: no budget line has shrunk, because saved hours diffused into other work rather than converting to headcount reduction, redeployment against funded demand, or output increase — the classic fate of efficiency benefits, and the reason "validated" and "cash-releasing" are different claims. So option A over-reports (the study validates time, not money), option C over-corrects ("failed" is false — the time is real and the error-cost line over-delivered at £1.9m against £1.8m), and option D — the midpoint — is the seductive disgrace: £4m corresponds to no state of the world; prudence means representing uncertainty honestly, not averaging incompatible claims into a number nobody defends. Option B's structure is the reporting standard your office should enforce portfolio-wide: decompose by benefit type, state each type's evidential status (banked and cash-releasing; validated-in-principle; unevidenced), and surface the conversion mechanism as the decision point — because whether £4.2m of diffuse time-savings becomes real value is a management choice (role redesign, establishment reduction, formal redeployment) that leadership must own or explicitly decline. At month 27 of a month-30 promise, that question is urgent, not academic. Benefits realisation lives or dies at the seam between operational fact and financial recognition; heads of portfolio who report the seam give leaders decisions, and those who report a single number give them comfort.

**Question 8 (hard) — Multi-constraint reasoning: the team design problem**

You are assigning four portfolio managers (PMs) to lead relationships with four programme clusters for the coming year. Constraints: (i) the Justice cluster requires its PM to hold current security clearance — only PM Asha and PM Dan hold it; (ii) PM Bella led the Health cluster for the past two years, and your rotation policy bars a third consecutive year on the same cluster; (iii) the Borders cluster's programme director has a documented unresolved conflict with PM Dan — pairing them is excluded; (iv) PM Carl is six months into the role and, by your development design, must be paired with the cluster whose governance is most mature — which is Health. Which assignment satisfies all constraints?

- A) Asha–Justice, Bella–Borders, Carl–Health, Dan–Corporate
- B) Dan–Justice, Bella–Corporate, Carl–Health, Asha–Borders
- C) Asha–Justice, Bella–Health, Carl–Corporate, Dan–Borders
- D) Both A and B satisfy all constraints; the choice between them is a judgement call on development and succession grounds.

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Solve systematically. Constraint (iv) fixes Carl–Health immediately, which also satisfies (ii) by displacement — Bella cannot have Health anyway. Constraint (i) restricts Justice to Asha or Dan. Test Asha–Justice: remaining PMs Bella and Dan take Borders and Corporate; (iii) bars Dan–Borders, forcing Dan–Corporate and Bella–Borders — exactly option A, all constraints checked: clearance ✓, rotation ✓ (Bella to Borders), conflict avoided ✓, Carl–Health ✓. Test Dan–Justice: remaining Asha and Bella take Borders and Corporate; (iii) is moot (Dan is placed), so either works — Bella–Corporate with Asha–Borders is option B: clearance ✓ (Dan cleared), rotation ✓, conflict ✓, Carl–Health ✓. Both A and B are fully feasible; option C fails on inspection (Bella–Health breaches rotation; Dan–Borders breaches the conflict exclusion). So the constraint set does not determine the answer — option D, and its second clause is the actual head-level content: when feasibility leaves a choice, the decision moves to unmodelled criteria, and naming them is your job. Here they are real: Asha–Justice keeps your most experienced cleared PM on the security-sensitive cluster but leaves Dan's conflict unaddressed by structure (he simply avoids Borders another year); Dan–Justice invests his clearance, gives Asha the Borders relationship — perhaps rebuilding cross-cluster depth — and buys time to resolve the conflict properly rather than route around it permanently. A leader who says "the constraints force this" when they don't has delegated judgement to a spreadsheet; one who says "two designs are feasible; here is why I choose this one" is doing the job. Building a skilled, motivated team is a constraint problem exactly until it isn't — and knowing where the arithmetic stops and leadership begins is the question's point.

**Question 9 (hard) — The leading indicator**

You track a basket of portfolio health indicators. Over four months: milestone delivery holds steady at 82–85%; spend tracks profile within 3%; but three quieter series move together — voluntary attrition among delivery-team staff rises from 8% to 14% annualised; average time-to-fill vacancies rises from 6 to 11 weeks; and the proportion of programme risks flagged as "resource-related" doubles from 12% to 24%. Programme RAG ratings remain unchanged. What is the strongest interpretation for your leadership insight report?

- A) Delivery is fine — the primary indicators (milestones, spend) are stable, and the people metrics belong to HR.
- B) The portfolio is consuming its resilience: three independent people-capacity series deteriorating in concert are a leading indicator, and stable milestones alongside rising resource risk flags suggest teams are currently absorbing the strain — so delivery metrics will follow with a lag, and the insight for leaders is a forecast ("expect delivery deterioration in six to nine months absent intervention on retention and recruitment"), not a reassurance.
- C) The attrition rise is within normal market variation; revisit next quarter.
- D) RAG ratings are unchanged, so programmes themselves see no problem; defer to their assessment.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The question tests the difference between reporting the present and providing insight into the future — which your role description names explicitly. Milestones and spend are lagging indicators: they record what the portfolio's capacity already accomplished. The three moving series are leading: attrition raises load on remaining staff; lengthening time-to-fill converts each departure into months of gap; doubled resource-risk flags are the programmes themselves reporting the strain in the only channel that doesn't yet trigger scrutiny. Their concert matters — three independent measurement routes agreeing is how weak signals earn strong conclusions — and the combination of stable output with rising strain flags has a specific meaning: absorption. Teams are covering gaps with overtime, deferred non-critical work, and the goodwill that attrition itself is spending; absorption has a horizon, and when it ends, milestone metrics move late and fast. Option B converts this into what leaders can actually use: a dated forecast with named intervention levers — precisely "insights into the portfolio's future so they can make informed decisions". Option A commits two head-level sins in one sentence: primary/secondary indicator confusion (lagging metrics ranked above leading ones) and boundary abdication (people capacity is delivery capacity; "belongs to HR" outsources your portfolio's core dependency). Option C treats each series alone — where any one might indeed be noise — and thereby discards the correlation that is the entire signal. Option D defers to RAG ratings whose stability the strain data explains: programmes rate delivery, and delivery hasn't broken yet; that is what "leading indicator" means. Foresight is the head of portfolio's distinctive product — anyone can report that the milestones were met.

**Question 10 (hard) — Prioritisation under a funding cut**

Mid-year, your portfolio's budget is cut 15% (£4.5m of £30m), effective in one quarter. Analysis of the four discretionary candidates for reduction: Programme W — £2m remaining-year cost, delivers statutory compliance capability eight weeks before its legal deadline; Programme X — £2.5m remaining-year cost, 75% complete, benefits £3m/yr starting in five months; Programme Y — £2.5m remaining-year cost, 15% complete, benefits £5m/yr starting in two years, no dependencies; Programme Z — £2m remaining-year cost, delivers the shared data platform that X's final integration (worth £0.8m of X's benefits) and two next-year initiatives depend on. Which reduction package is most defensible?

- A) Cut W and Z (£4m) plus trim £0.5m of Y — the two cheapest programmes take the burden.
- B) Cut Y (£2.5m) and find £2m through a negotiated descope of Z's platform to the minimum X and the committed next-year initiatives require — protecting the statutory programme absolutely, the near-complete benefits engine, and the dependency spine, while the least-committed, furthest-horizon investment pauses.
- C) Cut X and W (£4.5m exactly) — a clean match to the target.
- D) Spread 15% cuts evenly across all four programmes.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Establish the hierarchy the numbers impose. W is near-untouchable: statutory compliance with eight weeks of margin — cutting it converts a funding decision into a legal breach decision, which is not within a portfolio's gift to make casually (option A cuts it; option C cuts it while also cancelling X, the portfolio's best near-term value, at 75% complete with £3m/yr five months away — "clean arithmetic" selecting the two worst candidates). D — the even spread — is the abdication option: it treats prioritisation as unnecessary, imposes 15% pain on a statutory deadline and a programme in its final integration phase (where cuts bite disproportionately), and signals to leadership that the portfolio cannot rank its own contents; salami cuts are what portfolios do when their heads won't decide. Option B does the ranking the role exists for: Y pauses — not because it lacks value (£5m/yr is the portfolio's largest prize) but because it is the least irreversible choice: 15% sunk, no dependents, benefits two years out; a pause costs delay, not destruction, and repauses cleanly if funding returns. The residual £2m comes from Z via descope rather than cancellation, because Z is the dependency spine: killing it strands £0.8m of X's benefits and two committed next-year initiatives — its effective value includes its dependents' — but "minimum viable platform for the committed dependents" is a negotiable scope, and negotiated descope is where the £2m lives without breaking the chain. Note what B preserves that the others don't: every pound of near-term benefit, the legal position, and the option value of Y's future. The head-level disciplines on display: irreversibility ranks above headline value in cut decisions; enablers carry their dependents' worth; statutory obligations are constraints, not candidates; and even spreading is a decision to make everything slightly fail. This is defining strategy under pressure — which is when it counts.

**Question 11 (hard) — The metric that improved**

A year after you introduced delivery-confidence targets into portfolio managers' performance objectives ("portfolio average confidence ≥75%"), the average stands at 81%, up from 69%. Independent evidence: milestone delivery is flat year-on-year; two programmes had late-stage surprises that their high confidence scores never foreshadowed; and confidence submissions now cluster tightly at 78–84%, where they used to range 45–90%. What is the soundest conclusion?

- A) The target worked: confidence is up 12 points.
- B) The evidence pattern — improved scores, unimproved delivery, vanished variance, and failed predictions — indicates the metric was captured by the incentive: once scored against a target, confidence stopped measuring delivery risk and started measuring compliance; the fix is to remove confidence from individual performance objectives, restore it as a forecasting tool scored on calibration (did 80% confidence events happen ~80% of the time?), and find delivery incentives that reward honest early warning.
- C) The two late surprises were unforeseeable; otherwise the system works.
- D) Raise the target to 85% to drive further improvement.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Assemble the four evidence strands, because each alone is survivable and together they are diagnostic. Scores rose 12 points while the thing scores claim to predict — delivery — didn't move: the measure has decoupled from its referent. The range collapsed from 45 points to 6: genuine risk varies enormously across programmes, so tight clustering just above the 75% target line is the signature of target-seeking, not of convergent delivery reality (variance is information; its disappearance under incentive is the tell this guide's senior sibling calls the ritual number, now industrialised). And the two late surprises are the system failing at its only job — confidence scores exist to foreshadow exactly those events, and theirs didn't. This is Goodhart's law running at portfolio scale: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure — and the head-level twist is that you built the incentive, so the question doubles as a self-implication test. Option B carries the full response: diagnosis, mechanism, and the redesign — de-couple the metric from individual reward (people report honestly when honesty isn't taxed), rescore it on calibration, which is the one property a forecast can be honestly held to, and move performance incentives to behaviours that improve delivery rather than its self-description, such as early flagging of risks that later materialised. Option A reads the corrupted instrument at face value. Option C excuses the failed predictions individually, ignoring the pattern that explains them. Option D is the escalation of error: a higher target applied to a captured metric buys more capture — scores will cluster at 86–88 within two quarters, and the next surprises will be foreshadowed by nothing. Your duty to ensure the right controls are used includes retiring your own control when the evidence shows it now manufactures the signal it was meant to detect.

**Question 12 (hard) — The cascading commitment**

At the executive board, the chief operating officer proposes committing the organisation publicly to "all twelve citizen-facing services meeting the top accessibility standard within eighteen months". Your portfolio data: five services already comply; four can comply within 12–15 months inside existing plans; three run on a legacy platform whose accessibility remediation is technically unbounded — the platform's own replacement completes in 24 months, after which compliance is straightforward. The COO turns to you in the meeting: "Portfolio can deliver that, yes?" What is the most effective response?

- A) "Yes" — the ambition is right, the majority is achievable, and the details can be managed later.
- B) "No" — three services cannot make eighteen months, so the commitment is impossible.
- C) "Nine of twelve, yes, within the window. The remaining three sit on the legacy platform that replatforms at month 24 — for those, remediation before replacement would be spent twice. I'd support committing publicly to nine by eighteen months and all twelve by twenty-six, or alternatively fund interim assisted-digital provision for the three — I can bring the costed options within a week."
- D) "I'll need to take that away and come back in due course."

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The moment is engineered to test whether precision survives the room: a public commitment is being priced in real time, your data is loaded, and the socially fluent answers are the wrong ones at both ends. Option A mortgages the organisation's public word against three services you know cannot comply in-window — and note the mechanics of how that failure arrives: eighteen months from now the miss is public, the "yes" is minuted, and the remediation options will be exactly the ones available today, minus eighteen months. Option B is accurate and useless — "impossible" hands the board a wall where your data actually contains a bridge, and heads of portfolio who only ever say no stop being asked. Option D defers what your data can answer now; taken-away answers to in-meeting questions spend credibility too, and the commitment may be drafted before you come back. Option C is the executive-grade answer, and its anatomy is worth internalising: verified fact first (nine achievable — five done, four in-plan), the constraint named with its reason (double-spend on a dying platform — a value-for-money argument the board can weigh, not a technical mystification), and then the move that distinguishes counsel from commentary: two committable alternatives, both honest — the phased public commitment, or the interim-provision fund that buys the three services' users something real while the platform completes — with a dated offer of costed detail. The COO gets ambition that survives its own announcement; the board gets a decision instead of a dilemma; the three services' users get either a truthful date or interim help rather than a broken promise. Influencing and negotiating with senior stakeholders, as your framework has it, is rarely about resisting them — it is about giving their ambition a load-bearing structure in the thirty seconds after "yes?" This is also communicating complex recommendations at strategic level in its purest form: fact, constraint, options, offer — four sentences, in the room.

### Preparation tips

- **Practise on your own instruments.** Your escalation flows, delegation rules, indicator baskets and incentive designs are this section's question bank made real. Run the checks: what do my silences hide, what do my thresholds teach, which of my metrics has stopped varying, which average conceals my largest risk?
- **Drill the designer's second question.** For every rule or target you review this month: first "what does it require?", then "what will it teach people to do?" Control design fails at the second question far more often than the first.
- **Rehearse decomposition before aggregation.** Any average, total or net figure crossing your desk: decompose by size, by direction, by evidential status — then decide what the aggregate may honestly say. The executive pack is a lens; keep it ground true.
- **Time-box strategic answers.** The in-meeting "portfolio can deliver that, yes?" moment rewards prepared structures: fact, constraint, options, dated offer. Practise compressing a loaded position into four sentences — it is a trainable skill, not a temperament.
- **Distinguish forced from chosen.** When constraints leave multiple feasible answers — team designs, cut packages, schedules — say so, and name the judgement criteria that decide. "The analysis forces this" is only ever true when it does.
- **Audit your leading indicators quarterly.** If everything you report is lagging, you are reporting history. Build and rehearse the strain-signal basket — attrition, time-to-fill, resource-risk flags, correction rates — until forecasting deterioration feels as natural as recording it.
- **Review errors as the norm-setter.** Your team calibrates against your reasoning. When practice reveals a gap — a promoted possibility, a misread modal, a comfortable aggregate — name it aloud in your next team session. Leaders who audit themselves publicly license rigour in everyone else.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Reading your inbox as the portfolio.** What escalates to you is filtered by authority, fear and habit. The categories that never arrive need auditing precisely because they never arrive.
- **Signing the average that inverts the story.** Unweighted means over unequal programmes, netted variances, headline trajectories that hide the largest mover — aggregation choices are editorial choices; make them consciously and disclose them.
- **Accepting systems' self-explanations.** "We fund the strongest cases" from the committee whose bias is being measured, "unforeseeable" from the process that failed to foresee — when the controls contradict the account, the account is the hypothesis, not the data.
- **Designing rules without modelling the response.** Thresholds invite splitting, frequency caps invite bundling, scored metrics invite clustering. Every control reshapes behaviour; anticipate the reshaping or inherit it.
- **Ranking lagging indicators above leading ones.** Milestones and spend record the past. Attrition, vacancy lag and strain flags forecast the future — and forecasting is your distinctive duty.
- **Letting arithmetic make leadership's choices.** Feasibility analysis narrows; it rarely decides. Where multiple designs survive the constraints, the criteria that choose are yours to name and own.
- **Answering the room instead of the record.** In-meeting pressure optimises for the fluent yes, the safe no, or the deferral. The answer that serves is the one still true when the commitment matures — build it from fact, constraint and options, and give it in the room.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures accuracy and fluency with the quantitative material a head of portfolio certifies and decides on: multi-programme funding tables, weighted portfolio metrics, benefits trajectories, capacity economics, unit-cost comparisons, and the forecast arithmetic behind the insights you provide to leaders. Formats present tables, charts or data narratives followed by calculation and interpretation items — with head-level instruments weighting interpretation heavily: selecting the correct measure, detecting the misleading aggregate, and sizing what a figure can honestly support.

Typical format: online, timed, 20 to 35 minutes, 15 to 20 questions, calculator permitted, distractors constructed from predictable errors — wrong bases, averaged rates, conflated measures, boundary slips. Expect two-step questions where the arithmetic is modest but choosing the right arithmetic is the test.

The role relevance is precise. At head level you rarely compute figures from source — your team does that — but you are the last check before numbers become decisions: you sign the pack, present the allocation, and answer the "so can we afford it?" question in the room. That position demands a specific numerical capability: rapid verification (recomputing a chain fast enough to catch the error before signing), aggregation judgement (knowing which summary honestly represents unequal parts), and forecast discipline (holding every projection to its assumptions). It also demands the coaching version of all three, since you set the analytical standards your portfolio managers and analysts work to, and the professional development of the portfolio community — explicitly your duty — includes its numerical craft. Every question below shows complete working; the explanations are written to be reused as teaching material with your team.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Weighted aggregation** maps to **Communicating analysis and insight** and your duty to provide leaders with insights: portfolio-level figures aggregate profoundly unequal programmes, and choosing — and disclosing — the weighting basis is the difference between a lens and a distortion.
- **Funding and allocation arithmetic** maps to **Planning** and **Strategic thinking**: cut packages, prioritisation scores, marginal-value comparisons and affordability envelopes are the numbers with which strategy is actually executed; defining strategies means being fluent in their arithmetic consequences.
- **Forecasting and trajectory analysis** map to **Planning** and your duty to provide leaders with insights into the portfolio's future: run-rates with step changes, benefits realisation curves and capacity strain projections convert present data into decision-grade foresight.
- **Unit-cost and benchmark reasoning** map to **Governance and assurance**: ensuring the right controls are used includes the financial controls — and challenging a benchmark, a supplier rate or an overhead figure requires knowing exactly what its denominator contains.
- **Verification at speed** maps to **Governance and assurance** and your leadership of tracking: the pack you sign is the organisation's picture of its portfolio; recomputing its load-bearing figures quickly is the control of last resort, and it is yours.
- **Presentation-of-numbers judgement** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**: influencing and negotiating with senior stakeholders runs on numerical credibility — the right figure, on the declared base, with its assumption attached, produced at conversational speed.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Portfolio spend share**

Your portfolio's annual budget is £36m across three clusters: Citizen Services £16.2m, Case Working £12.6m, Corporate £7.2m. What percentage of the portfolio budget does Case Working hold?

- A) 30%
- B) 35%
- C) 36%
- D) 40%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Share = part ÷ whole × 100 = 12.6 ÷ 36. Compute: 12.6 ÷ 36 = 0.35, so 35% — option B. Mental route: 10% of 36 is 3.6; 12.6 ÷ 3.6 = 3.5 tens of percent, i.e. 35%. Cross-check by completing the distribution: Citizen Services 16.2 ÷ 36 = 45%; Corporate 7.2 ÷ 36 = 20%; and 45 + 35 + 20 = 100 ✓ — the reconciliation habit that catches both your own slips and your team's. Distractor B-adjacent values are single-step errors (35 misread as 36; 12.6 ÷ 31.5 ≈ 40% from a mistyped total). Trivial arithmetic, but the head-level use is real: cluster shares are the frame for every allocation conversation ("Corporate holds a fifth of the money"), and leaders remember the shares you quote — quote them reconciled.

**Question 2 (easy) — Percentage change with direction**

Portfolio headcount charged to programmes fell from 480 FTE to 432 FTE over the year. What was the percentage change?

- A) A fall of 10%
- B) A fall of 11.1%
- C) A fall of 4.8%
- D) A rise of 10%

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Change = 432 − 480 = −48 FTE. Percentage change = change ÷ original × 100 = 48 ÷ 480 = 0.10 — a fall of 10%: option A. Distractor B is the wrong-base error (48 ÷ 432 ≈ 11.1%), dividing by the new value; C quotes the raw 48 as if it were 4.8%; D fails the direction check. The verification habit: 10% of 480 is 48 exactly — original times rate reproduces the change, confirming the base was right. At your level the figure would immediately face the interpretation question the stem doesn't ask but the boardroom will: is a 10% reduction efficiency (same delivery, leaner teams), delivery contraction (fewer programmes), or strain (vacancies unfilled — recall the leading-indicator question from the cognitive section)? The arithmetic is one line; knowing that the line is the start of the analysis, not the end, is the head-level difference.

**Question 3 (easy) — Reading a funding table under time pressure**

The investment committee table shows approved funding by year:

| Programme | 2025–26 (£m) | 2026–27 (£m) | 2027–28 (£m) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alder | 3.2 | 4.1 | 2.2 |
| Birch | 2.8 | 3.0 | 3.4 |
| Cedar | 1.5 | 2.4 | 4.0 |
| Rowan | 2.5 | 1.5 | 0.9 |

Which programme's funding profile falls in every successive year?

- A) Alder
- B) Birch
- C) Cedar
- D) Rowan

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Test each row for strict year-on-year decline. Alder: 3.2 → 4.1 rises, then 4.1 → 2.2 falls — not monotonic. Birch: 2.8 → 3.0 → 3.4, rising throughout. Cedar: 1.5 → 2.4 → 4.0, rising throughout. Rowan: 2.5 → 1.5 → 0.9, falling in both transitions — option D. The table-discipline lesson survives seniority: profile shape questions ("which is ramping down?") are answered by scanning transitions, not endpoints — Alder's endpoints also fall (3.2 to 2.2), which is exactly the trap for endpoint-scanners. Profile shapes carry portfolio meaning worth reading fluently: Rowan is closing down or completing; Cedar is ramping up towards its delivery peak in 2027–28; Birch grows steadily; Alder spikes mid-profile — a delivery surge or a re-plan. When you present multi-year funding to leadership, narrating shapes ("one closing, one ramping, two steady") communicates more than reading cell values — and it starts with transition-by-transition reading done right.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Spend-weighted versus unweighted confidence**

Five programmes report delivery confidence:

| Programme | Annual spend (£m) | Confidence (%) |
|---|---|---|
| P1 | 12 | 55 |
| P2 | 6 | 80 |
| P3 | 4 | 85 |
| P4 | 5 | 70 |
| P5 | 3 | 90 |

What are the unweighted and spend-weighted average confidence figures, and which should lead the executive pack?

- A) Unweighted 76%, weighted 70%; lead with the weighted figure, showing both — the money is markedly less safe than the programme count suggests.
- B) Unweighted 76%, weighted 76%; either may lead.
- C) Unweighted 70%, weighted 76%; lead with unweighted.
- D) Unweighted 76%, weighted 72%; lead with unweighted, as weighting is a presentational choice.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Unweighted: (55 + 80 + 85 + 70 + 90) ÷ 5 = 380 ÷ 5 = 76%. Weighted: products first — 12 × 55 = 660; 6 × 80 = 480; 4 × 85 = 340; 5 × 70 = 350; 3 × 90 = 270. Sum of products: 660 + 480 = 1,140; + 340 = 1,480; + 350 = 1,830; + 270 = 2,100. Sum of weights: 12 + 6 + 4 + 5 + 3 = 30. Weighted average: 2,100 ÷ 30 = 70% exactly. Option A. Verify the direction logic as well as the numbers: P1 alone holds 40% of the spend (12 of 30) at the lowest confidence (55%), so the money-weighted view must sit well below the programme-count view — the four smaller, happier programmes cannot outvote the pound-weighted anchor. That six-point gap, not either single figure, is the executive insight: "76% average confidence" and "70% spend-weighted confidence, dragged down by the largest programme" tell materially different stories, and the pack must lead with the one that answers the board's actual question — how safe is the money? — while showing both bases explicitly and naming P1 as the driver. Option D's "weighting is presentational" is the error worth stamping out in your team's craft standards: basis selection is analytical, not cosmetic, and undisclosed basis-switching is how portfolio reporting quietly misleads. This is the numeric twin of the cognitive section's Question 3, met from the computation side — and the standing fix is the same: weighted and unweighted shown together, divergence explained, largest movers named.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Marginal value in an allocation decision**

You have £1.2m of unallocated year-end funding. Two bids compete. Bid One: £1.2m to accelerate Programme K, bringing £2.4m/yr of benefits forward by four months — one-off value of 4/12 × £2.4m. Bid Two: £1.2m to extend Programme L's rollout to three additional regions, adding £0.5m/yr of recurring benefits from next year, indefinitely. Using a three-year value horizon, which bid delivers more value, and by how much?

- A) Bid One, by £0.3m — acceleration always beats extension.
- B) Bid Two: £1.5m versus £0.8m over three years — a £0.7m advantage.
- C) They are equal at £1.5m each.
- D) Bid One: £2.4m versus £1.5m — a £0.9m advantage.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Value each bid on the stated horizon. Bid One's value is one-off: bringing £2.4m/yr forward four months banks 4/12 × £2.4m = £0.8m — once; nothing recurs, because after the acceleration the benefits arrive as they always would have. Bid Two adds £0.5m/yr recurring: over the three-year horizon, 3 × £0.5m = £1.5m. Comparison: £1.5m − £0.8m = £0.7m in Bid Two's favour — option B. Distractor D mistakes the accelerated stream's annual rate (£2.4m) for the acceleration's value — the commonest error in acceleration cases: the £2.4m was coming anyway; only the four months of earlier arrival is new value. Distractor A asserts a rule ("acceleration always beats") in place of arithmetic. The head-level judgement wrapped around the calculation: horizon choice decides such comparisons — on a one-year horizon Bid One wins (£0.8m versus £0.5m); recurring value compounds with horizon length while acceleration value is fixed — so state the horizon, and test sensitivity to it, in any allocation paper. Real decisions would also weigh delivery risk on each bid and the credibility of "indefinitely". But the disciplined core stands: value the increment, not the stream it touches; a bid's worth is what changes because you funded it. That sentence is worth issuing to every business-case author in your portfolio.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Run-rate, step change and the affordability question**

Your portfolio's staff-cost run rate is £1.5m/month through month 6 of the financial year (£9.0m spent). From month 7: a negotiated contractor conversion saves £150k/month, but a newly approved programme adds £250k/month of team costs from month 9. Annual staff budget: £18.5m. What is the forecast full-year position?

- A) £18.0m — £0.5m under budget
- B) £18.1m — £0.4m under budget
- C) £18.5m — exactly on budget
- D) £18.7m — £0.2m over budget

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Build the forecast in segments. Months 1–6: £9.0m (given). Months 7–12 baseline: 6 × £1.5m = £9.0m. Adjustment one — conversion saving from month 7: months 7–12 inclusive is 6 months × £150k = £0.9m saved. Adjustment two — new programme from month 9: months 9–12 is 4 months × £250k = £1.0m added. Forecast: 9.0 + 9.0 − 0.9 + 1.0 = £18.1m. Against £18.5m budget: £0.4m under — option B. The distractors are month-counting slips: A applies the new programme for 3 months or the saving for 7; D applies the addition from month 7 (6 × 250k = 1.5m). Month-window errors are the characteristic failure of step-change forecasting, and the defence is the segment table — write each effect with its start month and month count before multiplying anything. The head-level layer: £0.4m of headroom is 2.2% of budget — thin cover for a forecast carrying two behavioural assumptions (the saving lands on schedule; the new programme ramps exactly on month 9), so the affordability answer to leadership is "under by £0.4m on current assumptions, both of which have slipped-start risk — headroom rounds to one month of the new programme's costs." Numbers with their assumptions and their thinness declared: that is what "insights leaders can act on" means in financial form.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Unit cost with an honest denominator**

Challenged that your portfolio office is expensive, you assemble the figures: office annual cost £1.8m; portfolio annual spend under management £36m; the office also directly runs the assurance regime for 5 programmes in another portfolio, spend £9m, recharged at cost £0.3m (already netted out of the £1.8m). A benchmark report quotes comparable portfolio offices at 4.5–5.5% of spend under management. What is your office's honest comparable figure?

- A) 5.0% — £1.8m over £36m.
- B) 4.0% — £1.8m over £45m, counting all spend the office serves.
- C) 4.7% — £2.1m gross cost over £45m total spend served, aligning both numerator and denominator to the full service scope.
- D) 4.2% — £1.5m over £36m, netting the recharge again.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The trap is mismatched scope between numerator and denominator, and each wrong option commits a version of it. The office's full service scope is £36m + £9m = £45m of spend served, at a gross cost of £1.8m + £0.3m = £2.1m (the recharge is real cost of serving the second portfolio; it was netted from your budget line, not from the resources consumed). Aligned ratio: 2.1 ÷ 45 = 0.04666… ≈ 4.7% — option C, sitting at the favourable end of the 4.5–5.5% benchmark band. Option A divides own-portfolio cost by own-portfolio spend but leaves the second portfolio's service unrepresented on both sides — defensible only if you also disclose the recharged activity, and vulnerable to the challenge "your office does more than this ratio's scope". Option B is the flattering mismatch: full denominator (£45m), netted numerator (£1.8m) — cost scope narrower than spend scope, understating the ratio; it is the version a motivated presenter drifts into, which is exactly why you should never present it. Option D double-nets. The transferable rule for every benchmark, unit cost and overhead ratio that crosses your desk: interrogate what the denominator contains, then force the numerator to the same scope — and when challenging others' benchmarks (a supplier's day rate, a comparator's overhead claim), the denominator question is almost always where the body is buried. Ensuring the right controls includes the financial ones; this is that duty in ratio form.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Benefits trajectory versus business case**

A flagship programme's business case promised cumulative benefits of £10m by end of year 3, profiled £1m / £3m / £6m across years 1–3. Actuals: year 1 delivered £0.8m; year 2 delivered £2.1m. For the programme to hit the cumulative £10m, what must year 3 deliver, and what does that requirement imply?

- A) £6.0m — exactly the original year-3 profile; the programme remains on plan.
- B) £7.1m — 18% above the original year-3 profile; the shortfall to date (£1.1m) has been pushed into the final year, so the recovery assumption needs explicit evidence before leadership treats £10m as still credible.
- C) £4.9m — the profile adjusts downward automatically.
- D) £7.1m — but cumulative targets are met by definition if each year is "broadly close".

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Cumulative delivered: 0.8 + 2.1 = £2.9m against a two-year profiled cumulative of 1 + 3 = £4.0m — a £1.1m shortfall (27.5% behind profile). Year 3 requirement: 10 − 2.9 = £7.1m, versus the profiled £6.0m — an 18% uplift on the year the programme already expected to be its most demanding (7.1 ÷ 6.0 = 1.183). Option B carries both the number and its meaning. The pattern has a name in portfolio circles — the hockey stick: every year of under-delivery steepens the final-year claim while the headline ("still targeting £10m") stays serene, and the arithmetic of cumulative targets guarantees it — shortfalls never disappear, they migrate to the remaining periods and compound the required rate there. The head-level response the question rehearses: recompute the implied remaining-period requirement whenever any cumulative target is reaffirmed, then interrogate the recovery mechanism — what specifically delivers 18% above the plan's own best year? New rollout scale? Banked-but-unrecognised benefits? Or hope? Two years of 20–30% under-delivery is evidence about the delivery system's capability; a credible £10m defence must explain why year 3's system differs from years 1–2's. Absent that, the honest leadership line is a range: "£8.5–9m on current trajectory; £10m requires the recovery case to hold." Options A and C misread the arithmetic; D waves it away. Insights into the portfolio's future are made of exactly this: trajectory versus promise, with the implied ask made visible.

**Question 9 (hard) — Capacity economics of the portfolio team**

Your portfolio office employs 9 portfolio managers/analysts at a loaded cost of £80k each. Analysis shows 30% of their combined time goes to manually assembling reports that an automation investment of £250k (one-off) plus £40k/yr (running) would reduce to 10%. Released time would be redeployed to assurance and forward analysis, not headcount reduction. In year 1 and in steady state (year 2 onwards), what is the value released, and does the investment pay back within two years on time-value alone?

- A) Year 1: £144k value against £290k cost — no; two-year total £288k against £330k — payback just missed.
- B) Year 1: £144k against £290k — no; but two-year value £288k against £330k means payback arrives early in year 3, so the investment fails a strict two-year test while remaining sound on any longer horizon.
- C) Both A and B state the same arithmetic; the difference is the recommendation — and B's is the defensible one.
- D) Year 1: £216k against £290k; payback inside two years.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Work the value first. Combined staff cost: 9 × £80k = £720k/yr. Time released: 30% − 10% = 20 percentage points of the team's time, worth 0.20 × £720k = £144k/yr — from year 1, since automation and redeployment run in-year (assume full-year effect for this exercise). Costs: year 1 = £250k + £40k = £290k; year 2 = £40k. Two-year totals: value 2 × £144k = £288k; cost £290k + £40k = £330k. Strict two-year payback: £288k < £330k — missed by £42k; year 3 adds £144k against £40k, taking cumulative value past cumulative cost early in year 3 (cumulative: value £432k versus cost £370k by end year 3). Now the options: A and D differ on arithmetic — D's £216k misreads the released time as 30 points rather than the 30-to-10 reduction of 20 points, a boundary error worth naming because "reduce 30% to 10%" reads as "save 30%" to a fast reader. A's arithmetic is correct; B's arithmetic is identical to A's — the difference is that A stops at "payback just missed" while B completes the analysis: a strict two-year test fails; any three-year or longer view passes comfortably, and the released time is redeployed to assurance and foresight — capabilities your role is accountable for strengthening — which the pure time-value calculation doesn't even count. Option C is therefore right: same numbers, and B's framing is the defensible recommendation, because investment appraisal that halts at an arbitrary payback line, when the asset's life obviously exceeds it, is arithmetic without judgement. The meta-lesson is the head-level one: check the numbers, then check what question the numbers were allowed to answer. Ensuring the portfolio team meets the organisation's needs includes investing in the team's own productivity — with the case made honestly, horizon declared.

**Question 10 (hard) — The two-baseline funding claim**

A programme director tells the investment committee: "We've delivered 15% under budget this year." Your office's data: original annual budget £8.0m; in-year re-baseline (approved, after descoping a workstream) reduced the budget to £7.2m; actual spend £6.8m. Evaluate the claim.

- A) The claim is correct: £6.8m against £8.0m is 15% under.
- B) The claim is correct against the original budget (£1.2m ÷ £8.0m = 15%) but conflates two effects: £0.8m (10 points) came from an approved scope reduction — less was bought — and only £0.4m against the re-baselined £7.2m (5.6%) is genuine spend efficiency; the committee should hear both figures with the descope made explicit.
- C) The claim is false: the only valid comparison is £6.8m against £7.2m, so the underspend is 5.6%.
- D) The claim is false: re-baselining eliminates any right to quote the original budget.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Verify the arithmetic of each frame before judging the rhetoric. Against original: 8.0 − 6.8 = £1.2m; 1.2 ÷ 8.0 = 15% ✓ — the director's number is not fabricated. Against the approved re-baseline: 7.2 − 6.8 = £0.4m; 0.4 ÷ 7.2 = 5.55… ≈ 5.6%. The decomposition is the analytical heart: of the £1.2m headline, £0.8m is the budget reduction that travelled with the descope — the organisation spent less because it bought less, which is a scope decision, not a spending achievement — and £0.4m is spending less than the budget for what was actually bought: the only component that evidences cost efficiency. Option B holds all of it: the claim is arithmetically true, rhetorically loaded, and the committee needs the split — because "15% under budget" invites the inference "this team delivers cheaply", and two-thirds of the figure cannot support that inference. Options C and D over-correct into declaring the claim false: quoting performance against original baselines is not illegitimate (indeed the numeric section's re-baselining questions elsewhere in this series insist on original-baseline visibility for delivery); what is illegitimate is quoting it unlabelled, letting scope reduction masquerade as efficiency. The symmetric discipline matters at your level because you police it in both directions: programmes quote original baselines when flattering (underspend after descope) and current baselines when flattering (delivery percentage after descope) — the house rule your office should enforce is both baselines, always labelled, with scope changes stated. That single reporting standard, consistently applied, removes a whole class of committee-room illusions — and issuing such standards is exactly your governance-and-assurance duty.

**Question 11 (hard) — Reading the distribution, not the mean**

Your fourteen programmes' schedule variances (days late per completed milestone, current year) are: 0, 0, 1, 1, 2, 2, 2, 3, 3, 4, 5, 6, 28, 41. The pack drafts: "Mean schedule variance: 7 days (median 2.5), portfolio delivery broadly timely." What should the pack actually say?

- A) The draft is fine: both mean and median are quoted.
- B) Twelve of fourteen programmes run 0–6 days late — genuinely timely — while two extreme outliers (28 and 41 days) both inflate the mean nearly threefold and are themselves the finding: the pack should report the timely core and name the two outliers for individual attention, because averaging a bimodal reality into one number hides both truths.
- C) Remove the outliers and report a mean of 2.4 days.
- D) The median alone should be reported, as it is robust to outliers.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Compute to see the structure. Sum: 0+0+1+1+2+2+2+3+3+4+5+6 = 29 for the first twelve; +28 +41 = 98; mean = 98 ÷ 14 = 7 days. Without the two outliers: 29 ÷ 12 ≈ 2.4 days. Median (14 values): average of 7th and 8th = (2 + 3) ÷ 2 = 2.5 ✓. So the arithmetic in the draft is right and the sentence is still wrong, because the distribution is the story: this is not "a portfolio running about a week late" (what mean 7 implies) nor simply "a timely portfolio" (what median 2.5 implies) — it is a timely portfolio of twelve plus two programmes in schedule distress, 4× to 7× beyond the pack's own mean. Option B reports the reality at the resolution decisions need: leadership does nothing useful with "7 days average"; it does something very useful with "twelve timely; Programmes N and M at 28 and 41 days — reviews commissioned." Option C deletes the finding to purify the average — outlier exclusion is legitimate for estimating the typical, never for reporting the whole. Option D's robustness logic gets it exactly backwards for governance purposes: the median is robust to outliers precisely because it ignores them, and the outliers are what governance exists to catch. The standing rule for your pack: any summary statistic over a distribution with material outliers must be accompanied by the outliers, named. Means are for homogeneous data; portfolios are rarely homogeneous — which is why heads of portfolio read distributions, and why "broadly timely" is the kind of phrase your review pen should strike on sight.

**Question 12 (hard) — The in-room affordability check**

In the investment committee, a bid team states: "Our programme needs £5.4m over three years — £2.6m, £1.6m, £1.2m — but delivers £2.8m annual savings from year 2, so it's self-funding within the period." You have thirty seconds while the chair looks at you. What does quick arithmetic show, and what do you say?

- A) "Confirmed — cumulative savings of £5.6m by end of year 3 exceed the £5.4m cost."
- B) "Broadly right on the period total — £5.6m savings versus £5.4m cost — but not self-funding as it runs: cumulative cash breaks even only late in year 3, after a peak funding requirement of about £4.2m in year 2 wait — £2.6m + £1.6m = £4.2m spent by end year 2 against £2.8m saved: net £1.4m exposed — so the committee is being asked to carry roughly £1.4m of peak exposure, and the honest framing is 'repays within the period', not 'self-funding'."
- C) "The claim fails: £2.8m of savings is less than £5.4m of cost."
- D) "Savings claims from year 2 are never credible; discount them and assess the bid at full £5.4m cost."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Run the cash profile, year by year — the thirty-second skill the question exists to drill. Year 1: cost £2.6m, savings £0 → cumulative net −£2.6m. Year 2: cost £1.6m, savings £2.8m → year net +£1.2m; cumulative −£1.4m. Year 3: cost £1.2m, savings £2.8m → year net +£1.6m; cumulative +£0.2m. So: period totals do favour the bid (savings 2 × £2.8m = £5.6m versus cost £5.4m — option A's arithmetic is right as far as it goes), but the programme is cash-negative until late in year 3, with peak exposure of £1.4m at end of year 2 — money the organisation must actually find and carry, whatever the period total says. "Self-funding" is therefore the wrong claim in the way that matters to an approving committee: someone funds the trough. Option B — despite its deliberately thinking-aloud syntax — is the answer that serves: it confirms what is true (repays within the period), corrects the load-bearing word (self-funding → repays), and quantifies the exposure the committee is really being asked to approve. Option C compares one year's savings to three years' cost — a category error in the sceptical direction. Option D substitutes a prejudice for an assessment; year-2 savings claims deserve scrutiny (see Question 8's trajectory discipline), not blanket dismissal. The head-level craft: period totals answer "is it worth it?"; cash profiles answer "can we carry it?" — committees need both, bid teams reliably present only the first, and the person expected to produce the second in the room, at conversational speed, is you. Peak exposure, break-even timing, and the corrected verb: thirty seconds, three facts, decision served.

### Preparation tips

- **Drill the verification toolkit at speed.** Part-over-whole, change-over-original, products-over-weights, segment-built forecasts, cash profiles year by year. At head level the differentiator is not knowing the methods but running them fast enough to catch errors in the room — practise against a clock.
- **Reconcile every distribution you sign.** Shares to 100%, breakdowns to totals, weighted and unweighted views both computed. The five-second reconciliation is the last control before your signature makes a number official.
- **Interrogate denominators professionally.** Every benchmark, unit cost and overhead ratio: what does the denominator contain, and does the numerator match its scope? Practise on the next three ratios anyone shows you.
- **Decompose headline claims into their components.** Underspends into scope-versus-efficiency; benefits into banked-versus-claimed; savings into period-total-versus-cash-profile. The component split is almost always where the decision-relevant truth lives.
- **Recompute implied requirements on every reaffirmed target.** Cumulative promises minus actuals to date equals the remaining-period ask — make computing it a reflex, and make explaining it a house standard for your programmes.
- **Read distributions before statistics.** Sort the values, eyeball the shape, find the outliers — then decide what summary is honest. Teach your analysts the same order; packs written distribution-first need fewer corrections.
- **Rehearse the thirty-second check.** Take real bids and practise producing peak exposure, break-even year and the corrected claim at conversational speed. The committee-room moment is a performance skill built on arithmetic, and both halves respond to rehearsal.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Wrong-base division.** Change over the new value, cost over a mismatched denominator, savings over the wrong period. The oldest error, still the most common — name the base aloud before dividing.
- **Averaging the unequal.** Unweighted means over unequal programmes, averaged rates over unequal quarters, single statistics over bimodal distributions. Weight it, total it, or decompose it — but never let one number carry a mixed story unlabelled.
- **Valuing the stream instead of the increment.** Acceleration bids claim the whole benefit stream; extension bids claim gross rather than additional value. Fund what changes because you funded it.
- **Boundary and window slips.** "Reduce 30% to 10%" saves 20 points; step changes apply from their start month; month 9 to month 12 is four months. Segment tables prevent what mental arithmetic invites.
- **Period totals masquerading as cash.** "Self-funding over the period" can hide a seven-figure trough. Profile the cash before endorsing the adjective.
- **Unlabelled baselines.** Underspend against original budget after a descope, delivery percentage against re-based scope — both true, both misleading alone. Both baselines, always labelled: make it the house rule.
- **Verifying the arithmetic and not the question.** A payback calculation can be flawless and the two-year test still be the wrong test. After checking the numbers, check what they were allowed to answer — that second check is the head-level one.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment measures exactness in reading, inference and written judgement over the material a head of portfolio lives in: strategy documents, spending frameworks, assurance reports, board minutes, ministerial correspondence, machinery-of-government notices, and the policy language whose interpretation your teams inherit from you. The core format presents passages followed by statements to classify as **True** (supported), **False** (contradicted) or **Cannot say** (indeterminable from the text); head-level variants weight inference, assumption-detection, argument evaluation, and drafting judgement — which version of a sentence should go to the board.

Typical format: 15 to 25 minutes, four to six passages, twenty-plus items, distractors built on modal shifts, scope migration, dropped conditions, and outcome/process conflation. The governing discipline is strict textualism: the passage is the universe; professional knowledge waits outside.

At head level, this assessment measures something your role makes institutionally significant: you are where authoritative interpretation happens. When a portfolio manager asks whether the spend framework permits an approach, when a programme quotes strategy at a prioritisation forum, when a director's summary hardens your caveated assessment — the reading that settles the matter is yours, and it propagates: your interpretations become your community's precedents. The drafting dimension has equal weight: the insight reports, committee papers and single-sentence positions you produce are the interface between the portfolio and the leaders deciding about it, and imprecision at that interface is imprecision with organisational consequences. The passages below are drawn from these registers, and every explanation works both the reading and its institutional stakes — including how you coach the same precision into the portfolio managers you develop.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Exactness with frameworks and controls** maps to **Governance and assurance**: ensuring the right controls are used presupposes reading controls rightly — the mandate/permission/silence architecture, the exception that governs the hard case, the definition clause that decides the dispute.
- **Inference discipline** maps to **Communicating analysis and insight** and your duty to provide leaders with insights: the boundary between what evidence establishes and what it suggests is the boundary between reporting and speculation — and at your level, between an insight and a liability.
- **Assumption and argument evaluation** map to **Strategic thinking**: defining strategies and evaluating whether current strategies meet business requirements is textual work — finding what a strategy's promise silently depends on, where an argument's weight actually rests, and which clause will fail first under pressure.
- **Drafting judgement** maps to **Communicating analysis and insight** and **Stakeholder relationship management**: making complex recommendations at tactical and strategic level, and negotiating with senior stakeholders, both run on sentences that carry every governing condition in plain words — yours, and the ones you correct.
- **Interpretive leadership** maps to your duty to support the professional development of the portfolio community: your readings train your managers' readings; precision, coached explicitly, is a capability you multiply.

- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: C**

- B) False

According to the passage, when does the sending department's portfolio office cease to hold assurance responsibility for the transferring programmes?

**Question 5 (hard) — True, false or cannot say**

- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) False, because assurance and contracts must transfer together

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Construct the scenario from the passage's own machinery. The passage supports the scenario's possibility but cannot guarantee the prediction: Cannot say — option C. Option D invents a transfer-together doctrine the passage explicitly refutes (the tracks are expressly separate). The discipline being tested is the hardest one in verbal reasoning and in governance foresight alike: distinguishing "the rules as written produce this outcome in that scenario" (true) from "this outcome will obtain" (unknowable — rules get amended, scenarios shift). **Question 6 (moderate) — The statutory anchor**

Which function does the final sentence — "Nothing in these arrangements alters the statutory duties of either accounting officer" — perform in the note?

- A) It is boilerplate with no operative effect.
- B) It subordinates the transfer arrangements to a fixed accountability layer: however responsibility, assurance and contracts move between departments, each accounting officer's statutory duties persist unaltered — so no interim arrangement can be read as diluting either officer's personal accountability, and any conflict between the arrangements and those duties resolves in the duties' favour.
- C) It transfers statutory duties to the receiving accounting officer on 1 April.
- D) It makes the accounting officers responsible for completing novation by September.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Savings clauses of this form — "nothing in X alters Y" — are hierarchy statements: they declare that the instrument being read sits beneath something it cannot touch, and they instruct the reader to resolve any apparent conflict in favour of the preserved layer. Here the preserved layer is each accounting officer's statutory duties (in UK government practice, personal accountability to Parliament for regularity, propriety and value for money), and the clause's work is precisely to prevent the interim's complexity — dual reporting, split tracks, event-gated handovers — from being argued as having diluted anyone's personal accountability: option B. Option A ("boilerplate") mistakes ubiquity for emptiness; the clause is common because the risk it forecloses is common — interim arrangements are exactly where "I thought accountability had moved" gets pleaded. Options C and D read the clause as doing things (transferring, tasking) that its grammar — "nothing... alters" — is built to deny. The head-level payoff is a reading habit: in any governance document, locate the savings and precedence clauses first, because they tell you which parts of the text can bend and which cannot — and when you draft interim arrangements yourself (you will), the savings clause is how you keep improvised machinery from accidentally rewiring permanent accountability. Note also your own position in this passage: the interim reporting line to two accounting officers exists precisely because their unaltered duties both require assurance sight of the programmes. The clause is why your dual report is a necessity, not a courtesy.

**Question 12 (moderate) — Drafting the community briefing**

You are briefing your portfolio managers on the strategy. Which summary should open the briefing?

- A) "From now on, we grow our own: internal candidates get priority for portfolio manager roles, so get your teams through the scheme."
- B) "The strategy commits us to building internal leadership capability — every team member gets a documented development plan, participants are entitled to up to fifteen days' development time with deferrals over five days needing my approval, and within three years the aim is that half of appointments can be won by scheme graduates on merit. Two boundaries: individual appointments remain strictly merit-based with no internal preference, and external recruitment stays open at every level."
- C) "There's a new people strategy; the main point for you is more paperwork around development plans."
- D) "Development release is now capped at fifteen days and I must approve any deferrals."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A community briefing succeeds when every listener leaves able to apply the strategy correctly to their next real decision — a development conversation, a release request, an appointment panel — and option B alone equips all three. Audit its content against the passage: the supply-side commitments (plans, fifteen days, the over-five-days approval trigger stated at its correct threshold), the aggregate target framed exactly as Question 10 requires ("can be won... on merit" — the briefing pre-corrects the misreading your panels would otherwise import), and the two boundary clauses that prevent the two likely operational errors. Option A opens with precisely the unlawful misreading the strategy's final clause exists to prevent — briefed by their head of portfolio, "internal candidates get priority" would propagate as policy, and your briefing would be the citation; a leader's summary of a governing document becomes the document for most of its audience, which is why summary drafting is a governance act. Option C is accuracy-free cynicism that converts an investment in your community into an administrative grievance — and tone from the head travels further than content. Option D commits two precision errors in one sentence: fifteen days is an entitlement ceiling being reframed as a cap (connotation inverted — the strategy grants time, D rations it), and "any deferrals" misstates the threshold (only more-than-five-day deferrals need you — Question 11's boundary, erased). The craft standard for briefing governing documents: commitments with their numbers, boundaries with their reasons, misreadings pre-corrected — because your community will act on your sentence, not the source. Supporting the professional development of the portfolio community starts, literally, with how you tell them about the strategy that funds it.

### Preparation tips

- **Read for architecture before content.** Frameworks are built from thresholds, exceptions, severance clauses, savings clauses and escalation triggers. Identify the structure first — which clause trumps which — and the statements largely answer themselves. Practise on your own investment framework this week.
- **Track separate clocks separately.** Transfers, novations, assessments and deadlines routinely run on independent tracks in the documents you govern by. For any passage with dates and events, table them before judging statements — conflated clocks are a distractor factory.
- **Drill the severance clauses.** "Does not constitute endorsement", "nothing establishes a preference", "nothing alters statutory duties" — these clauses exist to prevent specific misreadings, and both tests and colleagues will attempt exactly those misreadings. Collect the severances in your own frameworks and be able to quote them.
- **Distinguish machine-description from prediction.** "The rules as written produce X in scenario Y" is checkable against the text; "X will happen" never is. Answer conditionals as conditionals, and brief leaders the same way.
- **Read scrutiny correspondence at its exact word.** What is alleged, what is expressly not alleged, what is requested, by when. Practise on published committee correspondence — it is the register your hardest reading will arrive in.
- **Draft the briefing sentence, then audit it.** For any governing document you must communicate, write your one-paragraph summary and check it clause-by-clause against the source: numbers right, thresholds right, boundaries present, connotations faithful. Your summary is the document for your audience; audit it like one.
- **Coach with the questions, not just the answers.** Use passages like these in your community sessions and have your managers argue the classifications. The argument is where interpretive precision transfers — and hearing their misreadings tells you what your next briefing must pre-correct.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Stopping at the threshold and missing the exception.** "Under £2m so light-touch" — until the data clause trumps value. In frameworks, exceptions are not fine print; they are the rule for the hard cases, which are the only cases that reach you.
- **Reading forecasts as deadlines.** "Expected in the second quarter" gates nothing; the event gates. Date-language and event-language do different work — keep them apart in tests, briefings and plans.
- **Merging signals the text keeps separate.** Certification is not endorsement; a target is not a preference; a return is not a rejection. When a document severs two things, the severance is load-bearing — for someone's accountability, usually yours.
- **Answering tone instead of text.** Scrutiny letters and stakeholder correspondence say exactly what they allege and request. Responding to imagined implications wastes the response and worsens the exchange.
- **Defending the technically accurate.** "True against the current baseline" can be the mechanism of misleading, not its refutation. When the reference point has moved, accuracy claims inherit the movement — concede the demonstrated, commit the verifiable.
- **Adding humane-sounding conditions.** Consent requirements, grace periods, implied preferences — unstated conditions feel plausible in exactly the way that fails tests and breeds workplace folklore. The text's conditions are the conditions.
- **Briefing the gist instead of the boundaries.** Summaries that carry the spirit but drop the thresholds, severances and triggers turn your community's next hundred decisions loose from the document. Numbers, boundaries, pre-corrected misreadings — every time.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement test (SJT) presents realistic dilemmas and asks you to evaluate responses — most effective, least effective, full rankings, or independent ratings. At head level it measures leadership judgement against behavioural benchmarks: how you weigh information integrity, delegation, accountability, people development, political pressure and institutional standards when they collide — which, at your altitude, is the normal condition. The scenarios that reach a head of portfolio's desk are definitionally the ones that could not be resolved below it: they arrive pre-stripped of easy answers.

Typical format: 15 to 25 scenarios, untimed or generously timed, scored against senior-practitioner consensus and the organisation's leadership behaviours — for UK government digital roles, expect honesty and transparency, ownership, empowering and developing others, inclusion, proportionate escalation (including upward to accounting officers and ministers' offices), and stewardship of public money and standards. Head-level instruments discriminate almost entirely among plausible options: the scoring rewards leaders who consistently choose responses that strengthen systems and people rather than merely resolving incidents, who locate decisions at the right level of authority — including above themselves — and who apply their standards reflexively, self included.

Employers deploy SJTs for head-level selection because the role's judgement is exercised mostly out of sight: in the moment before you sign, the sentence you choose in the committee room, the way you handle a portfolio manager's failure or a minister's office's request. Interviews sample rehearsed judgement; SJTs sample the operating kind. A further reason is institutional: your judgements become precedents — your team calibrates on what you did, not what you espoused — so the consistency the instrument measures is precisely the consistency your organisation will inherit. The scenarios below are built from the role's real dilemma set; every explanation names the behaviours at stake, works the options comparatively, and states the transferable principle, because at your level the explanations are also coaching material for the community you develop.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Information-integrity dilemmas** map to **Communicating analysis and insight** and your duty to provide leaders with insights: pressure to soften, delay, or over-firm the portfolio's picture — from programmes below or leaders above — tests whether the insight function stays trustworthy under load.
- **Delegation and development dilemmas** map to your duty to build and lead a diverse, skilled and motivated team and to support the portfolio community's professional development: when a portfolio manager struggles, errs, or excels, your response sets the profession's local terms — psychological safety, stretch, accountability, credit.
- **Standards-under-pressure dilemmas** map to **Governance and assurance**: ensuring the right controls are used is tested precisely where controls are most inconvenient — flagship programmes, ministerial timelines, your own initiatives — and the strong responses hold the control while engineering the legitimate route.
- **Escalation and accountability dilemmas** map to your duty to address risks and deal with, or escalate, issues: head-level escalation runs upward into accounting-officer and ministerial territory, and the scored skill is escalating the right things, at the right time, with decisions framed for the level that must own them.
- **Senior-stakeholder dilemmas** map to **Stakeholder relationship management**: influencing and negotiating with directors, accounting officers and external partners — holding the portfolio's evidence steady against rank, urgency and charm — is the recurring test.
- **Organisational-design dilemmas** map to **Strategic thinking** and **Planning**: the strongest head-level responses repair the structure that produced the incident — the forum, the incentive, the framework gap — which is how strategy and governance actually improve.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — The struggling portfolio manager's first big error**

A portfolio manager you appointed eight months ago — promising, hardworking, still growing — presented a flawed capacity analysis to a programme board yesterday: she double-counted a shared team's availability, and the board approved a schedule the portfolio cannot actually staff. The error is now embedded in an approved plan; she has not yet realised. What is the most effective response?

- A) Correct the analysis yourself and quietly re-route the revised version to the board secretary, sparing her the embarrassment.
- B) Meet her today: walk through the analysis until she finds the error herself, then support her to own the correction — she notifies the board, presents the corrected position, and you visibly back her; separately, add a peer-check step for board-bound analyses, since a single-author error reached an approval unchecked.
- C) Take the analysis off her, reassign board-facing work to a more experienced manager, and revisit her portfolio in six months.
- D) Wait for the board or the programme to notice, so the lesson lands naturally.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Three duties converge: the plan must be corrected fast (the board has approved an unstaffable schedule — every day it stands, downstream commitments accrete to it); the manager must grow through, not around, the failure; and the system that let a single-author error reach a board needs a control. Option B serves all three in the right order and manner. Guiding her to find the error herself — rather than presenting it found — is the coaching difference between a correction and a capability: she learns the checking discipline, not just this mistake. Having her own the notification and re-presentation, with your visible backing, teaches her — and everyone watching, which is everyone — that in your portfolio errors are surfaced by their authors and authors survive surfacing: the exact psychological-safety norm that keeps future errors reaching you early. The peer-check addition is the head-level completion: her error is also your process finding. Option A protects her feelings by amputating her growth, leaves her unaware she erred (she will repeat it), and quietly teaches your office that the head fixes things silently — the anti-norm. Option C converts one recoverable error by a developing appointee into a demotion: proportionality fails, her confidence and the team's risk appetite both take structural damage, and you lose the return on eight months of development. Option D gambles the portfolio's schedule integrity on someone else's vigilance to engineer a harsher lesson than the situation needs. Build and lead a skilled, motivated team: motivated is built exactly here, in how the first big error is handled.

**Question 2 (easy) — The director general wants a friendlier dashboard**

Reviewing your quarterly portfolio dashboard before it goes to the departmental board, the director general (DG) says: "Three reds out of ten looks terrible for us in front of the board. Two of those are basically ambers — the teams are on it. Can we reflect the direction of travel?" The two programmes' recovery plans are real but unproven; your criteria rate them red on current evidence. What is the most effective response?

- A) Re-rate the two programmes amber — the DG knows the board's context, and recovery plans exist.
- B) Keep the ratings as the criteria produce them; add for each red a dated recovery trajectory and the evidence that would move it to amber, so direction of travel is visible without moving the rating; and tell the DG that consistent criteria are what make the greens credible — if the criteria themselves are miscalibrated, you will gladly review them openly for next quarter, prospectively not retrospectively.
- C) Remove RAG ratings from the dashboard this quarter in favour of narrative reporting.
- D) Rate them amber but keep a private note that they are really red.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The request is the commonest integrity test at head level, and it arrives — as here — reasonably worded, from someone senior, with a kernel of legitimacy (direction of travel is genuinely relevant information). Option B is the full craft. It refuses the rating change on the only ground that survives repetition: ratings are criteria-driven, and criteria bent once are bent — the next quarter's "basically amber" argument starts from today's precedent. It serves the legitimate kernel honestly: dated recovery trajectories and evidence-thresholds-for-change give the board the direction of travel as information, not as recoloured assessment — and boards read "red, with a credible dated recovery" far more favourably than DGs fear. And its closing move is the sophisticated one: distinguishing rating integrity (non-negotiable, retrospectively) from criteria calibration (legitimately reviewable, prospectively, in the open) — which honours the possibility that the DG has a point about the criteria while removing the route by which that point erodes this quarter's truth. Option A trades the dashboard's entire evidentiary value — including its greens, which are only credible because reds are possible — for one comfortable meeting. Option D is A with a written confession attached. Option C abolishes the instrument to avoid the argument: narrative-only reporting under rating pressure is concealment by format change, and the board loses comparability portfolio-wide. The precedent point deserves the last word: the DG will remember whichever answer you give, and so will you — the first bent rating is the expensive one; all subsequent bends are merely its interest payments.

**Question 3 (moderate) — Credit, visibility and the diverse team**

Preparing the departmental awards nominations, you review the year's standout work: the portfolio's new dependency-mapping approach — now adopted by two other departments — was substantially designed by a junior analyst on your team, building on a rough concept one of your portfolio managers sketched. The portfolio manager has drafted the nomination naming himself as lead with the analyst "supporting". The analyst, early-career and from a group under-represented at senior digital levels, has said nothing. What is the most effective response?

- A) Submit the nomination as drafted — the portfolio manager originated the concept, and rank conventionally carries the credit.
- B) Correct the attribution before submission: speak with the portfolio manager — acknowledging his concept while establishing that design leadership was the analyst's — resubmit with roles accurately stated, ensure the analyst presents the work at the next cross-government portfolio community session, and treat the episode as a prompt to check how attribution flows in your office generally.
- C) Withdraw the nomination to avoid an awkward conversation.
- D) Submit as drafted but privately assure the analyst you know the truth.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Attribution accuracy is an integrity behaviour; its distribution is an inclusion behaviour; and at head level both are systems you own, not incidents you witness. Option B works every layer. The correction happens before submission because a false record, once official, compounds — awards histories feed promotion cases. The conversation with the portfolio manager is direct but fair: his concept is real and stays credited; what changes is the inflation of "sketched the concept" into "led the design" — and how you handle that conversation teaches him the norm more durably than any values statement. The visibility move — the analyst presenting to the cross-government community — is the part many leaders miss: accurate attribution on paper without platform access replaces one inequity with a quieter one; visibility is a career resource your role distributes, and your duty to support the professional development of the portfolio community includes distributing it deliberately. The general check completes it: one surfaced attribution error at nomination time usually indicates others that never surfaced. Option A launders inflation through convention — "rank carries credit" is precisely the mechanism by which under-represented junior staff stay invisible, and a head of portfolio who signs it has endorsed it. Option D lets the false record stand while privately compensating the person it disadvantages: the analyst learns her leader knew and filed it anyway. Option C destroys a deserved recognition to avoid a manageable conversation — conflict-avoidance priced at the team's expense. Building a diverse, skilled and motivated team is substantially this: making sure the credit lands where the work happened, visibly, every time.

**Question 4 (moderate) — The minister's office and the pre-announcement**

A special adviser calls: the minister wants to announce a new digital service's launch date at a conference in three weeks. Your portfolio data: the service's private beta is going well; the public beta gate review is in five weeks; the delivery team's own forecast for a launchable service is four to six months, contingent on the beta findings. The adviser says: "Just give us a date we can say. It can be soft — 'expected by spring'." What is the most effective response?

- A) Provide "expected by spring" — it is soft, plausible, and relations with the minister's office matter.
- B) Explain that any announced date lands ahead of the evidence — the gate review in five weeks is the point at which a date becomes forecastable — and offer what is announceable now: the beta's progress, its user numbers and satisfaction evidence, and a commitment to announce the launch date "following the beta assessment this autumn"; offer to brief the adviser on why gate-sequenced announcements protect the minister from exactly the correction-story that a slipped soft date generates.
- C) Refuse: "The portfolio office does not provide launch dates for political events."
- D) Give the team's forecast range — "four to six months" — and let the office round it as they wish.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Decode what each party actually needs. The minister needs something true and impressive to say — which exists: a well-performing beta with real user evidence is announceable substance. The adviser needs a line that survives; "soft" dates do not — "expected by spring" is remembered as spring, reported as spring, and its slippage is a story with the minister's name on it, sourced to your office's acquiescence. The service needs its gate review to stay a genuine decision point, which a pre-announced date — however soft — quietly forecloses: teams deliver to announcements, and the gate becomes theatre (this is the governance mechanism your controls exist to protect; a date given today spends the review's authority five weeks early). Option B serves all three: it explains the sequencing rather than merely invoking it, supplies the announceable substance now, commits to a dated announcement event ("following the beta assessment") that gives the office a second bite with better material — and its closing offer converts the refusal into service, briefing the adviser on why gate-sequenced dates protect ministers, which is both true and the framing that makes the answer land. Option A trades the gate's integrity and the minister's future correction-story for present warmth. Option D looks like transparency but functions as A: a range handed to a communications office becomes its optimistic endpoint by close of business, and "four months" is now the date — with the contingency clause ("contingent on beta findings") stripped in transit, as contingency clauses always are. Option C is correct in principle and useless in practice: flat refusal without an alternative teaches the office to route around your data next time — the worst of all outcomes, since routed-around portfolio offices read about launch dates in the press. Influencing and negotiating with senior stakeholders means giving power something better to say than the thing you cannot let it say.

**Question 5 (moderate) — The assurance finding on your own initiative**

An internal audit of portfolio governance — which you commissioned — reports a significant finding: the prioritisation framework you designed and championed two years ago is being gamed; programmes have learned to draft bids that score well against the criteria while their delivered scope diverges from what was scored, and three examples are documented. The audit lands two weeks before the annual investment round, which runs on the same framework. What is the most effective response?

- A) Accept the finding for action after the investment round — changing the rules two weeks out would be destabilising.
- B) Accept the finding now and apply an immediate compensating control to this round — a delivered-scope-versus-scored-bid reconciliation for the three documented gamers and spot-checks portfolio-wide, announced to all bidders — while committing the framework redesign (scoring tied to commitments that are tracked post-award) for completion before next year's round; report the finding, its examples, your authorship of the framework, and the compensating measures to the investment committee before it convenes.
- C) Challenge the audit's sample of three as too small to establish a pattern, and request further evidence before acting.
- D) Quietly rescore the three documented programmes' current bids downward and proceed.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Timing is the scenario's engine: a known-gamed framework is about to allocate a year's funding, so "act now versus act later" is really "run this round on rules you know are being played, or don't." Option A's stability argument inverts: what destabilises an investment round is discovering afterwards that it ran on gamed rules the head of portfolio knew about — every allocation becomes contestable retroactively; whereas a compensating control announced beforehand stabilises the round by making the gaming unprofitable within it. Option B's architecture is the general pattern for framework failure discovered at the worst moment: immediate compensating control (reconciliation and spot-checks — implementable in two weeks because it changes verification, not rules: bidders' scores stand, but scored claims now get checked against delivered reality, and the announcement itself deters), structural fix on a realistic clock (redesign before next year), and full disclosure upward before the committee convenes — the finding, the examples, the authorship, the measures. The authorship disclosure is not ritual self-criticism; it is what makes the rest credible: a head of portfolio who reports "my framework is being gamed; here is my fix" can hold every programme to standards afterwards, while one whose framework's failure emerged otherwise cannot. Option C attacks the messenger's sample size in the direction of your own convenience — three documented cases is not a statistical claim needing power calculations; it is three documented cases, and "more evidence before acting" means one more gamed round. Option D is quiet score-tampering: procedurally unauthorised, invisible to the committee that owns the allocation, unfair to the three relative to any undocumented gamers, and — when it surfaces — indistinguishable from the gaming it punishes. Ensuring the right controls are used is at its most exposed when the control that failed is your own; the audit you commissioned finding your framework's flaw, two weeks before it matters, is not your worst case — it is your governance working. Treat it that way in front of everyone.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Least effective: the failing supplier on the flagship**

The flagship programme's prime supplier has missed three consecutive delivery milestones; the programme director believes the supplier's key team has been quietly redeployed to a bigger client. Commercial colleagues confirm contractual remedies exist but warn that invoking them adversarially could stall delivery for months. The programme is politically visible; the accounting officer has asked to be kept informed. Which response is LEAST effective?

- A) Convene a supplier executive session with the programme director and commercial lead: present the milestone evidence, require a remediation plan with named resourcing within ten working days, and state — without yet invoking — the contractual position, while briefing the accounting officer on the strategy and triggers.
- B) Commission an immediate independent capability assessment of the supplier's actual team deployment, so any subsequent step — remediation, remedy, or replanning — runs on verified facts rather than the programme director's belief.
- C) Begin contingency planning in parallel: map what insourcing, re-procurement, or descoping would require, so the portfolio negotiates with options rather than dependence.
- D) Hold the current course for one more milestone cycle: three misses may reflect teething, invoking anything now risks the relationship, and the supplier's account director has promised improvement.

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** A least-effective item where three options compose the textbook response and the fourth is its absence. Notice first that A, B and C are not alternatives but a sequence-in-parallel: verify the facts (B — the redeployment is currently a belief, and beliefs make bad negotiating positions and worse contractual ones), confront with structure (A — executive-level, evidence-led, deadline-bound, contractual position stated but held in reserve, accounting officer briefed with triggers), and build the alternative (C — negotiating leverage is having options, and contingency mapping is what converts "we can't afford to upset them" into "we'd prefer not to switch"). Together they are how a head of portfolio handles supplier failure on a visible programme: evidence, structured pressure, options, and the accountability line kept informed. Option D fails on every axis the others serve, and its construction is instructive because each clause is a recognisable rationalisation: "teething" — after three consecutive misses on a flagship, the pattern hypothesis has outrun the teething hypothesis; "risks the relationship" — a relationship currently consisting of the supplier's best people serving someone else is being protected from repair; "account director has promised improvement" — the promise of the person whose job is promising, unverified, is being weighted against three verified misses. And the cost of the cycle D donates is concrete: another milestone period of drift on a politically visible programme, with the accounting officer — who asked to be kept informed — uninformed because nothing was done worth reporting. The head-level principle: with failing suppliers, patience without structure is not relationship management; it is unilateral disarmament on a public timetable. Deal with, or escalate, issues — the duty's verbs are active for a reason.

**Question 7 (hard) — The portfolio manager who wants to leave — or lead**

Your strongest portfolio manager tells you privately she has been offered a deputy director role in another department, starting in eight weeks. She is torn: she loves the portfolio, but "there's no obvious next step here." Your own succession thinking had her leading the portfolio office's planned expansion next year — which you have mentioned to no one, because funding is not yet confirmed. Losing her now would leave two major programmes' assurance without senior cover through the winter. What is the most effective response?

- A) Tell her about the expansion role now, making clear its funding is unconfirmed, so she can weigh a real (if uncertain) internal future against the certain external offer — and congratulate her genuinely on the offer either way; separately, regardless of her choice, fix the succession opacity her "no obvious next step" reveals: if your best people cannot see their futures here, the pipeline problem is yours.
- B) Counter-offer immediately: promise her the expansion leadership, and seek the funding confirmation afterwards.
- C) Accept her likely departure gracefully and begin recruiting; sharing unconfirmed plans would be unfair to her decision-making.
- D) Remind her of the two programmes needing winter cover and ask her, as a professional courtesy, to defer any move until spring.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The scenario hides its real finding in her sentence: "no obvious next step here" — your succession plan exists, features her, and she cannot see it. That is not her information failure but yours, and option A alone treats it as such. The immediate move is disclosure with honest uncertainty: telling her about the expansion role with its funding status plainly labelled gives her what a strong decision needs — a true comparison — while refusing to weaponise it; note the difference from option B, which converts an unconfirmed plan into a promise you may not be able to keep, the classic counter-offer failure mode: if funding falls through, you have bought eight more months of her at the price of her trust and her next external offer. Option C's fairness logic is exactly inverted: withholding true, decision-relevant information to keep her choice "clean" decides for her by omission — she may choose the external role believing there was nothing here, which is false; adults weigh uncertainty better than they weigh silence. Option D spends the relationship to solve the winter: framing her career move as a courtesy she owes the roster tells your best person that her function here is coverage — the sentence she will repeat to the peers she advises about working for you. A's closing clause is the head-level completion: whatever she chooses, the systemic finding stands — succession opacity is why your strongest people entertain external calls, and fixing it (visible pathways, development conversations that name futures, expansion plans socialised at the right confidence level) is the duty your role description states as building and leading a motivated team. And the genuine congratulation is not decoration: leaders whose people leave well — celebrated, connected, alumni — recruit the next generation through them. Some retention is won years earlier, in whether the next step was ever visible; the rest should not be won at all.

**Question 8 (hard) — The accounting officer's direction**

The accounting officer (AO) tells you the department will bid to host a cross-government shared platform — a prestigious win if secured — and asks you to confirm to the Cabinet Office that the portfolio can absorb the hosting programme "within existing capacity". Your analysis: absorbing it would consume the portfolio's entire contingency capacity for eighteen months, and delay two mid-tier programmes by a quarter each; it is absorbable, but only by spending resilience you would then not have. The AO's framing suggests the answer is expected to be yes. What is the most effective response?

- A) Confirm as asked — the AO owns the department's risk appetite, and the bid is strategically valuable.
- B) Decline to confirm, and advise the AO that the portfolio cannot support the bid.
- C) Tell the AO the true position and put the real decision to them: "Absorbable — by consuming our full contingency for eighteen months and delaying two named programmes a quarter each. If we accept that exposure, I can confirm; I recommend we say so internally in those terms, so the risk acceptance is explicit and the two programmes' stakeholders hear it from us. If the exposure is unacceptable, the honest bid says 'with two additional posts' — which may still win. Your call, and I'll make either version true."
- D) Confirm to the Cabinet Office as asked, but record your capacity concerns in the portfolio risk register for the file.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The question tests whether you understand what your advice is for at accounting-officer altitude. The AO does own the department's risk appetite — option A's premise is correct — but risk appetite can only be exercised over risks that have been stated: a "yes" delivered without the exposure attached does not respect the AO's authority, it disables it, converting their decision into your concealment. Option C is the model of head-level advice to power, and its anatomy rewards study: the true position in one sentence (absorbable; the price is named — full contingency, two quarter-delays); the decision framed as the risk acceptance it actually is, placed with its owner; the recommendation that the acceptance be explicit and communicated (because two programmes' stakeholders will otherwise discover their delays as betrayals rather than decisions); the constructive alternative (the two-posts bid — honest and possibly still competitive) that keeps the AO's strategic ambition alive on truthful terms; and the closing commitment — "I'll make either version true" — which is the sentence that distinguishes advice from obstruction: you are not resisting the bid, you are pricing it. Option B fails in the opposite direction from A: "cannot support" overstates your analysis (it is absorbable, at a price) and substitutes your risk appetite for the AO's — the mirror image of the deference error. Option D is the saddest pattern in governance: public confirmation plus private register entry — the risk documented precisely well enough to prove, at the inquiry, that you knew. A register is not a conscience. The principle, stated once for the whole section: senior advice means the decision-maker decides with the price tag visible — your job is neither to pay it silently nor to refuse it on their behalf, but to hold it in the light while they choose.

**Question 9 (hard) — Rank the responses: the whistleblower's email**

Monday, 07:40. An email from a mid-level analyst inside Programme Osprey — not in your line — sent to you directly, subject "in confidence": the programme's reported user-volume figures, cited in its last two board papers and in the department's public annual report, are "calculated in a way everyone inside knows is wrong" — a counting method that inflates active users roughly threefold; he has raised it twice with programme leadership and "been told to focus on my own work"; he is copying no one and asks that you protect him. Rank these responses from most to least effective:

1. Acknowledge to him today — confirming receipt, that raising it was right, and that protection is your explicit priority; commission an independent verification of the counting methodology through the portfolio's standing assurance authority, without naming any source; on its findings, correct the record wherever it is wrong (boards, annual report corrigendum if needed) and address the "told to focus on your own work" management response as its own serious finding.
2. Forward his email to Programme Osprey's director, asking for their account of the methodology, copying him for transparency.
3. Verify the methodology first without acknowledging his email, so no link between him and the review can exist; contact him only once it concludes.
4. Advise him to raise it through the department's formal whistleblowing channel, which exists for exactly this, and take no portfolio action while that process runs.

- A) 1, 3, 4, 2
- B) 1, 4, 3, 2
- C) 3, 1, 4, 2
- D) 1, 3, 2, 4

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Work the pairwise discriminations. Response 1 leads because it alone holds all four duties simultaneously: the discloser (acknowledged today — silence from the recipient is, to a person who has just taken a career risk, indistinguishable from hostility; and "protection is my priority" said explicitly is itself protective); the facts (independent verification through standing authority — no source named, because a methodology can be reviewed on its own announcement-free rhythm); the record (a threefold inflation cited publicly requires correction wherever it travelled, including the annual report — the step that separates information integrity from reputation management); and the system (leadership telling a twice-raising analyst to mind his own work is a culture finding as serious as the numbers). Ranking 3 against 4 is the fine discrimination: 3 gets verification right but the human wrong — weeks of silence to maximise unlinkability sacrifices the discloser's certainty that anyone acted, and unlinkability is achievable with acknowledgement (the review needs no source; the acknowledgement needs no publicity); still, 3 delivers the substantive outcome — facts verified, record correctable. Response 4 delivers process without outcome: the formal channel is real and mentioning it is fine, but "take no portfolio action while it runs" outsources your standing duty — the figures are in your boards' papers now, and verifying your own portfolio's reported numbers requires no whistleblowing referral to authorise; 4 also transfers the initiative burden back to the person with the most to lose. So 3 above 4: verified truth late beats process punctilio with the record left standing. Response 2 is catastrophic and anchors the bottom: forwarding a confidential disclosure to the leadership it accuses, with the discloser copied, is protection inverted — it identifies him to the people who already told him to be quiet, converts his risk into near-certain detriment, and teaches the entire portfolio that disclosure to the head is disclosure to the accused. Order: 1, 3, 4, 2 — option A. The principle: protect the person, verify the facts, correct the record, fix the culture — and the first of these is due the same day.

**Question 10 (hard) — The inclusion finding in your own leadership team**

Preparing your evidence for the department's inclusion review, your business manager shows you three years of your own leadership-team data: the five portfolio managers you have appointed are demographically near-identical to their predecessors; development-scheme nominations from your office skew heavily towards staff based in the headquarters building where you sit; and exit interviews from your office's leavers include two — both from under-represented groups — citing "limited visibility of progression". You genuinely believed your office was inclusive. What is the most effective response?

- A) Contextualise the data for the review: small numbers, strong appointees each individually justified, and two exit interviews are anecdote, not pattern.
- B) Present the data to the review unvarnished, with your reading — that individually defensible decisions have summed to a systemically narrow pattern — and a concrete plan you announce to your office: structured interviews and diverse panels for every appointment, development nominations balanced across locations with remote staff actively sought, progression pathways published, and the data reviewed with your leadership team quarterly with you accountable for movement.
- C) Commission a further year of data collection before drawing conclusions, to ensure the pattern is statistically robust.
- D) Delegate the response to your business manager as the data owner, endorsing whatever plan they produce.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The scenario is engineered to catch the leader who applies system-thinking everywhere except the mirror, and its structure deliberately rhymes with this guide's earlier scrutiny questions: individually defensible decisions summing to indefensible drift is the cumulative-change blindness — here operating on people instead of baselines, in your own office instead of a programme's. Option B applies your own standards reflexively. The data goes to the review unvarnished because contextualising your own adverse data (option A) is precisely what you would never accept from a programme — "each decision was justified" is the exact defence the re-baselining letter dismantled, and you knew it was inadequate when programmes offered it; note also A's asymmetric rigour: "two exit interviews are anecdote" from the leader who treats two late-stage surprises or three supplier misses as a pattern. Option C's statistical caution fails the same symmetry test — three years of appointments, nominations and exits is the decision-relevant record, and "another year of data" means another appointment round under the unexamined pattern; you would strike that deferral from any programme's response to an audit. The plan's contents matter as much as its existence: structural interventions (panels, structured interviews, location-balanced nominations, published pathways — the last answering the exit interviews' exact words, and echoing Question 7's succession-opacity finding one level down), announced publicly to your office (private resolutions do not change what staff can see), with quarterly data review and named accountability — yours. Option D delegates what cannot be delegated: the pattern is in your decisions; a plan produced by your business manager and endorsed from a distance tells the office this is an administrative matter, when the finding is about the appointing mind. Build and lead a diverse team is your role description's own adjective order; the review is not the threat here — the unexamined fourth year was. Leaders who audit themselves first, in public, license rigour everywhere; this is that principle's hardest and therefore most valuable application.

**Question 11 (hard) — Most and least effective: the peer head of portfolio**

At the cross-government portfolio leaders' network, a peer head of portfolio from another department — a friend, and someone whose community your own managers work with — asks to speak privately. Her department's flagship programme is in serious trouble; her leadership "doesn't want it red yet"; she has been keeping it amber for two quarters and the gap is now unbridgeable. "You've faced this," she says. "How did you handle it? And — between us — would you have a quiet look at my evidence pack and tell me if the red case is as unanswerable as I think?" Which response is MOST effective, and which LEAST?

Responses:

1. Review her evidence pack informally and give your honest reading; peer review between heads is how the profession self-corrects, and her department's governance is not your jurisdiction.
2. Decline any private review of another department's assurance evidence, but give her fully what peer support legitimately offers: how you handled the equivalent pressure — the criteria-integrity argument, the dated-recovery-trajectory device, the prospective-criteria-review offer — rehearse with her the conversation she must have with her leadership this week, and be clear that the two-quarter gap itself now needs disclosing, not just the rating; offer to be called after she has done it.
3. Tell her that what she has described — sustained knowing misrating under leadership pressure — obliges you to alert her department's audit function if she does not act, and set her a deadline.
4. Sympathise, share war stories in general terms, and leave the specifics alone — her department, her problem.

- A) Most effective: 2; least effective: 4
- B) Most effective: 1; least effective: 3
- C) Most effective: 2; least effective: 3
- D) Most effective: 1; least effective: 4

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Begin with what she actually needs, which is not what she asked for. The evidence question ("is the red case unanswerable?") is answered — she says so herself: the gap is unbridgeable; she is not seeking analysis but company in certainty, and perhaps a shared fingerprint on the file. The real need is the conversation she has been avoiding for two quarters, and response 2 aims everything at it: the transferable craft (this guide's own Question 2 devices — criteria integrity, dated recovery trajectories, prospective criteria review — which are precisely "how you handled it"), rehearsal of the hard conversation (peer support's highest form: not doing it for her, making her ready), the uncomfortable completion she has not faced — that two quarters of knowing misrating is now itself part of what must be disclosed, and the disclosure goes better volunteered than discovered — and the after-call offer that keeps support flowing without displacing her ownership. Response 2 declines the pack review on the right ground: not officiousness, but because an informal cross-departmental read of assurance evidence creates accountability without authority — your judgement in her department's chain, invisible to its governance — and because it answers the wrong question. Now the least-effective discrimination, 3 versus 4, which is the item's real test. Response 3 is heavy-handed, premature, and converts a colleague seeking help into a regulatory subject mid-conversation — likely ending both the friendship and, more importantly, the candour; but examine what it protects: it takes the misreporting seriously, drives towards disclosure, and would in extremis correct the record. It is the right instinct at the wrong speed with the wrong opening move. Response 4 protects nothing: it hears sustained knowing misreporting of a flagship programme, offers warmth, and walks away — "her department, her problem" from the person best placed to help her fix it, leaving the misrating running and the friend alone with it. Callous indifference dressed as respect for boundaries ranks below clumsy integrity: 4 is least effective. Most 2, least 4 — option A. The professional-community principle your role names as supporting the portfolio community across government: peers owe each other the hard rehearsal, not the quiet complicity — and not the abandonment either.

**Question 12 (hard) — The last question: what you leave behind**

You are eighteen months from a planned move — your successor unknown. An organisational effectiveness review asks each senior leader to name their single highest-priority institutional investment for their remaining tenure. For your portfolio function, four candidates: (1) personally leading the recovery of the portfolio's largest troubled programme, where your experience would materially raise its odds; (2) codifying the portfolio's operating model — the criteria, controls, escalation routes and reporting standards currently carried substantially in your head and habits — into a maintained, owned framework; (3) accelerating the leadership development of your two most senior portfolio managers, including deputising rotations, committee exposure and external assessment; (4) a portfolio-wide data platform consolidating the reporting pipeline, fully specified and fundable now. Which choice is most defensible as the single priority?

- A) 1 — nothing matters more than the flagship's recovery, and your odds-raising is unique and time-limited.
- B) 2 and 3 pursued as one investment — codify the operating model through the two senior managers who must inherit it: they lead the codification with you as reviewer, deputise through the committees the framework governs, and by departure the function's judgement exists outside your head and inside both a document and two people — because the review asked what strengthens the institution, and the honest audit of a role like yours is how much of it currently works only because you are in it.
- C) 4 — platforms outlast people; automate the pipeline and the rest follows.
- D) 3 alone — people are always the answer; frameworks and platforms date.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The question audits whether you understand what a head-level role is for at its end, and the honest starting diagnosis is uncomfortable: a function whose criteria, controls and judgement live "substantially in your head and habits" is a key-person risk wearing an org chart — everything this guide has practised (the criteria that hold under DG pressure, the thirty-second committee arithmetic, the escalation calibrations) currently depends on your presence, which is precisely what an eighteen-month horizon exposes. Option B's insight is that candidates 2 and 3 are not competitors but each other's method: a framework codified without formed successors is documentation nobody can operate with judgement (frameworks encode rules; the judgement that applies them transfers through practice — hence the deputising rotations and committee exposure); successors developed without codification inherit authority but rebuild the operating model from fragments (the leaver's tacit knowledge walking out the door — the oldest institutional failure there is). Having the two managers lead the codification with you as reviewer is the mechanism that fuses them: writing the framework is how they internalise it, and their draft-versus-your-review gaps are the precise curriculum of their remaining development. Against the alternatives: option A is the seductive one — the flagship matters and your contribution is real — but it invests your scarcest resource in an instance, not the institution; the honest test is what the review actually asked, and "the programme I personally rescued" is a legacy that depreciates from the day you leave, while a function that rescues programmes without you appreciates. (Note too: option B's deputising rotations can put a developing senior manager into the flagship's recovery — the instance becoming the curriculum.) Option C buys pipes for a function that hasn't secured the judgement flowing through them; option D bets everything on two careers — either of whom may also leave — with nothing written down. The principle that closes this guide's practice as it should close a tenure: the measure of a head of portfolio is not the portfolio's performance in post but the function's performance after — build the thing that works when you are not in the room, and staff it with people you formed. That is what "supporting the professional development of the portfolio community" means when the community's next leader is the deliverable.

### Preparation tips

- **Anchor on leadership behaviours, not tactics.** Head-level SJT keys cluster on a short list: information integrity under pressure from above and below, decisions priced and placed with their owners, people developed through (not around) their failures, standards applied reflexively to yourself, and systems repaired rather than incidents managed. Reread your organisation's leadership behaviours and the Civil Service Leadership expectations before the test; then notice that every scenario above scores against that list.
- **Practise pricing decisions for power.** The recurring senior move is neither yes nor no but "yes, at this named cost — your call, made explicit." Rehearse the form until you can produce it conversationally: true position in one sentence, exposure quantified, alternative offered, ownership placed.
- **Rehearse the self-implication reflex.** The hardest items put your framework, your appointment pattern, your succession opacity in the frame. Train the tell: whenever you notice yourself contextualising, sampling-size-challenging, or deferring on your own adverse data, ask what you would say to a programme offering the same move. Symmetry is the scored behaviour.
- **Learn the protection patterns cold.** Whistleblowers, struggling managers, torn high-performers: acknowledge fast, protect explicitly, verify through standing authority, correct the record, fix the culture. The ordering is stable across scenarios; fluency in it frees attention for each scenario's specifics.
- **Distinguish instance from institution.** Before choosing any response, ask what it invests in: this incident, or the system that produced it? Senior keys consistently reward the response that resolves the instance and repairs the structure — and, at the margin, the one that still works after you leave the room.
- **Compare options pairwise, especially at the bottom.** Least-effective discriminations (clumsy integrity versus polished indifference; process punctilio versus outcome) carry heavy scoring weight. Argue each pair explicitly in practice: which failure would you rather own, and why?
- **Coach with these scenarios.** Run them in your leadership team sessions and let your portfolio managers defend rankings before you reveal the reasoning. Their answers calibrate your development plans; your reasoning, spoken aloud, is the norm-setting your role performs — and teaching judgement is the most reliable way to sharpen your own before an assessment.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Deference dressed as respect.** "The DG knows the context", "the AO owns the risk", "the minister's office needs a date" — every version confirms something untrue or unpriced because someone senior preferred it. Rank determines who decides; it never determines what is true, and your function exists to keep the two distinct.
- **Protection that removes agency.** Fixing the manager's error silently, withholding the succession plan to keep a choice "clean", reviewing the peer's pack instead of rehearsing her conversation — kindness that does the work around people teaches them nothing and decides for them covertly. Protect people by equipping them.
- **The register as conscience.** Documenting privately what you confirmed publicly, noting "really red" beside a bent amber, filing concerns for the record while acting against them — the paper trail that proves you knew is not integrity; it is its receipt.
- **Asymmetric rigour.** Pattern-reading three supplier misses while calling two exit interviews anecdote; demanding programmes disclose cumulative drift while contextualising your own appointment data. The standard you apply to others, applied to yourself, is the whole test — and everyone can see the asymmetry but you.
- **Instance-victories over institution-repairs.** Rescuing the flagship personally, re-scoring the gamed bids quietly, absorbing the interim yourself — resolutions that depend on your continued presence are loans against the function's future, repayable with interest at your departure.
- **Speed without protection, protection without speed.** Forwarding the whistleblower's email is fast and fatal; verifying in total silence is safe and corrosive. The strong responses move on facts quickly and shield people explicitly — the pairing is the skill, and either half alone fails.
- **Answering the moment instead of the precedent.** Every response you choose — in the test and in the role — becomes the reference case for your community's next hundred decisions. When two options seem close, choose the one you want quoted back to you; at head level, it will be.

## Conclusion

You have worked through four assessments pitched at head of portfolio, and the threads are worth drawing together.

The cognitive section was about reading a portfolio as a system — distinguishing an instance that needs rescuing from an institution that needs repairing, and noticing that the same signal read across three suppliers and two exit interviews deserves the same rigour in both directions.

The numeric section was the arithmetic that governs portfolio decisions: thresholds and the exceptions attached to them, benefits stripped of what would have arrived anyway, costs of delay computed before a delay is discussed, and forecasts distinguished from the events that actually gate a decision.

The verbal section was an exercise in reading governing documents at their exact word. Certification is not endorsement. A target is not a commitment. "Expected in the second quarter" gates nothing. And a scrutiny letter says precisely what it alleges and precisely what it does not — which is the sentence you will be asked to brief upward, where dropping a qualifier changes what your organisation has conceded.

The situational judgement section was about the particular exposure of a head-level role. The hardest items put your own framework, your own appointment, your own assurance judgement under the same scrutiny you apply elsewhere, and the strong answers accepted that symmetry. They priced decisions rather than answering yes or no, protected people without removing their agency, and chose the institutional repair over the visible rescue.

If one thread runs through all of it, it is that every response you choose sets a precedent as well as settling a case. Deference dressed as respect, a risk register used as a private conscience, protection that quietly removes someone's agency — each solves the moment and teaches the organisation something you did not intend to teach.

Good luck. This is a role where the good decisions are largely invisible and the compounding is real, and the care you have given this is well placed.
