# United States legal aspects about workplace psychometric assessment

In the United States, workplace IQ testing is legal but carries immense legal risk under federal civil rights laws. No federal statute explicitly bans general cognitive testing. However, if a workplace intelligence test disproportionately screens out protected demographic groups, the employer must legally prove the test is directly job-related and a business necessity. [1, 2]
------------------------------

## Federal Statutory Frameworks

- Title VII of the Civil Rights Act (1964): Section 703(h) permits professionally developed tests, provided they are not designed or used to discriminate. If a test results in an adverse numerical impact against protected groups, it violates Title VII unless validated. [3]
- The 80% Rule (Four-Fifths Rule): Enforced by the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission (EEOC), this guideline states that if a protected group's pass rate is less than 80% of the highest-scoring group's rate, federal agencies presume discrimination. [4]
- Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA): Employers cannot use cognitive tests that screen out individuals with disabilities (such as neurodivergent candidates) unless the test is job-related. Tests must also accommodate applicant disabilities. [5]
- Civil Rights Act of 1991: This statute strictly prohibits "race norming." Employers are banned from adjusting test cut-off scores, using different scales, or altering raw scores based on the race or ethnicity of the test-takers. [6]

## The Supreme Court Precedent: Griggs v. Duke Power Co.

The definitive legal authority on workplace intelligence testing is the landmark Supreme Court ruling Griggs v. Duke Power Co. (1971). [1]

[Test Administered] ➔ [Disparate Impact Found] ➔ [Employer Must Prove Test Directly Predicts Job Performance]

- The Case: Duke Power Co. required a high school diploma and a passing score on two generic aptitude tests, including the Wonderlic Personnel Test, for high-paying positions. [7]
- The Ruling: The Supreme Court ruled unanimously against the employer. The court held that if an employment practice excludes protected groups, intent does not matter. [1]
- The Standard: The court established that tests must measure the person for the specific job, not the person in the abstract. Tests are illegal unless they are "demonstrably a reasonable measure of job performance." [1, 8]

---

## The Legal Burden of Proof Workflow

If an applicant files a discrimination charge regarding a cognitive test, federal courts follow a strict sequential framework to determine liability:

1.  Plaintiff Demonstrates Disparate Impact: The applicant uses statistical data (applying the 80% rule) to prove the IQ test disproportionately disqualifies a protected class.
2.  Employer Proves Business Necessity: The burden shifts to the employer. They must provide formal job analyses and industrial/organizational psychology validation studies showing that higher test scores directly correlate with superior on-the-job production. [9]
3.  Plaintiff Rebuts with Alternatives: Even if the employer proves business necessity, the plaintiff wins the lawsuit if they can show that a less discriminatory tool (e.g., a portfolio review or a situational work sample) could have achieved the same vetting result without the demographic skew.

## Comparing Risk: IQ vs. Skills Assessments

Because generic intelligence testing is highly susceptible to lawsuits, modern HR departments favor targeted assessment tools:

| Vetting Method          | Primary Focus                               | Legal Risk Level | Title VII Defensibility                                                                                                                          |
| ----------------------- | ------------------------------------------- | ---------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| General IQ Tests        | Abstract reasoning, logic, spatial patterns | High Risk        | Extremely difficult to defend for low-to-mid complexity roles.                                                                                   |
| Job-Specific Aptitudes  | Coding, data entry, mechanical operations   | Low-to-Moderate  | Highly defensible because the test directly mirrors daily physical or technical job tasks.                                                       |
| Personality Assessments | Behavioral traits, workplace culture fit    | Moderate Risk    | Safe under Title VII, but heavily scrutinized under the ADA to ensure questions do not function as medical screens for mental health conditions. |
