## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the reasoning your role demands, presented through practical materials rather than abstract puzzles. At head-of-profession level the materials are organisational and the reasoning is diagnostic and strategic. You will meet work produced by other people, standards applied unevenly, evidence gathered under conditions nobody recorded, and prioritisation problems whose units are shared assets and capabilities.

The format is typically an online test of fifteen to thirty minutes covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective against a norm group of comparable seniority.

Three things characterise this section at your level.

The first is reasoning at a distance. You rarely see the file; you see a rendered page, a screenshot, a claim in a board paper, and must judge from visible traces whether the thinking underneath holds.

The second is that the interesting questions concern mechanisms. Off-system values, contrast failures and inconsistent components are nearly always produced by something: a token unavailable in the tool teams design in, a scale missing a step, a review that happens too late to change anything, a component library offering only the non-compliant variant, or a brand palette optimised for print. Fixing the instance leaves the mechanism intact.

The third is the multiplier. A token adopted by fifteen services carries its properties fifteen times and is far harder to change than to set.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The cognitive dimensions map onto the skills named in your role summary:

- **Auditing at a distance** maps to your role level's requirement to define and assure best practice.
- **Cross-service synthesis** maps to **Evidence-based design**, where you use evidence to improve how the organisation works.

- **Strategic prioritisation** maps to **Designing strategically**, where you ensure design is part of organisational strategy and is included early enough to inform decisions.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Auditing against a standard**

Your standard requires every token entry to state: the problem it solves, approved backgrounds, contrast values against those backgrounds, minimum text size where applicable, known limitations, and the date last reviewed. An entry states the problem, contrast against white, minimum text size and date; lists no approved backgrounds; and gives limitations as "none known". How many requirements are unmet?

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Work through all six. The problem, minimum text size and date are present. "None known" is a statement about limitations — thin, and it addresses the requirement as written. Approved backgrounds are absent, so that fails. Contrast values are stated, but only against white; whether that satisfies "contrast values against those backgrounds" is ambiguous given that no backgrounds are listed, and the more defensible audit reading is that the contrast requirement is met in form while the backgrounds requirement is not. One unmet, giving A. Option B typically comes from failing both, which is a reasonable design judgement and shows the standard's real weakness: it makes contrast values conditional on a field that can simply be left blank, so a designer can satisfy the contrast requirement by omitting the backgrounds. That is a drafting defect and it is yours to fix, which is the actual output of this audit.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction from a governance rule**

Your standard states: "A custom colour may enter the token set only if no existing token is suitable, unless approved by the head of graphic design." A directorate reports adding one last month without your approval. Which must be true?

- A) The standard was breached.
- B) No existing token was suitable.
- C) Either no existing token was suitable, or the standard was breached.
- D) The value should be removed.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A conditional with an exception, plus facts silent on the branch, licenses only the disjunction. Options A and B each assume one. Option D prescribes an action rather than stating what must be true. Only C holds. Note the structure: the exception routes through your own approval, which you know was not given, so the remaining question is whether the general condition was met. That has a practical edge worth noticing — where a rule makes you the approver, you can settle half the disjunction from your own records rather than inferring it, which is a reason to keep those records and a reason to be careful about approvals given informally in conversation, since an approval you cannot evidence is one that generates exactly this ambiguity.

**Question 3 (moderate) — Reading conditions from an output**

A report states: "Participants read the interface comfortably and distinguished all status colours easily." Sessions ran in a usability lab on a calibrated 27-inch monitor. What is the most important observation?

- A) The sample is too small.
- B) A calibrated lab monitor at controlled brightness flatters exactly the two properties claimed — legibility and colour discrimination — so the finding describes near-ideal display conditions rather than the conditions users have.
- C) "Comfortably" is not measurable.
- D) A validated legibility scale should have been used.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The finding and the conditions match too neatly to be coincidence. A calibrated monitor makes low-contrast text look fine and similar hues look distinct; the same design on a dimmed laptop, a monitor with a colour cast, or a phone in daylight may fail both tests. That is the display equivalent of a sampling frame problem, and it is the characteristic methodological trap of visual disciplines — testing on the best screen in the building is the equivalent of recruiting only confident users. Option C is a fair secondary point. Option A misdiagnoses validity as size. Option D is a technique fix leaving the conditions untouched, and a validated scale administered on the same monitor yields the same flattered result with more authority — which is worse, because it is harder to challenge.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Strategic prioritisation**

You can direct substantial capacity to one of four things this quarter:

1. A token set is being fixed next month for adoption by eight services.
2. A live service has a known contrast failure on a secondary label.
3. A programme wants exploratory visual concepts for next year.
4. A directorate wants a brand refresh applied across existing services.

Which is strongest?

- A) Item 2, because it affects users now.
- B) Item 1, because the decisions are imminent, will be inherited by eight services, and a token set propagates its accessibility characteristics everywhere it is used — making it far harder to change after adoption than to set correctly now.
- C) Item 4, because brand consistency affects everything.
- D) Item 3, because early work has the highest leverage.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The right frame is what your input can still change and whether there is time, and item 1 carries a multiplier the others lack. Item 2 is one label with a known fix somebody can make in an hour, which is why it does not need a quarter of your capacity. Item 3 has no deadline. Item 4 is the interesting distractor at your level: a brand refresh applied across existing services is expensive, highly visible, and — unless the palette has been checked against every context it will meet — a substantial accessibility risk rather than an improvement. It is work you would want to shape early rather than lead late, and recognising that the right intervention is a usage specification rather than a quarter of capacity is the judgement being tested.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Cross-service synthesis**

Three items reach you in a month. One team reports users missing a status change. A second service's analytics show abandonment at a page whose only state indicator is colour. An audit of a third flags colour-only status. What is the strongest synthesis?

- A) Three teams have made the same mistake.
- B) The component library almost certainly offers coloured status chips with no non-colour variant, so three teams using it correctly produced colour-only status — the fix is in the shared asset, and it removes the problem for services that have not built the page yet.
- C) Teams should be reminded not to use colour alone.
- D) Each team should add an icon.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The synthesis that adds value asks why three teams independently made the same choice, and the usual answer is that the shared asset made it the easy one. Option B identifies that and locates the fix where it prevents recurrence rather than only correcting instances. Option D fixes three pages and leaves the fourth team to repeat it. Option C is the weakest available response to any recurring problem — teams using a component as supplied did not need reminding of a principle, they needed a variant that did not exist. Option A misattributes a systemic gap to individual error, and at your level that misattribution has a cost beyond the immediate: teams told they have made a mistake when they used your library correctly will trust the library less and you less.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Feedback on a token proposal**

A lead proposes a new secondary text grey at 4.6:1 against white for the token set. What is the most useful intervention?

- A) Approve it; it passes 4.5:1.
- B) Note that 4.6:1 leaves 0.1 of headroom and will fail against any tinted panel in the system, and that tokens get used wherever they look right rather than re-measured — so ask what backgrounds it will meet and require enough headroom to survive them.
- C) Reject it.
- D) Require testing on every background.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The value passes and passes by nothing. Contrast falls as a background darkens towards the text, so a grey passing on white fails on a light grey panel — and because a token is available everywhere, nobody re-measures it in context, which is both the convenience of tokens and their risk. Option B reframes the decision around the backgrounds the token will meet and, importantly for your level, expresses it as a requirement rather than a preference: headroom is a rule you can write into the standard once, whereas re-litigating individual values is work that never ends. Option D is not wrong and is impractical as a standing rule, since new tints appear faster than audits — which is precisely the argument for headroom. Option C reaches a defensible conclusion without reasoning that survives challenge.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Diagnosing a systemic problem**

Eight teams consistently use off-scale spacing values. Each has a plausible local reason. What is the strongest diagnosis and response?

- A) Remind teams to use the scale.
- B) Eight teams doing the same thing for plausible reasons is one systemic cause with eight rationalisations; establish which — the scale lacks a step designs need, tokens are unavailable in the design tool, or component defaults sit off-scale — and fix that, since the remedies differ entirely.
- C) Add spacing to the review checklist.
- D) Accept it; some layouts need bespoke values.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Eight teams behaving identically for individually plausible reasons is the signature of a systemic cause, and the plausibility is what makes case-by-case challenge ineffective. Option B names the realistic candidates, which imply entirely different remedies: adding a step is a morning's decision; getting tokens into the design tool is a tooling job; and component defaults sitting off-scale is a defect in the shared asset that will keep generating off-scale values however carefully teams work. Diagnosing wrongly costs a quarter and the credibility of the next attempt. Option A is the weakest available response. Option C adds a check for a defect the system generates. Option D accepts a rationalisation that is occasionally true and constantly used.

**Question 8 (harder) — Scoping a flaw**

A study you are assuring ran on a single calibrated monitor. Four findings: (i) participants could not find a secondary action; (ii) participants read body text comfortably; (iii) participants misinterpreted an icon; (iv) participants distinguished status colours easily. Which are most compromised?

- A) All four.
- B) None.
- C) Findings (ii) and (iv) are most compromised, because a calibrated display flatters legibility and colour discrimination — while (i) and (iii) concern position and comprehension, which the display affects far less.
- D) Only (iv).

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The skill is scoping precisely rather than treating a condition as invalidating everything or nothing. A calibrated monitor makes low-contrast text look fine and similar hues look distinct, which is exactly what findings (ii) and (iv) claim. Findings (i) and (iii) concern where something is and what an icon means, which display quality barely touches. Option A discards two sound findings, and at your scale that does organisational damage: a head of profession who condemns wholesale teaches the organisation that declaring test conditions invalidates the work, which is a direct incentive to stop declaring them — and you will then be assuring studies whose conditions are invisible, which is worse than the present situation because you no longer know what you are missing.

**Question 9 (harder) — Apparent contradiction between teams**

Two teams reach opposite conclusions about the same grey: one finds it legible, the other that users struggle. Both studies look sound. What is strongest?

- A) Commission a third study.
- B) Establish what differs before treating it as a contradiction — most likely the background, since a grey legible on white fails on a tint, or the size and weight, since the threshold differs — and the synthesis is that the token is context-dependent and its documentation must say so.
- C) Report both and note the inconsistency.
- D) Treat the larger study as authoritative.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Apparent contradictions between sound studies are usually context differences in disguise, and in this discipline the candidates are unusually specific and checkable: background, size, weight and display conditions each change whether the same grey works. Option B reaches the synthesis, and the synthesis is more valuable than either finding — it produces a concrete documentation change telling every team in the organisation that this token has conditions attached, which is exactly the kind of generalisable rule your role exists to extract. Option A spends money on a question that dissolves on inspection. Option C abdicates the synthesis; "note the inconsistency" is how a usable finding gets filed as a caveat. Option D picks a winner on an irrelevant criterion.

**Question 10 (harder) — Reading the distribution of failures**

Your standard requires an accessibility check before release. Sixteen of twenty services complied; the four that did not were all in one directorate, all under ministerial deadline pressure, all by experienced designers. What is the strongest response?

- A) Report the four and require remediation.
- B) Treat the concentration as diagnostic: four failures in one directorate under identical pressure by people who know the requirement indicates a process that cannot be complied with when time is short — investigate what it demands and how long it takes, build a fast route, and remediate the four alongside.
- C) Remind all designers of the requirement.
- D) Introduce a mandatory pre-release gate for every service.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The distribution is the most informative feature. Scattered breaches would indicate conduct; four concentrated under identical pressure by experienced people indicate a process that does not survive a deadline. Option B investigates the mechanism and fixes it, while the remediation clause preserves the point that a systemic diagnosis is not a reason to leave individual failures unaddressed. Option A treats the instances and guarantees recurrence. Option C is the weakest available compliance response. Option D imposes friction on sixteen compliant services to catch four, which is how organisational burden accumulates — and you will be defending that gate in two years when a director general asks why design sign-off is slow, having forgotten you introduced it.

**Question 11 (harder) — Evidence for a brand decision**

The organisation is considering a refreshed brand palette. Available: an agency's assertion that it is "accessible"; an internal check finding two of eight colours fail 4.5:1 against white; two directorates reporting it looks better; and a cost model. What is your most rigorous contribution?

- A) Oppose the refresh on the contrast finding.
- B) Set out what each source establishes and its limits — including that an agency's "accessible" usually means the palette *contains* compliant combinations rather than that every colour works for body text, so the two findings are not in conflict — and name what would most change the decision, which is how the palette performs on the tinted backgrounds and at the sizes services actually use.
- C) Support the refresh on the aesthetic reports.
- D) Recommend a full audit before deciding.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Your contribution at a decision like this is neither advocacy nor delay; it is making the evidence legible. Option B resolves an apparent contradiction that would otherwise consume the meeting, identifies that the internal check tested only against white and is therefore asking the wrong question for a palette that will meet tinted panels, and names the residual uncertainty. Option A opposes on partial evidence and will be read as the design profession obstructing a brand decision, which costs standing you need for the usage specification that will actually solve this. Option C ignores the finding. Option D defers a decision the evidence may already inform, and heads of profession who reflexively call for more evidence find themselves excluded from decisions.

**Question 12 (harder) — A pattern across your organisation**

Reviewing a year of output, you find two teams consistently produce accessible, on-system work and six do not. Both strong teams have a designer who checks every screen in a contrast tool before handover. What is the most useful inference and action?

- A) The six weaker teams need training.
- B) A pre-handover check is a cheap transferable routine that plausibly explains the difference — watch how they do it to establish what it actually catches, then build it into expectations and, where possible, into tooling so it happens automatically rather than depending on anyone remembering.
- C) Recruit more designers like those two.
- D) Increase assurance scrutiny on the six weaker teams.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The pattern is an opportunity to identify a practice, not to grade people. A check before handover catches defects when they cost minutes rather than after they propagate into a build. Option B goes and watches first, which matters because the routine may be catching something other than contrast — often the real benefit is that somebody looks deliberately at every screen rather than only at the ones they were working on, and that is a different thing to propagate. And it names the strongest form of propagation: contrast is one of the few design properties a script can compute, so automating the check removes reliance on discipline entirely. Option A posits a knowledge deficit without diagnosing one. Option C misattributes a routine to individuals. Option D catches more defects and creates no capacity to prevent them.

### Preparation tips

- **Read outputs for the conditions behind them.** A finding matching its test conditions too neatly is telling you something.
- **Scope flaws precisely.** Wholesale condemnation teaches the organisation to stop declaring conditions.
- **Require headroom rather than repeated testing.** It is a rule you write once.
- **Ask why several teams made the same choice.** Usually the shared asset made it the easy one.
- **Weight decisions by adoption.**
- **Read the distribution of failures.** Concentrated non-compliance diagnoses a process.
- **Resolve apparent contradictions by definition.** An agency's "accessible" and your audit measure different things.
- **Prefer automation to discipline.** A check that runs itself does not depend on anyone remembering.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Applying a standard as written when the drafting is the defect.** Fix the standard.
- **Approving values at the threshold.**
- **Telling teams they erred when they used your library correctly.** It costs trust in you and in the library.
- **Adding friction for the compliant majority.**
- **Picking a winner between contradictory studies.**
- **Testing on the best screen in the building.**
- **Reaching for training as the answer to a systemic problem.**
- **Opposing a brand decision on partial evidence.**

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A numeric reasoning assessment measures how accurately you handle quantitative material. At your level the emphasis is almost wholly on interrogation. You will need to compute correctly, but the questions that matter ask whether a figure means what somebody claims — and in most rooms you enter, you may be the only person who will ask.

The professional stake is unusually high for your discipline. Visual decisions are the ones colleagues feel most entitled to have opinions about, which leaves design leadership perpetually at risk of being treated as taste. A measured value is the only thing that reliably converts a matter of preference into a matter of fact, and a head of profession who can say "that token measures 4.4:1 and it is used across eleven services" is exercising authority that one who says "that grey is too light" is not. This is the section that underwrites the rest of your role.

The format is typically a timed online test of fifteen to thirty minutes, each item presenting a table, chart or paragraph of figures with a multiple-choice question. Calculators are usually provided and the arithmetic is tractable.

Four habits carry the load. Establish what the question asks before looking at the data. Identify the denominator and the population it represents. Estimate, calculate, compare. And sanity-check against the world.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The numerical skills tested map onto your named skills:

- **Interrogating quantitative claims** maps to defining and assuring best practice.
- **Aggregating across services** maps to **Evidence-based design**, where you use evidence to improve how the organisation works.
- **Comparing groups fairly** maps to **Designing for everyone**, where you guide organisations to adopt inclusive practices — and where "measurably" is what distinguishes guidance from exhortation.
- **Threshold and headroom reasoning** maps to **Designing strategically**, where shared assets carry their properties everywhere.
- **Presenting figures at defensible strength** maps to **Design communication**.
- **Detecting misleading metrics** maps to **Evidence-based design**, where a wrong measure misdirects effort across an estate.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Rate from counts**

Of 9,600 users who reached a page, 7,200 completed it. What percentage?

- A) 70%
- B) 75%
- C) 80%
- D) 85%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 7,200 ÷ 9,600 = 0.75, so 75%, and the answer is B. Estimate first: 7,200 of 9,600 is three quarters. Options A and C come from dividing by 10,286 and 9,000. The three-second estimate before every calculation is the cheapest available error insurance and costs nothing under a clock.

**Question 2 (easy) — Thresholds at the boundary**

A standard requires 4.5:1 for text under 24 pixels and 3:1 for text 24 pixels or larger. Four cases: 14px at 4.4:1; 18px at 4.5:1; 24px at 2.9:1; 28px at 3.0:1. How many meet the standard?

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 14px needs 4.5:1 — 4.4 fails. 18px needs 4.5:1 — 4.5 meets it exactly, and "at least" is inclusive. 24px falls into the *larger* category because the wording is "24 or larger" rather than "over 24", so needs 3:1 — 2.9 fails. 28px needs 3:1 — 3.0 passes. Two pass, giving B. Three boundary readings decide this, and each is a real distinction that produces either false failures — wasting effort and making the standard look unreasonable — or false passes, which ship. At your level the useful move is to write those three readings into the guidance explicitly, because they are exactly what teams get wrong and exactly what a one-line note can prevent.

**Question 3 (easy) — Grid arithmetic**

A 16-column grid spans 1,440 pixels with 24-pixel gutters between columns and no outer margin. What is one column's width?

- A) 67.5 pixels
- B) 72 pixels
- C) 84 pixels
- D) 90 pixels

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Sixteen columns have fifteen gutters between them, not sixteen. The gutters take 15 × 24 = 360 pixels, leaving 1,440 − 360 = 1,080 for the columns, and 1,080 ÷ 16 = 67.5. So A. Option D is 1,440 ÷ 16, ignoring gutters, the commonest error. The off-by-one is worth fixing permanently: for n columns there are n − 1 internal gutters. At your level the consequence is not one wrong sum but a system definition whose numbers never reconcile, which every designer using it notices and nobody can explain — and which quietly licenses the off-scale values you will later be trying to eliminate.

**Question 4 (easy) — Centre and spread**

Time to locate a control across eleven participants, in seconds: 3, 4, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 11, 14 and 96. Which summary is most defensible?

- A) Mean 15.2 seconds
- B) Median 7 seconds, range 3 to 96, with the 96-second case described separately
- C) Median 7 seconds
- D) Mean 15.2 seconds, range 3 to 96

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The values sum to 167, and 167 ÷ 11 = 15.2, so the mean exceeds ten of the eleven observations. The median is the sixth value, 7 seconds. But the median alone conceals a session lasting thirteen times the typical duration, which is the most informative observation — in a visual discipline that usually indicates a contrast, size or hierarchy failure the other ten worked around without noticing, and those are exactly the defects that reach live because nobody articulates them. Option B supplies centre, spread and explicit treatment of the outlier. Write into your reporting conventions that a central figure never travels alone.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Weighted aggregation**

Five services report accessibility defects per hundred components: 2 on 600 components, 8 on 100, 4 on 200, 6 on 200, and 3 on 400. What is the overall rate per hundred across all 1,500?

- A) 3.1
- B) 3.5
- C) 4.0
- D) 4.6

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Convert to counts. 2 per hundred on 600 = 12 defects. 8 per hundred on 100 = 8. 4 per hundred on 200 = 8. 6 per hundred on 200 = 12. 3 per hundred on 400 = 12. Total 52 defects across 1,500 components, so 52 ÷ 15 = 3.47 per hundred, about 3.5, giving B. Option D is the unweighted mean of 2, 8, 4, 6 and 3, which is 4.6 and over-weights the 100-component service against the 600-component one. The organisational point matters more than the arithmetic: the unweighted figure describes the average *service*, while the weighted figure describes the average *component* — and only the second describes what users encounter, which is the number your board should be seeing.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Comparing groups**

Of 1,500 users on wide screens, 1,350 completed. Of 1,000 on narrow screens, 500 completed. What is the gap in percentage points?

- A) 30
- B) 40
- C) 50
- D) 90

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Wide: 1,350 ÷ 1,500 = 90%. Narrow: 500 ÷ 1,000 = 50%. The gap is 40 percentage points, giving B. Options C and D are the two group rates offered as if they were the gap. A 40 point gap is enormous, and at your level the useful follow-up is not to investigate this service but to ask whether the same pattern appears in others using the same component library — because a reflow failure in a shared component produces this signature everywhere it is used, and finding it once across the estate is worth more than fixing it once in a service.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Percentage change with a caution**

Reported accessibility defects across the estate fell from 600 to 450 a quarter after a change to the review process. What is the change, and what should you check?

- A) 25% decrease, and it demonstrates the process change worked
- B) 25% decrease, but reported defects can fall because there are fewer or because fewer are being reported, and a review process change plausibly affects both — so check whether the same people are looking as hard
- C) 33% decrease, and it demonstrates the change worked
- D) 33% decrease, with the same caution

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The difference is 150, and 150 ÷ 600 = 0.25, a 25% decrease, so options C and D miscalculate by dividing by the new figure. Between A and B the interpretation decides it, and the caution is the substantive point: a reported-defect count is a product of both the defect rate and the inspection effort, and changing a review process is precisely the kind of intervention that alters the second. If the new process involves fewer reviewers or a shorter checklist, a fall in reported defects may mean nothing at all — or may conceal a rise. Option B names that. At your level this matters because a 25% improvement is exactly the figure that would go into an annual report on your signature.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Quantifying impact across an estate**

Fourteen services use a component whose focus indicator fails the contrast requirement. Combined annual sessions are 5.6 million, and roughly 3% involve keyboard-only navigation. How many sessions a year are affected?

- A) 16,800
- B) 168,000
- C) 1,680,000
- D) 56,000

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 3% of 5,600,000 is 0.03 × 5,600,000 = 168,000, giving B. Option A is 3% of 560,000; option D is 1%. The reason this belongs in a design guide is what it does to a conversation: "the focus indicator on this component is below 3:1" competes for attention with everything else in a backlog, and "168,000 sessions a year in which the user cannot see where they are" does not. Converting a shared-component defect into sessions is among the most effective moves available to you, precisely because the multiplier is what makes shared assets worth your attention in the first place — and it is an argument that works at a board, which a contrast ratio is not.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Choosing the denominator**

A journey shows 80,000 start, 64,000 reach a form, 48,000 reach review, 45,600 submit. What proportion of those reaching review submitted?

- A) 57%
- B) 60%
- C) 71%
- D) 95%

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** The stem names users who reached review, so the denominator is 48,000 and the part is 45,600: 45,600 ÷ 48,000 = 0.95, giving D. Option A uses 80,000, answering a whole-journey question nobody asked. Strategically the funnel shows where effort belongs: 16,000 lost between start and form and 16,000 between form and review, against 2,400 at submission. Establishing that in fifteen seconds is what lets you redirect a plan in the meeting rather than after a quarter.

**Question 10 (harder) — A value with no headroom**

A proposed token measures 4.6:1 against white. The system contains seven panel tints. What is the most useful observation?

- A) It passes.
- B) It passes by 0.1 and will fail against every tint, since contrast falls as the background moves towards the text — and because tokens are used wherever they look right rather than re-measured, a boundary value becomes a defect on a page nobody audited; the durable answer is a headroom requirement in the standard rather than a test.
- C) It should be tested on each of the seven tints.
- D) The tints should be removed.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The strongest observation names the mechanism rather than the measurement, and — at your level — the durable remedy rather than the immediate one. A value at 4.6:1 against white has 0.1 of margin, and any tint reduces the ratio. Because a token is available everywhere, nobody re-measures it, which is both the convenience and the risk. Option B draws the conclusion that scales: a headroom requirement written into the standard once removes this whole class of problem, whereas option C solves this token and leaves the next one, and is in any case impractical as a standing rule because new tints appear faster than audits. Option A treats the threshold as the whole question. Option D removes the design to solve the problem.

**Question 11 (harder) — Percentage points and relative change**

Completion on narrow screens rose from 50% to 62% after a component fix. Which statement is accurate?

- A) A 12% increase.
- B) A 12 percentage point increase, equivalent to a 24% relative increase.
- C) A 24 percentage point increase.
- D) A 19.4% increase.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The arithmetic gap is 12 percentage points; the relative increase is 12 ÷ 50 = 24%. Option B labels both. Option A is ambiguous and generally read as relative. Option C misapplies the label. Option D divides by the new value. At your level the useful move is making both figures a standing reporting convention rather than a case-by-case judgement, because it removes the temptation entirely and protects your teams from any suggestion the flattering figure was chosen — which, in a discipline already suspected of advocacy, is worth more than the accuracy alone.

**Question 12 (harder) — A directionless metric**

After a type size increase across a service, time on page rose 18% and completion was unchanged. A director general concludes the change slowed users down. What is the strongest analysis?

- A) Agree.
- B) Time on page has no direction — it rises when people struggle and when they read more — so unchanged completion with longer dwell is as consistent with people reading text they previously skipped as with being slowed; the way to distinguish them is whether errors or contact volume moved, and it is worth saying so before the conclusion becomes a decision.
- C) Revert the change.
- D) The change failed.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Time on page is among the most misused metrics in interface work because it has no direction. Unchanged completion rules out the most alarming interpretation. Option B names the ambiguity, identifies what would resolve it, and adds the clause specific to your level: saying so before the conclusion hardens into a decision, because once a director general has stated a view in a meeting it becomes expensive to revisit. Option C reverts on an ambiguous measure, which is a particular hazard for legibility improvements since their benefits appear in comprehension and error rates rather than speed — a service judging them on dwell time will systematically undo them. Option D declares failure on a metric that cannot support it.

### Preparation tips

- **Read thresholds at the boundary, and write those readings into your guidance.**
- **Remember n − 1 gutters for n columns.**
- **Return to counts before combining rates.** The weighted figure describes what users encounter.
- **Convert shared-component defects into sessions.** The multiplier is the argument.
- **Write headroom into the standard rather than testing every value.**
- **Ask whether a fall in reported defects means fewer defects or less looking.**
- **Distrust directionless metrics, and say so early.**
- **Make both framings a convention.**

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Rounding a contrast ratio.**
- **Ignoring gutters, or counting one too many.**
- **Averaging service-level rates without weighting.**
- **Approving values at the threshold.**
- **Claiming an improvement from reported-defect counts.**
- **Letting a directionless metric undo a legibility improvement.**
- **Confusing percentages with percentage points.**
- **Solving one token when the standard could solve all of them.**

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately you read. At your level the documents that matter are governance frameworks, organisational standards, accessibility regulations, audit reports, cross-government guidance, brand guidelines and contracts. A misread exception clause becomes an organisation-wide compliance failure; a misread audit finding becomes advice given confidently to a permanent secretary.

The classic format presents a short passage of dense workplace prose, typically three to six sentences, followed by statements to classify as True, False or Cannot Say. Alongside these come comprehension items, main-point items, and questions asking which of four summaries is most accurate.

The governing rule is that you answer from the passage alone, and the part specific to your level is this: expertise actively costs marks. The more accessibility criteria you have applied, the more readily you supply the clause you expect. Candidates at your level underperform on verbal reasoning at rates that surprise them.

Cannot Say is the answer experienced leaders avoid most, and the trap is sharpest where the claim would support something you want.

Small words carry the load, and one matters more in your discipline than in most: "or". Accessibility criteria routinely offer alternative routes separated by "or", and reading them as conjunctions produces needlessly dark palettes, unnecessary work and arguments with communications colleagues — which spends credibility you need for the requirements that are genuinely non-negotiable.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The verbal skills tested map onto your named skills:

- **Reading precisely** maps to defining and assuring best practice.
- **Separating statement from inference** maps to **Evidence-based design**.
- **Identifying the main point** maps to **Design communication**, where you gain support from senior leaders and communicate across boundaries.
- **Handling qualified language** maps to **Designing strategically**, where knowing which constraints are hard tells you where design can move.
- **Reading for absence** maps to **Designing for everyone**.
- **Reading cross-government prose accurately** maps to your role level's collaboration with counterparts, where shared standards are the medium.

### Practice questions

*Passage A — for Questions 1 to 4*

"Text smaller than 24 pixels must have a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 against its background. Text 24 pixels or larger, or 19 pixels or larger and bold, must have a contrast ratio of at least 3:1. Non-text elements that convey information, including icons and the boundaries of form fields, must have a contrast ratio of at least 3:1. Logotypes are exempt, as is text that is part of an inactive user interface component."

**Question 1 (easy)** — Statement: "All text must have a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The passage sets 3:1 for large and bold text and exempts logotypes and text in inactive components. The statement's "all" contradicts all three, so False. This is the most common real misquotation in the field, and at your level correcting it matters in both directions: a team applying 4.5:1 to a large heading produces a needlessly dark palette and an avoidable argument with communications, which then makes the necessary arguments harder to win.

**Question 2 (easy)** — Statement: "Bold text at 20 pixels needs 4.5:1." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The second sentence sets 3:1 for text "24 pixels or larger, **or** 19 pixels or larger and bold". Bold text at 20 pixels satisfies the second route, so the threshold is 3:1. False. This is the "or" trap and the most consequential misreading in applying contrast criteria. Read as "and", the clause would require text to be both 24 pixels and bold, making it nearly useless; read as written, it recognises that size and weight each aid legibility and either qualifies.

**Question 3 (moderate)** — Statement: "The boundary of a disabled form field must meet 3:1." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** Two provisions bear on this and reading them together settles it. Non-text elements conveying information, expressly including field boundaries, must meet 3:1. The final sentence exempts *text* that is part of an inactive component — and a boundary is not text. So the exemption does not reach it, the 3:1 requirement stands, and the statement follows. True. The item rewards noticing exactly what an exemption is drafted for: this one covers the label of a disabled control, not its visible boundary, and a reader who generalises "inactive components are exempt" will answer False. The practical distinction matters, because a disabled field whose boundary disappears is a field users cannot locate at all, which is a different and worse problem than a label they do not need to read.

**Question 4 (harder)** — Statement: "A decorative icon must meet 3:1." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The third sentence requires 3:1 for non-text elements *that convey information*, naming icons as an example. A decorative icon conveys none, so it falls outside the stated requirement — but the passage does not say decorative elements are exempt, only where the requirement applies. Silence leaves it undetermined, so Cannot Say rather than False. The distinction between stating where a rule applies and stating where it does not recurs constantly in standards, and at your level it is the kind of gap that produces two directorates applying the same standard differently, both defensibly — which is a drafting problem for you rather than a compliance problem for them.

*Passage B — for Questions 5 to 8*

"The cross-government review of design assurance found considerable variation between departments. Five departments run an automated contrast check in their build pipeline; the remainder check manually at review. Departments with automated checks reported fewer defects reaching live, although the review did not examine whether their designs were more accessible overall. The review recommends that the profession board consider whether a common approach would be proportionate."

**Question 5 (easy)** — Statement: "Some departments do not run an automated contrast check." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The passage states that five departments run automated checks and the remainder check manually, establishing directly that some do not. True. Answer quickly and bank the time. The only conceivable ambiguity — whether "the remainder" might be empty — is closed by the passage describing what the remainder does.

**Question 6 (moderate)** — Statement: "Automated checks produce more accessible designs." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The passage reports fewer defects reaching live and then states explicitly that overall accessibility was not examined. Those are different: automated checks might produce better designs, or might catch the same defects earlier — which produces the same statistic and is a genuine benefit — or those departments might differ in other ways. The passage flags its own silence, so Cannot Say. This is the specific trap for someone in your position, because "automation improves accessibility" is a claim you would like to be true and would probably cite when proposing a common approach. Citing it and being asked for the evidence is how a good proposal loses, and automation is worth arguing for on the grounds the evidence actually supports — earlier detection at lower cost.

**Question 7 (moderate)** — Statement: "The review recommends introducing a common approach." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The review recommends that the board *consider whether* a common approach would be proportionate, which includes concluding it would not be. The statement asserts a recommendation to introduce, which was not made, so False rather than Cannot Say. At your level you are likely to be both the recipient and the person reporting what was done, so misreading this commits your organisation to something nobody asked for and then reports delivery against it.

**Question 8 (harder)** — Statement: "Manual checking at review is a weaker practice than automated checking." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The passage describes two practices and reports one difference in outcome while declining to assess overall accessibility. It makes no judgement about relative strength. A reader may reasonably infer that automation is stronger, and the passage neither supports nor excludes that, so Cannot Say. What makes this harder than Question 6 is that the passage's structure leans towards automation — mentioned first, associated with a favourable outcome — and structural lean is not content. Noticing when you are responding to a document's implied stance rather than its assertions applies directly to audit reports, which routinely imply more than they will state so readers draw the conclusion the authors lacked evidence to assert.

*Passage C — for Questions 9 to 12*

"Interactive elements must have a visible focus indicator with a contrast ratio of at least 3:1 against adjacent colours. Touch targets must be at least 24 by 24 pixels, unless an equivalent target of at least 24 by 24 pixels is available elsewhere on the same screen. Where a component is provided in the design system, services must use it unless an exemption has been agreed with the head of graphic design. Components added to the system must be documented before use."

**Question 9 (easy)** — Statement: "Every touch target must be at least 24 by 24 pixels." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The requirement carries an exception: a smaller target is permitted where an equivalent of at least 24 by 24 pixels is available elsewhere on the same screen. The statement's "every" drops that, so False. The exception exists for a practical reason: an inline link inside a paragraph cannot always be enlarged without breaking the text, and the requirement accepts that provided the same action is reachable nearby. Knowing the exception is what stops your teams fighting an argument they should not be having, and stops your standard acquiring a reputation for being unreasonable.

**Question 10 (moderate)** — Statement: "A service using a custom component has breached the requirements." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The third sentence requires services to use the system component *unless an exemption has been agreed with the head of graphic design*. Whether one was agreed is not stated, so the case is undetermined. Cannot Say. This has an edge specific to your role: you are the approver, so in practice you can settle half of this from your own records rather than inferring it — which is a reason to keep them, and a reason to be careful about exemptions granted informally in a corridor, since an approval you cannot evidence produces exactly this ambiguity in an audit.

**Question 11 (harder)** — Statement: "An undocumented component sitting in the system has breached the requirements." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The final sentence requires components added to the system to be documented *before use*. A component in the system but undocumented has breached only if it has been used; if it has been added and not yet used, the deadline has not arrived. The statement does not say whether it is in use, so the case is undetermined. Cannot Say. The specific reading matters: "before use" locates the obligation at the point of use rather than addition, which permits a component to sit awaiting documentation without anyone being in breach — a distinction worth knowing before you tell a team they are non-compliant.

**Question 12 (harder) — Main point**

Which best captures the main point of Passage C?

- A) Services must use design system components.
- B) Interactive elements must be perceivable and operable regardless of how a user perceives or points, and services should draw on documented shared components unless an exemption is agreed — with visible focus, adequately sized targets or equivalents, and documentation preceding use.
- C) Touch targets must be at least 24 by 24 pixels.
- D) Components must be documented before use.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The main point must cover the whole passage while adding nothing. Option A is one clause and drops its exemption. Options C and D are each true of one sentence, and C drops the equivalence exception. Option B captures all four requirements and the logic joining them: two concern making elements usable by people who cannot see or point precisely, and two ensure those properties are inherited and understood rather than reinvented. Note that B preserves both exceptions — a summary dropping one is how a standard acquires a reputation for being unreasonable, and you are the person who would then have to defend it.

### Preparation tips

- **Point at the words.** Before marking True, identify the phrase that makes it true.
- **Read "or" as "or".**
- **Check what an exemption actually covers.** One drafted for text does not extend to non-text elements.
- **Establish what a deadline is triggered by.** "Before use" is not "on addition".
- **Trust Cannot Say, especially where the claim would support your proposal.**
- **Distinguish stating where a rule applies from stating where it does not.** Where it produces two defensible readings, that is your drafting problem.
- **Keep exceptions in summaries.**
- **Notice structural lean.**

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Answering from professional knowledge.** The dominant error at your level.
- **Reading "or" as "and".**
- **Extending an exemption beyond its stated subject.**
- **Marking True on a claim that would help your case.**
- **Treating a proxy outcome as the thing itself.** Fewer defects reaching live is not greater accessibility.
- **Converting a scope statement into an exemption.**
- **Granting approvals you cannot evidence.**
- **Dropping an exception when summarising.**

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment presents realistic workplace scenarios and asks what you would do. There is no passage to parse and no arithmetic. You get a dilemma of the kind your role produces, four or five candidate responses, and a question asking which is most effective and often which is least.

This section measures whether your judgement matches what the profession considers good practice at your level. Your role places you as an expert practitioner who defines and assures best practice, influences design and organisational strategy, collaborates with counterparts across government, and focuses on ensuring the right conditions and environment for designers to work effectively.

Three features distinguish your level.

The first is that almost nothing you do is executed by you. An option solving a problem through your personal intervention is usually second-best, because it does not survive your absence and does not scale past your calendar. The reliable tell of a strong option is that it changes a shared asset, a standard, a routine or a set of conditions.

The second is that your authority is informal and, in a visual discipline, contested by default — everybody has eyes and therefore opinions. Options relying on craft assertion score poorly; options bringing measurement score well.

The third is that you absorb pressure downwards. When a director general tells a designer their brand colour is non-negotiable, the designer should not be holding that line.

Four principles carry most scenarios. Users who cannot see, read or operate something come first. Bring the value, not the opinion. Fix the shared asset and the conditions, not the instance. And calibrate escalation to severity.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The judgement dimensions map onto your named skills:

- **Defining and assuring best practice** maps to your role level's core accountability.
- **Conditions and culture** map to **Leading design**, where you support a culture of learning and improve design maturity, and to your focus on the right conditions for designers.
- **Influence with senior leaders** maps to **Design communication** and **Designing strategically**.
- **Cross-government collaboration** maps to collaborating with counterparts and to **Designing together**.
- **Organisational inclusion** maps to **Designing for everyone**.
- **Embedding evidence practice** maps to **Evidence-based design**.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — A designer under pressure from a director general**

A lead tells you a director general has instructed a designer to use the brand's light grey for body text, at 3.2:1, saying the brand is non-negotiable. What is the most effective response?

- A) Tell the lead to instruct the designer to refuse.
- B) Work out with the lead the alternative from inside the brand — a darker variant of the same hue — so there is something to offer rather than a refusal, and take the conversation with the director general yourself.
- C) Contact the director general and cite the accessibility regulations.
- D) Approve the light grey.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Two things must happen: the text becomes readable, and neither the lead nor the designer is left arguing with a director general about a brand. Option B does both, and the alternative matters — arriving with a darker variant turns a refusal into a substitution, and most brand palettes contain one because the primary is optimised for impact rather than reading. Taking the conversation yourself is your job. Option D is least effective, publishing text some people cannot read because of who asked, and establishing that seniority settles design questions — which is the precedent that will cost you every subsequent argument. Option A leaves a designer refusing a director general by proxy. Option C leads with regulation rather than a solution, which is the version of this conversation that confirms every stereotype about design governance.

**Question 2 (easy) — A standard teams route around**

Teams routinely use off-scale spacing despite a documented system. They describe the scale as too coarse. What is the strongest approach?

- A) Reissue the scale with a message from you.
- B) Review recent work with the teams to establish whether the scale genuinely lacks steps or whether the tokens are unavailable in the tool they design in, then fix whichever it is and revise the scale with them.
- C) Introduce compliance checks.
- D) Run training on the scale.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A standard teams route around has already failed, and reissuing changes only the date. Option B treats the objection as data and names the two realistic causes, which imply completely different remedies — adding steps, or a tooling fix. Diagnosing wrongly wastes a quarter and the credibility of the next attempt. Revising with the teams also produces authorship, the only mechanism by which a standard becomes self-sustaining rather than dependent on your attention. Option A is least effective, the purest form of appearing to act. Option C enforces a standard people have told you does not work, producing compliance theatre. Option D is useful and insufficient alone.

**Question 3 (easy) — Conditions for designers**

Your graphic designers are embedded one per service across ten services, are pulled into delivery work, have no time for critique, and rarely see each other. Three have left in eight months. What is the strongest response?

- A) Discuss workload with each delivery manager.
- B) Treat it as structural: establish a protected critique routine, negotiate the time with delivery leadership by showing what the pattern costs in rework and attrition, and change how designers are deployed if single embedding is the cause.
- C) Introduce a wellbeing initiative.
- D) Recruit replacements quickly.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Three departures in eight months alongside no critique and no professional contact is a structural pattern, and your role explicitly focuses on the conditions and environment for designers. Option B addresses it on three fronts, and the deployment clause matters particularly here: a graphic designer working alone has nobody to look at their work, and visual judgement degrades faster than most when unchecked — you stop seeing a design you have looked at for three days. Single embedding therefore damages the work as directly as the people, and your assurance burden rises to compensate. The negotiation detail matters too: protected time not paid for in delivery's currency gets cancelled by the third sprint. Option D is least effective, reproducing the attrition on a lag in a small market where your reputation travels fast.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Assuring across a boundary**

A directorate you do not manage has shipped a component whose focus indicator measures 1.8:1. It is now in four services. What is most effective?

- A) Advise the four services to stop using it.
- B) Contact the designer and their lead with the measured value and a reproduction, agree who fixes it and by when, and ask whether the focus style replaced a compliant system default — while making clear this is live in four services.
- C) Raise it at the design community.
- D) Report it to the service owners.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** A focus indicator at 1.8:1 means keyboard users cannot see where they are in four services. Option B moves quickly while doing what distinguishes a head's response: going to the people who can fix it, with a measured value rather than an impression, and asking the diagnostic question — because a failing custom focus style has usually replaced a compliant default, in which case reinstating it fixes everything at once and the same substitution has probably happened elsewhere. Option C is least effective, deferring a live accessibility defect to a scheduled forum and broadcasting a team's error before they can fix it, which costs their cooperation for a year. Option A bypasses the people who can fix the component. Option D escalates before asking.

**Question 5 (moderate) — A recurring problem with a clear cause**

Six teams keep producing colour-only status indicators. Each says the component library offers coloured chips and nothing else. What is most effective?

- A) Remind teams that meaning must not be conveyed by colour alone.
- B) Accept the diagnosis they have given you and fix the library by adding chips with icons or text, since six teams using the supplied component correctly are not the problem.
- C) Add colour-only status to the review checklist.
- D) Require each team to add an icon.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Six teams giving the same plausible explanation is unusually good diagnostic information — they have told you where the problem is. Option B acts on it, and the framing matters: teams using a supplied component as supplied have not erred, and treating them as though they had is both unfair and ineffective, since they will keep using what the library provides. Fixing the library removes the problem for every service including those that have not built the page yet. Option A is least effective, telling six teams a principle they already know and cannot act on, which also teaches them that reporting a library gap gets them a lecture. Option D fixes six instances and leaves the seventh team to repeat it. Option C adds a check for a defect your own system generates.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Competing directorates**

Two directors want your design capacity in the same quarter. One has a failing live service; the other a ministerially visible launch. Both have raised it with your director general. What is most effective?

- A) Split capacity.
- B) Set out for each what design work would deliver, what decision it informs, and the cost of deferral; recommend a sequence with reasoning; and put that recommendation to both directors and the director general together rather than negotiating separately.
- C) Prioritise the visible launch.
- D) Ask the director general to decide.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A prioritisation problem is being conducted as a political one, and the way through is to make the trade-off explicit and simultaneously visible. Option B does the analytical work and handles the politics correctly: putting the recommendation to both at once prevents the parallel-negotiation dynamic in which each director hears something slightly different and trust collapses when they compare notes. Making a recommendation rather than presenting options is essential. Option A is least effective — splitting constrained design capacity produces two efforts too thin to inform either decision, and in a visual discipline something worse than that, because half-finished design work looks finished and gets built. Option C ranks by visibility. Option D discards your judgement.

**Question 7 (moderate) — A brand decision already taken**

A brand refresh has been approved. Two of eight new colours fail 4.5:1 against white and the palette has not been checked against your tinted panels. What is most effective?

- A) Oppose the refresh.
- B) Accept the decision and make it workable: establish which colours fail in which contexts, propose a usage specification saying where each colour may be used, and identify the darker variants needed for body text — framing it as completing the palette rather than correcting it.
- C) Require the palette to be redesigned.
- D) Document the risk and proceed.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** The decision is made and reopening it will fail, so the useful work is making it workable. Option B does that, and the framing is what makes it succeed: a usage specification is a normal part of any brand palette, so proposing one completes the work rather than correcting somebody's judgement, and it lets you say where each colour *can* be used rather than only where it cannot. Option D is least effective — documenting a risk with nothing following it is how an exclusion becomes permanent while everyone feels it has been handled, and your name is on the register entry. Option A opposes a decision already taken, spending standing for nothing and confirming a stereotype. Option C asks for a redesign that will be refused.

**Question 8 (harder) — Practice that is not improving**

After a year of guidance, templates and talks, components still ship with contrast failures. What is the strongest response?

- A) Produce clearer guidance and repeat the training.
- B) Stop adding guidance and find out what is preventing it — most likely that nobody checks before handover, or that checking is manual and easy to skip — then put a check where the work already flows, automated so it does not depend on anyone remembering.
- C) Introduce a mandatory accessibility checklist.
- D) Report the failure to your director general.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A year of guidance that has not changed practice is evidence that the barrier is not knowledge, and continuing to supply knowledge is the characteristic failure of well-intentioned improvement programmes. Option B does the diagnostic work first and names blockers concerning *when and how* checking happens rather than whether people know it should. The remedy is the strongest available in this discipline: contrast is one of the few design properties a machine can check, so a check in the pipeline removes reliance on discipline entirely and catches defects when they cost minutes. Option A is least effective, repeating an intervention that demonstrably did not work and spending the credibility of the next one. Option C adds a checklist to a system already full of guidance. Option D reports a failure you are accountable for solving.

**Question 9 (harder) — An unowned problem**

Synthesising across services, you conclude the organisation's largest visual problem is inconsistency at the same moments across services — different focus styles, different status conventions, different error treatments — for users who move between them. No director owns cross-service consistency. What is most effective?

- A) Report it in your annual synthesis.
- B) Build the case with evidence from the affected services, quantify the affected population using analytics, identify who would need to own it, and take it to a forum with cross-service authority — accepting that securing an owner is itself months of work.
- C) Raise it with each service owner separately.
- D) Propose research on cross-service journeys.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Unowned problems are the characteristic failure of service-shaped organisations, and surfacing them is what cross-service synthesis is for. Option B recognises that the hard part is ownership rather than insight: evidence from several services makes the problem undeniable, quantification makes it comparable to problems that have owners and budgets, and a forum with cross-service authority is the only route by which anything unowned becomes owned. The closing clause is realistic — this is a campaign, not a communication. Option A is least effective: correct and filed, it changes nothing, which is the quiet fate of most organisational insight. Option C produces conversations in which each owner reasonably concludes it is mostly someone else's problem. Option D proposes more evidence when the evidence is sufficient.

**Question 10 (harder) — Cross-government collaboration**

Three departments are independently building the same complex component, each solving it differently, and none is confident about its accessibility. You have no authority over the other two. What is strongest?

- A) Focus on your own department's version.
- B) Convene the three departments' heads of design, establish whether the requirement is genuinely common, and if so propose one properly tested shared component or a joint contribution — accepting that you are building consensus rather than exercising authority, and noting that one well-tested component is more achievable than three adequately tested ones.
- C) Ask the cross-government profession board to mandate a common component.
- D) Share your version for information.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Three departments building one complex component three ways, none confident about accessibility, is precisely the waste cross-government collaboration exists to prevent — and the accessibility argument is the strongest available, because properly testing a complex component is expensive and doing it once is the only realistic route to doing it well. Option B is calibrated to the authority you have: convening is available, mandating is not, and establishing whether the requirement is genuinely common avoids the failure in which a shared component is agreed and fits nobody. Option A is least effective, accepting solvable waste and leaving three inadequately tested components in public services. Option C reaches for a mandate before building the case; boards asked without evidence decline, spending the ask. Option D creates nothing.

**Question 11 (harder) — A token you approved**

Nine months ago you approved a token now used in five services. You realise it measures 4.4:1 rather than the 4.6:1 recorded. What should you do?

- A) Note it for the next review.
- B) Notify all five now, state the measured value and where the token is used, correct it, and check whether other tokens approved in the same session were measured the same way — then change what the approval process requires.
- C) Correct the token quietly.
- D) Leave it; 4.4 is close.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** A token is used wherever it is available, so 4.4:1 is a failure across five services. Option B does the four things that matter — notifies, states the value rather than describing the problem, corrects it, and asks the diagnostic question. A mis-measured token usually means it was checked against the wrong background or with the wrong tool, so everything approved alongside it is suspect, and the final clause is what makes this a head's response rather than a fix: changing what the approval process requires is the only part that prevents the next one. Option D is least defensible: "close" applied once to a threshold becomes the standard for every future judgement. Option C leaves published work and method unexamined. Option A defers a live failure.

**Question 12 (harder) — A standard that will be resisted**

You intend to require that every token entry state its contrast against every background it is approved for. You anticipate resistance: teams will say the combinations are too numerous. What is strongest?

- A) Introduce it and handle objections as they arise.
- B) Engage the objection before introducing it, because it is partly right: the full matrix is large. Require contrast against *approved* backgrounds only, with a rule that using a token elsewhere requires a check — and pair it with a script that generates the matrix, so the work is automated rather than manual.
- C) Introduce it as guidance.
- D) Require it only for text tokens.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** The objection is real: eight tokens against six backgrounds is forty-eight combinations, and documenting each manually is a job nobody does twice. Option B resolves the tension by changing what is required — approved backgrounds rather than all — which is both feasible and more useful, since it tells the next designer where a token may be used rather than burying that in a matrix. The automation clause is the other half, since contrast is computable and generating the matrix removes the objection entirely. Option A is least effective: introducing a requirement you know to be contestable and arguing it case by case spends enormous credibility for a worse outcome. Option C removes the force. Option D exempts non-text tokens, where the 3:1 requirement applies and is most often forgotten.

### Preparation tips

- **Bring the value, not the opinion.** Your authority in a visual discipline rests on measurement.
- **Absorb pressure downwards.**
- **Arrive with the alternative from inside the constraint.**
- **Take a team's diagnosis seriously when six of them give the same one.**
- **Ask whether a failing style replaced a compliant default.** It is frequently a one-line fix across an estate.
- **Automate what can be automated.** Contrast is computable; discipline is not.
- **Frame corrections as completing work rather than correcting judgement.**
- **Convene where you cannot mandate.**

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Reissuing a standard teams route around.**
- **Telling teams they erred when they used your library correctly.**
- **Documenting a risk and calling it management.**
- **Opposing a decision already taken.**
- **Splitting design capacity.** Half-finished visual work looks finished and gets built.
- **Treating "close enough" as a threshold.**
- **Leading with regulation rather than a solution.**
- **Filing a strategic insight rather than finding it an owner.**

## Conclusion

You have reached the end, and it is worth registering the scale: forty-eight questions across four assessment types, with the reasoning worked through on each. That is a substantial piece of professional preparation and more than most people at your level undertake.

Look back at the ground. In the cognitive section you found a drafting defect in your own standard rather than an error in an entry, read a finding that matched its test conditions too neatly, asked why six teams independently made the same choice, weighted a token decision by the eight services that would inherit it, and treated a pre-handover check as a transferable routine rather than a story about talent. In the numeric section you read thresholds at their boundaries, remembered that sixteen columns have fifteen gutters, weighted rather than averaged, converted a shared-component defect into 168,000 sessions a year, and declined to claim an improvement from a reported-defect count without asking who was looking. In the verbal section you read "or" as offering alternative routes, established that an exemption drafted for text does not reach a field boundary, noticed that "before use" locates an obligation at use rather than addition, and declined to mark True on a claim that would have supported your own proposal. And in the situational judgement section you absorbed a director general's pressure, fixed a component library rather than lecturing six teams, made a brand decision workable rather than opposing it, convened three departments you could not compel, and changed an approval process after your own approval failed.

Two threads run through all of it. The first is whether you know the limits of the evidence and will say so — including, and especially, when the claim would support something you want. The second is whether you act on shared assets and conditions rather than instances. Between them those are most of what your role consists of. Organisations can produce screens without you. What you supply is a set of shared properties that can be trusted wherever they are used, conditions in which that is the normal result, and a profession that will still be producing both after you leave.

A word about the particular difficulty of your level. Visual authority is the weakest kind, because everybody has eyes and therefore opinions, and that is why measurement matters disproportionately in your role — the numeric section of this guide is not an aside, it is the foundation of your standing. It is also why the errors that cost you most are the confident ones: a design you have looked at for three days stops being something you can see, a value you measured once stops being something you re-check, and nobody in your organisation is positioned to catch either.

If you want to build on this, a few things repay effort more than rereading. Measure every token in your system against every background it will meet, and count how many have no headroom. Complete a service you are accountable for using only a keyboard and watch the focus indicator. View it at 400% zoom, at 375 pixels, and on a phone outdoors. Ask whether a contrast check could run in your build pipeline, and if the answer is yes, do that before anything else on this list. Ask a head of design in another department to critique your standard as harshly as they would a stranger's. And go and watch what your strongest team does before assuming you know what it is.

Finally, for the day itself. An assessment samples your reasoning on one morning under artificial conditions. It is not a verdict on your judgement or your career. Read the stem twice, read thresholds at the boundary, find the denominator, trust Cannot Say, and remember that at your level the strongest answer is almost always the one honest about uncertainty and aimed at the shared asset rather than the instance.

Good luck. The profession is stronger for leaders who prepare like this, and so — which is the whole point — are the people who have to read what your organisation publishes.
