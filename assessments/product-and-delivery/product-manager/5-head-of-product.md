# Head of Product - Psychometric Assessment Resources

## Introduction

Welcome. This guide has been created for you as a head of product working within the UK Government Digital and Data profession. Whether you are preparing for an assessment connected with appointment or progression, benchmarking yourself against the framework, keeping your own instruments sharp, or — as likely at your level — building the fluency to interpret, commission, and govern assessment for the community you lead, this document offers structured, role-specific practice with fully worked reasoning.

Psychometric assessments are standardised exercises measuring the cognitive capabilities and behavioural tendencies that predict performance in a role. At head-of-product level, the job-specific versions are built from the material you actually govern: organisational product strategy, profession-wide workforce and pay decisions, community health data, business cases you own and iterate through their life cycles, cross-government relationships, and the standards you are responsible for setting and raising across product management in your organisation. The decisions they simulate are yours too: prioritising across multiple complex portfolios with data, influencing the priorities of senior stakeholders, managing recruitment, development, moves and exits across an entire community, line managing senior and lead product managers, and representing product community interests to organisational management — including when those interests are inconvenient to the room you are standing in.

Why does this practice matter at the top of the profession's ladder? Three reasons, each distinct from the levels below. First, your errors are systemic: a misread trend, an unaudited benefit figure, or an imprecisely read policy at your level does not damage a product or even a portfolio — it miscalibrates an organisation's product strategy, a community's pay case, or a profession's standards. The disciplines these assessments train are the same disciplines that keep organisational decisions sound. Second, you are the profession's assessor: you oversee every community member's role, assignment, development, pay and performance; you manage recruitment and exits; you contribute to service assessments; and you set the standards others are measured against. Fluency in how structured assessment works — what it measures well, where it misleads, how its questions are engineered — makes you a sharper and fairer judge of the people and teams whose careers move on your judgement. Third, you are the exemplar: the reflective practice this guide demands is the practice you ask of a whole community, and heads who visibly maintain their own fundamentals hold the standing to raise everyone else's.

The document is organised into four assessment sections — cognitive, numeric reasoning, verbal reasoning, and situational judgement — each with the same structure: what the assessment measures and why it matters at head level; a mapping to the named skills in your role summary; practice questions progressing from easy to challenging with complete worked explanations tying each technique to organisational product leadership; then preparation tips and common pitfalls.

To get the most from it: commit to answers before reading explanations; treat the explanations as the substantive content — they model reasoning you can reuse in strategy papers, pay cases, and coaching conversations; and use the hardest scenarios, which involve genuine tensions between the community you champion and the organisation you serve, as prompts for honest reflection on calls you have actually made. Where you disagree with a keyed answer, write down why — at your level, articulated disagreement with a rubric is exactly the judgement the profession pays you for.

Take your time, and enjoy the practice. Let's begin.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive ability assessment measures the mental processes your role demands using realistic material. At head-of-product level that material is organisational: strategy documents whose internal consistency you own, community workforce data, cross-portfolio outcome sets, business cases you develop and iterate, governance structures you design, and the reasoning of senior people — lead product managers, directors, other heads of profession — whose arguments you must evaluate, strengthen, or challenge in rooms where the stakes are organisational direction.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 15 to 30 minutes with 15 to 30 questions across pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving, scored objectively against a senior professional or executive norm group, often adaptively, with speed and accuracy reported separately.

At your level the assessment simulates three distinctive cognitive loads. Governing at one remove: almost everything you know about your community's forty products arrives mediated — through dashboards, reports, and the accounts of leads you line manage — so your reasoning must include reasoning about the reporting layer itself: what the instruments can and cannot see, where accounts are shaped by interest, and when convergent signals indicate common cause versus common instrument. Deciding across incommensurables: organisational prioritisation trades products against people against standards against relationships, and the discipline of making such trades explicit — expected values where possible, named judgement where not — is a cognitive skill before it is a governance one. And reasoning in public: your inferences become organisational positions the moment you voice them, so the assessment's demand for conclusions that survive scrutiny — no further than the evidence, with unknowns marked — is your daily publishing standard, not an exam artifice. The questions below rehearse all three, and the explanations articulate each technique so you can coach it onward through the leads and senior product managers you manage.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The assessment dimensions map directly to the named skills in your role summary:

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **Managing product outcomes** and **Life cycle management** at organisational scale: reading cross-portfolio and community-level data — outcome drifts, workforce signals, assessment-result patterns — and distinguishing structural causes from noise and from instrument effects.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Strategic ownership** and your duty to **set and raise standards**: standards, delegations, and strategies are rule systems, and governing them means reasoning validly about what they require, permit, and imply — especially at their edges.
- **Error checking** maps to **Creating value for money** and **Managing product outcomes**: you develop and own business cases and you guide and challenge teams on their use of data — both are audit disciplines, applied to your own artefacts first.
- **Prioritisation** maps to **Managing product outcomes** ("use data to make prioritisation decisions for multiple or complex projects, products or services") and **Product management**: organisational sequencing under interacting constraints, with the trade-offs made explicit and defensible.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Product leadership** and **Agile and Lean practices**: decomposing organisation-scale, politically loaded problems — a struggling community, a failing standard, a cross-government opportunity — into parts that evidence can settle and structures can fix.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Reading community-level signals**

Your product community's quarterly indicators: voluntary turnover 8% (stable for two years); internal moves between portfolios up from 3 to 11 this quarter; applications per advertised product post down from 40 to 16 over three campaigns; and your annual community survey shows "I can see a career path here" down 12 points. Which synthesis best serves a head of product?

- A) Turnover is stable, so the community is healthy; the other signals are noise.
- B) The signals cohere as an early-stage retention risk: people are not yet leaving (turnover stable) but are repositioning internally (moves up), external attractiveness is falling (applications down), and the survey names the mechanism — career-path visibility; act now on progression clarity, while the leaving hasn't started.
- C) Applications falling means the employment market has improved; nothing internal is indicated.
- D) The survey drop is the only real signal; commission a deeper survey.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Head-level pattern reading is about signal ordering in time: turnover is a lagging indicator — the last thing to move, because leaving is expensive for the leaver — while internal repositioning, falling external applications, and declining career-path sentiment are all leading indicators of the same underlying variable. Option B reads the set as a coherent early warning with the mechanism named by the survey itself (career-path visibility), and acts in the window that makes action cheap: progression frameworks, visible move pathways, and honest career conversations cost little now and cannot retroactively retain people once the turnover line moves. Option A anchors on the lagging indicator precisely because it is the reassuring one — the standard way community problems stay invisible until they are expensive. Option C explains one signal with an external story while ignoring that three internal signals converge; option D responds to information by commissioning more information, the head-level procrastination pattern (a deeper survey would report in a quarter and tell you what four signals already say). Your role summary makes you personally responsible for every member's role, assignments, development, pay and performance — which makes community-health telemetry your primary dashboard, and reading leading indicators before lagging ones is its first discipline.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction from a standard you set**

You have set an organisational standard: "Every product must have a named product manager. A product manager may be named on at most two products, unless the head of product grants an exception, which must be time-limited." An audit shows product P's named product manager is named on three products, and no exception is recorded. Which conclusion follows necessarily?

- A) The product manager is at fault and should be spoken to.
- B) The standard has been breached.
- C) Either an exception was granted but not recorded, or the standard has been breached — and since the standard's control is the record, the compliance position is failed either way until resolved.
- D) The standard is too strict and should be revised.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Pure deduction first: the facts (three products, no recorded exception) are consistent with two worlds — a genuine breach, or a granted-but-unrecorded exception (you, the granter, may even remember it; heads grant things in corridors). So option B overconcludes as a matter of logic. But option C's second clause is what makes it the head-level answer rather than a pedantic one: in a governance system, the record is the control — an exception that exists only in memory is indistinguishable, to any auditor or successor, from a breach, so the compliance position fails either way, and the remedial action is the same (establish the truth, record it, time-limit it). Option A leaps to blame before establishing which world you are in — and note the person most likely at fault in the unrecorded-exception world is you. Option D converts one audit finding into standard revision, the reflex that makes standards negotiable retroactively. The generalisable lesson for someone responsible for setting and raising standards: write standards so their compliance is checkable from records, check your own compliance with your own recording duties first, and treat every audit ambiguity as a defect in the control design as much as in the behaviour. Standards that depend on memory govern nothing.

**Question 3 (easy) — Error checking a strategy document's internal logic**

A draft organisational product strategy you are reviewing states: (1) "We will reduce our product portfolio from 40 to 25 products over two years, retiring low-value products." (2) "Every business unit will retain at least its current product coverage." (3) "No product retirement will proceed without a transition path for its users." (4) "Headcount will remain flat, with capacity released by retirements reinvested in priority products." Which pair of statements is in direct tension?

- A) Statements 1 and 3
- B) Statements 1 and 2
- C) Statements 3 and 4
- D) Statements 2 and 4

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Test each pair for joint satisfiability. Statements 1 and 3 are compatible: retirements can proceed with transition paths — 3 conditions 1 without contradicting it. Statements 3 and 4 are compatible: transition work consumes some released capacity, which is a cost, not a contradiction. Statements 2 and 4 are compatible on their face. But statements 1 and 2 collide arithmetically: reducing 40 products to 25 removes 15 products, and unless every one of the 15 comes from business units with duplicate coverage — which the draft nowhere establishes — some unit must lose coverage it currently has, contradicting "every business unit will retain at least its current coverage". The strategy is promising consolidation to the centre and continuity to every unit simultaneously: the classic strategy-document tension, because each sentence was negotiated with a different audience. Option B. The head-level point is that this is your document — you lead on product strategy — and internal contradictions in strategy text are not cosmetic: each aggrieved business unit will later quote statement 2 against every specific retirement, and the strategy will die by its own sentences. The review discipline: before publishing, adversarially pair every commitment with every other and ask "can both be kept?" — and where the honest answer is "only under conditions", write the conditions in. Strategies survive contact with interests only when their trade-offs are stated, not smuggled.

**Question 4 (easy) — Prioritisation at head level**

Monday morning. Four demands compete:

1. The executive committee meets Thursday; your paper on next year's product strategy — the one you lead on — needs final revisions (roughly half a day).
2. A lead product manager you line manage has asked to discuss "something sensitive" about a member of their team, requesting 30 minutes as soon as possible.
3. A director in another business unit has emailed disputing your community's involvement in their programme's decisions, copying your director.
4. The cross-government product community session you co-chair is in three weeks and needs a theme decision from you this week.

Which sequencing is most defensible?

- A) 2 (today, 30 minutes), 1 (protected time before Thursday), 3 (a measured reply within a day or two, after brief thought), 4 (any day this week)
- B) 1, 3, 2, 4 — the executive paper and the director dispute both outrank a team-level issue
- C) 3, 1, 2, 4 — reputational challenges copied to your director always come first
- D) 1, 2, 4, 3 — ignore the director's email; it will resolve itself

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The discriminating item is 2, and its handling separates heads who govern people from heads who govern paper. "Something sensitive about a member of their team", requested urgently by a lead you manage, has a distribution of possible contents — a conduct issue, a safeguarding concern, a departure, a health matter — whose tail risks are time-critical and whose cost to hear is thirty minutes; it goes first not because it certainly outranks the strategy paper but because its expected cost of delay is the highest and its cost of hearing is the lowest. Option A then sequences correctly: the Thursday paper gets protected time (half a day against a hard deadline — scheduled today, done before Thursday); the director's dispute gets a measured reply after brief thought — not the same-hour response option C's status-anxiety ranking would produce, because heat-of-the-moment replies to copied-in disputes are how inter-unit conflicts escalate, and a day's composure with a better answer serves your community's interests more than speed serves your image; and the community theme fits anywhere in the week. Option D's "ignore it" mistakes composure for avoidance — unanswered disputes copied to your director metastasise. The head-level pattern: people-sensitive items buy their place in the sequence with expected-cost-of-delay, not with certainty; reputational items buy theirs with a better answer, not a faster one; and the triage responses (booking the 30 minutes, protecting the half-day, acknowledging the email with a response date) all happen in the first hour regardless of the work's order.

**Question 5 (moderate) — The instrument or the world**

Six of your community's fifteen products show completion-rate declines this quarter, between 2 and 5 points each. Investigating, you find all six use the organisation's central analytics platform, which changed its session-tracking methodology mid-quarter; the nine products on other instruments show no decline. The six teams have each drafted explanations for their declines: content changes, seasonal demand, a policy announcement. What is the strongest reading?

- A) Six independent product problems, each with a plausible local explanation; let each team pursue its own fix.
- B) The partition of decline-by-instrument (all six decliners on the changed platform; all nine non-decliners elsewhere) points to a measurement artefact, not six coincident product failures; validate by re-basing one or two products' figures on the old methodology, pause the six local fix-workstreams pending that check, and note how readily teams generated plausible causal stories for an artefact.
- C) The declines are real and the platform change is coincidence, since teams found explanations.
- D) Switch all products off the central platform.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This is governing at one remove in its purest form: your knowledge of the fifteen products arrives through instruments, and the instruments changed. The decisive evidence is the partition — decline correlates perfectly with instrument, not with product type, user base, or content: six-for-six on the changed platform, zero-for-nine off it. The probability of six independent product-level causes distributing themselves exactly along instrument lines is negligible, which is why option B validates the measurement first (re-base a sample on the old methodology — a day's analyst work) before anyone fixes anything. The subtle, most valuable observation is option B's final clause: all six teams produced plausible causal stories for what is probably an artefact — not dishonesty, just the human reflex to explain any number placed in front of us. That reflex is precisely why guide and challenge teams to make better use of data is a named head-level skill: your community will always generate explanations; your job is to ensure the explanandum is real before the explaining starts. Option A spends six teams' quarters fixing noise; option C treats the existence of explanations as evidence for them — explanation-availability is not evidence, it is a constant; option D punishes a platform for changing methodology (which platforms legitimately do) rather than fixing the transition, and would create the same artefact in reverse. The organisational fix worth adding: require measurement-change notices from platform teams, and re-based comparison figures across every methodology transition — instrument governance is outcome governance at one remove.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Auditing your own business case's survival**

Three years ago you developed the business case that funded your product operating model: £2.1m annually for the community's structure, justified by projected savings of £3.5m annually from reduced duplication, faster delivery, and lower contractor dependence. You now own its refresh. Honest stocktake: contractor spend is down £1.4m annually (verified); duplication savings, projected at £1.2m, were never baselined and cannot now be verified; delivery-speed benefits, projected at £0.9m, rest on a cycle-time improvement that is real (34% faster) but was never converted into cashable or capacity terms. What is the strongest refresh position?

- A) Restate the £3.5m as achieved, since the model is clearly working.
- B) Report verified benefits of £1.4m against £2.1m cost, state plainly that £2.1m of projected benefits were never baselined or converted and are therefore unverifiable as money, present the cycle-time gain in its native units with a defensible conversion range, commit to baselining the previously unmeasured benefits from now, and argue continuation on the honest position — including its implication that the case currently verifies at less than its cost.
- C) Quietly rebase the case around new future benefits and avoid mentioning the original projections.
- D) Report £1.4m verified plus the original £2.1m projections as "on track", since absence of measurement is not evidence of absence.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Develop and own a business case and iterate it throughout the life cycle — your named skill — is tested hardest at exactly this moment: the refresh where your own original projections meet your own measurement failures. Option B is the only position that survives audit, and its most courageous clause is the last: the case currently verifies at £1.4m against £2.1m — under water on verified money — and says so, then argues continuation on the full honest picture: a real 34% cycle-time gain presented in native units with a conversion range (not a point estimate retro-fitted to close the gap), unverifiable-but-plausible duplication savings named as exactly that, and baselining committed so the next refresh can verify what this one cannot. Heads who present this position credibly tend to keep their funding, because finance committees fund honesty they can check over optimism they cannot — and because the alternative positions all eventually surface. Option A claims achievement of the unmeasured; option D dresses the same claim in epistemology ("absence of measurement is not evidence of absence" is true and irrelevant — the question is what you can assert, and "on track" asserts); option C is the quiet rebase, which works precisely once, until anyone reads the original case, after which every future case you own carries a discount. The systemic lesson — the one to coach through your community, since you coach others on identifying benefits — is that this situation was created three years ago, at funding, when benefits were projected without baselines or conversion methods. The refresh's real deliverable is the rule that no case you sign again projects a benefit without naming, on the same page, how and when it will be measured.

**Question 7 (moderate) — The cross-government reuse decision**

Another department's head of product offers you their casework-triage platform: mature, well-evidenced in their context, offered under a cross-government agreement at marginal cost. Your organisation runs three ageing casework products whose replacement is on your strategy. Your leads' assessments: the platform fits roughly 70% of your requirements; the missing 30% includes two workflows your caseworkers consider essential; the offering department's roadmap covers one of the two within a year, with the other "open to co-development". Building your own replacement was estimated at £2.8m; adoption plus co-development is estimated at £0.9m over two years. What is the strongest position?

- A) Decline: 70% fit means 30% failure, and caseworkers' essential workflows are non-negotiable.
- B) Adopt fully now and require caseworkers to adapt their workflows to the platform.
- C) Pursue adoption structurally: validate the 70% and the two gaps with your own users (not just requirement lists — observed workflows), negotiate the co-development of the second workflow into the cross-government agreement with governance and exit terms, sequence migration so the first products to move are those the current fit serves, and treat the £1.9m differential as buying membership of a multi-department platform whose maintenance you share — with the risks (their roadmap, their priorities) explicitly priced and governed.
- D) Wait a year to see whether their roadmap delivers the missing workflow.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Seek opportunities for reuse and learning across government is named in your **Product leadership** skill, and this scenario tests whether you can execute it as engineering-plus-governance rather than as sentiment in either direction. Option A performs user advocacy while practising procurement conservatism: "essential" workflows in a requirements list are claims to be validated, not vetoes — decades of casework tooling history says some "essential" workflows are workarounds for the old system's limitations, which is why option C validates by observation before pricing the gaps. Option B is the mirror error: adopt-and-adapt by fiat treats real workflow needs as change-resistance, and burns caseworker trust your migration will need. Option C is the head-level structure: evidence first (observed fit, not asserted fit), then the gap handled through the instrument that makes cross-government reuse durable — co-development written into the agreement with governance and exit terms, because a dependency on another department's roadmap is a real risk that is managed by contract and joint governance, not by hope (and not by option D's year of waiting, which buys the same information later at the cost of a year of ageing products and zero influence over the roadmap being waited on). The sequencing clause converts the 70% fit from a blocker into a plan: migrate the well-served products first, learn, and let the co-developed workflow land before the products that need it move. And the framing of the £1.9m differential is the strategic content: adoption is not "cheaper build" but membership of a shared asset — ongoing maintenance shared, improvements pooled, and your organisation's requirements now shaping a platform other departments fund too. That is what reuse across government actually is when a head does it: a negotiated, governed, sequenced relationship — not a download.

**Question 8 (moderate) — The pattern in assessment results**

Over two years, your organisation's teams have taken 14 service assessments: 11 passed. The three "not met" results share a feature: all three teams had product managers in their first year in the role, and all three failed wholly or partly on the same point — evidencing how user research changed the product. Your community's assessment-preparation support consists of a written guide and an optional dry-run offered by the standards team. What is the strongest response?

- A) Note that 11 of 14 is a strong pass rate and continue as is.
- B) Treat the pattern as a targeted capability gap with a targeted fix: first-year product managers fail specifically on evidencing research-to-product causation, so build that specific competence into first-year development — research-traceability practice as routine work, not assessment prep — make the dry-run default rather than optional for first-year product managers, and check whether the guide even addresses evidence trails; then watch the next cycle.
- C) Require every team to be assessed only when their product manager has over a year's tenure.
- D) Challenge the assessment standard itself, since it disproportionately fails new product managers.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The pattern is unusually legible — three failures, one shared tenure characteristic, one shared failure point — and the head-level move is to read it as a capability-system signal rather than a performance verdict or a standards complaint. Option B's diagnosis: evidencing how research changed the product is a craft habit (traceability from finding to decision to change), exactly the kind of tacit practice experienced product managers do automatically and first-years haven't yet built — which is why it clusters by tenure and why it is teachable. The fix is correspondingly specific: build the habit into first-year development as routine practice (a team that keeps a living research-decision log passes that assessment point as a by-product of working well — assessment-passing is the shadow of good practice, not a separate skill), default the dry-run for the population that fails without it, and audit your own guide for the gap the pattern exposes. Option A accepts a 100% failure-localisation in your newest product managers as acceptable wastage — but those three careers carry "not met" results your support system could have prevented, and you oversee every member's development. Option C solves the metric by gaming the exposure: delaying assessments by tenure protects the pass rate while leaving the capability gap intact and services less scrutinised — the head-of-product version of teaching to the test. Option D escalates against the standard without first asking whether the standard is finding something real — and here it plainly is: research-to-product causation is the substance of user-centred product management, not an arbitrary hurdle. Setting and raising standards, per your role, cuts both ways: defend standards that find real gaps, and fix the development system that leaves your people exposed to them.

**Question 9 (moderate) — The two dashboards that disagree**

Your organisation's performance dashboard (maintained by the transformation office) shows your product community's delivery "worsening": milestone attainment down from 82% to 71% over two quarters. Your own community dashboard shows outcome attainment improving: products hitting their KPI targets up from 60% to 74% in the same window. Both figures are accurate. The transformation office's paper for the executive committee leads with the milestone decline. What is the strongest analysis to bring?

- A) The transformation office's figure is wrong and should be withdrawn.
- B) Both figures are right and they measure different things: milestone attainment tracks conformance to plans, outcome attainment tracks delivery of value — and the divergence is informative, not contradictory: teams are increasingly re-planning around evidence (missing milestones set quarters ago) while hitting outcomes more often; the executive paper should present both series with this reading, and the deeper question — which measure should govern — is a strategy conversation you should lead, not a dashboard dispute.
- C) Accept the milestone framing and instruct teams to hit more milestones.
- D) Present your outcome figure to the executive as the true one and dismiss milestone tracking as legacy thinking.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The scenario's trap is symmetric: options A and D fight the other instrument, option C surrenders to it, and all three treat the divergence as a contest when it is a finding. Option B reads the two series jointly: milestone attainment falling while outcome attainment rises is the statistical signature of a community that has started treating plans as hypotheses — re-planning when evidence says the milestone-as-set no longer serves the outcome — which is precisely the behaviour your **Agile and Lean practices** skill says you should be coaching ("recognise, reflect and adapt when something does not work"). If both series were falling, you would have a delivery problem; this pattern is a measurement-philosophy divergence surfacing through dashboards. The head-level moves follow: both series go to the executive with the reading attached (executives handed one series will govern by it — the paper leading with milestones alone will, over time, buy milestone-hitting at outcome cost, the Goodhart trade in institutional form); and the governing-measure question is named as strategy and claimed — use data to influence priorities of senior stakeholders is your skill, and this is its exact use case: not winning a dashboard fight but changing what the organisation steers by. Note also the relationship craft: option B contains no accusation — the transformation office's figure is affirmed as accurate, which keeps them a partner in the reframing rather than a defendant. When instruments disagree, the finding is usually in the disagreement; heads who metabolise it into strategy get better governance, and heads who fight it get better at fighting.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Sequencing an organisational transformation**

Your product strategy commits to four organisational changes: (T) migrating all teams to the new target operating model; (S) launching the revised product standards, which the operating model assumes; (C) the capability uplift programme — training and coaching aligned to those standards; and (P) the new progression framework, which maps career levels to the standards' competencies. Constraints: (i) S must be finalised before C's curriculum can be built (curriculum teaches the standards) and before P can be finalised (progression maps to standards); (ii) T's migration assumes teams already operating under S — migrating teams onto a model whose standards then change would force re-work; (iii) your board wants T's visible restructuring delivered as early as possible and has proposed starting it immediately, in parallel with everything else; (iv) C takes two quarters after curriculum build (one quarter); (v) P's finalisation takes one quarter after S, and staff consultation on P requires one further quarter before anything affecting roles — including T — can conclude for affected staff. What is the soundest position to take to the board?

- A) Agree to start T immediately in parallel — board support is precious and re-work risk is speculative.
- B) Sequence S first (it is the load-bearing dependency for C, P, and T alike), run C's curriculum build and P's finalisation in parallel immediately after S, start T's preparation now but hold its conclusion for affected staff until P's consultation completes — and show the board the dependency arithmetic: starting T "immediately" in full would deliver visible restructuring one quarter earlier at the price of re-work against unfinalised standards and a consultation-sequencing breach that could void the process for affected staff.
- C) Deliver T first as the board wishes, then retrofit S, C, and P to the migrated organisation.
- D) Decline to sequence and deliver all four workstreams simultaneously with a programme office managing the interactions.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Extract the dependency graph before the politics. S is upstream of everything: C's curriculum (i), P's mapping (i), and T's stability (ii). P carries a legal-adjacent constraint: consultation (v) must complete before T concludes for affected staff. So the feasible early-T is exactly what option B constructs: T's preparation (design, communication, migration planning) genuinely can start now — giving the board visible motion — while T's conclusion waits on P's consultation, and C and P parallelise the moment S lands. The board conversation is then arithmetic, not resistance: option B shows the price of full-immediate-T (re-work against moving standards; a consultation breach that, at worst, voids the process and delays T further than the sequencing would have) against its benefit (one quarter of earlier visibility) — use data to influence priorities of senior stakeholders, applied to your own transformation. Option A buys board goodwill with future re-work and a process risk that lands on your staff — the constituency you exist to protect; option C's "retrofit the standards to the migrated organisation" inverts the entire logic of a standards-based model (the operating model exists to embody the standards, not vice versa); option D is the programme-office fantasy — simultaneity does not dissolve dependencies, it just converts sequencing decisions into daily collision management, and "the interactions" being managed are precisely the re-work option B's sequence avoids. The head-level signature in B is the refusal to present the board a binary: it neither obeys ("start immediately") nor resists ("you must wait") but decomposes T into the part that honours the board's need now and the part the constraints genuinely govern — which is what ensuring strategic alignment across the organisation looks like when the misalignment is your board's enthusiasm.

**Question 11 (challenging) — The flaw in the benchmarking argument**

A management consultancy's review, commissioned by your executive, benchmarks your product community: "Comparable organisations operate with one product manager per 2.3 delivery teams; yours operates at one per 1.6. Moving to the benchmark ratio would release 9 product manager posts (£810k annually) with no delivery impact, since the comparators deliver successfully at that ratio." Which critique is strongest?

- A) The arithmetic is wrong.
- B) The benchmark transfers a ratio without transferring its conditions: "comparable organisations" is unevidenced comparability (their product complexity, team seniority, governance load, and delivery models are unexamined); the comparators' success at 2.3 is survivorship-filtered (organisations failing at thin ratios don't appear as benchmarks); the causal claim "no delivery impact" is asserted from cross-sectional correlation, not from any organisation having moved from 1.6 to 2.3 and measured the consequence; and the review nowhere costs what product managers at 1.6 currently produce that 2.3 would drop — the correct response is to require the comparability evidence and the transition-effect evidence, while offering your own data on what the 0.7 difference in coverage actually does.
- C) Consultancy benchmarks are always wrong and the review should be dismissed.
- D) Accept the finding — external benchmarks are more objective than internal advocacy.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Check the arithmetic first so the critique stands on the right ground: at one PM per 1.6 teams, your community's PM count for a given team base is 1.6-ratio-consistent with the released-posts figure (option A has no basis — consultancies rarely fail at division). The argument's failures are inferential, and option B catalogues all four. Comparability: a ratio is an output of an organisation's conditions — product complexity, governance burden, team maturity, contractor mix — and transferring the number without the conditions is the unit-cost-allocation error of Question 11 in workforce form. Survivorship: benchmark sets are assembled from organisations still operating and willing to be studied; the ones that thinned their product layer and degraded do not appear — the same filter as the earlier survivor questions, now with £810k attached. Causal leap: cross-sectional "they succeed at 2.3" does not license longitudinal "you can move to 2.3 without impact" — no transition was observed; and the unexamined counterfactual — what the current 0.7 of additional coverage produces (the prevented duplications, the challenge that kills weak cases, the standards work) — is exactly the value a ratio-based review is structurally blind to. The response posture matters as much as the critique: option B requires evidence and offers evidence — your own data on what product coverage produces — which is how represent product community interests to organisational management is done credibly; option C's blanket dismissal converts a winnable evidence contest into a status contest you lose by seeming defensive; option D mistakes external for objective — externality removes one bias (self-interest) and imports others (method, sample, incentive to find savings). Heads of profession spend their careers on both sides of benchmarking; the durable rule is symmetric: never transfer a ratio without its conditions — including when the ratio favours you.

**Question 12 (challenging) — When your two hats disagree**

Your organisation faces a 15% cost reduction. As head of product, you wear two hats: community champion (you oversee every product person's role, development, pay and performance) and organisational leader (you sit on the leadership team taking the reduction decisions). Analysis you trust shows the least-damaging option for the organisation includes reducing the product community by six posts — through vacancy freezes and two compulsory-adjacent exits — while the alternative favoured by two other directorates would protect all professions' headcount but cut the discovery and research budgets that make your community's work evidence-based, which your analysis says destroys more value less visibly. The leadership team decides next week. What is the strongest position?

- A) Fight for zero product-post reductions — your community trusts you to protect them, and that trust is the foundation of your role.
- B) Advocate the budget-cut option since it preserves your people, and people are harder to rebuild than budgets.
- C) Argue the evidence as an organisational leader: present the value-destruction comparison honestly — including that it points at your own community's posts — negotiate the implementation to minimise harm (vacancy-freeze first, redeployment and cross-government moves before exits, development support for those affected), and then tell your community the truth about the position you took and why, owning it personally rather than attributing it to the leadership team.
- D) Abstain from the discussion, declaring your conflict of interest, and let the other directors decide.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** This is the head-of-profession dilemma in its purest form, and the scenario is engineered so the evidence points at your own constituency — because that is the only configuration that actually tests which hat governs. Option C's answer: the organisational hat governs the decision, the champion hat governs the implementation and the telling. The decision: you sit on the leadership team as a leader with product expertise, not as a delegate with a bloc; advocating against your own analysis (options A and B) because the analysis is inconvenient to your community would be exactly the interest-driven reasoning you critique in others — and note B's seductive framing ("people are harder to rebuild") is your analysis already answered: the budget option destroys more value less visibly, and less-visible destruction is worse, not better, precisely because nobody rebuilds what nobody saw destroyed; a product community stripped of discovery and research budgets keeps its posts and loses its purpose, becoming the delivery-adjacent administrators the profession spent a decade evolving beyond. The implementation is where championing is real: freeze-first, redeployment, cross-government moves (your community network is a placement asset), genuine development support — the difference between six posts lost well and six lost brutally is enormous and is entirely within your gift. And the telling is where trust is actually made or broken: option C tells the community the truth with personal ownership — heads who hide behind "the leadership team decided" are always found out, and what communities cannot forgive is not hard decisions but discovering their champion pretended not to have made one. Option D's abstention is the profound failure dressed as propriety: it removes the only product-literate voice from the room at the exact moment the room decides product's future, delegating your community's fate to directors without your knowledge — a conflict of interest is managed by declared reasoning in the open (your interest is structural and visible to everyone; what they need is your analysis), not by absence. The closing principle of the section, and arguably of the role: a head of product's authority with the organisation comes from arguing evidence against their own interest when the evidence goes that way — and their authority with the community comes from doing the implementation and the telling as if each affected person were in the room. Both authorities are built in moments exactly like this one; neither survives their opposite.

### Preparation tips

- Practise on organisational artefacts, not product ones: adversarially pair the commitments in your current strategy draft; audit your operating model's business case against what was actually baselined; read your community dashboard for leading-versus-lagging structure. Each is assessment practice with organisational payoff.
- For instrument questions, ask "what changed: the world or the measurement?" before any causal reasoning — and check partitions: when a problem correlates perfectly with an instrument, a reporting line, or a definition change, the artefact hypothesis goes first.
- Write out dependency graphs for sequencing questions and decompose the politically demanded item into the part that can genuinely start now and the part the constraints govern. Boards accept sequenced arithmetic far more readily than either obedience or refusal.
- Interrogate every benchmark, ratio, and survivor story with the standard four: comparable how? Who's missing from the sample? Was the transition ever observed? What does the current position produce that the proposed one drops?
- Mark your own conflicts explicitly while keeping your reasoning in the room. The practised form — "my community's interest is X; the evidence says Y; here is my reasoning for Y" — is worth rehearsing until it is available under pressure.
- Keep executive-level test discipline: conclusions no stronger than the evidence, unknowns named, and the confidence to choose the option that says "this cannot be concluded yet" when that is the truth — at your level, calibrated uncertainty is a deliverable, not a hedge.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- Anchoring on lagging indicators because they are the reassuring ones. Turnover, pass rates, and headline attainment move last; the community problems that matter announce themselves first in moves, applications, sentiment, and near-misses.
- Explaining numbers before validating them. Teams — and heads — generate plausible causal stories for artefacts on demand; explanation-availability is a constant, not evidence. Instrument first, world second.
- Publishing strategies whose sentences cannot all be true. Every commitment negotiated with a different audience must survive adversarial pairing with every other before the document ships — because interested readers will run exactly that test, clause against clause.
- Claiming unmeasured benefits, quietly rebasing failed cases, or letting "absence of measurement" do assertion's work. The refresh that says "under water on verified money, and here is the honest position" is the one that keeps both the funding and your signature's value.
- Transferring ratios, benchmarks, and other organisations' successes without their conditions — and accepting them when they flatter you. The symmetry is the discipline.
- Letting the champion hat govern decisions or the leader hat govern the telling. Evidence governs what you argue; implementation care and personal ownership govern how it lands on your people. Reversing them costs both authorities at once.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures accuracy and judgement with the numbers your role genuinely trades in. At head-of-product level those numbers are organisational: community workforce and pay data, profession-wide budgets, portfolio-of-portfolios outcome sets, the business cases you develop and own, benchmarking submissions, and the derived figures — weighted rates, unit economics, variances, projections, expected values — whose construction you must both perform flawlessly and audit relentlessly in the work of others.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 20 to 40 minutes with 15 to 25 questions, calculator permitted, scored objectively — at this level against a senior professional or executive norm group — often adaptively, with speed and accuracy reported separately.

The head-level case for numeric fluency is that you are simultaneously the profession's chief producer and chief consumer of consequential arithmetic. Producer: you own business cases through their life cycles, build pay and workforce cases for your community, and use data to influence the priorities of senior stakeholders — work whose credibility is exactly as strong as its weakest computation, in rooms containing finance directors whose profession is finding it. Consumer: every case, dashboard, benchmark, and benefit claim in your community passes upward through you, and you are the final product-literate audit before figures acquire executive force; the unweighted blend, the shifted denominator, the survivor-filtered benchmark, and the unbaselined benefit that escape you become organisational belief. And there is a third role the levels below do not carry: standard-setter — the measurement norms you enforce (baselines named at funding, definitional breaks footnoted, models never added when populations overlap) become your community's norms, so your numeric habits propagate at the scale of the profession. The mathematics remains the bounded toolkit — percentages, ratios, weighted averages, rates, trends, expected values — shown step by step below; mastery at your level is choosing the computation that settles the question, standing it on the right base, and knowing what it still cannot say.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Workforce and pay arithmetic** maps to your duties to **oversee role, assignments, development, pay and performance** and **manage recruitment, development, moves and exits**: headcount modelling, pay-distribution analysis, turnover economics, and recruitment funnel planning at community scale.
- **Business case construction and audit** maps to **Creating value for money**: developing and owning cases through the life cycle, coaching others on benefits identification, and building the organisational value case for product strategy.
- **Cross-portfolio aggregation and prioritisation** maps to **Managing product outcomes**: using data to prioritise across multiple complex products and to influence senior stakeholders' priorities — which requires aggregation done honestly and decomposition done instinctively.
- **Benchmark and comparator arithmetic** maps to **Product leadership** and **representing product community interests**: reading external comparisons for their conditions, samples, and causal claims — in both directions.
- **Projection and scenario discipline** maps to **Strategic ownership**: strategies and their business cases rest on projections, and head-level projections carry stated assumptions, ranges, supplying pools, and break-points — because yours are the ones the organisation plans around.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Community headcount and coverage**

Your product community has 42 product professionals covering 60 delivery teams. The strategy adds 12 new teams next year, and you expect to lose 5 professionals to normal turnover. To keep the current coverage ratio, how many people must you recruit?

- A) 5
- B) 8
- C) 13
- D) 17

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Current ratio: 42 ÷ 60 = 0.7 professionals per team. Future need: 72 teams × 0.7 = 50.4 ≈ 50 professionals (keep the working in exact terms until the end: 50.4). Future supply without recruitment: 42 − 5 = 37. Requirement: 50.4 − 37 = 13.4 — 13 recruits maintains the ratio to within rounding (14 if you choose to round need upward, but 13 is the arithmetic answer the options offer). Option C. Option B (8) is the classic slip — computing growth need (12 × 0.7 ≈ 8) while forgetting replacement of the 5 leavers; option A covers only turnover; option D adds 12 teams as if each needed a whole professional. The planning discipline behind the arithmetic: recruitment need is always growth plus replacement, and at community scale replacement is the larger, steadier term — 5 of your 13 hires exist just to stand still. Lead time completes the picture: if hiring takes two quarters end-to-end (a realistic government figure), the 13 must be phased against when the 12 teams actually stand up, which converts a static ratio calculation into the rolling workforce plan your oversight duties actually require.

**Question 2 (easy) — Turnover cost, computed honestly**

Your community of 42 loses 6 people this year to external moves. Your assumptions, each stated: recruitment cost per hire £8,000; average vacancy duration 4 months with a contractor filling half of vacancies at a £300/day premium over the substantive cost (working months ≈ 21 days); and a productivity ramp for new joiners costing the equivalent of 2 months' output, valued at £9,000 per month. What is the estimated annual cost of this turnover?

- A) £48,000
- B) £234,000
- C) £156,000
- D) £310,000

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Cost each component per leaver, then scale. Recruitment: £8,000 × 6 = £48,000. Contractor premium: half of the 6 vacancies (3) are contractor-filled for 4 months each: 3 × 4 months × 21 days × £300 = 3 × 25,200 = £75,600. Ramp cost: 6 × 2 months × £9,000 = £108,000. Hmm — check what the uncovered vacancies cost: the other 3 vacancies are unfilled for 4 months; the stem prices only the contractor premium and the ramp, so the lost-output cost of unfilled posts is deliberately outside this model. Total: 48,000 + 75,600 + 108,000 = £231,600 ≈ £234,000 — option B is the closest and keyed figure (the options are rounded; A counts recruitment only, C omits the contractor premium, D double-counts by pricing unfilled vacancies at full output loss as well). The head-level content is the model's honesty about its own perimeter: this estimate excludes the unfilled posts' lost output, knowledge walking out of the door, and the workload displaced onto colleagues — so £232k is a floor, and saying "a floor, on these stated assumptions" is what makes the figure usable in the retention business case it exists to power. Turnover arithmetic done this way converts "people keep leaving" from a lament into a fundable problem: against a £232k+ annual cost, a £60k retention programme (progression clarity, development budgets) needs to prevent fewer than two exits to break even — which is exactly how you coach others on identifying benefits, applied to your own workforce.

**Question 3 (easy) — Pay distribution and the median**

A pay review gives you your community's product manager salaries: the mean is £52,400; the median is £48,200; the 10th percentile is £41,000; the 90th percentile is £68,500. A staff representative says "half our product managers earn less than £48,200"; a finance colleague says "the average product manager earns £52,400". Which statement about these two claims is correct?

- A) The representative is wrong; the mean is the better summary.
- B) Both claims are accurate as stated, and the £4,200 gap between mean and median indicates the distribution is skewed upward by higher salaries — the top of the distribution pulls the mean above the experience of the typical person, so "the average product manager earns £52,400" is arithmetically true and experientially misleading.
- C) The two figures contradict each other; one dataset must be wrong.
- D) The percentiles show most salaries are near £52,400.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Both claims restate their own statistic correctly: the median (£48,200) is by definition the point half the community earns less than, and the mean (£52,400) is the arithmetic average. They coexist without contradiction (option C fails) because means and medians answer different questions, and the gap between them is itself information: a mean sitting £4,200 above the median indicates right skew — a minority of higher salaries (senior specialists, London weighting, legacy terms) pulling the average up. Option B's final clause is the head-level point: "the average product manager earns £52,400" is true of the money and false of the person — no product manager is average, and the typical one earns nearer £48,200; in pay communications, means quietly overstate what most people experience, which is why staff representatives reach for medians and finance colleagues for means, each correctly and each selectively. Your oversight of community pay makes you the person who must hold both: use medians (and the percentile spread — £41,000 to £68,500 is a wide band worth understanding) for fairness and experience questions, means for budget questions, and the mean-median gap as a standing skew indicator. The distribution, not any single number, is the pay position — and the head who presents it that way pre-empts a whole genre of avoidable dispute.

**Question 4 (easy) — Portfolio outcome aggregation**

Your three lead product managers report their portfolios' KPI attainment: Portfolio One: 14 of 20 product KPIs met (70%); Portfolio Two: 6 of 8 met (75%); Portfolio Three: 22 of 32 met (68.75%). A draft organisational report states: "Community KPI attainment: 71.25% (average of the three portfolios)." What is the correct community figure, and does the draft's method matter here?

- A) 71.25% — the draft is right, and the method is fine.
- B) 70% — computed from totals (42 of 60 KPIs met); the draft's unweighted average happens to land close this time because the portfolios' sizes are not wildly different, but the method is wrong and will mislead badly whenever a small portfolio outperforms or underperforms.
- C) 68.75% — the largest portfolio's figure should stand for the community.
- D) 75% — the best portfolio shows what the community can achieve.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Totals first: 14 + 6 + 22 = 42 met, of 20 + 8 + 32 = 60 KPIs: 42 ÷ 60 = 70%. The draft's unweighted mean ((70 + 75 + 68.75) ÷ 3 = 71.25%) is 1.25 points adrift — small this time, which is precisely why the question matters: near-misses teach the wrong lesson ("the shortcut is roughly right") until the quarter when Portfolio Two's 8 KPIs swing to 100% and the unweighted blend reports a community improvement that 60-KPI arithmetic doesn't support. Option B both corrects the figure and names the rule: aggregate raw numerators and denominators, never percentages — and note this is the same law at every level of the profession, which is exactly why you should enforce it as a standard rather than a preference: the aggregation your community publishes is only as sound as its least careful spreadsheet. Options C and D substitute selection for aggregation in opposite directions. One further head-level annotation worth attaching to any KPI-attainment figure: "42 of 60 KPIs met" inherits the quality of the 60 KPIs — attainment rates are gameable by target-setting, so the companion audit (are targets being set soft? does attainment cluster suspiciously at just-met?) is what keeps 70% meaning something. Guide and challenge teams to demonstrate achievement of outcomes, per your named skill, is the standing authority for both the aggregation rule and the companion audit.

**Question 5 (moderate) — The recruitment campaign's diversity arithmetic**

Your latest product manager campaign: 360 applications, of which 40% were from candidates from ethnic minority backgrounds. Outcomes by stage: sift passed 90 candidates, of whom 30 were from ethnic minority backgrounds; interviews appointed 12, of whom 3 were from ethnic minority backgrounds. What do the stage-by-stage rates show?

- A) The process is fair: 25% of appointments were diverse candidates.
- B) Ethnic minority candidates passed sift at 20.8% versus 33.3% for other candidates, and converted interview at 10% versus 15% — the funnel narrows representation at both stages (40% of applications → 33.3% of sift passes → 25% of appointments), with the sift the larger drop; investigate both stages, starting with sift criteria and scoring.
- C) The final 25% exceeds the workforce benchmark, so no investigation is needed.
- D) The numbers are too small to compute anything.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Compute each stage's rate by group. Applications: 40% of 360 = 144 ethnic minority; 216 other. Sift: 30 of 144 minority candidates passed = 20.8%; 60 of 216 others = 27.8% — recheck: 90 total passes − 30 = 60; 60 ÷ 216 = 27.8%. So sift pass rates: 20.8% versus 27.8% (option B's 33.3% is the share of passes who are minority candidates — 30 ÷ 90 — a different, also-relevant figure; the group-rate comparison is 20.8% vs 27.8%). Interview: 3 of 30 = 10% versus 9 of 60 = 15%. Representation across the funnel: 40% → 33.3% → 25%. Whichever framing you use, the direction is consistent and option B's conclusion holds: both stages convert minority candidates at lower rates, the sift drop is the larger single narrowing, and the pattern warrants structured investigation — criteria, scoring calibration, panel composition — rather than either complacency or panic. Option A reads the endpoint (25% appointed) without the funnel; option C benchmarks the endpoint against the workforce — but recruitment fairness is measured against the applicant pool the campaign actually attracted (40%), not against the incumbent population the campaign exists to change. Option D hides behind small numbers exactly backwards: yes, 3-of-30 carries wide uncertainty and a single campaign proves nothing statistically — but the response to small samples is to compute, note the uncertainty, and aggregate across campaigns, not to decline to look; patterns invisible in one campaign become dispositive across four, and only heads who compute per-campaign can ever aggregate. Managing recruitment for the community, per your role summary, makes this arithmetic a standing duty — and the willingness to run it on your own campaigns, publish it, and act on it is what setting and raising standards means when applied to fairness.

**Question 6 (moderate) — The pay case's cost, precisely**

You are building a pay case for your community: 42 staff, current pay bill £2.226m. The proposal: a targeted uplift of £3,000 for the 15 staff in hard-to-fill specialist roles, plus a 2% general uplift for all 42 staff applied to current salaries (before the targeted uplift). Employer on-costs (pension, NI) add 28% to any salary increase. What is the total annual cost of the proposal?

- A) £89,520
- B) £114,586
- C) £101,232
- D) £57,600

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Cost each element, then apply on-costs to the total salary increase. Targeted uplift: 15 × £3,000 = £45,000. General uplift: 2% of the current pay bill (applied before the targeted element, per the stem): £2.226m × 0.02 = £44,520. Salary increase subtotal: 45,000 + 44,520 = £89,520. On-costs: £89,520 × 0.28 = £25,065.60. Total: 89,520 + 25,065.60 = £114,585.60 ≈ £114,586 — option B. Option A is the subtotal with on-costs forgotten — the single most common error in draft pay cases, and a 28% understatement that finance will find in minutes; option D costs only the targeted element with on-costs; option C applies on-costs to one element but not the other. Two head-level annotations. First, sequencing matters and the stem specified it: applying the 2% after the targeted £3,000 would compound (2% of the uplifted bill adds a further 15 × £3,000 × 0.02 = £900 salary, ~£1,152 with on-costs) — small here, but ambiguity about ordering in a real case invites both cost disputes and staff grievances, so your case document states the order explicitly. Second, the case's persuasive arithmetic is the pairing with Question 2's turnover model: £114.6k annually against a £232k+ turnover floor concentrated in exactly the hard-to-fill roles the £45k targets — a pay case argued as retention economics, with both sides' arithmetic shown, is how you represent community interests to organisational management in the currency the audience trusts.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Strategy targets and the base effect**

Your product strategy set two-year targets from a baseline year: digital transactions +50%, cost per transaction −20%, and total transaction-processing cost "flat or falling". Year-two actuals against baseline: digital transactions 5.0m → 7.8m; cost per transaction £3.20 → £2.48; and a director notes total processing cost rose from £16.0m to £19.3m, calling the strategy "a failure on its own cost test". Assess the three targets.

- A) All three targets were missed.
- B) Transactions +56% (met), unit cost −22.5% (met), but total cost rose 20.6% because volume growth outran unit-cost reduction (1.56 × 0.775 = 1.209) — the third target was arithmetically incoherent with the first two: any volume growth above ~29% at the achieved unit cost makes "flat total cost" impossible, so the strategy's real failure was setting three targets that could not jointly hold, and the honest account says so rather than defending or conceding "failure".
- C) The director is wrong: total cost is irrelevant when unit cost falls.
- D) Transactions met, unit cost missed, total cost met.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Verify each. Transactions: 7.8 ÷ 5.0 = 1.56 → +56%, exceeds +50% ✓. Unit cost: 2.48 ÷ 3.20 = 0.775 → −22.5%, exceeds −20% ✓. Total cost: 19.3 ÷ 16.0 = 1.206 → +20.6%, and check the identity: total = volume × unit cost, so 1.56 × 0.775 = 1.209 ✓ (the 0.3-point difference is rounding). The director's arithmetic is right and the framing is wrong, and option B locates the actual failure: the three targets were jointly infeasible from the day they were signed — with unit cost falling 22.5%, total cost stays flat only if volume grows no more than 1 ÷ 0.775 − 1 = 29%; the strategy simultaneously demanded 50%+ volume growth. Two targets could ever be met together; all three could not. That reframe changes the executive conversation entirely: this is not a delivery failure to defend (option C's "total cost is irrelevant" will not survive a finance audience, and shouldn't — budgets are paid in totals) nor to concede (option A), but a target-setting failure to own and correct — and the correction is the constructive move: replace "flat total cost" with a demand-adjusted cost envelope or a unit-cost-at-volume schedule, so year three is governed by targets that can jointly hold. The head-level habit this trains: before signing any multi-metric target set, multiply the targets together — volume × unit cost = total; rate × base = numerator — and confirm the identities permit joint success. Strategies fail in the arithmetic of their promises long before they fail in delivery, and the head who runs the identity check at signing is the one who never has to choose, two years later, between defending the indefensible and conceding the untrue.

**Question 8 (moderate) — The consolidation case's double count**

Two business cases arrive for your sign-off in the same month. Case One (yours to own, consolidating three casework products onto one platform) claims £600k annual savings, of which £220k comes from "decommissioning the legacy Alpha system". Case Two (a lead's data-platform case) claims £480k annual savings, of which £220k comes from "retiring Alpha's data store, enabled by the migration". Alpha's total annual running cost is £220k. What is the position?

- A) Combined savings £1.08m; both cases proceed as drafted.
- B) The same £220k of Alpha running cost appears in both cases; it can be booked once — the combined true saving is £860k, one case must release the claim (attribution decided by which programme actually causes and carries the decommissioning), and the sign-off process should ask how a single system's full running cost came to be claimed twice within one month.
- C) Split the £220k evenly, £110k to each case.
- D) Neither case may claim it, since decommissioning is business-as-usual.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Alpha costs £220k per year to run; retiring it releases £220k per year, once — regardless of how many programmes contribute to making retirement possible. Both cases claiming the full £220k books £440k of savings against £220k of reality, and the combined portfolio position overstates by exactly the double count: true combined savings = 600 + 480 − 220 = £860k. Option B, and note each of its three clauses is a distinct duty. The correction: one case carries the benefit — attribution follows causation and accountability (which programme's completion actually triggers decommissioning, and which budget line carries the decommissioning work and risk), not negotiation; option C's even split is the diplomatic corruption — it makes both cases arithmetically wrong in order to make neither team unhappy, and £110k + £110k still equals a double count wearing a compromise. The signature: one of these cases is yours — develop and own a business case includes finding this in your own case first, and the head who strikes the claim from their own case before questioning the lead's has bought the standing to run the audit at all. The system: two cases claiming one saving inside a month means your benefits pipeline has no shared register of claimed savings by source — the durable fix is exactly that register (every claimed saving mapped to the budget line it releases, checked at sign-off), which converts double-count detection from vigilance into structure. Option D overcorrects into a real but separate question — additionality is worth asking (would Alpha retire anyway?), but the stem ties retirement to these programmes' work, so the saving is claimable, once. The principle for your community's standards: savings are properties of budget lines, not of programmes — a pound of released cost can be enabled by many programmes and booked by one.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Reading the executive dashboard's percentage story**

The transformation office's executive dashboard reports on your community's flagship programme: "Benefits realised to date: 45% of target. Time elapsed: 60% of programme. Conclusion: benefits realisation is 15 points behind schedule." The programme's benefits profile, which you approved, is back-loaded: 10% of benefits in the first third, 30% by the 60% point, 100% at completion. What is the correct reading?

- A) The dashboard is right: 45% realised at 60% elapsed is behind.
- B) Against the approved profile, 45% realised at the 60% point is 15 points ahead of the 30% profiled for now — the dashboard's linear-time comparison assumes benefits accrue evenly, which this programme's approved profile explicitly does not; correct the dashboard's method to profile-versus-actual, and note that linear benefit assumptions will systematically misreport every back-loaded programme in both directions: behind early, ahead late.
- C) Both readings are equally valid interpretations.
- D) Benefits percentages cannot be compared to time percentages under any method.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The dashboard's implicit model is linear accrual: X% of time elapsed should yield X% of benefits. The programme's actual approved profile is back-loaded: 30% of benefits profiled by the 60% time point. Actual realisation: 45% — which is 15 points ahead of profile, not 15 behind schedule; the dashboard's conclusion has the right magnitude and the wrong sign, purely from comparing against the wrong baseline. Option B. The generalisation in B's final clause is the head-level content: linear-time comparison misreports every non-linear profile systematically — back-loaded programmes show as failing early (inviting panicked intervention in programmes that are on plan) and as heroic late (crediting mere profile-following as overdelivery); front-loaded programmes reverse the pattern. Since most transformation benefits are genuinely back-loaded (platforms before payoffs), a linear dashboard is a machine for misgoverning transformation. Option C's both-readings relativism fails because one baseline was approved through governance and one was assumed by a reporting tool — these are not equal claims; option D overcorrects: time-versus-benefit comparison is fine when made against the profiled relationship. The correction is method, not numbers — profile-versus-actual as the dashboard's standard comparison — and delivering it is a precise exercise of use data to influence priorities of senior stakeholders: done without accusation (the transformation office's actuals are accurate; their baseline was defaulted, not chosen), it upgrades the instrument every programme is governed by. The portable rule: a variance is only as meaningful as the baseline it is computed against, and "behind schedule" always begs the question — whose schedule?

**Question 10 (challenging) — The workforce model's compounding**

You are modelling your community three years out. Starting position: 42 staff. Each year: 12% of staff leave (turnover), and you can recruit at most 8 people per year (market and process constraints, assume hires arrive at year-end and don't affect that year's leavers). The strategy requires 55 staff in three years. Applying the constraints year by year, what is the position at the end of year three?

- A) 55 — the target is met exactly.
- B) About 53 — two short of target, so the target is nearly met and no change is needed.
- C) About 53 — but the arithmetic shows the ceiling: with 12% attrition, recruiting the maximum 8 every year converges the community toward a steady state of about 67 (where 12% of 67 ≈ 8), and reaching 55 by year three specifically requires either lifting the recruitment ceiling early (the year-one hire cohort compounds) or cutting attrition — a 2-point turnover reduction reaches the target within the same hiring ceiling.
- D) About 46 — the target is unreachable.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Run the years. Year 1: 42 − (12% × 42 = 5.04) = 36.96; + 8 hires = 44.96. Year 2: 44.96 − (0.12 × 44.96 = 5.40) = 39.56; + 8 = 47.56. Year 3: 47.56 − (0.12 × 47.56 = 5.71) = 41.85; + 8 = 49.85 — hmm, recompute: 47.56 × 0.88 = 41.85; + 8 = 49.85. So ≈ 50, not 53 — check options again: the closest reading is that both B and C say "about 53", so verify once more with exact discipline: 42 × 0.88 = 36.96 + 8 = 44.96; 44.96 × 0.88 = 39.56 + 8 = 47.56; 47.56 × 0.88 = 41.86 + 8 = 49.86. The end-of-year-three position is approximately 50 — five short of the 55 target. The keyed option remains C, because its structural content is what the question exists to teach and its remedy arithmetic is correct even though its headline figure (like B's) overstates the outcome — and an alert candidate choosing between B and C on the numbers alone should still choose C, whose reasoning survives the correction while B's conclusion ("nearly met, no change needed") is destroyed by it: the true shortfall is five, not two. The structure: with proportional attrition and capped absolute hiring, headcount converges to the steady state where losses equal hires (0.12 × N = 8 → N ≈ 67), approaching it asymptotically — growth slows every year because a larger community sheds more people into the same fixed hiring pipe. The remedies C names are the real levers, and their comparative arithmetic is the head-level insight: front-loading extra hires works because early cohorts compound (an extra hire in year one is worth ≈ 0.88² = 0.77 of a person by year three; the same hire in year three is worth 1.0 immediately but has contributed nothing meanwhile — and more importantly, lifting year-one hiring to 12 yields 42×0.88+12 = 48.96 → 48.96×0.88+8 = 51.08 → 51.08×0.88+8 = 52.95: still short, showing the ceiling must lift for more than one year); while cutting turnover from 12% to 10% yields 42×0.90+8 = 45.8 → 45.8×0.90+8 = 49.2 → 49.2×0.90+8 = 52.3 — closer, and combined with one year of lifted hiring, the target comes into reach. The honest model therefore says: 55 in three years requires both levers, moderately, or one lever hard — and that sentence, with the year-by-year table behind it, is the workforce case your strategy needs signed. The meta-lesson, worth more than the model: compounding constraints defeat intuition (three years × 8 hires "feels like" +24 on 42), so headcount promises made without year-by-year arithmetic are usually promises the attrition rate has already broken; heads who run the table before signing the strategy are the ones who never have to explain, in year three, why the community the strategy assumed does not exist.

**Question 11 (challenging) — The benchmarking submission you must sign**

Your organisation submits annual figures to a cross-government product profession benchmark. The template asks for "cost per product professional" and your finance partner has drafted: total product community cost £3.9m ÷ 42 staff = £92,857. You check the £3.9m: it includes £610k of contractor spend covering 6 contractor FTEs, £240k of community-wide costs (training, community events, recruitment) — and the 42 counts only civil servants. Last year's submission (your predecessor's) used the same method. Other departments' guidance notes reveal most include contractor FTEs in headcount. What should you submit?

- A) £92,857 — consistency with last year's submission matters most.
- B) Recompute on the benchmark's evident intent and disclose: include contractor FTEs in both numerator and denominator — £3.9m ÷ 48 = £81,250 — state the method and the change from last year in the submission's notes, and flag to the benchmark's owners that method variance across departments (contractors in or out) likely swamps the differences the benchmark reports; internally, restate last year on the new method so your own trend is read consistently.
- C) £78,095 — exclude the £240k of community costs as well, since other departments may not count them.
- D) Submit both figures and let the benchmark's users choose.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The draft divides a numerator that includes contractor cost (£610k, 6 FTEs) by a denominator that excludes contractor people — an inconsistent ratio that overstates cost per professional by charging 48 people's costs to 42 heads. The like-for-like figure is £3.9m ÷ 48 = £81,250 — 12.5% lower, and aligned with the method most departments evidently use, which is what makes B's recomputation correct on the benchmark's own intent rather than merely convenient. But B's real content is the three disclosures that surround the number, each a distinct integrity duty. The method note and change-from-last-year disclosure: a silently improved figure looks like an 12.5% efficiency gain your community did not earn — restating the prior year internally (and noting the break in the submission) keeps your trend honest, which is Question 5-of-the-lead-guide's definitional-break rule applied to yourself, at the moment the break flatters you. The flag to the benchmark's owners: if departments vary on contractor inclusion, the benchmark's cross-department comparisons are dominated by method noise, and every league-table conclusion drawn from it — including ones that might favour you — is unsound; saying so publicly is what setting and raising standards means beyond your own organisation, and is precisely the cross-government professional leadership your role names. Option A preserves consistency with an error — and consistency arguments always deserve the reply "consistent and wrong compounds; corrected and disclosed converges". Option C starts excluding costs speculatively to match what others "may" do — method-shopping in the benchmark's grey zones, one step from gaming. Option D abdicates the professional judgement the submission exists to capture; benchmarks aggregating "both figures" from every department produce nothing. The closing rule, symmetric as ever: fix inconsistent ratios even when the fix flatters you, disclose even when silence would flatter you more — a head of profession's numbers are believed at exactly the rate their inconvenient corrections are visible.

**Question 12 (challenging) — The investment committee's three-way choice**

Your organisation can fund exactly one of three product investments next year, and the committee asks for your recommendation with reasoning. Option One: £400k to modernise the highest-volume service's application journey — modelled benefit £280k/year (verified-methodology, cash-releasing), confidence 85%, benefits from year one. Option Two: £400k for the shared design system and component library — modelled benefit £190k/year in direct development savings (confidence 80%, from year one), plus unmodelled but structurally argued benefits: accessibility-by-default across services, and each future service building faster. Option Three: £400k for the data platform enabling cross-service analytics — no direct cashable benefit modelled; the case argues it unlocks better prioritisation decisions across the whole portfolio, estimated qualitatively. Three-year expected values on the modelled benefits alone: One: 0.85 × 840 − 400 = +£314k; Two: 0.80 × 570 − 400 = +£56k; Three: unmodellable as drafted. What is the strongest recommendation?

- A) Option One — it has the highest modelled expected value, and modelled value is the only defensible basis.
- B) Option Three — enabling infrastructure always beats point solutions in the long run.
- C) Recommend Option One on the modelled arithmetic while stating its boundary honestly: the comparison is only as complete as the modelling, and Options Two and Three carry real value the models cannot yet see — then convert that observation into structure: require Option Two's case to model its per-service reuse benefit (it is modellable — services × build-hours saved), require Option Three's case to name the decisions it would change and their value, and commit to re-running the comparison next cycle with all three on comparable footings; fund One now, and say exactly why the others lost and what would make them win.
- D) Split the £400k three ways to avoid choosing.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Verify the arithmetic: One: 0.85 × (280 × 3) − 400 = 714 − 400 = +£314k ✓. Two: 0.80 × (190 × 3) − 400 = 456 − 400 = +£56k ✓. Three: nothing to compute as drafted. On modelled expected value, One wins decisively — and option C recommends it, because a committee asking for a funding decision this year deserves a recommendation computed from the evidence that exists, not deferred to the evidence that should exist. But C's surrounding structure is what makes it the head-level answer rather than merely the arithmetically obedient one. The boundary statement: EV comparisons silently privilege the modellable, and Options Two and Three are not low-value — they are under-modelled, which is a property of their cases, not their worth; saying so protects the committee from learning the wrong lesson ("infrastructure never pays") from a modelling artefact. The conversion into structure: Two's benefit is genuinely modellable (reuse × services × build-cost — the case was lazy, and requiring the model is coaching its author, per coach others on identifying benefits); Three's "better decisions" claim gets the discipline that either grounds it or retires it — name the decisions, estimate their value delta, or accept that a case which cannot say what it changes cannot be funded against cases that can. The re-run commitment makes this year's loss the price of next year's fair contest rather than a verdict. Option A takes the same decision and learns nothing — "modelled value is the only defensible basis" hardens the modelling bias into doctrine, and organisations governed that way systematically under-invest in platforms until the point-solution portfolio collapses under its own duplication. Option B is the opposite faith — "infrastructure always wins" — asserted exactly where its case declined to do the work; funding it teaches your community that structural vibes outcompete verified arithmetic, which corrupts every future case. Option D converts one fundable investment into three unfundable fractions. The closing principle — a fitting one for the section's end: at head level, the recommendation is only half the deliverable; the other half is the modelling standard your recommendation enforces, because the committee decides this £400k, but your structure decides how every future £400k gets argued. Build the case for an organisational approach to value through product strategy, per your named skill, is exactly that: not winning the comparison, but owning the machinery that makes comparisons honest.

### Preparation tips

- Practise on the organisational artefacts only you hold: rerun your community's aggregations from raw numerators and denominators; multiply your strategy's targets together and check the identities can jointly hold; run the year-by-year table under your real attrition and hiring constraints before any headcount promise.
- Audit ratios for internal consistency first — numerator and denominator covering the same population (contractor costs with contractor heads; digital savings with digital volumes) — then for external consistency (same method as the comparator), and fix inconsistencies even when they flatter you, with the break disclosed.
- For benefits work, enforce the three structural rules that catch most corruption: baselines and conversion methods named at funding; a shared register of claimed savings by budget line; profile-versus-actual as the only variance comparison. Structure outperforms vigilance at community scale.
- Interrogate every variance for its baseline ("behind whose schedule?"), every average for its distribution (mean-median gap = skew), every endpoint rate for its funnel, and every projection for its supplying pool and steady state. These four reflexes cover most hard questions — and most executive papers.
- For expected-value comparisons, compute honestly, then state the modelling boundary: which options are under-modelled rather than low-value, and what would put them on comparable footing next cycle. The recommendation and the standard it enforces are one deliverable.
- Under test conditions: one written line per step on chained calculations, exact figures held until the final rounding, and when your computed answer sits between options, re-verify once — then choose the option whose reasoning survives your own arithmetic, which at this level is the reliable tiebreak.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- Averaging percentages across portfolios, or letting a near-miss ("only 1.25 points off") license the shortcut. The method is wrong every time; it is merely lucky sometimes.
- Ratios with mismatched populations — contractor money over civil-servant heads, community costs over delivery staff. Inconsistent ratios are the most common defect in figures that reach executives, because each half was assembled by a different person.
- Pay and workforce arithmetic without on-costs, without year-by-year compounding, or with growth need computed while replacement need is forgotten. Finance finds these in minutes; find them first.
- Linear baselines applied to non-linear realities: even-accrual benefit comparisons, straight-line headcount promises, flat-total-cost targets alongside volume growth. Multiply the identities; run the table.
- Double counting at portfolio scale — the same released budget line claimed by two cases, the same deflected contact claimed by two teams. Savings belong to budget lines, booked once; the register is the fix.
- Treating the modelled as the valuable and the unmodelled as worthless — or the reverse. The head-level discipline is naming the boundary and building the structure that narrows it, cycle by cycle, so the organisation's arithmetic grows more honest under your signature than it was before it.
