## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the thinking skills your role actually uses, through scenarios drawn from real work rather than abstract puzzles. Instead of shapes and sequences with no context, you will see the everyday materials of portfolio support work: project trackers, RAG status tables, milestone lists, risk logs, and meeting actions. The questions ask you to spot patterns, follow rules to their logical conclusions, find errors, put tasks in a sensible order, and solve small practical problems.

The typical format is an online, timed test lasting 15 to 30 minutes, usually with somewhere between 15 and 30 questions. Many modern platforms are adaptive, which means the questions get slightly harder when you answer correctly and slightly easier when you do not — so do not panic if the test feels like it is getting tougher; that is often a sign you are doing well. Scoring is objective: your answers are compared against a norm group of people at a similar level. Employers usually receive a balanced view of your speed and accuracy, so working carefully matters just as much as working quickly. You will almost always be offered a few ungraded practice questions before the real test starts, so you can get used to the screen layout without any pressure.

Why do employers use this kind of assessment for a digital portfolio analyst? Because your core value to the portfolio team is reliable, accurate information handling. When you update a tracker, check a status report, or prepare figures for your senior portfolio manager, a small unnoticed error can travel a long way — into governance boards, stakeholder updates, and even ministerial briefings. A cognitive assessment simulates, in miniature, exactly the careful checking and clear reasoning your role depends on. Practising it will genuinely make you better at the job, not just better at the test.

### How this assessment maps to your role

Each dimension of the cognitive assessment lines up with a named skill in your role summary:

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **Communicating analysis and insight**: before you can present analysis and visualisations in clear ways, you have to notice the patterns in the data — which projects keep slipping, which risks keep recurring, which figures do not fit the trend. Spotting the pattern is the first step of every piece of analysis you support.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Governance and assurance**: you follow governance and assurance processes under supervision, and those processes are essentially rules — "if a project is rated red for two consecutive months, then it must be escalated." Applying rules correctly to real cases is logical deduction in action.
- **Error checking** maps to **Communicating analysis and insight** and **Governance and assurance**: the reports and trackers you help maintain must be internally consistent. Finding the row that contradicts the summary, or the RAG rating that does not match the narrative, is one of the most valuable things an analyst does.
- **Prioritisation** maps to **Planning**: you are building your understanding of the importance of planning and forecasting, and prioritisation questions rehearse the core planning judgement — deciding what matters most and what comes first when several tasks compete for your time.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Agile working** and **Strategic thinking**: taking an open-minded approach, iterating quickly, and understanding the strategic context of your work all require you to think a problem through step by step and choose a sensible next move rather than freezing or guessing.

Notice that none of these dimensions asks for knowledge you do not have. They ask for careful thinking applied to material you see every week. That is the spirit in which to approach the practice questions below.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern recognition in a project tracker**

Your portfolio tracker lists projects using a fixed code format: two letters for the directorate, a hyphen, then three digits (for example, "DS-104" for Digital Services project 104). While tidying the tracker, you see these entries:

1. DS-104
2. CT-092
3. DS-17
4. HR-233
5. CT-310

Which entry breaks the coding pattern?

- A) Entry 2
- B) Entry 3
- C) Entry 4
- D) Entry 5

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The rule requires two letters, a hyphen, then three digits. Entry 3, "DS-17", has only two digits after the hyphen, so it breaks the pattern; it should read "DS-017" if it is project 17. Entries 1, 2, 4 and 5 all follow the two-letters-hyphen-three-digits format. This may look like a small thing, but consistent project codes are what let a portfolio tracker sort, filter and match records reliably. As an analyst, catching a malformed code before it spreads into reports and dashboards saves your whole team time — and it is exactly the kind of quiet quality check that builds your senior portfolio manager's trust in your work.

**Question 2 (easy) — Logical deduction from a governance rule**

Your portfolio governance process states: "Any project rated red at the monthly review must be discussed at the next portfolio board." At this month's review, Project Kestrel was rated amber and Project Heron was rated red. Which one of the following must be true?

- A) Project Kestrel will not be discussed at the portfolio board.
- B) Project Heron must be discussed at the next portfolio board.
- C) Project Heron will be re-rated amber before the board.
- D) Both projects must be discussed at the next portfolio board.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The rule says red projects must be discussed at the board. Project Heron is red, so it must be discussed — that is option B, and it follows directly from the rule. Be careful with option A: the rule tells you what must happen to red projects, but it says nothing about what cannot happen to amber projects. Kestrel might still be discussed for other reasons, so A is not guaranteed. Option C invents information — nothing suggests Heron's rating will change. Option D goes further than the rule requires. The discipline here is one you use whenever you follow governance processes under supervision: apply the rule exactly as written, and do not add assumptions of your own. If you are unsure whether an amber project should also go to the board, the right move at your level is to ask your senior portfolio manager — not to guess.

**Question 3 (easy) — Error checking a status summary**

You are checking a monthly portfolio summary before your senior portfolio manager sends it out. The summary says: "Of our 12 projects, 7 are green, 4 are amber and 2 are red." What is wrong with this sentence?

- A) Nothing — the sentence is correct.
- B) The RAG categories are listed in the wrong order.
- C) The numbers add up to 13, not 12.
- D) Red projects should always be listed first.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Add the category counts: 7 green + 4 amber + 2 red = 13, but the sentence claims there are 12 projects in total. Work it through: 7 + 4 = 11, and 11 + 2 = 13. Either the total is wrong or one of the category counts is wrong, and you would need to check the underlying tracker to find out which. Options B and D describe style preferences, not errors — there is no universal rule about the order of RAG categories in a sentence. This simple totals check is one of the most useful habits an analyst can build: whenever a summary gives both a total and a breakdown, add the breakdown and compare. It takes ten seconds and catches a surprising number of real mistakes before they reach stakeholders.

**Question 4 (easy) — Prioritising your morning**

It is 9:00 on Tuesday. You have four tasks:

1. Your senior portfolio manager needs the updated milestone tracker for a 11:00 meeting with a director.
2. A project manager has emailed asking you to explain a figure in last month's report — no deadline given.
3. The weekly team stand-up is at 9:30 (15 minutes long).
4. You planned to tidy the shared folder structure this week.

Which order of attention is most sensible?

- A) 4, 3, 1, 2 — finish your planned work first
- B) 3, 1, 2, 4 — attend the stand-up, then complete the tracker for 11:00, then reply to the email, then tidy
- C) 2, 1, 3, 4 — always answer emails first
- D) 1, 3, 2, 4 — skip the stand-up until the tracker is done

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Work out what is fixed and what is urgent. The stand-up is a fixed 15-minute commitment at 9:30 — attending keeps you connected to the team, and it still leaves you roughly 75 minutes before 11:00 for the tracker, which is the most urgent deliverable because a director meeting depends on it. The email has no deadline, so it can wait until after 11:00; the folder tidy-up is a background task for later in the week. Option A puts routine work ahead of an urgent deadline. Option C treats email as automatically urgent, which it is not. Option D sacrifices a short, valuable team ritual unnecessarily — and at a stand-up you could even flag that you are heads-down on the tracker until 11:00, which is good Agile communication. The pattern to remember: sequence tasks by deadline and impact together, and protect short fixed commitments that keep the team aligned.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Pattern recognition across monthly reports**

You support four projects and note their milestone completion each month. Over the last three months, the number of milestones completed per month looks like this:

- Project A: 4, 4, 4
- Project B: 6, 5, 3
- Project C: 2, 3, 4
- Project D: 5, 5, 4

Your senior portfolio manager asks: "Which project's trend most needs a conversation with the team?" Which is the best answer?

- A) Project A, because it never improves
- B) Project B, because its completions are falling month on month, and the drop is accelerating
- C) Project C, because it started with the lowest number
- D) Project D, because any drop is a concern

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Look at the direction and speed of each trend. Project A is stable at 4 — steady delivery, no alarm. Project C is improving: 2, then 3, then 4. Project D dipped slightly from 5 to 4 in the latest month — worth a glance, but a one-month dip of one milestone is normal variation. Project B fell from 6 to 5 (a drop of 1), then from 5 to 3 (a drop of 2): the decline is not only continuing but getting steeper. That accelerating downward pattern is the one most likely to signal a real blocker, so B most needs a conversation. Notice the technique: compare trends, not single numbers. Option C judges on the starting point rather than the direction of travel — a classic misreading. Spotting which trend matters is the heart of your communicating analysis and insight skill: your tactical recommendation ("let's talk to Project B's team") flows straight from reading the pattern correctly.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Logical deduction with two conditions**

Your assurance checklist says: (i) a project may move to the "in delivery" stage only if its business case is approved; (ii) a project with an approved business case must have a named senior responsible owner (SRO). Project Osprey is in the "in delivery" stage. Which one of the following must be true?

- A) Project Osprey has a named SRO.
- B) Project Osprey's business case was approved late.
- C) Project Osprey is rated green.
- D) Project Osprey has no open risks.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Chain the rules together step by step. Rule (i): Osprey is in delivery, so its business case must be approved — otherwise it could not have moved to that stage. Rule (ii): every project with an approved business case must have a named SRO. Since Osprey's business case is approved, Osprey must have a named SRO. That is option A, reached in two deductive steps. Options B, C and D all introduce information the rules say nothing about — timing, RAG rating, and risks are simply not mentioned. In assessments, and in real assurance work, only conclude what the rules guarantee. This mirrors how you follow governance processes under supervision: the checklist lets you verify, from a project's stage alone, which other records must exist — and if you find an in-delivery project with no SRO recorded, you have found a genuine governance gap to raise with your senior portfolio manager.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Error checking dates in a milestone plan**

A project plan you are checking contains these entries:

| Milestone | Planned date |
|---|---|
| Discovery complete | 12 May |
| Alpha assessment | 3 July |
| Alpha starts | 19 May |
| Beta starts | 28 June |

The delivery stages must run in order: discovery, then alpha (which ends with the alpha assessment), then beta. Which two dates are inconsistent with the required order?

- A) Discovery complete and Alpha starts
- B) Alpha starts and Alpha assessment
- C) Alpha assessment and Beta starts
- D) Discovery complete and Beta starts

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Put the plan in stage order and check each transition. Discovery completes 12 May; alpha starts 19 May — that is fine, alpha begins after discovery ends. Alpha ends with its assessment on 3 July. But beta is planned to start on 28 June — five days before the alpha assessment on 3 July. Beta cannot start before alpha has finished, so the alpha assessment date and the beta start date contradict each other; one of them must be wrong. The other pairings are all consistent. The useful technique here is to re-sort the list into logical order before checking, because the table's jumbled row order is exactly what hides the error. Real plans and trackers are often jumbled too. When you find a contradiction like this, the analyst's job is not to silently "fix" it but to query it with the project team — either date could be the wrong one, and only they know which.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Applied problem solving with a data gap**

You are compiling the monthly portfolio report, due to your senior portfolio manager by 16:00 today. Eleven of the twelve project teams have sent their updates. The twelfth, Project Linnet, has not responded to two reminder emails, and its project manager is on leave until tomorrow. What is the most sensible course of action?

- A) Delay the whole report until tomorrow so it is complete.
- B) Copy last month's Linnet update into this month's report without comment.
- C) Fill in Linnet's section yourself based on what you think is probably happening.
- D) Send the report on time with Linnet's section clearly marked "update not received — carried over from last month, to be confirmed", and tell your senior portfolio manager.

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** This is a problem-solving question about handling imperfect information honestly. Option D keeps the deadline, preserves accuracy by labelling the gap clearly, and keeps your senior manager informed — three things portfolio teams value highly. Option A sacrifices eleven good updates for one missing one, and deadlines for portfolio reporting often connect to board dates that cannot move. Option B is the dangerous one: presenting stale data as current misleads every reader, and if Linnet's position has worsened, the report actively hides a risk. Option C is worse still — inventing status information breaks the trust your whole role is built on. The general principle, which echoes Agile working's preference for transparency and iteration: deliver on time with known gaps clearly flagged, then iterate when the missing information arrives. An honest "we don't know yet" is always better analysis than a confident guess.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Pattern recognition in risk log entries**

Scanning the portfolio risk log, you notice four projects have each logged a risk in the last month:

- Project A: "Key developer leaving in June; knowledge transfer not planned."
- Project B: "Supplier contract expires in August; renewal not started."
- Project C: "Test environment shared with another programme; availability uncertain."
- Project D: "Lead user researcher role vacant for eight weeks; recruitment ongoing."

Your senior portfolio manager asks whether you see any common theme worth raising at portfolio level. What is the strongest common theme?

- A) All four risks are about software quality.
- B) Three of the four risks (A, B and D) concern people or supplier resourcing gaps, suggesting a portfolio-wide resourcing theme.
- C) All four risks are the fault of poor project management.
- D) There is no meaningful pattern in only four entries.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Group the risks by underlying cause. A is about a person leaving, D is about a vacancy, and B is about a supplier arrangement lapsing — all three are variations of "the people or partners we rely on may not be there." C is different: it is about shared infrastructure. So three of four risks share a resourcing theme, and that is exactly the kind of cross-project pattern that individual project teams cannot see but a portfolio analyst can. Option A misdescribes the risks. Option C leaps to blame, which the entries do not support and which would damage relationships. Option D is too dismissive — spotting emerging themes early, even from small samples, is precisely why portfolios aggregate risk logs. Raising a tentative theme ("three of our four new risks look resourcing-related — worth a portfolio-level look?") is a perfect example of giving tactical recommendations based on your analysis to inform decision making.

**Question 10 (hard) — Multi-step deduction about a reporting cycle**

Your portfolio's reporting cycle has these rules: (i) project teams submit updates by the last Friday of each month; (ii) the portfolio report is issued three working days after the submission deadline; (iii) the portfolio board meets on the first Wednesday of each month, and must receive the report at least one working day before it meets. In one particular month, the last Friday is the 27th, and the first Wednesday of the next month is the 1st. Assuming no bank holidays, can the standard cycle deliver the report to the board on time, and why?

- A) Yes — the report is issued on the 30th, one working day before the board.
- B) Yes — the report is issued on the 1st, the day of the board.
- C) No — the report would be issued on Wednesday the 1st, the day the board meets, leaving no working day's notice.
- D) No — updates are due after the board meets.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Step through the calendar carefully. Updates are due Friday the 27th. Three working days after that: Monday the 30th is one, Tuesday the 31st is two, Wednesday the 1st is three — so the report is issued on Wednesday the 1st. The board meets that same Wednesday the 1st, but the rule requires the board to receive the report at least one working day before it meets. Issued on the day of the meeting, the report is a working day late for the notice rule. So the standard cycle fails this month, and the answer is C. Option A miscounts the working days (issuing on the 30th would be only two working days after the 27th... check: Mon 30th = day 1, so no — three days lands on the 1st). Option D is simply wrong about the dates. This is the kind of calendar reasoning that supports your planning skill: when a governance timetable cannot work in a particular month, the analyst who spots it in advance — and suggests, say, an earlier submission deadline that month — saves the board from receiving a late report.

**Question 11 (hard) — Prioritisation with a governance conflict**

At 14:00 you receive four requests:

1. Your senior portfolio manager asks you to check the figures in a paper going to tomorrow morning's portfolio board.
2. A project manager asks you to add a new risk to the log "sometime this week".
3. A director's assistant asks for portfolio spend figures "as soon as possible" for an unspecified purpose.
4. A teammate asks for help formatting a slide deck for next Monday.

You realise the board paper contains a figure that contradicts the tracker. Checking and correcting it will take most of the afternoon. What is the best course of action?

- A) Do the director's request first because directors are the most senior.
- B) Focus on the board paper correction, tell your senior portfolio manager about the contradiction immediately, and ask the director's assistant what deadline and purpose the spend figures are for; schedule the other two tasks for later in the week.
- C) Split your afternoon evenly across all four tasks so nobody is kept waiting.
- D) Correct the board paper figure yourself without mentioning the contradiction, to save your manager worry.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Two judgements are being tested. First, prioritisation: the board paper is both urgent (tomorrow morning) and high impact (a wrong figure at a governance board damages decisions and credibility), so it comes first; the risk log and slide deck have comfortable deadlines; and the "as soon as possible" request needs clarifying, not instant obedience — asking for the deadline and purpose is the professional move, and it often reveals the real need is smaller or later than it sounded. Second, escalation: you found a contradiction between the paper and the tracker, and at analyst level the right response is to flag it to your senior portfolio manager straight away, because they may know which source is correct or may need to warn the paper's author. Option D is the trap: silently changing a governance paper's figure — even with good intentions — bypasses the supervision your governance and assurance skill level is built around, and you might "correct" it the wrong way. Option A confuses seniority with urgency; option C confuses fairness with effectiveness.

**Question 12 (hard) — Applied problem solving across the portfolio**

Three projects in your portfolio each depend on the same central design team. The design team can support at most two projects in any one month. The projects' plans show: Project X needs design support in May and June; Project Y needs design support in June only; Project Z needs design support in May, June and July. Nobody has cross-checked these plans. What should you tell your senior portfolio manager?

- A) There is no problem, because each project's own plan is achievable.
- B) In June all three projects need the design team at once, exceeding its capacity of two; the portfolio needs to re-sequence at least one project's design work.
- C) The design team should be told to work faster in June.
- D) Project Y should be cancelled because it only needs one month of support.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Build a simple month-by-month picture. May: X and Z need design — that is two projects, within capacity. June: X, Y and Z all need design — three projects, but the team can support only two. July: only Z — fine. The clash is June, and it is invisible to each individual project because each project's own plan looks reasonable; only someone looking across the portfolio can see it. That is the essence of portfolio analysis. The recommendation follows naturally: re-sequence at least one project's design need (for example, could Y move to July, or X finish design in May?). Option A is exactly the mistake portfolios exist to prevent — locally fine, globally impossible. Option C wishes the constraint away rather than solving it. Option D is wildly disproportionate — you resolve a one-month scheduling clash by re-sequencing, not by cancelling a project. Presenting this finding clearly — a small table of months and demands would do it — is your communicating analysis and insight skill working hand in hand with planning.

### Preparation tips

- **Practise with your real artefacts.** The best rehearsal for a job-specific cognitive test is the job itself. Next time you update the tracker, do a deliberate totals check. Next time you read a governance rule, ask yourself "what exactly does this require, and what does it not say?" Ten minutes of mindful checking a day builds the habits these tests measure.
- **Learn the totals-and-breakdown check.** Whenever a document gives a total and a breakdown, add the breakdown. It is the single highest-value error check in portfolio reporting, and versions of it appear in almost every cognitive test.
- **Slow down on rule questions.** When a question quotes a rule, read it twice and underline (mentally or literally) the words "must", "only", "unless" and "may". Most wrong answers come from adding assumptions the rule never made.
- **Sequence before you check.** If information arrives jumbled — like the milestone table in Question 7 — reorder it logically before looking for errors. Errors hide in disorder.
- **Use the ungraded practice questions.** Real test platforms nearly always offer a warm-up. Take it, even if you feel ready — it settles nerves and familiarises you with the interface.
- **Time yourself gently.** Practise with a timer, but at your level prioritise accuracy first and let speed grow with confidence. A steady 80% correct beats a rushed 50%.
- **Ask, don't guess — in real life.** Several questions above reward escalating or querying rather than guessing. That is deliberate: at analyst level, knowing when to ask your senior portfolio manager is a strength, and assessments for supervised roles are written to reward it.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Adding assumptions to rules.** "Red projects must go to the board" does not mean "amber projects must not." Deduce only what the rule guarantees. This is the most common error in logical reasoning tests and in real governance work alike.
- **Judging trends by a single number.** A project that starts low but is improving is in better shape than one that starts high but is falling fast. Always look at direction and pace, not just the latest figure.
- **Confusing "no deadline" with "not urgent" — or the reverse.** An undated request from a senior person needs a clarifying question, not instant panic and not indefinite delay.
- **Silently fixing discrepancies.** When two sources disagree, the wrong move is to pick one quietly. Flag it. In tests, the "raise it with your manager" option is usually there for a reason.
- **Rushing the easy questions.** Adaptive tests often start gently. Careless slips on easy questions cost more than struggling on hard ones, because they lower the difficulty ceiling the test lets you reach.
- **Letting one hard question derail you.** If a question stumps you, give it your best reasoned answer and move on. One question rarely decides an outcome; a rattled next ten minutes can.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures how confidently and accurately you work with the kinds of numbers your role genuinely handles. For a digital portfolio analyst, that means the arithmetic of portfolio dashboards: percentages of projects at each RAG status, spend against budget, milestone completion rates, simple averages across projects, and month-on-month trends. You will typically be shown a small table or chart and asked three or four questions about it.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 20 to 30 minutes with 15 to 20 questions. A basic on-screen calculator is usually allowed — check the instructions for your specific test — and rough paper is your friend. The questions are multiple choice, and the wrong options ("distractors") are usually the results of common mistakes, such as dividing by the wrong total or forgetting a step. That is worth knowing: if your answer matches an option, it does not prove you are right, because the test writers predicted the popular errors too. The defence is simple — follow the method carefully and sense-check the size of your answer.

Employers use numeric reasoning tests for portfolio analyst roles because so much of the role's output is numerical. When you support the tracking and analysis of outcomes across a portfolio, you are the person who turns raw figures into the percentages and trends that appear in reports. A misplaced decimal or a percentage calculated against the wrong base can misinform a whole governance board. The test does not require advanced mathematics — no algebra beyond simple rearranging, no statistics beyond averages — but it does require care, method, and comfort with the four operations, percentages, ratios and averages under mild time pressure.

One more reassurance: every numeric question in this practice section shows its arithmetic in full, step by step. If you can follow the walkthroughs, you can do the test. Speed comes from repeating the same reliable methods until they feel automatic.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Percentages and proportions** map to **Communicating analysis and insight**: portfolio reporting speaks in percentages — "58% of milestones met", "spend at 92% of budget". Presenting analysis clearly to specialist and non-specialist audiences starts with calculating those figures correctly.
- **Averages and rates** map to **Planning**: understanding forecasting begins with simple averages — average milestones completed per month, average time from approval to project start. These are the building blocks of the plans and forecasts your team makes.
- **Reading tables and charts** maps to **Communicating analysis and insight** and **Governance and assurance**: the dashboards and papers that flow through governance boards are tables and charts. Extracting the right number from the right row is a daily task, and tests reward exactly that discipline.
- **Budget and spend arithmetic** maps to **Strategic thinking** and your duty to support strategic planning in an administrative capacity: even in a supporting role you handle spend figures, and knowing whether the portfolio is over or under budget — and by what percentage — is administrative support with strategic value.
- **Trend comparison** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**: when you work to improve stakeholder relationships using evidence to explain decisions, that evidence is very often a trend — "delivery has improved three months running." Calculating change correctly keeps your evidence trustworthy.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Percentage of projects at green**

Your portfolio has 20 projects. At this month's review, 13 are rated green. What percentage of the portfolio is green?

- A) 13%
- B) 55%
- C) 65%
- D) 70%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Percentage = (part ÷ whole) × 100. Here the part is 13 green projects and the whole is 20 projects. Step 1: 13 ÷ 20 = 0.65. Step 2: 0.65 × 100 = 65%. So 65% of the portfolio is green. The distractor A (13%) is the classic error of quoting the raw count as a percentage without dividing by the total. A quick sense-check: 13 out of 20 is more than half (10) but less than three-quarters (15), so the answer must sit between 50% and 75% — which confirms 65% and eliminates A and D at a glance. Sense-checking against halves and quarters is a habit worth building for every dashboard figure you produce.

**Question 2 (easy) — Spend against budget**

Project Wren has an annual budget of £250,000. Spend to date is £180,000. How much budget remains?

- A) £60,000
- B) £70,000
- C) £80,000
- D) £430,000

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Remaining budget = budget − spend to date. Step 1: £250,000 − £180,000. Take away £150,000 first: £250,000 − £150,000 = £100,000. Then take away the remaining £30,000: £100,000 − £30,000 = £70,000. The remaining budget is £70,000. Option D (£430,000) is the result of adding instead of subtracting — an easy slip under time pressure, and one the sense-check catches instantly: remaining budget cannot be larger than the whole budget. Options A and C are near-misses from small subtraction errors. Breaking a subtraction into two friendly steps, as shown, is slower to describe than to do — and it is far more reliable under test conditions than trying to do it in one leap.

**Question 3 (easy) — Reading a RAG table**

The portfolio dashboard shows:

| Directorate | Green | Amber | Red |
|---|---|---|---|
| Digital Services | 5 | 2 | 1 |
| Corporate | 3 | 3 | 0 |
| Operations | 4 | 1 | 2 |

How many amber projects are there across the whole portfolio?

- A) 5
- B) 6
- C) 7
- D) 12

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Read down the Amber column only: Digital Services 2, Corporate 3, Operations 1. Step 1: 2 + 3 = 5. Step 2: 5 + 1 = 6. There are 6 amber projects. Option D (12) comes from adding the Green column instead — the most common table-reading error is drifting into the wrong column, especially when columns sit side by side on screen. The defence: put your finger (or cursor) on the column heading and trace down it. In your real work, this same discipline stops a report from claiming the wrong number of at-risk projects. It sounds almost too simple to practise, but under a countdown clock, column discipline is what separates accurate analysts from hasty ones.

**Question 4 (easy) — Milestone completion rate**

In April, the portfolio planned 25 milestones and completed 19 of them. What was the milestone completion rate, to the nearest whole percent?

- A) 19%
- B) 74%
- C) 76%
- D) 79%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Completion rate = (completed ÷ planned) × 100. Step 1: 19 ÷ 25. A neat trick: multiply top and bottom by 4 to make the denominator 100 — 19 × 4 = 76 and 25 × 4 = 100, so 19 ÷ 25 = 76 ÷ 100 = 0.76. Step 2: 0.76 × 100 = 76%. The completion rate is 76%. Option A repeats the raw count error; options B and D are near-misses designed to catch small arithmetic slips (a wrong multiplication like 19 × 4 = 74 gives B). The ×4 trick works whenever the total is 25, and its cousins — ×2 for totals of 50, ×5 for totals of 20 — cover many of the round totals that portfolio dashboards use. Milestone completion rate is a figure you will genuinely calculate, so this method earns its keep.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Average milestones per project**

Five projects completed the following numbers of milestones this quarter: 6, 3, 8, 5, and 3. What is the mean number of milestones completed per project?

- A) 4
- B) 5
- C) 5.5
- D) 6

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The mean = sum of values ÷ number of values. Step 1, sum the values: 6 + 3 = 9; 9 + 8 = 17; 17 + 5 = 22; 22 + 3 = 25. Step 2, divide by the number of projects: 25 ÷ 5 = 5. The mean is 5 milestones per project. Sense-check: the values range from 3 to 8, and 5 sits comfortably inside that range — a mean can never fall outside the range of its values, so any option below 3 or above 8 could have been eliminated instantly. Distractor C (5.5) is what you get by dividing by 4 (perhaps after missing a value in the sum) — always count your values before dividing. Averages like this feed portfolio reporting all the time: "on average, projects completed five milestones this quarter" is exactly the kind of clear, non-specialist-friendly statement your communicating analysis and insight skill calls for.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Percentage change in delivery**

The portfolio completed 40 milestones in Q1 and 50 milestones in Q2. What is the percentage increase from Q1 to Q2?

- A) 10%
- B) 20%
- C) 25%
- D) 80%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Percentage change = (change ÷ original) × 100. Step 1, find the change: 50 − 40 = 10. Step 2, divide by the original (Q1) value: 10 ÷ 40 = 0.25. Step 3: 0.25 × 100 = 25%. Delivery increased by 25%. The critical word is original: you divide by the starting value, 40, not the new value, 50. Dividing by the new value gives 10 ÷ 50 = 20% — which is distractor B, placed there precisely to catch that error. Option A quotes the raw change (10) as a percentage. Option D (80%) is 40 ÷ 50 × 100, a muddle of the two values. When you report a trend to stakeholders — "milestone delivery rose 25% this quarter" — dividing by the wrong base overstates or understates the improvement, so this one rule (always divide by the original) protects the credibility of your evidence.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Ratio of spend across projects**

Two projects share a combined budget of £360,000 in the ratio 5:4 (Project Swift : Project Martin). How much is Project Martin's share?

- A) £144,000
- B) £160,000
- C) £180,000
- D) £200,000

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A ratio of 5:4 means the money is split into 5 + 4 = 9 equal parts. Step 1, find one part: £360,000 ÷ 9 = £40,000. Step 2, Project Martin has 4 parts: 4 × £40,000 = £160,000. (Check: Swift gets 5 × £40,000 = £200,000, and £200,000 + £160,000 = £360,000 — the total is recovered, so the split is right.) Distractor D is Swift's share, there to catch anyone who grabs the wrong side of the ratio; the order of names in "Swift : Martin" matters. Distractor A (£144,000) comes from wrongly taking 40% of £360,000 — note that 4 parts out of 9 is not 40%. Ratios appear in portfolio work whenever funding, people or capacity are shared between projects, and the parts method shown here — total the parts, find one part, multiply — solves every simple ratio question the same way.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Reading a spend trend table**

Monthly portfolio spend (£000s) for the first four months of the year:

| Month | Jan | Feb | Mar | Apr |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Spend | 110 | 120 | 135 | 155 |

Between which two consecutive months was the largest increase in spend, and how big was it?

- A) Jan to Feb: £10,000
- B) Feb to Mar: £15,000
- C) Mar to Apr: £20,000
- D) Jan to Apr: £45,000

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Calculate each month-on-month change. Jan to Feb: 120 − 110 = 10, so £10,000. Feb to Mar: 135 − 120 = 15, so £15,000. Mar to Apr: 155 − 135 = 20, so £20,000. The largest consecutive-month increase is Mar to Apr at £20,000. Option D is a trap: £45,000 is indeed the total change from Jan to Apr (155 − 110 = 45), but Jan and Apr are not consecutive months, and the question asks about consecutive ones — a reminder to re-read the question's exact wording before answering. Note also the pattern in the data: the increases are 10, then 15, then 20 — spend growth is accelerating, which is precisely the kind of observation worth adding to a report. An analyst who reports not just the numbers but the accelerating pattern is giving the tactical insight the role calls for.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Percentage of budget spent, portfolio-wide**

Three projects report the following:

| Project | Budget (£) | Spend to date (£) |
|---|---|---|
| Swift | 200,000 | 150,000 |
| Martin | 160,000 | 120,000 |
| Wren | 240,000 | 90,000 |

What percentage of the combined budget has been spent, to the nearest whole percent?

- A) 55%
- B) 60%
- C) 63%
- D) 75%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Work with the totals, not the average of the percentages. Step 1, total budget: 200,000 + 160,000 + 240,000 = 600,000. Step 2, total spend: 150,000 + 120,000 = 270,000; 270,000 + 90,000 = 360,000. Step 3, percentage: 360,000 ÷ 600,000 = 0.6, and 0.6 × 100 = 60%. The portfolio has spent 60% of its combined budget. Distractor C (63%) is what you get by averaging the three individual percentages (75%, 75% and 37.5% average to 62.5%) — but that treats a small project as equal in weight to a large one, which misrepresents the money. Distractor D is Swift's individual percentage. The rule: for a combined percentage, always divide total by total. This exact calculation — portfolio spend against portfolio budget — is one your senior portfolio manager will ask you for, and knowing why the "average of percentages" is wrong will let you explain your figure with confidence.

**Question 10 (hard) — Forecasting spend to year end**

By the end of month 4, Project Curlew has spent £96,000 of its £300,000 annual budget. If spending continues at the same average monthly rate, what will total spend be at the end of month 12, and will the project be over or under budget?

- A) £288,000 — under budget
- B) £288,000 — over budget
- C) £312,000 — over budget
- D) £192,000 — under budget

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Step 1, find the average monthly spend so far: £96,000 ÷ 4 = £24,000 per month. Step 2, project forward over the full 12 months: £24,000 × 12 = £288,000. Step 3, compare with the budget: £288,000 is less than £300,000, so on current trend the project ends the year £12,000 under budget. The answer is A. Distractor C comes from multiplying by 13 or slipping in the multiplication; distractor D stops at month 8 (£24,000 × 8 = £192,000). Note the assumption stated in the question — "continues at the same average monthly rate" — real forecasting is only as good as that assumption, and a thoughtful analyst would add a caveat if, say, a big procurement lands in month 9. This straight-line method (spend so far ÷ months so far × total months) is the simplest forecasting tool in portfolio work, and it directly supports your planning skill's foundation: understanding the importance of forecasting.

**Question 11 (hard) — Combined completion rate across two quarters**

In Q1 the portfolio completed 36 of 48 planned milestones. In Q2 it completed 44 of 52 planned milestones. Across the two quarters combined, what was the overall completion rate, to the nearest whole percent?

- A) 75%
- B) 80%
- C) 82%
- D) 85%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Again, totals first — never average the two rates. Step 1, total completed: 36 + 44 = 80. Step 2, total planned: 48 + 52 = 100. Step 3, rate: 80 ÷ 100 = 0.80 = 80%. The combined completion rate is exactly 80% — the numbers were chosen kindly, with a total of 100 making the percentage direct. Check the traps: Q1's rate alone is 36 ÷ 48 = 0.75 = 75% (distractor A); Q2's rate alone is 44 ÷ 52 ≈ 0.846 = 85% to the nearest percent (distractor D); the simple average of 75% and 85% is 80% — which happens to match here because the planned totals (48 and 52) are nearly equal, but that is luck, not method. The safe habit is always totals over totals. Reporting a two-quarter completion rate is a genuine portfolio task, and doing it the right way means your figure stays correct even when quarters differ greatly in size.

**Question 12 (hard) — Interpreting a stacked dashboard figure**

The portfolio dashboard reports: "Portfolio spend to date: £1.08 million, which is 90% of profiled spend for this point in the year. Full-year budget: £1.8 million." Two board members draw different conclusions. Member 1 says the portfolio has spent 90% of its annual budget. Member 2 says the portfolio is 10% behind its planned spend profile. Who is right, and what fraction of the annual budget has actually been spent?

- A) Member 1 is right; 90% of the annual budget is spent.
- B) Member 2 is right; 60% of the annual budget is spent.
- C) Both are right.
- D) Member 2 is right; 54% of the annual budget is spent.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Untangle the two different baselines. "Profiled spend" is the amount the plan expected to have been spent by now — not the annual budget. Spending £1.08 million when the profile expected more means the portfolio is spending less than planned so far: £1.08m is 90% of the profile, so it is 10% behind profile — Member 2 is right. Member 1 has confused the profile with the annual budget. Now the fraction of annual budget actually spent, step by step: £1.08 million ÷ £1.8 million = 1.08 ÷ 1.8 = 0.6, so 60% of the annual budget is spent. (For interest, the profiled spend itself was £1.08m ÷ 0.9 = £1.2 million.) The answer is B; distractor D (54%) comes from wrongly multiplying 60% by 90%. Percentages are only meaningful with their base named — "90% of what?" is one of the most valuable questions a portfolio analyst can ask, and gently clarifying baselines for stakeholders is your communicating analysis and insight skill at its most useful.

### Preparation tips

- **Rebuild your percentage toolkit.** Three methods cover almost everything: part ÷ whole × 100 for "what percentage is this?"; change ÷ original × 100 for "what's the increase?"; and total ÷ total for combined rates. Write them on a card and practise each until automatic.
- **Practise on your own dashboard.** Take last month's portfolio report and recalculate three of its figures yourself. Nothing prepares you better than the real data your role handles — and you might even catch a live error, which is a great story for your development conversations.
- **Always name the base.** Before calculating any percentage, say to yourself "percentage of what?" Most numeric-test traps — and most real reporting mistakes — are base confusions like Question 12's.
- **Sense-check every answer's size.** Is the answer bigger than the total when it shouldn't be? Is a mean outside the range of the values? Is a "remaining budget" larger than the budget? Five-second checks catch the errors that time pressure causes.
- **Use rough paper deliberately.** Write each step on paper rather than juggling numbers in your head. Written steps can be checked; mental steps vanish.
- **Learn the friendly-fraction shortcuts.** Out of 20? Multiply by 5. Out of 25? Multiply by 4. Out of 50? Double it. Portfolio dashboards love round totals, and these shortcuts turn division into quick multiplication.
- **Do a timed mini-set weekly.** Ten questions in fifteen minutes, once a week in the run-up to your test, builds pace without burnout. Review every wrong answer until you can explain the mistake in one sentence.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Dividing by the wrong base.** The single most common numeric error: percentage change uses the original value; combined rates use combined totals; "percentage of profile" is not "percentage of budget". Name the base every time.
- **Averaging percentages.** Adding two percentages and halving them is only correct when the underlying totals happen to be equal. Totals over totals, always.
- **Column drift in tables.** Under time pressure it is remarkably easy to read the Green column when you meant Amber. Anchor your eye on the column heading before tracing down.
- **Answer-matching as false comfort.** Test writers include the results of popular mistakes among the options. Matching an option proves nothing — trusting your method does.
- **Ignoring the question's exact words.** "Consecutive months", "to the nearest whole percent", "Project Martin's share" — precision words change the answer. Re-read the question once after calculating, before selecting.
- **Skipping the sense check to save time.** The five seconds you save are the most expensive five seconds in the test. A wrong answer confidently entered costs more than a slow right one.
- **Panicking at big numbers.** £1.08 million behaves exactly like 1.08. Strip the zeros, calculate, then put the zeros back. The arithmetic never gets harder — only longer.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately you read, understand and draw conclusions from written material of the kind your role handles every day. For a digital portfolio analyst, that means governance guidance, status report narratives, emails from project managers and stakeholders, extracts from standards such as the Government Service Standard, and short policy passages. The classic format presents a passage of 100 to 250 words followed by statements you must judge as **True** (the passage supports it), **False** (the passage contradicts it), or **Cannot say** (the passage gives too little information to decide either way). Other question types test inference — what follows from the passage — and clear-writing judgement, such as choosing the best summary or the clearest rewrite of a clumsy sentence.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 15 to 25 minutes with 20 to 30 statements spread across several passages. The golden rule throughout: judge every statement using only the passage, not your outside knowledge. This feels strange at first — you may know from experience that something is true in general, but if the passage does not say it, the answer is "cannot say". Assessors are testing your discipline as a careful reader, because in governance work the difference between "the report says X" and "I assume X" really matters.

Employers use verbal reasoning tests for portfolio analyst roles because so much of the role is reading and relaying written information faithfully. When you summarise a project's status narrative for a portfolio report, or extract the key point from a governance minute, or act on an emailed instruction, any gap between what was written and what you understood becomes an error in the portfolio's records. The test also reflects the writing side of your role: choosing the clearest way to express a finding is a daily task for anyone who shapes and shares communications relevant to the audience.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Reading comprehension** maps to **Governance and assurance**: following governance and assurance processes under supervision starts with reading process documents accurately — what must happen, when, and who decides. Misreading "should" as "must", or missing an exception clause, causes real assurance mistakes.
- **True/false/cannot-say discipline** maps to **Communicating analysis and insight**: when you relay project information into portfolio reports, you must separate what the source actually says from what you assume. "Cannot say" is the test's version of the analyst's honest "the update doesn't tell us that — I'll ask."
- **Inference** maps to **Strategic thinking**: explaining the strategic context of your work means connecting written strategy statements to your portfolio's activities — drawing sound conclusions from documents without over-reaching beyond what they support.
- **Clear-writing judgement** maps to **Communicating analysis and insight** and **Stakeholder relationship management**: choosing the appropriate medium and shaping communications for the audience includes plain-language judgement. Tests that ask "which rewrite is clearest?" rehearse the same skill you use turning project jargon into a sentence a non-specialist stakeholder can act on.
- **Identifying the main point** maps to **Agile working**: iterating quickly on reports and updates requires you to grasp the essence of feedback and source material fast — what is the one thing this email is actually asking for?

### Practice questions

**Passage for Questions 1–3:**

"All projects in the digital portfolio must submit a monthly status report by the last working day of the month. Reports must include a RAG rating, progress against milestones, and an updated risk summary. Projects rated red must additionally provide a recovery plan. The portfolio office reviews all reports within three working days of submission and may request clarification from project teams. Projects that miss two consecutive submission deadlines will be raised at the portfolio board."

**Question 1 (easy) — True, false or cannot say**

Statement: "Projects rated red must provide a recovery plan."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, but only for large projects

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The passage states directly: "Projects rated red must additionally provide a recovery plan." The statement repeats this almost word for word, so it is True. Option D adds a qualification ("only for large projects") that appears nowhere in the passage — a reminder that in verbal reasoning, options that add conditions to a clean statement are almost always wrong. This question is deliberately gentle: it rewards you for locating the relevant sentence and matching it carefully. In real work, this is the skill of quoting a governance requirement accurately when a project manager asks "do we really have to do that?" — you answer from what the process document says, not from memory.

**Question 2 (easy) — True, false or cannot say**

Statement: "The portfolio office reviews reports within one working day of submission."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True for red projects only

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage says the portfolio office reviews all reports "within three working days of submission". The statement claims one working day, which contradicts the stated three. A direct contradiction makes the statement False — not "cannot say", because the passage does give the information needed to judge it. This distinction trips people up: "cannot say" is for missing information, "false" is for contradicted information. Here the information is present and the statement gets it wrong. When you check documents in real life — for example, confirming a review timescale before promising it to a project team — the same precision applies: the difference between one day and three days is the difference between a promise you can keep and one you cannot.

**Question 3 (moderate) — True, false or cannot say**

Statement: "A project that misses one submission deadline will be raised at the portfolio board."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, if it is also rated red

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage says projects that miss two consecutive deadlines will be raised at the board. It says nothing about what happens after a single missed deadline. Careful: this does not mean a single miss will not be raised — the portfolio office might still choose to raise it, and the passage neither promises nor rules this out. Because the passage gives no information either way about single misses, the answer is Cannot say. Choosing B ("False") is the classic over-inference: assuming that because the rule triggers at two misses, nothing happens at one. Rules state what must happen, not everything that may happen — the same lesson as the cognitive section's governance questions, appearing here in verbal form. This reading discipline protects you in real assurance work from telling a project team "you're fine, nothing happens until you miss two" when the process makes no such promise.

**Passage for Questions 4–6:**

"The department's digital strategy commits to reducing legacy technology risk across the portfolio. From April, business cases for new projects must include an assessment of their impact on legacy systems. The portfolio office will pilot a quarterly legacy-risk dashboard for six months, initially covering the ten highest-spend projects. If the pilot proves useful to decision makers, coverage will extend to the whole portfolio. Project teams remain responsible for the accuracy of the data they submit."

**Question 4 (easy) — Locating a detail**

According to the passage, which projects will the pilot dashboard initially cover?

- A) All projects in the portfolio
- B) The ten highest-spend projects
- C) Projects with the most legacy risk
- D) Projects that started after April

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage states the pilot will initially cover "the ten highest-spend projects". Option C is the tempting one: since the dashboard is about legacy risk, surely it covers the riskiest projects? But the passage says the selection is by spend, not by risk — perhaps because spend data is readily available for choosing a pilot group. This is a small but important reading lesson: plausible logic is not what the passage says. When you populate a report or answer a stakeholder's question about scope, "what would make sense" and "what the document actually specifies" can differ, and your value as an analyst lies in reporting the latter accurately — and querying it if it seems odd.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Inference**

Which one of the following can be properly inferred from the passage?

- A) The legacy-risk dashboard will definitely extend to the whole portfolio.
- B) Extension of the dashboard beyond the pilot depends on whether decision makers find it useful.
- C) Legacy technology risk is the department's biggest problem.
- D) Project teams have been submitting inaccurate data.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage makes extension conditional: "If the pilot proves useful to decision makers, coverage will extend." Option B restates that condition faithfully — extension depends on usefulness to decision makers. Option A drops the "if", turning a conditional into a certainty, which is the most common inference error in these tests. Option C inflates "commits to reducing legacy technology risk" into "biggest problem" — the passage never ranks the department's problems. Option D reverses a responsibility statement into an accusation: saying teams are responsible for accuracy does not imply they have been inaccurate. Notice the pattern across the wrong options: dropping conditions, inflating strength, and reversing implications. Being alert to all three will serve you when you summarise strategy documents for your team — a summary that turns "if the pilot works" into "the dashboard is rolling out" misinforms everyone downstream.

**Question 6 (moderate) — True, false or cannot say**

Statement: "Business cases for projects that started before April must be resubmitted with a legacy impact assessment."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, but only for the ten pilot projects

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage says that "from April, business cases for new projects must include an assessment of their impact on legacy systems." It addresses new projects only. About existing projects and resubmission it says nothing at all — it neither requires nor rules out retrospective assessments. With no information either way, the answer is Cannot say. Option B is tempting if you reason "the rule is for new projects, so old projects are exempt" — but the passage does not state an exemption; it is simply silent, and silence is not exemption. In portfolio work this matters practically: if a project manager of a pre-April project asks whether the requirement applies to them, the honest analyst answer is "the guidance doesn't cover that case — let me check with the portfolio office", not a confident guess in either direction.

**Passage for Questions 7–9:**

"Email from a project manager to the portfolio office: 'Hi — quick update ahead of the monthly report. We've completed the supplier selection milestone two weeks early, which is great news. However, the data migration workstream has hit a problem: the legacy extract took longer than expected, and we now expect to complete migration four weeks later than planned. We think we can recover two of those weeks in testing, as the test environment is now available earlier than assumed. On that basis we're proposing to keep our RAG rating at amber rather than moving to red. Happy to discuss.'"

**Question 7 (moderate) — Identifying the main point**

Which sentence best summarises the email for the portfolio log?

- A) "Project reports supplier selection completed two weeks early."
- B) "Project reports data migration will be four weeks late."
- C) "Project reports migration delay of four weeks, expects to recover two weeks in testing, and proposes to remain amber."
- D) "Project requests to be re-rated red."

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A good summary preserves every element a reader would act on: the delay (four weeks), the expected recovery (two weeks), and the rating proposal (stay amber). Option C carries all three. Options A and B are each true but dangerously partial — A logs only the good news, B only the bad, and either alone would misrepresent the project's position in the portfolio record. Option D states the opposite of the proposal. Summarising is not shortening at any cost; it is selecting what changes decisions. Here the portfolio office must decide whether to accept the amber proposal, and that decision needs the delay, the recovery plan and the proposal together. This is the everyday craft of your communicating analysis and insight skill: a portfolio log entry that a senior manager can trust without re-reading the original email.

**Question 8 (moderate) — True, false or cannot say**

Statement: "The project's net expected delay to migration, after recovery, is two weeks."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True only if the portfolio office agrees

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Follow the email's arithmetic in words: migration is expected four weeks late, and the team thinks it can recover two of those weeks in testing. Four minus two leaves a net expected delay of two weeks — exactly what the statement says, so it is True. Note the word "expected": the statement is careful to claim only the expectation, which the passage supports ("we expect... we think we can recover"). Had the statement said "the migration will be exactly two weeks late", the answer would shift towards Cannot say, because the passage expresses expectation, not certainty. Reading — and writing — with this precision about certainty is a core analyst habit: portfolio reports live or die by the difference between "will", "expects to" and "hopes to". Option D confuses the delay estimate with the rating decision; the recovery expectation does not depend on the portfolio office's agreement.

**Question 9 (hard) — Evaluating the reasoning**

The project manager argues the rating should stay amber "on that basis" — meaning the expected two-week recovery. Which unstated assumption does this argument most depend on?

- A) The supplier selection milestone was completed early.
- B) The recovery of two weeks in testing will actually be achieved.
- C) The portfolio office reads emails promptly.
- D) Other projects in the portfolio are also amber.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** An assumption is something the argument needs to be true but does not prove. The case for staying amber rests on the delay being only two weeks net — which is only the case if the hoped-for recovery in testing really happens. The email itself signals uncertainty ("we think we can recover"), so the argument leans on an unproven expectation: that is option B. Option A is stated in the email, not unstated, and supplier selection is not the basis of the rating argument anyway. Options C and D are irrelevant to the logic. Spotting load-bearing assumptions is a genuinely useful portfolio skill: when your senior portfolio manager reviews this proposal, the sharp question is exactly "how confident are we in the two-week recovery — what does it depend on?" An analyst who can surface that question from a friendly, well-written email is adding real assurance value, not just processing correspondence.

**Question 10 (moderate) — Clear-writing judgement**

You are drafting a portfolio report sentence about the email above. Which version is clearest for a non-specialist senior audience?

- A) "Utilisation of the earlier-than-anticipated availability of the test environment will facilitate partial mitigation of the migration timeline slippage."
- B) "Migration is expected four weeks late; the team expects to recover two weeks during testing and proposes remaining amber."
- C) "There are some timing issues on the project but the team is on top of it."
- D) "The legacy extract ETL overran, impacting the E2E migration critical path, though early NFT environment availability derisks the glide path."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B is plain, specific and complete: it gives the delay, the expected recovery, and the proposal in nineteen words a non-specialist can act on. Option A is the classic officialese trap — "utilisation", "facilitate", "mitigation of slippage" — long words doing the work short ones do better; government plain-language guidance exists precisely to prevent this. Option D drowns the reader in unexplained jargon (ETL, E2E, NFT) that excludes non-specialists. Option C fails in the opposite direction: it is friendly but empty, giving the reader nothing to act on and hiding the size of the delay — vagueness in a governance report is not kindness, it is a risk. The craft your role requires is B's balance: concrete numbers, everyday words, no padding. When you present analysis "in clear ways to both specialist and non-specialist audiences", this is what it looks like in a single sentence.

**Question 11 (hard) — True, false or cannot say**

Statement: "Portfolio staff may retire a statutory report if they judge that it no longer serves a clear purpose."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, with the portfolio board's approval

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage encourages retiring unused metrics and challenging purposeless reporting — but then draws a hard boundary: "statutory and audit reporting obligations must always be met in full, regardless of local views on their usefulness." A statutory report therefore cannot be retired on a usefulness judgement; the statement directly contradicts the passage's final sentence, making it False. This question rewards reading to the end: the first four sentences all push towards "True", and only the final sentence reverses the answer for the statutory case. Test writers love this structure, and so does real governance guidance, where general principles often carry specific exceptions. The workplace parallel is exact: an enthusiastic analyst who "streamlines away" a statutory return has created a serious compliance problem out of good intentions. When guidance contains a "however", treat it as load-bearing.

**Question 12 (hard) — Applying the passage's principles**

A project manager complains to you that a monthly report field duplicates data already held in the delivery tool, and asks whether they can stop filling it in. Based only on the passage, what is the best response?

- A) "Yes — the guidance says duplicated reporting should stop, so leave the field blank from now on."
- B) "No — every existing reporting requirement must be met in full."
- C) "The guidance supports reusing data rather than re-keying it, so I'll raise this with the portfolio office to check whether the field can be sourced from the tool or retired — unless it feeds a statutory obligation."
- D) "Fill it in for now and I'm sure it will be reviewed eventually."

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Map the passage's principles onto the situation. The guidance does support the project manager's instinct: information already collected "should be reused rather than re-keyed", and staff "should challenge any reporting requirement that no longer serves a clear purpose". But the guidance authorises challenge, not unilateral abandonment — and the statutory exception means some fields cannot be dropped whatever their apparent usefulness. Option C follows the passage faithfully: acknowledge the principle, raise the challenge through the portfolio office, and check the statutory question. Option A converts "challenge" into "just stop", skipping both the process and the statutory check. Option B contradicts the passage's explicit encouragement to challenge. Option D ducks the analyst's role entirely — the guidance says staff should challenge, and at your level, "raising it with the portfolio office" is exactly how you do that. This is verbal reasoning at its most job-like: reading a policy correctly and turning it into the right next action.

### Preparation tips

- **Practise the three-way judgement daily.** Take any work document and write three statements about it: one it supports, one it contradicts, one it does not address. Deciding which is which is the entire skill of true/false/cannot-say, and making the questions yourself teaches it fastest.
- **Read the passage first, fully, once.** Then tackle statements, returning to the passage for each. Skimming statements first tempts you to hunt for confirmation rather than read for meaning.
- **Quarantine your outside knowledge.** Before answering, ask "does the passage say this?" — not "is this true in my experience?" Your portfolio knowledge is an asset at work and a liability in the test.
- **Watch for condition-droppers.** Wrong options often take a conditional sentence ("if the pilot proves useful...") and restate it as certain. Whenever a passage says "if", "unless", "may" or "should", expect a question to test whether you noticed.
- **Read to the final sentence.** Exceptions and reversals live at the ends of passages, exactly as "however" clauses live at the ends of governance paragraphs. Never answer from the first half alone.
- **Build your plain-English ear.** For clear-writing questions, favour the option with concrete facts, everyday words and no padding. Reading GOV.UK content — written to exactly those standards — for a few minutes a day tunes your ear quickly.
- **Time-box each passage.** In a 20-minute test with four passages, five minutes per passage is your budget. Practise moving on when a single statement eats your time; an unanswered easy question later costs more than a hard one now.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Confusing "false" with "cannot say".** False means the passage contradicts the statement; cannot say means the passage is silent. "The passage doesn't mention it" is never grounds for False.
- **Treating silence as exemption.** A rule about new projects tells you nothing about old ones. Silence is missing information, not a hidden "no".
- **Importing job knowledge.** You may know how portfolio reporting really works in your department — the test passage is its own tiny world, and only its contents count.
- **Falling for plausible-but-unstated options.** "The dashboard covers the riskiest projects" sounds sensible; the passage said highest-spend. Plausibility is the bait; the passage is the judge.
- **Answering from the passage's first half.** The "however" that reverses everything is usually near the end. Read it all, every time.
- **Choosing the impressive-sounding rewrite.** In clear-writing questions, formality and jargon are decoys. The clearest option is usually the shortest one that keeps all the actionable facts.
- **Rushing statements that quote the passage almost exactly.** "Almost" is the operative word — a changed number, a dropped "if", or a swapped "must"/"should" turns a match into a contradiction. Compare word by word.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement test (SJT) presents realistic workplace scenarios and asks you to judge possible responses. Unlike the other assessments in this guide, it has no arithmetic and no passages to parse — it measures your professional judgement: how you balance honesty, helpfulness, process, relationships and escalation when situations get awkward. For a digital portfolio analyst, the scenarios involve exactly the situations your role produces: conflicting instructions, errors discovered late, sensitive information requests, team frictions, and moments when you must choose between asking for help and pressing on alone.

The typical format is untimed or generously timed — 20 to 40 minutes for 15 to 25 scenarios. Question styles vary: you may be asked to pick the most effective response, the least effective response, both, or to rank all the options from best to worst. Some tests ask you to rate each response independently on a scale. There is rarely a perfectly clean answer; the test measures whether you can tell "good" from "slightly flawed" from "actively harmful". Scoring compares your judgements with those of experienced professionals and the organisation's behavioural framework — for UK government digital roles, think of behaviours like user focus, collaboration, ownership, honesty and transparency, appropriate escalation, and inclusion.

Employers use SJTs for analyst-level roles because judgement, unlike technical skill, is hard to teach quickly and easy to observe in scenarios. At your level, they are especially interested in whether you know the boundaries of a supervised role: when to act, when to ask, and when to escalate. Getting this right is not about timidity — it is about understanding that a portfolio's information flows to senior decision makers, so errors and surprises need managing openly, and that your senior portfolio manager can only support you if they know what is happening. The best preparation is not memorising "correct" answers but understanding why certain responses serve users, colleagues and the organisation better than others — which is what every explanation below aims to teach.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Escalation and openness scenarios** map to **Governance and assurance**: following processes under supervision means knowing what you can decide alone and what needs your senior portfolio manager. SJTs probe exactly this boundary — and reward candidates who escalate promptly without either hiding problems or dumping every trivial decision upwards.
- **Stakeholder dilemma scenarios** map to **Stakeholder relationship management**: identifying important stakeholders, communicating clearly and regularly, tailoring communication to their needs, and using evidence to explain decisions all get tested through scenarios about difficult requests, impatient senior people, and disagreements between teams.
- **Team and delivery scenarios** map to **Agile working**: taking an open-minded approach, valuing iteration, and being honest about work in progress underpin the SJT's collaboration scenarios — including how you respond to feedback and how you handle imperfect first versions.
- **Information-handling scenarios** map to **Communicating analysis and insight**: several dilemmas turn on how you present findings — whether you soften bad news, oversell certainty, or share draft data prematurely. The professional behaviours here are accuracy and audience-appropriate honesty.
- **Workload and priority scenarios** map to **Planning**: when the test asks how you handle competing demands, it is measuring the planning instincts your role is building — sequencing by impact and urgency, protecting commitments, and renegotiating openly when everything cannot be done.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — A mistake in a sent report**

Two hours after the monthly portfolio report went out to stakeholders, you realise one project's spend figure is wrong: you transposed two digits, showing £129,000 instead of £192,000. Nobody has commented yet. What is the most effective response?

- A) Say nothing — it may go unnoticed, and you can quietly correct it next month.
- B) Tell your senior portfolio manager immediately, propose sending a short correction to recipients, and check how the error slipped through so it does not recur.
- C) Email the correction to recipients yourself straight away, without telling your senior portfolio manager, to fix it before they find out.
- D) Wait to see if anyone notices, and correct it only if someone asks.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The behaviours being tested are honesty, ownership and appropriate escalation. Option B does all three: it surfaces the error promptly (honesty), proposes the fix (ownership), involves the person accountable for the report (escalation), and adds the professional finishing touch — learning from the miss. Options A and D gamble the portfolio's credibility on nobody noticing; a stakeholder who later discovers the error, and that you knew, loses trust in every future report. Option C fixes the data but hides the event from your manager — who may be asked about the correction by a stakeholder and be caught unaware, and who also needs to know for assurance reasons. A £63,000 difference could genuinely change how a reader views the project. The deep lesson for a supervised role: errors are recoverable; concealment is what does the lasting damage. Managers consistently say the analysts they trust most are the ones who report their own mistakes fastest.

**Question 2 (easy) — Asked for data you are unsure about sharing**

A friendly project manager from another directorate calls: "Could you send me the spend figures for all the projects in your portfolio? I'm benchmarking for my director." You are not sure whether portfolio spend data can be shared outside the portfolio team. What is the most effective response?

- A) Send the figures — being helpful builds relationships.
- B) Refuse — data should never leave the portfolio team.
- C) Explain you need to check the sharing rules first, confirm with your senior portfolio manager, and respond as soon as you know — taking the requester's details and purpose.
- D) Send the figures but ask the project manager to keep them confidential.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** This tests information-handling judgement under social pressure. Option C balances helpfulness with proper care: you neither refuse nor comply blindly, you capture the purpose (which your manager will ask about), and you commit to a prompt answer — good stakeholder relationship management on all counts. Option A shares data of unknown sensitivity because someone was friendly; spend data can be commercially sensitive or pre-decisional, and "for my director" is a purpose, not an authorisation. Option D is worse than it looks: it acknowledges the data might be sensitive (hence the secrecy request) while sharing it anyway. Option B swings to the opposite failure — an unexplained refusal damages a relationship unnecessarily, and may be wrong: sharing might be perfectly fine. At analyst level, "let me check and come back to you today" is almost always a strong answer to uncertain requests; it is honest, professional and keeps every relationship intact.

**Question 3 (easy) — Feedback on your first analysis**

You spent three days building your first portfolio trend analysis. In the team review, your senior portfolio manager suggests significant changes: different groupings, a simpler chart, and removal of a section you were proud of. What is the most effective response?

- A) Defend each original choice in detail so the effort is not wasted.
- B) Make the changes silently but feel the analysis is no longer really yours.
- C) Ask questions to understand the reasoning behind each suggestion, make the changes, and note the principles for next time.
- D) Ask a teammate afterwards whether they think the feedback was fair.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The behaviours tested are openness to feedback and learning orientation — core to Agile working's open-minded approach and iteration. Option C treats the review as what it is: a senior practitioner teaching you the craft. Asking about reasoning ("what makes the simpler chart better for this audience?") converts each change into a transferable principle, which is how analysts grow quickly. Option A confuses effort with quality — three days of work does not make a grouping right for the audience, and defensiveness in reviews teaches seniors to soften future feedback, which slows your development. Option B complies without learning and breeds quiet resentment; iteration is meant to improve the work, not diminish the author. Option D is not wrong as a supplement, but as a primary response it routes around the person best placed to explain. A useful reframe from Agile practice: the first version of any analysis is a draft for improving, and review changes are the process working, not the author failing.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Conflicting instructions**

Your senior portfolio manager asked you to spend today preparing data for Thursday's portfolio board. Mid-morning, a programme manager — senior to you, but not in your line management — asks you to drop that and spend the day building a slide pack for her programme review tomorrow. What is the most effective response?

- A) Do what the programme manager says — she is more senior than you.
- B) Politely explain your current priority, and say you will check with your senior portfolio manager straight away about whether and how to fit the slide pack in.
- C) Refuse, because only your line manager can give you work.
- D) Try to do both by working late, telling no one.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This scenario tests whether you understand tasking lines in a supervised role while staying helpful and courteous. Option B does exactly the right things: it is transparent with the programme manager (no false promises), it protects the board preparation until told otherwise, and it puts the prioritisation decision where it belongs — with the manager who owns your workload. That is not passing the buck; it is correct escalation of a genuine conflict between two legitimate demands. Option A lets seniority override an agreed priority without the accountable person knowing — and Thursday's board could receive late or rushed data as a result. Option C gets the principle right but the manner badly wrong; blunt refusals damage stakeholder relationships that the portfolio team depends on. Option D is the classic well-meaning trap: silent heroics hide the resource conflict that managers need to see, set unsustainable expectations, and risk both pieces of work being done tired and badly. Visible, honest workload negotiation beats invisible overwork every time.

**Question 5 (moderate) — A project manager asks you to soften a report**

A project manager reviewing your draft portfolio entry says: "Could you take out the line about the testing delay? We'll catch up, and I don't want the board worrying unnecessarily." The delay is real and confirmed. What is the most effective response?

- A) Remove the line — the project manager knows their project best.
- B) Keep the line but promise not to mention it verbally at the board.
- C) Explain the report needs to reflect the known position, offer to include the project's recovery plan and context alongside the delay, and if the project manager still objects, refer the disagreement to your senior portfolio manager.
- D) Remove the line but keep a private note of it in case it becomes a problem later.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** This is an integrity-under-pressure scenario, and it tests the honesty and transparency behaviours that portfolio reporting depends on. Option C holds the line on accuracy while treating the project manager as a partner, not an adversary: adding recovery context is a genuinely fair offer — the board should hear both the delay and the plan — and referring an unresolved disagreement upwards is the correct move at analyst level, since the report is issued under your senior portfolio manager's authority. Option A hands editorial control of governance information to the people being reported on, which quietly destroys the report's assurance value; a board that "isn't worried" because information was removed is being misled, however kindly. Option B is incoherent — a report you will not stand behind verbally is not a report. Option D creates a hidden record while publishing a sanitised one: the worst of both worlds if it later emerges. The principle to remember: bad news plus context and a plan is good reporting; bad news deleted is a governance failure.

**Question 6 (moderate) — You spot a possible risk nobody has raised**

While updating trackers, you notice that three separate projects list the same small supplier as critical to their autumn milestones. No one has mentioned this overlap; you are not sure it is a real problem, and supplier management is not your area. What is the most effective response?

- A) Do nothing — supplier risk is outside your role.
- B) Raise it with your senior portfolio manager as an observation: three projects depend on one supplier in the same period, and it may be worth checking capacity.
- C) Email all three project managers telling them their plans are at risk.
- D) Add a red risk to the portfolio risk log yourself.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This scenario tests ownership and initiative within sensible boundaries. Option B is the model analyst move: you noticed a cross-project pattern precisely because your role sees across the portfolio, you framed it honestly as an observation rather than a conclusion ("may be worth checking"), and you routed it to the person who can judge and act. That is the aggregation value of portfolio work in action. Option A wastes the observation — "not my area" is the wrong instinct when the whole point of a portfolio team is connecting information across areas; passing an observation upward costs nothing and might prevent a shared-supplier failure hitting three projects at once. Option C broadcasts an unverified alarm, which could worry teams needlessly and makes the portfolio office look erratic; verification comes before notification. Option D overreaches your supervised governance level — logging a red portfolio risk is a formal act with consequences, made on your own authority about something you are "not sure is a real problem". The pattern for analyst-level initiative: notice boldly, verify humbly, escalate promptly, act within your level.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Least effective: handling a quiet week**

Your senior portfolio manager is at a conference for three days and left you no specific tasks. Routine work will fill perhaps half your time. Which response is LEAST effective?

- A) Work through your backlog of tracker tidy-ups and data quality checks.
- B) Draft improvements to a report template that stakeholders have found confusing, for your manager to review on return.
- C) Wait to be given work, keeping your time free in case something urgent arrives.
- D) Ask teammates whether they need help with anything this week.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** This is a least-effective question — read carefully, because the mental switch from "pick the best" to "pick the worst" catches many candidates. Options A, B and D are all good uses of unsupervised time: A invests in the data quality that underpins everything the portfolio office produces; B shows initiative while correctly leaving approval to your manager (drafting for review, not unilaterally changing a stakeholder-facing template); D strengthens the team. Option C is the least effective: three days of idleness justified by a hypothetical urgent task is neither planning nor responsiveness — if something urgent did arrive, you could reprioritise then, as interruptions do not require an empty diary to be handled. SJTs often include one "passive" option like this, and it almost always scores worst for analyst roles, because the behaviour employers most want to see at your level is proactive usefulness within your authority: finding valuable work that needs no permission, and preparing — not executing — work that does.

**Question 8 (moderate) — A stakeholder challenges your figures in a meeting**

You are presenting portfolio milestone data at a delivery group meeting — one of your first solo presentations. A senior stakeholder interrupts: "Those numbers are wrong. My team completed six milestones last month, not four." You believe your data is right, but you are not certain. What is the most effective response?

- A) Insist your figures are correct — backing down undermines the portfolio office.
- B) Immediately accept their number and promise to change the report.
- C) Thank them, say you will verify against the source submissions after the meeting and confirm to them and the group, then continue with the presentation.
- D) Ask them to prove their claim in front of the group.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** This tests composure, honesty about uncertainty, and stakeholder handling under public pressure. Option C neither capitulates nor digs in: it takes the challenge seriously, commits to a verifiable check against the source data, promises a definite follow-up to both the stakeholder and the group (protecting everyone's confidence in the data), and keeps the meeting on track. Notably, either party could be right — the stakeholder may be counting milestones your process defines differently, which is itself useful to discover. Option A defends a position you are not certain of; if you turn out to be wrong, the portfolio office's credibility takes double damage. Option B corrupts the report on one person's say-so — sympathetically meant, but data changed under social pressure rather than evidence is no longer assurance data. Option D turns a data question into a public confrontation with a senior stakeholder, damaging exactly the relationship your role is meant to build. The transferable script — "thank you, I'll verify against source and confirm to you today" — serves analysts in every profession.

**Question 9 (hard) — Discovering your own team's process gap**

Preparing evidence for an internal audit, you discover that for the past three months the portfolio office — including you — has not been carrying out a required monthly data quality check. The gap predates your joining, and nobody seems aware of it. The audit meeting is next week. What is the most effective response?

- A) Start doing the check from this month and say nothing about the previous gap.
- B) Tell your senior portfolio manager what you found, with the evidence, so the team can decide how to address it and be transparent with the audit.
- C) Quietly perform the three missed checks now and backdate the records so the audit finds no gap.
- D) Mention it informally to the auditor first, since audits value candour.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This scenario raises the stakes on honesty: the tempting options protect the team's image at the cost of integrity. Option B is right on every axis. It is honest, it is properly sequenced — your senior portfolio manager owns the team's audit response and must not be ambushed — and it enables the good outcome: a team that finds its own gap, fixes it, and discloses it typically earns audit credibility rather than losing it. Option A compounds a process failure with a concealment decision made at the wrong level; you do not have the authority to decide the gap stays hidden, and audits often find gaps anyway — at which point the story becomes "they knew and said nothing". Option C is the serious one: backdating records is falsification — an integrity breach that can end careers and, in government, undermine the audit function that protects public money. The presence of a clearly unethical option is common in SJTs; it must always rank last. Option D has the right value (candour) but the wrong order — going to the auditor before your own manager breaks trust inside the team and denies your manager the chance to respond properly. Route findings up your line first; disclose through the accountable person.

**Question 10 (hard) — Balancing inclusion in a rushed team task**

Your team must produce a data pack in two days. The senior analyst suggests splitting the work between herself and you, "since there's no time to bring the new starter up to speed" — the new starter joined three weeks ago and is sitting nearby, looking uncomfortable. What is the most effective response?

- A) Agree — deadlines come first, and the new starter can learn next time.
- B) Suggest the new starter take a well-defined, manageable slice of the work with a short handover, so the pack still lands on time and they build capability and belonging.
- C) Insist the work be split three ways equally, whatever the deadline.
- D) Say nothing in the meeting but reassure the new starter privately afterwards.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This tests inclusion and collaboration under delivery pressure — a combination SJTs increasingly probe because it reflects real team life. Option B finds the genuine balance: a bounded, well-defined slice (say, one standard section of the pack) with a brief handover costs the team perhaps half an hour and buys a colleague's development, confidence and belonging — while explicitly protecting the deadline. It also models constructive challenge: you offer the senior analyst a better plan rather than either obeying or opposing her. Option A treats inclusion as a luxury for quiet weeks; in practice "next time" recurs, the new starter stays deskilled, and the team builds a habit of excluding its newest member precisely when involvement teaches most. Option C elevates equality of task division over the actual goals — inclusion does not require pretending three weeks' experience equals ten years', and a rigid three-way split could genuinely risk the deadline. Option D comforts the person while leaving the exclusion intact — sympathy is not support. Note the general SJT pattern: the best options usually serve both the delivery goal and the people, not one at the other's expense.

**Question 11 (hard) — Rank the responses: an impossible Friday**

It is Friday, 13:30. Three things land at once: (1) your senior portfolio manager, travelling and offline until Monday, left a note asking for the risk log updated by end of day; (2) a director's office calls asking for a portfolio summary slide by 16:00 for a Monday-morning meeting; (3) a project manager reports their status update, submitted Wednesday, contained a wrong milestone date that is now in the draft board pack. Rank these responses from most to least effective:

1. Correct the board pack date (with a note of the change), build the 16:00 slide from existing verified data, then update the risk log — flagging by email to your manager anything you could not finish.
2. Update the risk log first because your manager asked first, then do the slide if time remains.
3. Tell the director's office a slide is impossible today and suggest Monday.
4. Build the slide first, skip the correction until Monday since the board pack is only a draft.

- A) 1, 2, 4, 3
- B) 1, 4, 2, 3
- C) 2, 1, 3, 4
- D) 1, 2, 3, 4

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Work through the ranking. Response 1 is clearly best: it sequences by impact and urgency — the pack correction is quick and prevents wrong data reaching a board; the 16:00 slide is a hard external deadline met with verified (not hastily assembled) data; the risk log fills the remaining time, with honest communication about anything unfinished. Response 2 is second: it is conscientious and does real work, but it sequences by "who asked first" rather than impact, risking the director's hard 16:00 deadline for a task whose owner will not even see it until Monday. Response 3 is third: it at least communicates honestly rather than silently failing, and offers an alternative — but it surrenders a deliverable that response 1 shows is achievable, and portfolio credibility with a director's office matters. Response 4 is worst: it leaves known-wrong data in a governance pack on the reasoning "it's only a draft", but drafts become finals precisely by such deferrals, and the correction takes minutes. So the order is 1, 2, 3, 4 — option D. Ranking questions reward comparing options against each other on impact, honesty and feasibility, not judging each in isolation.

**Question 12 (hard) — Most and least effective: a teammate's shortcut**

You notice a teammate copies last month's figures into sections of the portfolio report when projects submit late, without marking them as carried over. He says: "Everyone knows late projects rarely change much — it keeps the report complete and nobody has ever complained." Which is the MOST effective response, and which is the LEAST effective?

Responses:

1. Say nothing — he is more experienced, and no harm has come of it.
2. Tell him you think unmarked carry-overs could mislead readers, suggest the report adopt a clear "carried over — to be confirmed" label, and if he disagrees, raise the practice (not the person) with your senior portfolio manager.
3. Report him to your senior portfolio manager immediately without speaking to him first.
4. Start doing the same yourself for consistency.

- A) Most effective: 2; least effective: 4
- B) Most effective: 3; least effective: 1
- C) Most effective: 2; least effective: 3
- D) Most effective: 1; least effective: 4

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Take most-effective first. Response 2 wins because it addresses the practice directly and collegially (colleague first, not manager first), proposes a constructive fix — the "carried over" label, which solves the completeness problem honestly, exactly as Question 8 in the cognitive section modelled — and has a principled escalation path framed around the practice rather than the person. Response 3 has the right concern but skips the collegial step; going straight over a teammate's head for something a conversation might fix damages team trust, though it is still better than silence or participation because the misleading practice would at least stop. Response 1 normalises quiet data corruption. Response 4 is the least effective of all: it converts one person's bad habit into team practice, actively spreading the problem — you would now be presenting stale data as current yourself. So: most effective 2, least effective 4, which is option A. The underlying behaviour map: challenge constructively, prefer fixing systems over blaming people, escalate what conversation cannot fix, and never adopt a shortcut just because it is established. "Nobody has ever complained" is not evidence that nobody has ever been misled.

### Preparation tips

- **Learn your organisation's behaviours, not scripts.** SJTs score against behavioural frameworks — for government digital roles: honesty, ownership, collaboration, user focus, inclusion, appropriate escalation. Before the test, reread your organisation's values and the Civil Service behaviours for your grade; the "right" answers express them.
- **Master the analyst-level escalation pattern.** Across almost every scenario above, the strong answer involves acting within your authority, being transparent, and involving your senior portfolio manager for decisions above your level. Practise spotting which parts of a scenario are yours to do and which are yours to raise.
- **Read the question stem twice.** "Most effective", "least effective", "rank all four" and "rate each" need different mental settings. More SJT marks are lost to stem-misreading than to bad judgement.
- **Compare options against each other.** Especially in ranking questions, do not judge options in isolation — the test is whether you can tell good from slightly-flawed from harmful. Ask of each pair: which serves users, colleagues and honest information better?
- **Beware options that protect image over accuracy.** Softening reports, hiding gaps, quiet fixes — SJTs for governance-adjacent roles are full of them, and they score badly however kind their motives sound.
- **Rehearse with real memories.** Think of three awkward moments from your own work life and what the best response would have been. Connecting scenarios to lived experience makes your test judgements faster and more consistent.
- **Answer as your best professional self.** Not as a fantasy hero, and not as your most cautious self either — as the analyst who is honest, helpful, and knows when to ask. Consistency of character across scenarios also matters, as some tests measure it.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Choosing deference over transparency.** "They're senior, so do what they say" fails whenever it hides a conflict or corrupts information. Seniority determines who decides, not what is true.
- **Confusing kindness with concealment.** Removing worrying lines from reports, covering for teammates, sparing the board "unnecessary worry" — these all mislead decision makers and score poorly.
- **Silent heroics.** Working late secretly, absorbing impossible workloads, fixing others' errors without a word — these hide problems the organisation needs to see, and SJTs mark them down.
- **Overreach disguised as initiative.** Logging formal risks, changing templates, or emailing alarms on your own authority skips the supervision your level is designed around. Initiative at analyst level means noticing, preparing and proposing — then confirming.
- **Passivity disguised as prudence.** "Wait and see", "keep your time free", "someone else will raise it" — the passive option is almost always among the worst for a role whose value is proactive information flow.
- **Skipping the colleague conversation.** Escalating a teammate's behaviour before speaking to them usually ranks below the direct, constructive conversation. Escalation is the second step, not the first — unless the behaviour is serious misconduct like falsifying records.
- **Treating "least effective" questions as "most effective".** The classic careless error. When your chosen answer seems too obviously right, re-read the stem — you may be answering the wrong question.

If a test is coming up soon, a simple plan serves you well. In the days before, revisit the sections where your practice answers were shakiest, redo those questions, and read the explanations aloud if it helps them stick. The night before, stop early and rest — a fresh mind outperforms a crammed one at every level of seniority. On the day, set up somewhere quiet, use the ungraded warm-up questions, keep an eye on the clock without letting it rush you, and remember that adaptive tests are supposed to feel challenging. Afterwards, whatever the outcome, ask for your feedback report if one is available: it is free professional development, and it will tell you where to focus next.

And if no test is imminent, treat this guide as a development companion. The skills it exercises — careful checking, honest numbers, precise reading, sound judgement — are the foundations of the portfolio profession you are at the start of. Every senior portfolio manager and head of portfolio once sat where you sit, learning to reconcile a tracker and draft a clear status line. Keep practising, keep asking good questions, keep flagging what you notice, and be kind to yourself when you get things wrong — that is what iteration means, and you now know why iteration matters.

Good luck with your assessment, and even more importantly, good luck with the career it is part of. You are building exactly the right skills, in exactly the right way. Keep going.
