# Associate Service Designer - Psychometric Assessment Resources

## Introduction

Welcome, and congratulations on beginning your career as an associate service designer in the UK Government Digital and Data profession. You might be here because an assessment is coming up, because you are thinking about your next step, or simply because you are curious about how psychometric tests relate to the work you are learning to do. Whatever brought you, you are in the right place, and this guide was written with someone at the very start of a service design career firmly in mind.

Let us begin by taking the fear out of these assessments, because they make almost everyone nervous. Psychometric assessments are not intelligence tests. They are not designed to catch you out. They are not a verdict on your talent or your potential. They are structured, standardised exercises that measure the everyday thinking a particular job actually uses. For you, that means noticing when a journey map and a set of research findings disagree, spotting that a service step assumes something users do not have, reading a design standard carefully and applying it, working out what a set of numbers about a service is really telling you, and making sensible decisions when something in a design session goes sideways. None of that is exotic. It is the ordinary, careful thinking that good service design rests on.

Your role level shapes this whole guide. As an associate service designer you are a trainee in an entry-level position, working under supervision, expected to show design aptitude, potential and an understanding of the role. Nobody assessing you at this level expects you to have mapped a cross-government service or negotiated with a policy team. What they are looking for is exactly what your role description names: aptitude, potential, and understanding. The questions here are pitched accordingly. Every rule, definition and figure you need is given to you inside the question — you never have to have memorised anything.

There is one thing worth saying about service design specifically before we start. Your discipline is unusual in that its subject is the whole journey — not a screen, not a page, but everything a person has to do, across digital and offline channels, sometimes across several organisations, to get something done. That means your thinking constantly has to zoom out. Where does this person come from before they reach us? What happens after they submit? Who else is involved — a carer, an employer, a local authority? What happens to the person who cannot do this step at all? A surprising amount of what these assessments measure is precisely that habit of looking at the whole thing rather than the part in front of you.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the thinking your role genuinely demands, using practical workplace materials rather than abstract shapes and sequences. Instead of rotating cubes in your head, you will work with the sorts of things an associate service designer really handles — a journey map, a set of service steps, a design standard, a list of research findings, a channel comparison, a short extract from a team's design principles.

The typical format is an online test lasting fifteen to thirty minutes. Question types cluster into five families: pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective, comparing your answers against a norm group of people at a similar level. Two features of modern testing platforms are worth knowing in advance, because both are unsettling if they surprise you. Many tests are adaptive, meaning the questions get harder after correct answers and easier after wrong ones — so if the test suddenly feels difficult, that usually means you are doing well. And most employers receive an acuity report showing speed against accuracy rather than a single score, which means careful work is rewarded and rushing is not.

For an associate service designer, this section matters because so much of your early value comes from noticing. You notice that a journey map shows a step users described completely differently in research. You notice that a service assumes everyone has a bank account. You notice that two teams have designed the same letter in two incompatible ways. You notice that the "quick" route through a service takes three weeks if a document has to arrive by post. Each of those is a small act of cognition — comparison, deduction, error detection — and each protects the quality of the design.

One reassurance before the questions begin. At your level, employers are looking for aptitude and potential, not encyclopaedic knowledge. You will never need to recall a real accessibility regulation or a real design pattern from memory. Every rule you need is stated in the question. Your job is simply to apply the stated rules carefully — which is exactly what you do at work when a senior designer gives you direction and you carry it out.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The cognitive dimensions map onto the skills named in your role summary:

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **Evidence-based design**, where you explain how research and analytics inform user-centred design and identify common sources and types of data. Recognising that four different findings describe the same underlying problem is the first step in using evidence at all.
- **Error checking** maps to **Designing for everyone**, where you explain the importance of making services inclusive and accessible, and describe different access needs and ways to meet them. Spotting that a design step excludes somebody is an error-checking act with a human consequence.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Evidence-based design** again, where you frame your ideas as design hypotheses to be tested. A hypothesis is a claim about what follows from what, and framing one well means knowing exactly what your evidence does and does not support.
- **Prioritisation** maps to **Iterative design**, where you describe the basics of iterative methodologies such as agile. Working in short cycles means constantly deciding what to do first and being able to explain why.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Designing together**, where you explain techniques for bringing together perspectives from multiple people, and to **Design communication**, where you describe methods for explaining design decisions.
- **Seeing the whole journey** cuts across everything and maps to **Designing strategically**, where you describe how your design work aligns to the wider goals and vision of your team.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Error checking a journey map**

Your team's rule is that every step on a journey map must record three things: what the user does, what the service does in response, and how long the step takes. You review four steps:

| Step | User action | Service response | Duration |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Completes online form | Confirmation email sent | 15 minutes |
| 2 | Uploads evidence | — | 10 minutes |
| 3 | Waits for decision | Case assessed | 3 weeks |
| 4 | Receives letter | — | — |

How many steps are missing at least one required item?

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Check each step against all three requirements. Step 1 has all three. Step 2 records the user action and duration but no service response, so it is incomplete. Step 3 has all three. Step 4 has the user action but neither a service response nor a duration, so it is incomplete. Two steps are missing at least one item, giving B. Note the wording of the question: it asks how many *steps* are incomplete, not how many *items* are missing — there are three missing items across two steps, and confusing the two would lead you to C. Read the stem carefully every time. In real work this distinction matters as well, because "two steps need finishing" and "three pieces of information are missing" prompt different conversations.

**Question 2 (easy) — Logical deduction from a service rule**

Your team's rule states: "A service change may go live only if it has been tested with users, unless it is a correction to published content approved by the service owner." A colleague says: "The change went live this morning, and it was not tested with users." Which one of the following must be true?

- A) The team broke the rule.
- B) The change was a content correction approved by the service owner.
- C) Either the change was an approved content correction, or the rule was broken.
- D) The change will cause problems for users.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The rule is a conditional with one permitted exception, and the facts do not tell you whether the exception applies. Option A assumes it does not; option B assumes it does; you have evidence for neither. Option D introduces a claim about consequences that nothing in the question supports — an untested change is not automatically a harmful one, though it may be. Only option C is guaranteed in every possible version of events. Assessments reward this discipline of concluding only what the information actually guarantees, and it matters at work too: before you suggest a colleague skipped a step, the collegial and accurate move is to ask which route was used.

**Question 3 (easy) — Pattern recognition across research findings**

Your team collects findings from six user research sessions about a licensing service. The difficulties participants mentioned were:

- P1: did not know which documents were needed
- P2: did not know which documents were needed; found the fee confusing
- P3: could not find the phone number
- P4: did not know which documents were needed
- P5: did not know which documents were needed; could not find the phone number
- P6: found the fee confusing

Which difficulty is most widely shared?

- A) Not knowing which documents were needed
- B) Finding the fee confusing
- C) Not being able to find the phone number
- D) All three are equally common

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Count how many different participants raised each issue rather than counting total mentions. Documents were raised by P1, P2, P4 and P5 — four of six participants. The fee was raised by P2 and P6 — two. The phone number was raised by P3 and P5 — two. Four out of six is clearly the most widely shared, so the answer is A. The counting discipline here is important and generalises: what matters is how many different people hit a problem, not how many times it appears in your notes, because one talkative participant can otherwise make an issue look far more common than it is. Notice also that spotting the pattern is not the same as explaining it — users might not know which documents are needed because the guidance is unclear, or because it is on a page they never see, or because the requirements genuinely vary.

**Question 4 (easy) — Prioritisation in a design week**

It is Monday morning. Four things need your attention:

1. A live service page tells users the wrong fee — people may pay the wrong amount today.
2. Your senior designer asked for a rough journey map by Wednesday.
3. The team's design session is at 11:00 and you agreed to bring printed personas.
4. A newsletter about a cross-government design community event has arrived.

What should you do first?

- A) Start the journey map while the morning is quiet.
- B) Raise or fix the wrong fee on the live page.
- C) Print the personas for the 11:00 session.
- D) Read the newsletter before the week gets busy.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Prioritisation questions ask you to weigh impact and urgency together. Item 1 is both: a wrong fee on a live page can cause real people to pay the wrong amount today, which costs them money and creates work for the department to unpick. That harm outranks everything else on the list. As an associate you may not fix it yourself — the right first action might be to alert your senior designer or the content owner immediately — but either way it comes first. Item 3 is genuinely time-bound but takes ten minutes and is not due until 11:00. Item 2 has until Wednesday. Item 4 supports your connection to the wider design community, which matters, but is the least urgent here. The pattern to remember: harm to users outranks internal commitments, and internal commitments outrank nice-to-haves.

**Question 5 (easy) — Error checking against instructions**

A senior designer asks you to make exactly three changes to a service map: (i) add the offline channel to step 2, (ii) correct the duration of step 4 from two days to two weeks, and (iii) remove the duplicate step 6. You get the updated map back from a colleague and find: the offline channel has been added to step 2, step 4 now reads two weeks, the duplicate step 6 has gone, and the colour scheme of the whole map has been changed. How many of the changes made were requested?

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Count carefully. Four changes were made in total, but the question asks how many were *requested*. Changes (i), (ii) and (iii) were all requested and all done — that is three. The colour change was not on the list, so it is an extra, unrequested change. The answer is 3. This mirrors an important habit at your level: when you work independently after being given direction, you deliver exactly what was agreed and you flag anything else rather than slipping it in. Unrequested changes are how inconsistencies creep into shared artefacts, and a map whose colours no longer match the team's other maps is a small but real cost.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Deduction about a service journey**

A service has these rules: applicants must submit evidence within 28 days of applying; a decision is issued within 10 working days of evidence being received; and applicants may request one extension of 14 days to the evidence deadline. An applicant applied on 1 March, requested an extension, and submitted evidence on 10 April. Which must be true?

- A) The applicant missed the deadline.
- B) The applicant submitted within the extended deadline.
- C) The applicant will receive a decision by 20 April.
- D) The application is invalid.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Work the dates through step by step. Applying on 1 March gives an original deadline 28 days later, which is 29 March. With one 14-day extension, the deadline moves to 12 April. Evidence submitted on 10 April is therefore inside the extended deadline, so B is correct and A and D both fail. Option C is tempting but wrong for a specific reason: the decision comes within 10 *working* days of evidence being received, and 10 working days from 10 April is around 24 April, not 20 April — and the exact date depends on weekends and any bank holidays. The lesson is to check whether a rule says days or working days, because service journeys routinely mix the two and users routinely misread them, which is itself a design problem worth noticing.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Pattern recognition across channels**

A service is available online, by telephone and by post. The percentage of applications completed successfully by each channel is:

| Channel | Applications started | Completed |
|---|---|---|
| Online | 8,000 | 6,400 |
| Telephone | 1,000 | 900 |
| Post | 1,000 | 400 |

Which channel most clearly needs design attention, and why?

- A) Online, because it has the most incomplete applications in absolute terms.
- B) Post, because only 40% of postal applications complete, far below the other channels.
- C) Telephone, because it has the fewest applications.
- D) None; the overall completion rate is acceptable.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Convert to rates before comparing, because the channels handle very different volumes. Online completes 6,400 of 8,000, which is 80%. Telephone completes 900 of 1,000, which is 90%. Post completes 400 of 1,000, which is 40%. Post is dramatically worse, so the answer is B. Option A is the trap and it is a reasonable-sounding one: online loses 1,600 applications against post's 600, so in absolute terms online loses more people. That matters, and it might well justify work on the online journey too. But a 40% completion rate signals something badly broken in the postal route, and postal users are often those who cannot use the online channel — which makes their failure rate a likely accessibility and inclusion problem rather than a mere inefficiency.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Error checking against a design standard**

Your team's standard says a service page must: use language a user would use rather than internal terminology, tell the user what happens next, state how long the step will take, and not ask for information the service already holds. You review this page: "Your application has been received. Your case will be triaged by the assessment team. Please confirm your National Insurance number." Assuming the service already holds the National Insurance number, how many parts of the standard does the page break?

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Check each requirement. "Triaged by the assessment team" is internal terminology a member of the public would not use, so the first requirement fails. The page does say something about what happens next — the case will be assessed — so that requirement is arguably met, if only just. It gives no indication of how long the step takes, so that fails. And it asks for a National Insurance number the service already holds, so that fails too. Three requirements are broken, giving C. A better version would read: "We have received your application. We will check it and write to you within 10 working days." Notice how much shorter and more useful that is, and that fixing the language and the timing costs nothing.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Applied problem solving in a design session**

You are helping run a design session. Twenty minutes in, two senior stakeholders are dominating the discussion and the three frontline caseworkers you specifically invited have not spoken. You are the most junior person present. What is the most sensible way to think about this?

- A) Let it run; the stakeholders are senior and know the service.
- B) Recognise that the session's value depends on the caseworkers' knowledge, and use a structural technique — such as asking everyone to write ideas on cards for two minutes, then going round the table — to change who is speaking without confronting anyone.
- C) Interrupt and ask the stakeholders to let others speak.
- D) Speak to the caseworkers separately afterwards instead.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The problem is that the room is producing one perspective when you invited it to produce several, and the elegant solutions are structural rather than confrontational. Writing before speaking gives quieter people time to form a thought and gives you a legitimate reason to go round the table, which changes the dynamic without anyone being told off. Option B is available even to the most junior person in the room, which is exactly why it is the right answer at your level. Option A wastes the caseworkers' time and produces a design based on how senior people imagine the service works. Option C might work and puts you in direct conflict with senior stakeholders as a trainee, which is a risk with a gentler alternative available. Option D is a useful backup and does not rescue this session. Your role names explaining techniques for bringing together perspectives from multiple people, and this is that skill in action.

**Question 10 (moderate) — Deduction about what evidence supports**

In research with nine participants, six said they would prefer to receive updates by text message rather than letter. Which conclusion is best supported by this evidence alone?

- A) Most users of the service prefer text messages.
- B) The service should replace all letters with text messages.
- C) Six of nine participants preferred text messages, which is worth exploring further — including with users who may not have a mobile phone.
- D) Letters are unpopular and should be phased out.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Nine participants is a normal and useful sample for qualitative research, but it does not support a claim about a whole user population, so option A overreaches. Options B and D turn a preliminary finding into a service-wide decision, and D additionally invents a claim about letters being unpopular that the evidence does not establish — preferring one channel is not the same as disliking another. Option C describes exactly what the evidence shows and proposes a proportionate next step, and the clause about users without a mobile phone is what makes it a service designer's answer rather than a generic one. In service design, a channel decision is never only about preference; it is about who becomes unable to use the service if you change it. Framing ideas as hypotheses to be tested is a named skill in your role, and this is what a well-framed hypothesis looks like.

**Question 11 (harder) — Prioritisation with competing pressures**

Your senior designer is away for the day. At 10:00 the following are all true:

1. A caseworker has emailed to say the new form is generating cases they cannot process, and they are handling them manually.
2. The team's design review is at 14:00 and your part of the map is not finished.
3. A content designer needs your confirmation of step names by 12:00 to publish new guidance.
4. A colleague has asked you to review their prototype this week.

What is the most sensible order of work?

- A) 2, 3, 1, 4 — finish your own commitments first.
- B) 1, 3, 2, 4 — understand the live problem, unblock the content designer, then your map, then the review.
- C) 3, 2, 1, 4 — deadlines in time order.
- D) 1, 2, 3, 4 — in the order listed.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Rank by consequence and deadline together. Item 1 is a live problem affecting a colleague's ability to do their job and, behind that, real users whose cases are being handled by workaround; you may not be able to fix it, but understanding it and passing it to the right person is urgent and takes fifteen minutes. Item 3 has a hard 12:00 deadline and blocks somebody else's work, so it comes next. Item 2 is your own commitment with a 14:00 deadline, and it is genuinely important, but it is within your control and can absorb the earlier interruptions. Item 4 has a week. Option C is close and defensible, but it puts a 12:00 task ahead of an unfolding live problem that is costing someone hours today. Option A ranks by ownership rather than consequence, which is the classic trap: your own deadlines feel most pressing precisely because they are yours.

**Question 12 (harder) — Applied problem solving about inclusion**

A team is designing a service where applicants must upload a photograph of a document. The service is used heavily by people in temporary accommodation and people over 75. The team proposes a phone-only upload with no alternative, arguing it is simpler to build and most people have a smartphone. What is the strongest analysis?

- A) The team is right; most people have smartphones, and the alternative routes add cost.
- B) A single channel with no alternative excludes anyone without a suitable phone, without data, without somewhere private to photograph a document, or without the confidence to do it — and these overlap heavily with the service's main user groups, so the design would fail exactly the people it most needs to serve.
- C) The team should add a postal route as well.
- D) The team should test the phone upload with older users first.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The strongest analysis names the mechanism and its scale, not just the gap. A phone-only upload assumes four separate things at once: that the person has a smartphone, that it has a working camera and data, that they have somewhere suitable to photograph a document, and that they are confident doing it. People in temporary accommodation may lack the last two even if they have a phone, and people over 75 are disproportionately affected by the first and fourth. Because those groups are the service's core users, the exclusion is not an edge case — it is the main case. Option B says all of this. Option A treats a majority statistic as sufficient, which is precisely the reasoning that produces inaccessible public services. Option C names a good solution without the analysis that would justify it, and jumping to a solution is weaker than establishing the problem. Option D is a sensible research step and does not address the design decision, which is currently to offer no alternative at all. Your role asks you to explain different access needs and ways to meet them, and this question is that skill applied to a real trade-off.

### Preparation tips

- **Practise on real artefacts.** Take a journey map or a service page from your team and check it against the standards it should meet. This is exactly the comparison muscle the assessment measures, and your team gets a free review.
- **Slow down to speed up.** Trainees lose more marks to rushing than to not knowing. Read each rule twice before applying it.
- **Write the rules down.** For deduction questions, jot each condition as a short line. Getting rules out of your head frees your attention for reasoning.
- **Decide what you are counting.** People, steps, items, rule breaches — these are different numbers, and the question always specifies one.
- **Check every option.** These items usually include one nearly-right distractor built from a specific error.
- **Watch for days versus working days.** Service journeys mix them constantly and so do assessment questions.
- **Do a timed drill.** Ten minutes, four questions. You are practising composure, not speed.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Stopping at the first problem you find.** "How many" questions need a full count.
- **Counting mentions instead of participants.** One talkative person can invent a pattern.
- **Comparing raw counts across channels of different sizes.** Convert to rates first, every time.
- **Confusing "could be true" with "must be true".** Deduction rewards only conclusions guaranteed in every case.
- **Ranking by ownership rather than consequence.** Your own deadlines feel most urgent because they are yours.
- **Treating a majority as sufficient.** "Most people have a smartphone" is how public services exclude people.
- **Jumping to a solution before establishing the problem.** Naming the fix is weaker than naming the mechanism.
- **Panicking when questions get harder.** On adaptive tests, rising difficulty usually means you are doing well.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A numeric reasoning assessment measures how well you work with the numbers your job produces. This is the section people worry about most, so let us be clear straight away: this is not a maths exam. There are no equations to solve and no formulae to memorise. Most platforms provide a calculator, and where they do not, the arithmetic is deliberately simple. What is being measured is whether you can read a table correctly, pick the right numbers out of a cluttered display, choose the right operation, and interpret the result sensibly.

The format is usually a timed online test of fifteen to thirty minutes, in which each question shows a small table, chart or short paragraph of figures followed by a multiple-choice question. Time per question is tight, often around a minute, so the real skill is finding the relevant numbers quickly and ignoring the rest. Test designers routinely include figures you do not need, precisely to see whether you can tell the difference.

Some designers arrive here convinced they are "not a numbers person". That belief is worth challenging, because service design is full of numbers even though it is not a quantitative discipline. You look at how many people drop out at each step. You compare how many use the phone against the online route. You work out how long a journey actually takes when a two-week wait sits in the middle of it. You check how many people would be affected if a channel closed. You read a completion rate and decide where to focus. None of this requires advanced mathematics, and all of it requires numerical care, because getting it wrong sends a team to redesign the wrong part of a service.

At associate level the numbers are deliberately modest: percentages, fractions, proportions, simple averages, ratios, and reading values from tables. The techniques that carry you through are equally modest. Work out what the question is actually asking before you look at the data. Identify the two or three numbers you need. Do the arithmetic one clear step at a time. Then sanity-check — if you have calculated that 400 out of 1,000 is 60%, a two-second check catches it.

A last practical note about the options. Wrong answers in numeric tests are almost never random. They are the results of specific, predictable mistakes: dividing by the wrong total, confusing a percentage with a percentage point, using the new figure instead of the old one. That means finding your answer among the options is not proof that you are right — and it also means that once you know the traps, you can often see them coming.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The numerical skills tested map onto the skills named in your role:

- **Reading tables accurately** maps to **Evidence-based design**, where you identify common sources and types of data that inform user-centred design. Journey analytics, channel volumes and completion rates all arrive as tables.
- **Percentages and proportions** map to **Evidence-based design** and to **Designing for everyone**, because comparing completion between user groups or channels is how exclusion becomes visible in numbers rather than only in stories.
- **Averages** map to **Iterative design**, where understanding how long a step actually takes tells you whether an iteration improved anything.
- **Reading a journey end to end** maps to **Designing strategically**, where aligning to wider goals means understanding the whole service, including the waiting times nobody designed.
- **Interpreting figures cautiously** maps to **Design communication**, where you describe methods for explaining design decisions. A design decision justified by a misread number is hard to defend twice.
- **Spotting an implausible number** maps to **Evidence-based design**, where framing hypotheses well starts with trusting the right data.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Completion rate**

Of 250 people who started an application, 200 completed it. What percentage completed?

- A) 70%
- B) 75%
- C) 80%
- D) 85%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A percentage is the part divided by the whole, times 100: 200 ÷ 250 = 0.8, which is 80%, so the answer is C. Estimate first as insurance — 200 of 250 is clearly more than three quarters and less than everything, so an answer around 80% is expected. Option B would come from 200 ÷ 267 and option A from 200 ÷ 286, both the kind of slip a mis-keyed calculator produces under time pressure. The three-second estimate before every calculation is the cheapest error insurance in this whole section.

**Question 2 (easy) — Drop-off between steps**

A journey shows 1,200 users reaching the evidence step and 900 continuing past it. How many users dropped off, and what percentage of those reaching the step is that?

- A) 300 users, 25%
- B) 300 users, 33%
- C) 900 users, 75%
- D) 300 users, 30%

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The drop-off is 1,200 − 900 = 300 users. As a proportion of those who reached the step, that is 300 ÷ 1,200 = 0.25, or 25%, giving A. Option B is 300 ÷ 900, which uses the number who continued rather than the number who arrived — a denominator error and the most common mistake in journey analytics. The rule worth fixing in memory: a drop-off rate is always measured against the number who reached that step, not the number who got past it. Reporting the wrong one to your team means describing the step as a third worse than it is.

**Question 3 (easy) — Reading a channel table**

| Channel | Applications | Completed |
|---|---|---|
| Online | 4,000 | 3,400 |
| Telephone | 500 | 450 |
| Post | 500 | 200 |

Which channel has a completion rate of exactly 85%?

- A) Online
- B) Telephone
- C) Post
- D) None of them

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Convert the target into what it would mean for each channel, or work out each rate. Online is 3,400 ÷ 4,000 = 85%. Telephone is 450 ÷ 500 = 90%. Post is 200 ÷ 500 = 40%. So the answer is A. A faster route when the totals are round is to ask what 85% of each total would be: 85% of 4,000 is 3,400, which matches immediately. Looking for the number rather than computing every rate saves real time under a clock, and it works whenever a question gives you a target percentage.

**Question 4 (easy) — Average handling time**

Five telephone calls about a service lasted 6, 9, 4, 11 and 10 minutes. What was the mean call length?

- A) 7 minutes
- B) 8 minutes
- C) 9 minutes
- D) 10 minutes

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Add the values: 6 + 9 = 15, plus 4 = 19, plus 11 = 30, plus 10 = 40. Divide by 5: 40 ÷ 5 = 8 minutes, so B. A useful shortcut for numbers clustered around a round figure is to work from a guess: try 8, and the differences are −2, +1, −4, +3, +2, which sum to zero, confirming the mean is exactly 8. That method uses much smaller numbers than long addition, which matters when you are working quickly and cannot afford a slip.

**Question 5 (easy) — Total journey time**

A service journey has these steps: complete the form (20 minutes), wait for a reference number (2 working days), upload evidence (15 minutes), wait for assessment (3 weeks), receive the decision letter (2 working days in the post). Roughly how long does the whole journey take from start to finish?

- A) About 35 minutes
- B) About 3 weeks
- C) About 4 weeks
- D) About 6 weeks

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Add the waiting periods, because they dominate everything else. Two working days, plus three weeks, plus two working days is roughly three weeks and four working days — call it just under four weeks. The 35 minutes of actual user activity is negligible by comparison. So the answer is C. Option A is the trap and it is the most instructive one in this whole section: it counts only the time the user spends doing something, which is exactly how services come to be described internally as "a twenty-minute application" when the person experiencing it waits a month. Seeing the whole journey, including the parts where nothing visible happens, is close to the definition of your discipline.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Comparing groups fairly**

In a service, 340 of 400 users under 65 completed an online application, and 60 of 200 users over 65 completed it. Which statement is correct?

- A) Both groups completed at similar rates.
- B) The rate was 85% for under-65s and 30% for over-65s.
- C) Under-65s completed 280 more applications, so the difference is modest.
- D) The groups cannot be compared because they are different sizes.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Convert each to a rate so the different group sizes stop distorting the comparison. Under-65s: 340 ÷ 400 = 0.85, which is 85%. Over-65s: 60 ÷ 200 = 0.3, which is 30%. Option B states both correctly. Option A is simply wrong once calculated. Option C compares raw counts across groups of different sizes, which is the error this question is built to catch. Option D gets it backwards — converting to rates is precisely how you compare unequal groups fairly. A gap of 55 percentage points is enormous and would be a serious inclusion signal, pointing at something in the online journey that older users cannot get past.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Percentage change**

After a redesign, weekly completed applications rose from 480 to 600. What is the percentage increase?

- A) 20%
- B) 24%
- C) 25%
- D) 120%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Percentage change divides the difference by the original value, not the new one. The difference is 600 − 480 = 120, and 120 ÷ 480 = 0.25, which is 25%, giving C. Option A is 120 ÷ 600, dividing by the new figure — the single most common error in this question type, and one that under-reports every improvement you ever make. Option D expresses the new figure as a percentage of the old rather than the change. The rule to memorise is short: for percentage change, the denominator is always where you started from.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Working back to a total**

A report says 20% of applicants abandon the service at the identity step, and that this is 1,400 people a month. How many people reach the identity step each month?

- A) 1,680
- B) 2,800
- C) 7,000
- D) 28,000

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** If 20% equals 1,400, the total is 1,400 ÷ 0.2 = 7,000, giving C. If dividing by a decimal feels awkward, step it out: 20% is 1,400, so 10% is 700, and 100% is 7,000. Verify by reversing — 20% of 7,000 is 1,400, which matches. Option B is 1,400 × 2, and option A adds 20%. This calculation matters in real work because colleagues quote percentages far more readily than base numbers, and 20% of 7,000 is a very different design problem from 20% of 70.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Ratio in service volumes**

A service receives applications through two channels in a 4:1 ratio of online to telephone. It receives 3,500 applications a month in total. How many arrive by telephone?

- A) 700
- B) 875
- C) 2,800
- D) 1,400

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** A 4:1 ratio divides the total into 4 + 1 = 5 shares. With 3,500 applications, each share is 3,500 ÷ 5 = 700. Online takes four shares, which is 2,800, and telephone takes one share, which is 700. The question asks about telephone, so the answer is A. Option C answers the other half of the split, which is the trap for anyone who calculates correctly and then reads the final sentence carelessly. Write down what each share represents before you calculate, and re-read the question before choosing. This matters in service work because a channel that carries 700 applications a month is far from negligible — closing it would displace 8,400 people a year.

**Question 10 (moderate) — Choosing the right denominator**

A journey shows: 10,000 users start, 8,000 pass the eligibility check, 6,000 upload evidence, 5,400 submit. What percentage of users who uploaded evidence went on to submit?

- A) 54%
- B) 67%
- C) 68%
- D) 90%

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Identify the group the question names before touching any figures. It asks about users who uploaded evidence, so the whole is 6,000 and the part is 5,400: 5,400 ÷ 6,000 = 0.9, which is 90%, giving D. Option A uses 10,000, answering a whole-journey question nobody asked. This is the most common trap in funnel data and it has a real consequence: telling a team that 90% of people who upload evidence go on to submit is a completely different message from telling them 54% of all starters finish, and the two point at different parts of the service. Underline the group the stem names, then calculate.

**Question 11 (harder) — Percentage points versus percentages**

Before a change, 30% of users completed a step unaided. Afterwards, 45% did. Which statement is accurate?

- A) Completion rose by 15%.
- B) Completion rose by 15 percentage points, which is a 50% increase.
- C) Completion rose by 50 percentage points.
- D) Completion rose by 33%.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The arithmetic gap between two percentages is measured in percentage points: 45 − 30 = 15 percentage points. The relative increase is a separate calculation: 15 ÷ 30 = 0.5, which is a 50% increase. Option B labels both correctly. Option A uses "15%" loosely, which is ambiguous and usually read as a relative change. Option C misapplies the percentage-point label. Option D divides by the new value. Why this matters beyond the test: "completion rose 50%" and "completion rose 15 percentage points" describe the identical change, and the first sounds more than three times as impressive. Being precise about which you mean is part of explaining a design decision honestly, which your role names explicitly.

**Question 12 (harder) — When an average hides something**

Seven users took the following times, in minutes, to complete a step: 3, 4, 4, 5, 6, 7, and 62. A colleague reports "the average was 13 minutes". What is the best comment?

- A) The calculation is wrong; the average is 5 minutes.
- B) The calculation is right, but the mean is misleading here: six of seven users finished within seven minutes and one extreme case is pulling the figure up. Report the median of 5 minutes with the range, and describe the 62-minute case separately.
- C) The figure is fine as reported.
- D) The 62-minute case should be excluded as an anomaly.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Check the arithmetic first: the values sum to 91, and 91 ÷ 7 = 13, so the mean is correct and option A is wrong. But no user took anywhere near 13 minutes — six finished within seven minutes, and one took 62. The median, being the fourth value in the ordered list, is 5 minutes, which describes the group far better. Option B says all of this and proposes the right fix. Option C lets a misleading summary stand. Option D is the most damaging answer: excluding an inconvenient data point is a serious failing, and that 62-minute session is almost certainly the most interesting thing in the dataset, because something went badly wrong for that person and finding out what is the whole point of looking. Report the distribution, and treat the outlier as a lead rather than noise.

### Preparation tips

- **Drill four operations only.** Percentages, percentage change, averages and ratios cover nearly everything at this level.
- **Estimate before you calculate.** Three seconds of "about four fifths" catches most serious errors.
- **Find the denominator first.** Underline the group the question names. Most numeric errors are denominator errors.
- **Measure drop-off against the number who arrived.** Not the number who continued.
- **Add up the waiting, not just the doing.** A service journey's real duration is dominated by the gaps.
- **Learn the percentage point distinction properly.** Write yourself one sentence and re-read it before any assessment.
- **Practise on your own service's numbers.** Calculate the drop-off at each step of a real journey. It is more memorable than drills and it makes you useful.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Dividing by the wrong total.** Funnel questions are built around this single error.
- **Confusing percentages with percentage points.** Both labels are correct for different things; they are not interchangeable.
- **Comparing raw counts across groups of different sizes.** Convert to rates first.
- **Counting only active time in a journey.** This is how "a twenty-minute application" comes to mean a month of waiting.
- **Trusting an answer because it appears in the options.** Distractors are built from the mistakes candidates make.
- **Rounding too early.** Carry a decimal place and round at the end.
- **Reporting a mean without checking the spread.** Glance at the range first.
- **Assuming you are bad at this.** These are learnable techniques, not a talent.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately you read. That sounds too simple to be worth testing, but it turns out to be one of the strongest predictors of performance in roles that run on documents — and service design is such a role. You read service standards, accessibility guidance, policy summaries, operational procedures, letters sent to users, and the words users themselves say. Getting any of those slightly wrong changes what you design.

The classic format presents a short passage — typically three to six sentences of dense workplace prose — followed by statements you must classify as True, False or Cannot Say. True means the statement follows necessarily from the passage. False means the passage contradicts it. Cannot Say means the passage neither confirms nor contradicts it, so answering would need information you have not been given. Alongside these you will meet comprehension questions, main-point questions, and questions asking which of four summaries is most accurate.

The golden rule is that you answer only from the passage. Everything you know about accessibility regulations, everything your team does in practice, everything that seems obviously true about the world — set it aside. If the passage says something you believe is wrong, the passage still wins. This feels unnatural, and it is the single biggest source of lost marks. Candidates read a statement, recognise it as true of the world, and mark it True even though the passage never said it. That is a Cannot Say.

Cannot Say deserves particular attention because people avoid it. It feels like an admission of defeat, so candidates reach for True or False to seem decisive. In reality Cannot Say is very often the correct answer, and choosing it confidently is a mark of good reading. Whenever you find yourself supplying a link the passage does not make, you have found a Cannot Say.

Watch the small words, because they carry most of the meaning. "All" is not "most". "May" is not "must". "Should" is not "will". "Where practicable" and "unless" flag conditions that govern everything around them. Test writers build their wrong answers almost entirely from swaps of exactly these words, so a statement that reads almost identically to the passage may still be False.

For a service designer this is a section where you can do well, because the discipline it demands is one your discipline demands too. Distinguishing what a policy actually requires from what everyone assumes it requires is the same mental move — and it is frequently where the best design opportunities are hiding.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The verbal skills tested map onto your named skills:

- **Reading precisely** maps to **Designing for everyone**, where you explain the importance of making services accessible. Accessibility requirements are written documents, and meeting one means reading it as written.
- **Distinguishing statement from inference** maps to **Evidence-based design**, where you frame ideas as hypotheses to be tested. A hypothesis that quietly assumes something the evidence did not say is not testable.
- **Identifying the main point** maps to **Design communication**, where you describe methods for explaining design decisions. Finding the one thing a long document actually requires is a service to everyone.
- **Understanding qualified language** maps to **Designing strategically**, where you align to organisational goals. Strategy documents are full of "should", "may" and "where practicable", and reading them literally is how you find the room you actually have.
- **Reading for what is absent** maps to **Designing for everyone**, where noticing that a procedure never mentions people without internet access has a direct human consequence.
- **Following written direction exactly** maps to **Designing together**, where constructive feedback only works if it is understood as given.

### Practice questions

*Passage A — for Questions 1 to 4*

"All new public-facing services must meet the accessibility standard before launch. Where a service cannot meet a specific requirement, the team must publish an accessibility statement explaining the gap and the plan to close it. Services launched before this policy came into effect must meet the standard within 18 months. Internal-facing tools are not covered by this policy, although teams are encouraged to apply the same standard."

**Question 1 (easy)** — Statement: "New public-facing services must meet the accessibility standard before launch." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The first sentence states this directly, so it follows necessarily and is True. This is the most straightforward item type, and the right move is to answer it fast and confidently so you bank the time for harder items. Resist hunting for a trick that is not there — sometimes the passage simply says the thing, and candidates lose marks by over-thinking easy items just as often as by rushing hard ones.

**Question 2 (easy)** — Statement: "Internal tools must meet the accessibility standard." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The passage says internal-facing tools are not covered by the policy, and adds that teams are *encouraged* to apply the same standard. Encouragement is not a requirement, so the statement's "must" contradicts the passage and the answer is False. Note the two-part structure, which appears constantly in policy prose: an exclusion followed by a softer aspiration. Candidates who read only the second half will mark this True, and candidates who read only the first half will get it right for a slightly incomplete reason.

**Question 3 (moderate)** — Statement: "A service that cannot meet one requirement has breached the policy." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The passage anticipates exactly this situation and provides a route through it: where a service cannot meet a specific requirement, the team must publish an accessibility statement explaining the gap and the plan to close it. So failing to meet a requirement is not itself a breach — the breach would be failing to publish the statement. The statement claims a breach from the failure alone, which contradicts the passage, making it False. Well-written standards commonly work this way: they set an expectation and then define what honesty looks like when it cannot be met. Reading them as absolute produces unnecessary alarm and, worse, encourages teams to conceal gaps rather than declare them.

**Question 4 (moderate)** — Statement: "A service launched two years ago must now meet the standard." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The passage says services launched before the policy came into effect must meet the standard within 18 months. But you are not told when the policy came into effect. A service launched two years ago might have launched before the policy, in which case the 18-month clock may or may not have expired depending on the policy's start date — or it might have launched after the policy, in which case it should have met the standard at launch. Because the passage does not give you the policy date, you cannot determine the answer, so it is Cannot Say. This is a good example of a question that feels answerable until you look for the specific fact you would need.

*Passage B — for Questions 5 to 8*

"The review of the licensing service found that most applicants completed the online journey without help, but that applicants using the postal route took considerably longer and were more likely to submit incomplete applications. The review team notes that postal applicants differed from online applicants in several respects, including age and prior use of the service. The review recommends that the service team consider whether additional support for postal applicants is needed. Funding for any changes has not been agreed."

**Question 5 (easy)** — Statement: "Postal applicants were more likely to submit incomplete applications than online applicants." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The first sentence states this directly. The statement restates it without addition, so it is True. Note the passage's careful comparative framing — "more likely" rather than "usually" or "always" — and the statement respects it. If the statement had said "postal applicants usually submit incomplete applications", that would be a much stronger claim than the passage supports, and the answer would shift to Cannot Say.

**Question 6 (moderate)** — Statement: "The postal route causes applications to be incomplete." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The passage reports an association and then explicitly flags that postal and online applicants differed in other respects including age and prior use of the service. Either of those could be responsible for the difference in completeness. The passage neither asserts nor rules out that the route itself is the cause, so the answer is Cannot Say. This distinction matters far beyond the test: if the cause is the postal form's design you fix the form, and if the cause is that postal applicants are largely first-time users who need more guidance, you fix something else entirely. Treating an association as a cause is one of the most reliable ways for a team to spend three months improving the wrong thing.

**Question 7 (moderate)** — Statement: "Additional support for postal applicants will be provided." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** Two things stand between the passage and the statement. The review recommends only that the team *consider whether* support is needed, which is a recommendation to evaluate rather than to provide. And the passage adds that funding for any changes has not been agreed. Neither the decision nor the means is established, so the answer is Cannot Say. "Consider whether" is a phrase worth learning to spot, because it appears constantly in real review and audit documents and is very frequently reported afterwards as though it were a firm recommendation to act.

**Question 8 (harder)** — Statement: "Most online applicants did not need help to complete the journey." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The passage says most applicants completed the online journey without help. Completing without help is what "did not need help" describes, and "most" is carried over unchanged. The statement follows and is True. This item is slightly harder than it looks because it requires you to recognise that a rephrasing which changes the words but not the claim is still supported — "completed without help" and "did not need help to complete" express the same thing. Compare that with a statement like "online applicants found the journey easy", which would add a claim about their experience that completing without help does not establish, and would be a Cannot Say.

*Passage C — for Questions 9 to 12*

"Teams should offer users a choice of channel wherever practicable. A digital channel must not be the only option for services used predominantly by people at risk of digital exclusion. Where a service withdraws a channel, the team must assess the impact on affected users and record the assessment. Users who need assistance must be able to reach a person, by telephone or in person, during published service hours."

**Question 9 (easy)** — Statement: "A digital-only service is always prohibited." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The prohibition in the passage is specific: a digital channel must not be the only option for services used predominantly by people at risk of digital exclusion. That narrow scope implies digital-only may be acceptable elsewhere, and in any case the statement's "always" goes well beyond what the passage says. So it is False. Reading the scope of a prohibition accurately is the skill here, and it has an obvious workplace parallel: a rule that forbids something in defined circumstances is telling you about the undefined circumstances too.

**Question 10 (moderate)** — Statement: "A team that withdraws a channel without recording an impact assessment has breached the requirements." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The passage requires that where a service withdraws a channel, the team must assess the impact on affected users and record the assessment. Withdrawing without recording fails that requirement directly, so the statement follows and is True. Contrast this with Question 3 in Passage A, where the passage supplied a route for handling a failure and the breach lay in not taking that route. Here the requirement is unconditional, so failing it is straightforwardly a breach. Distinguishing between a rule with an escape route and a rule without one is one of the most useful reading habits this section builds.

**Question 11 (harder)** — Statement: "Users must be able to reach a person at any time." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The passage says users who need assistance must be able to reach a person during *published service hours*. The statement drops that qualification and replaces it with "at any time", which the passage does not support and effectively contradicts. It also drops "who need assistance", broadening the requirement further. Two qualifiers removed, so the answer is False. This is a compact demonstration of why the small words matter: a statement can be built entirely from the passage's own vocabulary and still say something the passage does not.

**Question 12 (harder) — Main point**

Which of the following best captures the main point of Passage C?

- A) Digital services are unsuitable for many users.
- B) Users should be able to access services through a channel that works for them, with digital-only ruled out where exclusion is likely, changes assessed for impact, and human help reachable during service hours.
- C) Teams must record impact assessments when withdrawing a channel.
- D) Users must be able to speak to a person.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The main point must cover the whole passage without adding anything. Option A overstates a targeted restriction into a general judgement the passage never makes. Options C and D are each true of one sentence and silent on the rest, making them details rather than main points — and D also drops the "during published service hours" qualification. Option B captures all four elements and the logic joining them: channel choice is the principle, digital exclusion is the boundary case, impact assessment protects people when things change, and human contact is the backstop. Main-point questions reward the option broad enough to cover everything and disciplined enough to invent nothing, and three of these four are true details promoted too far.

### Preparation tips

- **Answer only from the passage.** Before marking anything True, point at the words that make it true.
- **Trust Cannot Say.** It is a confident answer, not a hedge. If you are supplying a link, that is your signal.
- **Circle the qualifiers.** All, some, most, may, must, should, unless, wherever practicable, predominantly, during published hours.
- **Read the statement first, then the passage.** Knowing what you are checking makes the second read fast.
- **Look for the missing fact.** Some items are Cannot Say because one specific piece of information — a date, a threshold — was never given.
- **Separate rules with escape routes from rules without them.** The first produces False when you assume a breach; the second produces True.
- **Practise on real documents.** Read a page of the GOV.UK Service Manual and write three statements about it — one True, one False, one Cannot Say.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Answering from world knowledge.** The most frequent cause of lost marks by a distance.
- **Avoiding Cannot Say.** Reaching for True or False to look decisive converts good reading into wrong answers.
- **Reading "encouraged" as "required".** Policy prose is full of soft aspirations sitting next to hard rules.
- **Dropping a qualifier.** "During published service hours" and "who need assistance" are doing real work.
- **Treating association as causation.** A passage that names confounds is telling you not to conclude a cause.
- **Reading "consider whether X" as "do X".** This appears constantly in reviews and is constantly misreported.
- **Confusing a true detail with the main point.** Main-point items punish accurate but partial options.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment presents realistic workplace scenarios and asks what you would do. There is no passage to decode and no arithmetic to perform. Instead you are shown a dilemma of the kind your role produces, given four or five possible responses, and asked to identify the most effective and often the least effective one. Some versions ask you to rank all the options; others ask you to rate each response on a scale from counterproductive to highly effective.

This section measures judgement rather than knowledge, and judgement in a specific sense: whether your instincts align with what the profession considers good practice at your level. That last phrase is the whole calibration. The same scenario has different best answers for a trainee and for a head of profession. An associate service designer who redesigns a journey unilaterally is overstepping; one who never raises anything is under-performing. Answer as the person your role description says you are — a trainee working under supervision, showing aptitude and potential, expected to notice things and to say so.

Four principles will carry you through most scenarios here, and they generalise well beyond any single question.

The first is that users come first, and in service design that specifically means the users who are struggling most. When an option protects someone who cannot complete a journey, it will almost always outrank an option that protects a schedule, a budget or somebody's convenience. Service design exists because whole journeys fail people even when every individual part looks fine.

The second is to act within your remit and be transparent at its edges. At associate level the best answers usually involve doing what you can competently do and promptly telling the right person about what you cannot. Neither silent struggling nor reflexive escalation of everything scores well. The pattern is: attempt, then tell.

The third is to address problems directly and early, with the person involved, rather than going around them or letting things drift. Options involving an honest private conversation beat options involving public criticism, silence, or complaining to someone else.

The fourth is to look at the whole journey rather than the step in front of you. This is the distinctively service-design version of a strong answer: when an option addresses only the screen, the letter or the form, and another option asks what happens before and after it, the second is usually stronger.

One trap to name. Some options are attractive because they are pleasant — agreeing, smoothing over, being maximally helpful to whoever asked most recently. Others are attractive because they are decisive — taking charge, sorting it out yourself. Neither pleasantness nor decisiveness is the criterion. The criterion is what genuinely produces the best outcome for users, for the design and for the team, given your level of experience.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The judgement dimensions map onto your named skills:

- **Putting users first, especially excluded users** maps to **Designing for everyone**, where you explain the importance of inclusive and accessible services, describe different access needs, and describe how privilege and power influence design.
- **Explaining and defending a design position** maps to **Design communication**, where you explain the importance of articulating design decisions and describe methods for doing so.
- **Working with others' perspectives** maps to **Designing together**, where you explain techniques for bringing together multiple perspectives and the importance of constructive feedback.
- **Using evidence honestly** maps to **Evidence-based design**, where you explain how research and analytics inform design and frame ideas as hypotheses.
- **Working within a cycle** maps to **Iterative design**, where you describe agile methodologies and explain how iteration supports user-centred design.
- **Understanding where design sits** maps to **Leading design**, where you explain the role of user-centred design in an organisation and identify the leaders designers need to work with, and to **Designing strategically**.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — You do not understand the task**

Your senior designer asks you to "put together a service blueprint for the renewals journey by Thursday". You know roughly what a blueprint is but have never made one. What is the most effective response?

- A) Say yes, then search online and attempt it alone.
- B) Say yes, and add that you have not made one before and would find half an hour of guidance really useful before you start.
- C) Say you cannot do it because you have not been trained.
- D) Say yes, and quietly ask another associate to make it with you instead of telling your senior.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** Your role description says explicitly that you work under supervision and need to develop your skills, so asking for guidance is doing your job correctly rather than falling short. Option B combines willingness with honesty and makes a specific, small, easy-to-grant request — half an hour is far cheaper for your senior than reviewing a misconceived blueprint on Wednesday night. Option A risks producing something unusable and, worse, hides the gap so it never gets filled. Option C is least effective because it declines a development opportunity entirely and hands the work back without offering anything. Option D avoids the awkwardness but leaves your senior with an inaccurate picture of your skills and quietly pulls a colleague off their own work. The general principle for this whole section: at your level, "yes, and here is what I need to do it well" is almost always the strongest available answer.

**Question 2 (easy) — You notice something in a journey map**

Reviewing a journey map your team has been using for months, you notice that it shows users receiving a decision letter but says nothing about what happens if the letter does not arrive. You are the most junior person in the team. What should you do?

- A) Say nothing; the map has been used for months and someone would have noticed.
- B) Raise it at the next team session or with your senior designer, describing the gap you have spotted and asking what does happen in that case.
- C) Add the missing step to the map yourself.
- D) Note it for the retrospective in three weeks.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** You have spotted a genuine gap, and gaps like this are exactly where service failures live — a letter that does not arrive leaves a person with no decision, no notification and often no obvious way to ask. Option B raises it through the right channel, framed as a question rather than a correction, which makes it easy for colleagues to engage with and leaves room for the possibility that the process exists and simply is not mapped. Option A is least effective: "someone would have noticed" is how gaps survive for years, and being new is precisely why you can see it. Option C acts beyond your remit on a shared artefact, and you do not yet know what the real process is, so you would be inventing a step. Option D delays a five-minute conversation by three weeks for no reason.

**Question 3 (easy) — Conflicting instructions**

Your senior designer has asked you to finish the channel comparison this morning. The delivery manager then asks you to spend the morning preparing slides for tomorrow's show and tell instead, calling it urgent. What is the most effective response?

- A) Do what the delivery manager asked, because they are more senior.
- B) Do what your senior designer asked and tell the delivery manager you are busy.
- C) Tell the delivery manager about the existing task, offer a specific workable option, and ask the two of them to confirm the priority.
- D) Try to do both by working through lunch.

**Correct answer: C. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** You have two legitimate instructions and cannot serve both. The conflict is not yours to resolve alone, but surfacing it promptly and helpfully is. What lifts option C above bare escalation is the offer of a specific option — "I could do the slides this morning and the channel comparison straight after lunch, if that works" — which turns you from a bottleneck into part of the solution. Option A ranks by seniority; the delivery manager probably does not know about the other task. Option B is abrupt and leaves a real need unaddressed. Option D is least effective, because working through lunch hides the conflict, sets an unsustainable expectation, and means nobody learns that the team is over-committing you. In an agile team, making competing demands visible is a contribution rather than a complaint.

**Question 4 (easy) — A design decision you disagree with**

The team decides to remove a "save and come back later" feature to simplify a form. From the research you have read, several users completed the journey over multiple sittings because they had to find documents. You are the most junior person in the room. What should you do?

- A) Say nothing; the decision has been made.
- B) Raise the specific evidence — that several participants completed over multiple sittings because they needed to find documents — and ask how those users would manage without the feature.
- C) Raise it privately with your senior designer afterwards.
- D) Accept the decision but note your disagreement in the team channel.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** You hold evidence the room may not be weighing, and the useful contribution is the evidence itself rather than an opinion about the decision. Option B is phrased in the strongest possible way for someone at your level: it states what the research showed and asks a question rather than mounting a challenge, which makes it easy for the team to engage without anyone needing to back down. Option A is least effective, because a decision made without evidence that was available in the room is a worse decision, and your grade does not make the evidence less relevant. Option C is a reasonable backup and misses the moment when the decision is being made. Option D expresses disagreement in a way that is visible but useless — it neither informs the decision nor gives anyone anything to act on.

**Question 5 (moderate) — A user problem outside your service**

Research shows that users of your service routinely fail at a step that belongs to a different department's service, before they ever reach yours. Your team says it is out of scope. What is the most effective response?

- A) Accept it; the team cannot fix another department's service.
- B) Document the problem and its effect on your users clearly, share it with your senior designer, and ask whether there is a route to raise it with the other department — while considering whether your own service can reduce the harm, for example by explaining the earlier step better.
- C) Contact the other department yourself.
- D) Add a note about it in the research findings and move on.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** This is the characteristic service design situation, and the characteristic service design answer is that the user's journey does not respect organisational boundaries even though budgets do. Option B does three useful things: it makes the problem visible and evidenced, it looks for a legitimate route to the other department through someone with the standing to use it, and — most practically — it asks what your own service can do to reduce the harm in the meantime, which is often more than a team assumes. Option A is least effective, because "out of scope" describes a budget, not a user's experience. Option C acts beyond your remit and risks cutting across relationships you cannot see. Option D records the problem without doing anything about it. Your role description talks about work across both digital and offline channels provided by different parts of government, and this is precisely what that means in practice.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Pressure to simplify the story**

You have made a journey map showing that a service that appears to take twenty minutes actually takes about five weeks because of two waiting periods. A stakeholder asks you to remove the waiting periods from the map because "they are not part of the design". What should you do?

- A) Remove them; the stakeholder knows what the map is for.
- B) Explain that the waiting periods are the largest part of the user's experience and that removing them would make the map describe something users do not recognise — and offer to show them differently, for example greyed out, so the design steps remain clear.
- C) Refuse and send the map unchanged.
- D) Make two versions, one with and one without.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** The stakeholder has a legitimate underlying concern — they want the design work to be legible and not lost in a sea of grey — and the wrong solution. Option B holds the substance while addressing that concern directly, and the offer of a visual treatment is what turns a disagreement into a design problem. It also makes the key point in one sentence: a map that omits five weeks of waiting is not a map of the user's experience. Option A is least effective, because once the waiting is invisible nobody designs for it, and waiting is exactly where users give up, phone the contact centre, or assume they have been forgotten. Option C protects the map and throws away the relationship and the chance to explain. Option D is the worst kind of compromise — two versions of the truth means the convenient one gets used, and you have quietly agreed that the inconvenient one is optional.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Something you designed is not working**

A form section you drafted has gone live, and caseworkers report that users are consistently entering the wrong information in it. What is the most effective response?

- A) Wait for the next research round to confirm the problem.
- B) Thank the caseworkers, ask for specific examples of what users are entering, look at whether the wording invites that misunderstanding, and take a proposed fix to your senior designer quickly.
- C) Explain to the caseworkers that the section was based on research.
- D) Ask for the section to be reverted to the previous version.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** Caseworkers seeing the same wrong answer repeatedly is high-quality evidence arriving free, and the first move is to treat it that way. Option B gathers the specifics, because the actual wrong answers usually reveal the misunderstanding immediately — if everyone enters a date where you wanted a duration, the label is the problem. It then diagnoses and proposes rather than simply reporting. Option C is least effective: defending a design against evidence that it is not working is the fastest way to lose both the design and the caseworkers' willingness to tell you anything next time. Option A wastes weeks when the problem is visible now. Option D reverts before understanding, which may reintroduce a different problem the section was solving. Being wrong about a design is entirely normal and is not a reflection on you; not noticing, or arguing with the people who noticed, would be.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Feedback that stings**

In a design critique, a senior designer says your journey map is "confusing and does not show the offline channel at all". You feel it does show the offline channel. What is the most effective response?

- A) Explain why they are wrong and point to where the offline channel appears.
- B) Ask them to show you where they got lost, since if an experienced designer cannot find the offline channel, the map is not communicating it clearly enough — then fix that.
- C) Accept the criticism silently and redo the map.
- D) Ask for the feedback in writing so you can consider it later.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Both things can be true: the offline channel is on the map, and the map does not communicate it. Option B recognises that and turns the feedback into information — asking where someone got lost is far more useful than establishing who was right, and it converts an uncomfortable moment into a specific fix. It is also the response that will make senior colleagues want to review your work again. Option A is least effective because winning the argument leaves the map exactly as confusing as it was, and it teaches the room that giving you feedback is effortful. Option C accepts the verdict without extracting the useful detail, which means you may redo the map and reproduce the same problem. Option D is not unreasonable and delays a conversation that would take two minutes now. Your role names the importance of constructive feedback in the design process, and receiving it well is at least half of that skill.

**Question 9 (harder) — An accessibility problem discovered late**

Two weeks before launch you realise a key step in the journey cannot be completed by someone using a screen reader. Fixing it will delay the launch. What is the most effective response?

- A) Launch and add it to the backlog.
- B) Raise it immediately with your senior designer and the delivery manager, describing precisely what cannot be done and by whom, and ask for a decision — while offering any interim option that would let affected users complete the journey another way.
- C) Try to fix it yourself in the two weeks available.
- D) Launch and publish an accessibility statement noting the gap.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A step that cannot be completed with a screen reader means some people simply cannot use the service, and that is a decision for people with the authority to weigh a delay against an exclusion — which is not you, but raising it is entirely yours. Option B does everything within your remit: it surfaces the problem now while there is still time, it describes the impact precisely rather than vaguely, and it looks for an interim route so that the choice is not simply launch-or-delay. Option A is least effective because "add it to the backlog" is where accessibility problems go to remain unfixed, and in the meantime real people cannot use a public service. Option D looks more responsible and is often not: an accessibility statement is for gaps you are genuinely working to close, not a mechanism for launching with a known blocker. Option C is well-intentioned and risky — you may not have the skills, and quietly attempting it means nobody with authority knows there is a problem.

**Question 10 (harder) — Being asked to design around a constraint you doubt**

A policy colleague says the service must ask users for their previous three addresses because "the policy requires it". Research shows this question causes many users to abandon the journey. What is the most effective response?

- A) Design the question as well as possible and accept the requirement.
- B) Share the evidence about abandonment, ask specifically what the requirement is for and where it comes from, and explore with them whether the underlying need could be met differently — while being ready to accept the constraint if it is genuinely fixed.
- C) Challenge the policy requirement directly and ask for it to be removed.
- D) Escalate to your senior designer without speaking to the policy colleague.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** "The policy requires it" is sometimes exactly true, sometimes a summary of a requirement that could be met another way, and sometimes an inherited practice nobody has revisited — and you cannot tell which without asking. Option B asks the productive question, which is not "must we?" but "what is this for?", because knowing that the underlying need is identity verification or fraud prevention opens up alternatives that arguing about the question itself never will. Bringing the abandonment evidence makes the cost concrete. The closing clause matters too: some constraints really are statutory, and a designer who cannot accept one loses credibility for the ones that are negotiable. Option C is least effective at your level: challenging a policy requirement head-on, as a trainee, without understanding its basis, will not succeed and will make the next conversation harder. Option A gives up before asking. Option D bypasses a colleague who has not yet had the chance to explain.

**Question 11 (harder) — A mistake of your own**

You realise that a journey map you produced, which the team has been using for a fortnight to plan work, shows the wrong order for two steps. What should you do?

- A) Correct it quietly and say nothing.
- B) Tell the team straight away, explain exactly which steps were wrong and what the correct order is, and flag any decisions made in the last fortnight that may have relied on the error.
- C) Correct it and mention it at the next retrospective.
- D) Wait to see whether anyone noticed.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** A shared artefact has been wrong for two weeks and people have planned work against it, so the correction alone is not enough — the important part is the last clause of option B, flagging decisions that may have relied on the error. That is what turns an admission into something useful, and it is the step people most often skip because it is the most uncomfortable. Option D is least effective: waiting to see whether anyone noticed means the error persists, and if it is discovered later the question becomes why you did not say so. Option A corrects the artefact and leaves the downstream consequences unexamined. Option C delays disclosure to a scheduled meeting when the information is needed now. Everyone makes mistakes like this; what distinguishes people is what they do in the following ten minutes, and being straightforward about your own errors is one of the fastest ways to earn trust early in a career.

**Question 12 (harder) — Designing for people the team does not think about**

Your team is designing a service used mainly by working-age adults. In a session, someone says "we don't need to worry about people without internet access — this is a digital service". You have read that a significant minority of the service's users currently apply by post. What is the most effective response?

- A) Let it go; the team has decided the scope.
- B) Bring the specific fact into the room — that a significant minority currently apply by post — and ask what the team thinks should happen to those users, so the decision is made knowingly rather than by assumption.
- C) Argue that all services must be accessible to everyone.
- D) Raise it privately with your senior designer afterwards.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** The comment reveals an assumption, and assumptions dissolve much more readily against a specific fact than against a principle. Option B supplies the fact and then asks a question, which is both the most effective form of challenge available to a junior person and genuinely the right thing to do: the team may have a perfectly good answer, or may realise it does not. Framing it as "what should happen to those users?" also keeps the conversation about the design rather than about who is right. Option A is least effective, because a design decision made on an assumption that is factually wrong will exclude real people, and you had the fact. Option C states a true principle in a way that invites a debate about principles, which rarely changes a room. Option D is a reasonable backup and misses the moment. Your role names describing how privilege and power influence the design and delivery of services, and this is the everyday version of that: whose needs get assumed away when nobody in the room shares them.

### Preparation tips

- **Answer as a trainee, not as a hero or a bystander.** The best options combine competent action with prompt, honest communication upwards.
- **Put the struggling user first.** When an option protects somebody who cannot complete the journey, start by assuming it is strongest.
- **Zoom out.** If one option addresses the step and another asks what happens before and after it, the second is usually stronger.
- **Bring the fact, not the principle.** A specific number or finding changes a room; a general principle invites a debate.
- **Ask what a requirement is for.** "What is this question for?" opens doors that "must we ask it?" never will.
- **Look for the option that does two things.** Fixing today's problem and improving tomorrow's process is a signature of a strong answer.
- **Read the least-effective question separately.** The worst option is usually the one that conceals something or lets harm continue, not simply the opposite of the best.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Ranking by seniority.** Who is asking matters far less than the consequences.
- **Silence out of newness.** "Someone would have noticed" is how gaps survive for years.
- **Defending a design against evidence.** It loses the design and the goodwill of whoever told you.
- **Accepting "out of scope" as an answer about a user's experience.** Budgets have boundaries; journeys do not.
- **Making two versions of the truth.** The convenient one gets used.
- **Treating an accessibility statement as a way to launch with a blocker.** It is for gaps you are genuinely closing.
- **Winning the argument about feedback.** If an experienced designer got lost, the artefact is unclear regardless of who is right.
- **Delaying disclosure of your own error.** Speed is what turns a mistake into evidence of integrity.

## Conclusion

You have reached the end, and that is worth a moment. You have just worked through forty-eight practice questions across four assessment types and read the reasoning behind every one. That is a substantial piece of preparation and considerably more than most people do.

Look back at what you covered. In the cognitive section you checked journey maps and service pages against the standards they should meet, worked through rules with exceptions, counted participants rather than mentions, prioritised a morning where a live error outranked your own deadlines, and traced how a phone-only upload would exclude exactly the people a service was built for. In the numeric section you handled completion rates, drop-off denominators, ratios, percentage change and funnel data — and, most usefully for a service designer, you learned to add up the waiting rather than only the doing, which is how a "twenty-minute application" turns out to take a month. In the verbal section you practised reading exactly what is written: separating a rule with an escape route from one without, refusing to read "encouraged" as "required", and catching a qualifier being quietly dropped. And in the situational judgement section you thought through raising a gap as the most junior person in the room, holding the waiting periods on a map under pressure, hearing hard feedback usefully, and finding a specific fact that dissolved an assumption nobody had examined.

Notice how little of that was really about tests. Every one of those is a working skill. A designer who counts properly, reads the standard as written, holds the whole journey in view, brings the fact rather than the principle, and says so within ten minutes of finding their own mistake — that is simply a good designer. Preparing for these assessments and getting better at the job turn out to be very nearly the same activity, which is the most encouraging thing about the whole exercise.

A word about where you are. You are an associate service designer: a trainee, working under supervision, expected to show aptitude, potential and an understanding of the role. Please read that as the invitation it is. Nobody expects the judgement of someone with a decade behind them. What they are looking for is care, curiosity, honesty about what you do not yet know, and steady improvement — all of which are choices available to you today. And remember the advantage you currently have: you have not yet learned to work around the confusing parts of the services you look at, which means you can still see them. That is worth protecting.

If you want to keep going from here, a few things will help more than rereading. Walk a real service end to end as a user would, including the offline parts, and time it honestly. Ask to see the letters your service sends, because they are frequently the part of a journey nobody has designed. Sit with a caseworker or a contact centre adviser for an hour and listen to what people ring up about. Ask your senior designer how they would have handled the trickiest scenario in the last section, and expect their answer to differ in places from this guide, because real judgement is contextual and hearing the reasoning is how you build your own. And practise saying findings out loud at the right strength — "six of nine participants", not "most users" — until the accurate version is the one that comes naturally.

Finally, for the day itself. An assessment is a snapshot taken on one particular morning. It is not a verdict on your ability, your potential or your future in this profession. The designers whose work you admire have all had assessments that went badly and days when the right answer arrived ten minutes too late. What actually determines how far you go is the quality of your attention to the people who use these services and your willingness to keep learning — and someone who has read this far clearly has plenty of both.

Good luck. Go carefully, ask well, and keep zooming out.
