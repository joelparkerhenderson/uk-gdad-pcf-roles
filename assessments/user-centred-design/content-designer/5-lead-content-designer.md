# Lead Content Designer - Psychometric Assessment Resources

## Introduction

Welcome. This guide is written for you as a lead content designer in the UK Government Digital and Data profession — an expert practitioner and leader who directs a team of content designers, assures content quality across whole teams, and makes sure content aligns to strategy and objectives while meeting the needs of users. Your role description adds that you work closely with service managers, programme directors and senior stakeholders to resource teams, resolve problems and develop future projects, and that you promote the content design discipline, engage with the cross-government community and keep up to date with industry changes.

That is a role in which most of what you achieve happens through other people. You may be facing a formal assessment as part of a selection process, or you may want to re-examine reasoning that has become fluent enough to be worth checking. Both are good reasons, and the second is the more interesting: at your level the risk is not that you lack technique but that technique has become automatic, automatic reasoning feels certain, and few people around you are positioned to catch you.

Three features of the role shape everything that follows. First, you assure content you did not write, frequently on services you do not work on, judged from the output rather than from the thinking behind it — which makes diagnostic reasoning at a distance a core competence. Second, you are accountable for alignment as well as quality: content that is well written and pulls against the organisation's objectives is a failure of your role rather than the writer's. Third, you resource teams and resolve problems with senior stakeholders, which means a substantial part of your work is negotiation conducted from a position of informal authority, resting on your evidence being unassailable.

There is a discipline-specific point worth naming. Content quality is unusually hard to argue about, because everybody reads and most people believe they can write. That makes evidence disproportionately valuable to you: a content designer who can say "two in five calls to this service ask when something will happen" wins arguments that a content designer with a strong opinion about clarity does not. A good deal of this guide is about that.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the reasoning your role demands, presented through practical materials rather than abstract puzzles. At lead level the materials are organisational and the reasoning is diagnostic. You will meet content produced by other people, standards applied unevenly, evidence gathered for other purposes, and prioritisation problems whose units are teams and services rather than pages.

The format is typically an online test of fifteen to thirty minutes covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective against a norm group of comparable seniority. Adaptive platforms raise difficulty after correct answers, and acuity reports distinguish speed from accuracy, both rewarding precision.

Three things characterise this section at your level.

The first is reasoning at a distance. You rarely see the work; you see a page, a report, a claim in a board paper, and must judge from visible traces whether the thinking behind it holds.

The second is that the interesting questions concern mechanisms. Bad content is nearly always produced by something: a policy that cannot be summarised because it is not settled, a sign-off process that takes so long teams route around it, a template that omits the field everyone forgets, a stakeholder relationship in which nobody feels able to say no. Fixing the page leaves the mechanism intact, and the page comes back.

The third is that prioritisation is genuinely hard because nothing dominates. The discriminating factor is whether you reason from what your input can still change rather than from importance or visibility.

You need to memorise nothing. What you need is the discipline not to let fluency substitute for examination.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The cognitive dimensions map onto the skills named in your role summary:

- **Cross-service synthesis** maps to **Strategic thinking (content design)**, where making sure content aligns to strategy requires seeing across the content estate rather than page by page.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Auditing against a standard**

Your standard requires every transactional page to: use words users would use, say what happens next, state how long it will take, avoid requesting held information, offer a route to help, and name the team responsible. A page uses internal terminology, says the case will be assessed, gives no timescale, re-requests a held reference, has a "contact us" link in the footer, and names no responsible team. How many requirements are unmet?

- A) 3
- B) 4
- C) 5
- D) 6

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Work through all six. Internal terminology fails the first. "The case will be assessed" satisfies the second, minimally. No timescale fails the third. Re-requesting held information fails the fourth. The footer link satisfies the fifth, thinly. No named team fails the sixth. Four unmet, giving B. Option C typically comes from rejecting the footer link, which is a defensible content judgement and the wrong audit judgement — the discipline is to apply the standard as written. That distinction matters particularly at your level, because it tells you which problems are compliance problems and which are standard problems, and the second kind is yours to fix. If a footer link satisfies your standard for offering help, the standard needs a sentence about proximity, and changing it once is worth more than arguing the point on twenty pages.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction from a governance rule**

Your standard states: "Content may be published without second review only if it is a factual correction approved by the content lead." A team reports publishing without second review. Which must be true?

- A) The standard was breached.
- B) It was an approved factual correction.
- C) Either it was an approved factual correction, or the standard was breached.
- D) The content should be withdrawn.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The rule is phrased as "only if", which makes the approved factual correction the sole route to publishing without review — so the disjunction has exactly two branches with no third possibility. Options A and B each assume a branch. Option D prescribes an action rather than stating what must be true, and the question asks the latter; at this level a sensible action is frequently offered as a distractor in a logic item, and it may well be what you would do. Only C holds. Recognising the shape of "only if" as distinct from "unless" is worth a moment, because both appear in governance prose you write as well as read, and "only if" is the stronger construction when you want a single defined exception rather than an open-ended one.

**Question 3 (moderate) — Reading the thinking behind a page**

A page you are assuring is well written, follows the style guide, and answers its heading question clearly. It also generates the highest contact volume in the service. What is the most important observation?

- A) The page must have a defect the style guide does not cover.
- B) A page that reads well and still generates calls is usually answering a question users are not asking, arriving after an expectation has been set elsewhere, or correctly explaining a rule too conditional to be explained in prose — so the diagnostic step is finding out what callers actually say, which the contact centre already knows.
- C) The contact centre needs a better script.
- D) The page should be rewritten more simply.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The informative fact is the combination: well written, compliant, clear, and still generating calls. That combination rules out the obvious explanations and points outside the page. Option B names the realistic candidates and identifies the cheapest way to distinguish them. Each implies a completely different intervention: if callers ask whether their specific situation qualifies, the rule is too conditional for prose and needs a different content form; if they arrive having read a letter saying something else, the fix is upstream; if they are asking something the page never addresses, the heading is answering the wrong question. Option A assumes a defect before checking. Option C treats the symptom, and at your level authorising a script is the intervention that permanently obscures the content problem. Option D proposes rewriting a page that already works, which is where a great deal of content effort is wasted.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Strategic prioritisation**

You can direct substantial content capacity to one of four things this quarter:

1. A new service's core journey content is being written next month and will set patterns reused across six related services.
2. A live page has generated steady contact volume for a year.
3. A programme wants exploratory content work for next year.
4. A team wants to revisit a page written six weeks ago after minor design changes.

Which is strongest?

- A) Item 2, because it affects users now.
- B) Item 1, because the decisions are imminent, will be reused across six services, and content input is the only thing that could still shape them — and an established pattern is far harder to change than to set.
- C) Item 3, because early work has the highest leverage.
- D) Item 4, because unverified changes are a risk.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The right frame is what your input can still change and whether there is time, and item 1 carries a multiplier the others lack. Patterns set once and reused across six services embed either good decisions or bad ones six times, and undoing an established pattern later means coordinating six teams and overcoming the fact that people defend what they have adopted. Item 2 matters and has been stable for a year, so deferring costs comparatively little, though it should be next. Item 3 has no deadline pressure. Item 4 is weakest. Option A ranks by current impact and misses the compounding, which is precisely the strategic thinking your role names — and at your level the ability to explain that trade-off to a programme director who is asking about item 2 is the harder half of the skill.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Cross-service synthesis**

Three items reach you in a month. A team reports users struggling to understand a benefit rule. A second service's site search shows the same rule's key phrase as its top term. The contact centre reports queries about the rule across four services. What is the strongest synthesis?

- A) Three teams have found content problems.
- B) The organisation has one rule that cannot be understood as currently expressed, appearing across at least four services — so it is a single content problem to be solved once, most likely with an agreed form of words, rather than four teams each writing their own explanation.
- C) The rule should be simplified.
- D) Each team should improve its own page.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The synthesis that adds value identifies one problem behind several symptoms and reframes the unit of work. Four services struggling with the same rule is not four content problems; it is one, and solving it once produces an explanation that is consistent, properly tested and maintained in a single place. Option B says so. Option D is the default in most organisations and produces four different explanations, at least one of which will be wrong, and all of which diverge further over time — the exact situation that generates the inconsistency audits find later. Option A describes without synthesising. Option C is true and instructs nobody, and in any case simplifying the rule is a policy matter while explaining it clearly is yours. Note that this is precisely the material your role's cross-government engagement is for: a rule appearing in four of your services almost certainly appears in other departments too.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Feedback on a colleague's reasoning**

A content designer proposes removing a warning paragraph, arguing that eight of ten participants said it was "off-putting". What is the most useful feedback?

- A) Approve it; eight of ten is a clear signal.
- B) Note that "off-putting" is a reaction to tone rather than evidence that the information is unnecessary, and that the useful question is whether users who did not read it went on to make the mistake the warning exists to prevent — so the finding supports rewriting the paragraph, not removing it, unless separate evidence shows the risk it describes is not real.
- C) Reject the proposal.
- D) Suggest testing with more participants.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The evidence is real and supports a different conclusion from the one drawn. Participants disliking a paragraph tells you about its tone; it tells you nothing about whether the information in it prevents harm, and those are separable. Option B makes that separation and names what would settle it, which is the outcome for users who skip the warning rather than their feelings about it. This distinction matters enormously in content design, because "users found it off-putting" is one of the most common justifications for deleting exactly the content that protects people — and the deletion tests well, because a page without a warning is more pleasant to read. Option A approves a removal on evidence that does not support it. Option C rejects without redirecting. Option D adds participants to a study asking the wrong question.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Diagnosing a systemic problem**

Five teams consistently publish content that omits timescales. Each has a plausible local reason — the timescale is uncertain, policy would not confirm it, the template has no field for it. What is the strongest diagnosis and response?

- A) Remind all teams that timescales are required.
- B) Five teams doing the same thing for plausible local reasons is one systemic cause with five rationalisations; establish which it is — the template genuinely lacks a field, or teams cannot get a timescale confirmed, or the standard does not say what to publish when a timescale is uncertain — and fix that, since the remedies are entirely different.
- C) Add timescales to the content checklist.
- D) Accept it; some timescales genuinely cannot be given.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Five teams behaving identically for individually plausible reasons is the signature of a systemic cause, and the plausibility of each local reason is what makes case-by-case challenge exhausting and ineffective. Option B names the realistic candidates, and they imply entirely different remedies: adding a template field is a half-hour job, getting policy to confirm timescales is a negotiation you should be having on all five teams' behalf, and the third — that the standard is silent on what to publish when a timescale is genuinely uncertain — is the most likely and the most interesting, because the answer is not silence but a form of words such as "we usually reply within X, and will tell you if it will take longer". Option A is the weakest available response to any compliance problem. Option C adds a checklist item to a system where five teams already know the requirement. Option D accepts the rationalisation without testing it.

**Question 8 (harder) — Scoping a flaw**

A study you are assuring recruited entirely through the service's online panel. Four findings: (i) participants did not understand "relevant supporting evidence"; (ii) participants found the journey easy to navigate; (iii) participants completed in under ten minutes; (iv) participants did not notice a link in the footer. Which are most compromised?

- A) All four.
- B) None; the sample is adequate.
- C) Findings (ii) and (iii) are most compromised, because a panel recruited through the online service consists of people already comfortable with it, which flatters navigation and speed — while (i) and (iv) concern comprehension of a phrase and noticing an element, less dependent on prior familiarity though not wholly independent of it.
- D) Only (i).

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The skill is scoping precisely rather than treating a sampling flaw as total or negligible. A panel recruited through the online service consists of people filtered twice — for using it successfully and for volunteering afterwards — which most affects findings about ease and speed, since those are what familiarity produces. Comprehension of an unfamiliar legal phrase is less dependent on familiarity. The footer link is intermediate. Option C says all of this including the honest qualifier. Option A discards findings that survive, and at your level that does organisational damage: a lead who condemns wholesale teaches teams that admitting a sampling limitation invalidates their work, which is a direct incentive to stop declaring it — and you will then be assuring studies whose limitations are invisible. Option B leaves two flattered findings in circulation, which are more dangerous than absent ones because they generate confidence.

**Question 9 (harder) — Content that should not exist**

A page explaining how to correct a common form error is heavily used and well rated across three services using the same form component. What is the strongest analysis?

- A) The page works and should be maintained in all three services.
- B) Heavy use of a recovery page measures how often the upstream defect occurs, and the defect is in a shared form component used by three services — so the highest-value work is on the component, after which the page should shrink or disappear in all three at once.
- C) The page should be made more prominent.
- D) Each service should maintain its own version.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Two things combine here. The first is that a page existing to help people recover from a mistake is a symptom, and heavy use measures the failure rate rather than the page's quality — good ratings mean users are grateful to be rescued from a problem they should not have had. The second, which is what makes this a lead-level question, is that the defect sits in a shared component, so fixing it once removes the problem from three services simultaneously and makes three pages unnecessary. Option B identifies both. Option A maintains the symptom three times over. Option C makes recovery easier while leaving the failure rate untouched. Option D is the worst structural answer, entrenching three separately maintained pages for one shared defect — which is how organisations accumulate content nobody can rationalise later.

**Question 10 (harder) — Reading the distribution of a problem**

Your standard requires plain-language review before publication. Across the year, seventeen of twenty services complied; the three that did not were all in one directorate, all under ministerial deadline pressure, all by experienced content designers. What is the strongest response?

- A) Report the three and require remediation.
- B) Treat the concentration as diagnostic: three failures in one directorate under identical pressure by experienced people indicates a process that cannot be complied with when time is short — investigate what the review demands and how long it takes, build a fast route, and remediate the three alongside.
- C) Remind all content designers of the requirement.
- D) Introduce a mandatory pre-publication gate for every service.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The distribution is the most informative feature. Scattered breaches would indicate conduct or capability; three concentrated under identical pressure by experienced people indicate a process that does not survive a deadline. Option B investigates the mechanism and fixes it, while the remediation clause preserves the point that a systemic diagnosis is not a reason to leave individual failures unaddressed — those three services have unreviewed content live now. Option A treats the instances and guarantees recurrence. Option C is the weakest available response to any compliance failure. Option D imposes friction on seventeen compliant services to catch three, which is how organisational burden accumulates — and you will be defending that gate in two years when a programme director asks why content sign-off is slow.

**Question 11 (harder) — Alignment as well as quality**

A team has produced content that is clear, well tested and popular with users, and that describes a service in terms that conflict with how the organisation's strategy describes it. What is the strongest response?

- A) Approve it; the content works for users.
- B) Establish which description is right before deciding — if the strategy is aspirational and the content describes the service as it is, the content is correct and the strategy is out of date; if the content misdescribes what the service actually does, that is an accuracy problem regardless of testing well.
- C) Require the content to match the strategy.
- D) Escalate the discrepancy.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Your role names making sure content aligns to strategy and objectives *while* meeting the needs of users, and this question is the case where those pull apart. The resolution is not to pick a side but to establish what is actually true, because the conflict has two very different explanations. Strategies routinely describe services as they are intended to become, and content describing them as they are will conflict with that while being entirely correct — in which case the content stands and somebody should know the strategy has drifted. Alternatively the content may describe the service inaccurately in a way that happens to test well, which is a real problem: users can find a wrong description clear and reassuring. Option B distinguishes these. Option A treats user testing as settling a question of fact. Option C subordinates accuracy to a document. Option D escalates before diagnosing.

**Question 12 (harder) — A pattern across your own teams**

Reviewing a year of output, you find two teams consistently produce strong content and five consistently do not. Both strong teams have a content designer who runs a weekly session where drafts are read aloud. What is the most useful inference and action?

- A) The five weaker teams need training.
- B) Reading drafts aloud is a cheap, transferable routine that plausibly explains the difference — go and watch one session to establish what it actually does, then introduce it as a routine rather than attributing the difference to individual talent.
- C) Recruit more content designers like those two.
- D) Increase assurance scrutiny on the five weaker teams.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The pattern is an opportunity to identify a practice, not to grade people. Reading drafts aloud is one of the most reliably effective content routines in existence — it exposes sentences that cannot be said, qualifiers nobody would speak, and the exact points where a reader would need to stop and re-read — and it is free, requires no training, and transfers in a way that individual skill does not. Option B goes and watches first, which is the step almost everyone skips, and matters because the session may be doing something other than what its name suggests. Option A posits a knowledge deficit without diagnosing one. Option C misattributes a routine to individuals and is unusable as an inference from two people. Option D catches more problems and creates no capacity to prevent them. Your role names directing a team and promoting the discipline, and a naturally occurring comparison telling you what good looks like in your own organisation is the best material that work will get.

### Preparation tips

- **Read outputs for the thinking behind them.** A page that is compliant and still failing is telling you the problem is elsewhere.
- **Ask what produces bad content.** A policy, a template, a sign-off process, a relationship — rarely a writer.
- **Scope flaws precisely.** Wholesale condemnation teaches teams to stop declaring limitations.
- **Weight decisions by reuse.** Patterns set once and repeated six times are worth six times the attention.
- **Separate a reaction to tone from evidence about need.** "Off-putting" is not "unnecessary".
- **Read the distribution of failures.** Concentrated non-compliance diagnoses a process.
- **When content and strategy conflict, establish which is right.** Strategies drift.
- **Go and look before inferring a cause.** Especially when the inference flatters an assumption.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Applying a standard as you would have written it.** If a footer link satisfies it, change the standard.
- **Rewriting a page that already works.** The problem is probably upstream.
- **Deleting protective content because it tests as off-putting.**
- **Reminding teams of rules they already know.** The weakest available compliance response.
- **Adding friction for the compliant majority.**
- **Letting four teams each solve one shared problem.**
- **Treating heavy use of a recovery page as success.**
- **Treating good user testing as settling a question of accuracy.**

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A numeric reasoning assessment measures how accurately you handle quantitative material. At lead level the emphasis is almost wholly on interrogation. You will need to compute correctly, but the questions that matter ask whether a figure means what somebody claims — and in most rooms you enter, you may be the only person who will ask.

The format is typically a timed online test of fifteen to thirty minutes, each item presenting a table, chart or paragraph of figures with a multiple-choice question. Calculators are usually provided and the arithmetic is deliberately tractable. Time per item is tight, so the operative skill is locating what matters and discarding what does not.

The professional stake is unusually high for your discipline. Content work is chronically under-prioritised because its value is hard to express in numbers other people use, and content quality is unusually hard to argue about because everybody reads and most people believe they can write. Both problems have the same remedy: figures. A lead content designer who can say "two in five calls to this service ask when something will happen, which is 28,800 calls a year" wins resourcing arguments that a lead with a strong view about clarity does not. Your role names working with service managers and programme directors to resource teams, and that conversation happens in numbers.

Four habits carry the load. Establish what the question asks before looking at the data. Identify the denominator and the population it represents. Estimate, calculate, compare. And sanity-check against the world.

Distractors come from a predictable catalogue: wrong denominator, percentage confused with percentage point, new value as the base in a percentage change, rates averaged without weighting.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The numerical skills tested map onto your named skills:

- **Interrogating quantitative claims** maps to assuring content quality across whole teams.
- **Aggregating across services** maps to **Strategic thinking (content design)**, where alignment requires seeing the estate rather than the page.
- **Quantifying content value** maps to your role level's requirement to resource teams and develop future projects.
- **Comparing groups fairly** maps to **User focus (content design)**.
- **Presenting figures at defensible strength** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management (content design)**.
- **Detecting misleading metrics** maps to **User focus**, where a wrong measure sends content work to the wrong place across several teams at once.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Rate from counts**

Of 4,800 users who answered a feedback prompt, 1,200 said they did not find what they needed. What percentage?

- A) 20%
- B) 25%
- C) 30%
- D) 40%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 1,200 ÷ 4,800 = 0.25, so 25%, and the answer is B. Estimate first: 1,200 of 4,800 is a quarter. Options A and C come from dividing by 6,000 and 4,000. Worth noting what the figure is: the percentage of people who *answered the prompt*, not of everyone who saw the page. Prompts are answered disproportionately by people with a strong reaction, which makes the absolute level unreliable and comparisons between pages far more useful — a distinction worth writing into whatever reporting convention your teams use, because otherwise the absolute number gets quoted as if it were a satisfaction rate.

**Question 2 (easy) — Comparing pages fairly**

| Page | Views | Negative feedback |
|---|---|---|
| A | 40,000 | 1,200 |
| B | 8,000 | 960 |
| C | 20,000 | 600 |

Which page has the highest negative rate, and how many users does it disappoint?

- A) Page A, 3%, 1,200 users
- B) Page B, 12%, 960 users
- C) Page C, 3%, 600 users
- D) Page A, 12%, 1,200 users

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Rates are: A 1,200 ÷ 40,000 = 3%; B 960 ÷ 8,000 = 12%; C 600 ÷ 20,000 = 3%. Page B is highest at 12% and disappoints 960 users, giving B. The raw counts tell a different story, since Page A disappoints more people in total. Both readings answer different questions and a good report gives both, because they support different decisions: if you are reducing total harm you go to Page A, and if you are finding the page that is most clearly broken you go to Page B. At your level the more useful observation may be that Pages A and C sit identically at 3%, which establishes a baseline and makes Page B interpretable — without that baseline, 12% is just a number.

**Question 3 (easy) — Reading a contact log**

A month of contact centre calls: "when will I hear" 3,600, "what documents" 2,250, "how do I cancel" 600, "fee query" 600, "other" 1,950. What percentage concern timescales, and how many calls a year is that?

- A) 40%, 43,200
- B) 40%, 3,600
- C) 36%, 43,200
- D) 45%, 43,200

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Total the list: 3,600 + 2,250 + 600 + 600 + 1,950 = 9,000. Then 3,600 ÷ 9,000 = 0.4, which is 40%. Annualising: 3,600 × 12 = 43,200 calls a year. So A. Option B stops at the monthly figure, which is the commonest error in this question type because the calculation feels finished before the annualisation. The annual figure is the one that matters at your level: 43,200 calls a year is a number a programme director and a finance colleague both recognise, and it converts a missing sentence into a resourcing argument. Two in five calls asking when something will happen is a content problem with a one-sentence solution, and expressing it this way is how the sentence gets prioritised over a redesign.

**Question 4 (easy) — Centre and spread**

Word counts of seven pages in a guide: 130, 145, 150, 160, 170, 185 and 2,600. Which summary is most defensible?

- A) Mean 506 words
- B) Median 160, range 130 to 2,600, with the 2,600-word page flagged as probably several pages that have not been split
- C) Median 160
- D) Mean 506, range 130 to 2,600

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The values sum to 3,540, and 3,540 ÷ 7 = 505.7, so the mean of about 506 is correct and describes no page in the guide. The median is the fourth value, 160, which describes it well. But the median alone conceals a page sixteen times the typical length, which is the most informative item in the set. Option B supplies centre, spread and the content-specific interpretation that makes it the strongest answer: a 2,600-word page among 150-word pages is not an outlier to be noted but almost certainly a page doing several jobs, which is a structural finding rather than a statistical one. Option D pairs the range with a misleading centre. Option C buries the interesting item. The rule to write into your reporting conventions: a central figure never travels alone.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Weighted aggregation**

Four pages have negative feedback rates of 4% on 25,000 views, 10% on 5,000 views, 6% on 10,000 views, and 8% on 10,000 views. What is the overall negative rate across all 50,000 views?

- A) 5.8%
- B) 6.5%
- C) 7%
- D) 8%

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Convert to counts before combining. Page one: 4% of 25,000 = 1,000 negative responses. Page two: 10% of 5,000 = 500. Page three: 6% of 10,000 = 600. Page four: 8% of 10,000 = 800. Total 1,000 + 500 + 600 + 800 = 2,900, across 50,000 views, so 2,900 ÷ 50,000 = 0.058, which is 5.8% and the answer is A. Option C is the unweighted mean of 4, 10, 6 and 8, which is exactly 7 and over-weights the two small pages against the 25,000-view page that should dominate the result. Always return to counts when combining rates: the unweighted average answers what the average *page* does, while the weighted figure answers what the average *user* experiences, and those diverge whenever traffic is unevenly distributed — which is almost always, and the second is nearly always the figure a decision needs. At your level there is a second habit worth pairing with it: re-check the total before it leaves your hands, because an aggregation error attributed to the lead is one nobody downstream will question.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Did the change work?**

Before a rewrite, 1,600 of 4,000 users answered a prompt negatively. After, 1,200 of 4,800 did. Did the page improve, and by how much in percentage points?

- A) Yes, by 400 responses
- B) Yes, by 15 percentage points
- C) Yes, by 25 percentage points
- D) No, it got worse

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Convert to rates because the totals differ. Before: 1,600 ÷ 4,000 = 40%. After: 1,200 ÷ 4,800 = 25%. The improvement is 15 percentage points, giving B. Option A compares raw counts across different-sized groups, and note the direction here: more people answered after the change, so the raw fall of 400 understates the improvement. That is worth registering because the usual warning about raw counts assumes they flatter, and they can equally conceal a genuine win — which is the argument for converting every time rather than only when you suspect a problem. At your level this matters for how teams report improvements to you: a team reporting raw counts may be underselling good work as easily as overselling poor work.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Percentage change and its interpretation**

Views of a guidance page fell from 80,000 to 60,000 a month after a form was simplified. What is the change, and what does it most likely mean?

- A) 25% decrease, and the guidance is failing to reach people
- B) 25% decrease, which may be the intended outcome — if the form no longer produces the confusion the guidance resolved, fewer people needing it is success
- C) 33% decrease, and the guidance is failing
- D) 33% decrease, which may be success

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The difference is 20,000, and 20,000 ÷ 80,000 = 0.25, a 25% decrease, so options C and D miscalculate by dividing by the new figure. Between A and B, the interpretation decides it. A fall in views of guidance that exists to resolve confusion is exactly what you would expect if the confusion has gone, and treating page views as a success measure for that content gets the sign backwards. Option B says so. This is worth holding at your level because your teams will be asked to justify content work in traffic terms, and for a significant class of content the honest answer is that success looks like decline. Establishing that with a programme director *before* the numbers arrive is far easier than explaining it afterwards, and it is the kind of expectation-setting that protects your teams from a metric that would otherwise punish them for succeeding.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Reconstructing an implied value**

A business case claims that reducing contact volume by 30,000 calls a year is worth £180,000. What is the implied cost per call, and is it plausible?

- A) £6 per call, which is plausible for a short telephone interaction
- B) £60 per call, which would be implausibly high
- C) £0.60 per call, which would be implausibly low
- D) £600 per call

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** £180,000 ÷ 30,000 = £6 per call, giving A. Whether £6 is plausible depends on the service, and for a short telephone interaction handled by a contact centre it is a reasonable order of magnitude — typical figures cited across government sit somewhere between a few pounds and low tens of pounds depending on call length and channel. The value of doing this arithmetic is not the number but the check: reconstructing an implied unit value takes thirty seconds and immediately tells you whether a business case is grounded. If the answer had come out at £60, the case would need explaining; if at £0.60, the saving is being understated and your content work is worth more than the paper claims, which is worth knowing too.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Choosing the denominator**

A journey shows 80,000 start, 64,000 reach the evidence page, 48,000 reach the declaration, 45,600 submit. What proportion of those reaching the declaration submitted?

- A) 57%
- B) 60%
- C) 71%
- D) 95%

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** The stem names users who reached the declaration, so the denominator is 48,000 and the part is 45,600: 45,600 ÷ 48,000 = 0.95, giving D. Option A uses 80,000, answering a whole-journey question nobody asked. This is the most common trap in funnel data and it determines where content effort goes: 95% of people reaching the declaration submit, so the declaration is not the problem. The losses are 16,000 between the start and the evidence page and 16,000 between evidence and declaration, which is where the content work belongs — and being able to establish that in fifteen seconds is what lets you redirect a team's plan in the meeting rather than after they have spent a sprint on the wrong page.

**Question 10 (harder) — A metric that hides a split**

Average time on a guidance page is 45 seconds. Sessions show participants either glancing for a few seconds or reading carefully for two to three minutes. What is the most useful response?

- A) The average is wrong.
- B) The average is probably correct and uninformative: it sits between two clusters and describes neither, so ask for the distribution — and note that a single edit may serve one group at the other's expense, which is the argument for structure rather than length.
- C) The participants were unrepresentative.
- D) The page should be shorter.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** When behaviour splits into two clusters the mean lands in the gap and describes nobody. Forty-five seconds is consistent with half the users spending five seconds and half two and a half minutes, and those are different problems: glancers may be skipping content they need, readers may be struggling with it. Option B asks for the distribution and names the consequence that matters for content work — shortening the page helps the glancers and removes what the readers needed, while adding a summary at the top serves both, so the answer is structural. Option D is the change that looks obvious and may be exactly wrong, and at your level it is worth noticing how often "make it shorter" is the instruction a team receives from a stakeholder who has seen only the average. Option A disputes a correct figure. Option C blames the research.

**Question 11 (harder) — Percentage points and relative change**

A page's usefulness score rose from 50% to 62% after a rewrite. Which statement is accurate?

- A) A 12% increase.
- B) A 12 percentage point increase, equivalent to a 24% relative increase.
- C) A 24 percentage point increase.
- D) A 19.4% increase.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The arithmetic gap is 62 − 50 = 12 percentage points. The relative increase is 12 ÷ 50 = 0.24, or 24%. Option B labels both. Option A is ambiguous and generally read as relative. Option C misapplies the percentage-point label. Option D divides by the new value. The stake is unusually direct for content designers, because your teams write the sentences these numbers appear in, which means your reporting convention determines whether the organisation's content reporting is honest. Requiring both figures as a standing convention costs nothing, protects your teams from the accusation of choosing the flattering framing, and — the practical benefit — means nobody later discovers that the impressive number was selected, which would discount everything else in the document.

**Question 12 (harder) — Sampling adequacy**

A team proposes measuring whether a rewrite improved completion by running 25 moderated sessions before and 25 after. Baseline is 60%; a 5 percentage point improvement is meaningful. What is the strongest response?

- A) Agree; 50 sessions is substantial.
- B) Explain that a 5 point difference on 25 per group is one or two participants and well inside ordinary variation, so the comparison cannot detect it — recommend measuring the rate with live analytics across all users and spending the 50 sessions on mechanisms, which moderated research does uniquely well.
- C) Increase to 40 per group.
- D) Run it and report the difference with caveats.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Twenty-five per group cannot distinguish a five point difference from chance; reliable detection needs hundreds per group. Option B says so and redirects rather than merely objecting: analytics measures the rate across the whole user base at no recruitment cost, while sessions are the only instrument that explains why. Option A endorses a design that cannot answer its own question. Option C moves towards adequacy without approaching it. Option D is the most damaging at your level, because a reported difference from an underpowered comparison invites action on noise and — worse for content specifically — establishes a precedent that content effectiveness can be measured on twenty-five people, which will be used against your teams the next time a rewrite is judged on a handful of sessions and found not to have "worked".

### Preparation tips

- **Find the denominator and the population first.**
- **Calculate the total when given a list, then annualise.** A monthly figure understates a resourcing case twelvefold.
- **Return to counts before combining rates, and recompute when nothing matches cleanly.**
- **Reconstruct implied unit values.** Thirty seconds tells you whether a business case is grounded.
- **Express content value in currencies other people use.** Calls avoided, pounds, hours.
- **Ask whether a decline is a failure.** Set that expectation before the numbers arrive.
- **Ask for the distribution.** A mean between two clusters invites the wrong edit.
- **Write both framings into your reporting convention.** It protects your teams as much as the reader.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Dividing by the wrong total.**
- **Stopping at a monthly figure.**
- **Averaging page-level rates without weighting.**
- **Selecting an option because it is close.** Recompute.
- **Treating page views as a success measure for problem-solving content.**
- **Letting an absolute feedback rate be quoted as a satisfaction rate.**
- **Endorsing an underpowered comparison.** It sets a precedent used against content work.
- **Accepting "make it shorter" from someone who has seen only the average.**

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately you read. For most disciplines it is a proxy skill; for yours it is close to the job, which makes this the section where you should score best and where a settled habit costs most.

The classic format presents a short passage of dense workplace prose, typically three to six sentences, followed by statements to classify as True, False or Cannot Say. True means the statement follows necessarily. False means the passage contradicts it. Cannot Say means the passage neither establishes nor excludes it. Alongside these come comprehension items, main-point items, and questions asking which of four summaries is most accurate.

The governing rule is that you answer from the passage alone, and here is the part specific to your level: expertise actively costs marks. The more policy and governance prose you have translated, the more readily you supply the clause you expect and feel entirely confident doing it. Candidates at your level underperform on verbal reasoning at rates that surprise them, and this is nearly always the mechanism. The counter-habit must be mechanical: before marking anything True, put your finger on the words.

Cannot Say is the answer experienced practitioners avoid most. Forming a view under incomplete information is what your job consists of, and here it is a liability.

Small words carry the load. "All" is not "most". "May" is not "must". "Should" is not "will". "Consider whether" is not "recommend". "Unless", "provided that" and "solely" govern everything around them.

The payoff is direct. Your teams are handed policy prose and asked what it requires so they can write the plain-language version, and you assure the result. Getting it wrong in either direction is serious: understate a requirement and users miss something that matters; overstate it and the organisation has published an obligation government does not impose. At your level you are the person who catches both.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The verbal skills tested map onto your named skills:

- **Reading precisely** maps to assuring content quality across whole teams.
- **Separating statement from inference** maps to **User focus (content design)**.
- **Identifying the main point** maps to the core task your teams perform and you assure — turning dense source material into content that says what it means and no more.
- **Handling qualified language** maps to **Strategic thinking (content design)**, where knowing what is required and what is recommended tells you where the organisation has room.
- **Reading for absence** maps to **User focus**, where noticing a source never states a timescale is what stops a page being published without one.
- **Reading stakeholder and governance prose accurately** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management (content design)**.

### Practice questions

*Passage A — for Questions 1 to 4*

"All content published on the service must be reviewed against the style guide before publication, unless an exemption has been agreed in writing by the head of content design. Where content is a factual correction of published material, it may be published immediately and reviewed within five working days. Content including legal wording supplied by the legal team must not be altered without the legal team's agreement, although surrounding explanatory content may be edited freely. All published content is reviewed annually."

**Question 1 (easy)** — Statement: "Every piece of content must be reviewed against the style guide before publication." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The passage attaches two routes around the general rule: a written exemption from the head of content design, and immediate publication of factual corrections. The statement's "every" contradicts both, so False. Note that this passage carries two distinct mechanisms that are easy to conflate — an exemption, which removes the requirement, and the factual-correction route, which reverses the order while keeping the review obligation. They do different things, and Question 4 turns on the distinction.

**Question 2 (easy)** — Statement: "Legal wording may never be altered." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The passage prohibits altering legal wording *without the legal team's agreement*, which means alteration with their agreement is permitted. The statement's "never" drops the condition and asserts an absolute prohibition the passage does not impose, so False. This has real consequences for how you brief your teams: content designers who believe legal wording is untouchable never ask, and legal colleagues are frequently more willing to agree a clearer formulation than anyone assumes — particularly when you bring evidence that the current wording is not understood, since an incomprehensible warning serves their purposes no better than yours.

**Question 3 (moderate)** — Statement: "A factual correction is exempt from style guide review." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The passage says a factual correction may be published immediately *and reviewed within five working days*. The review obligation is not removed; it is deferred. The statement asserts an exemption, which is a different thing, so it is False. This is the distinction flagged in Question 1, and it matters practically: a team believing corrections are exempt will publish them and never review them, and after a year the service contains a layer of content that has never been through the style guide at all — which is exactly the kind of drift an annual review is too coarse to catch.

**Question 4 (harder)** — Statement: "A page containing legal wording is reviewed annually." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The final sentence states that all published content is reviewed annually, without exception. A page containing legal wording is published content, so it falls within that requirement, and the statement follows. True. The item tests whether you notice that the annual review sentence carries no carve-out for legal content — a reader who has just absorbed the strong constraint on *altering* legal wording may assume such pages are treated differently throughout, which is a reasonable expectation and not what the passage says. Reviewing is not altering, and the passage keeps them separate. In practice this is worth knowing, because a review that finds legal wording nobody understands is the evidence that starts the conversation Question 2 makes possible.

*Passage B — for Questions 5 to 8*

"The review of content practice across the department found considerable variation in how teams assure quality. Four teams operate peer review before publication; the remainder rely on individual sign-off. Teams with peer review reported fewer post-publication corrections, although the review did not examine whether their content was more accurate. The review recommends that the head of content design consider whether a common assurance approach would be proportionate."

**Question 5 (easy)** — Statement: "Some teams do not operate peer review before publication." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The passage states that four teams operate peer review and the remainder rely on individual sign-off, establishing directly that some do not. True. Answer quickly and bank the time. The only conceivable ambiguity — whether "the remainder" might be empty — is closed by the passage describing what the remainder does.

**Question 6 (moderate)** — Statement: "Peer review produces more accurate content." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The passage reports fewer post-publication corrections and then states explicitly that accuracy was not examined. Those are different things, and the gap is instructive: peer-reviewed teams might produce more accurate content, or might catch errors before publication rather than after — which produces the same statistic and is a genuine benefit — or might simply be less likely to have errors reported, if they are also the teams with better relationships with the people who would report them. The passage flags its own silence, so Cannot Say. At your level this is exactly the substitution to guard against, because "peer review improves accuracy" is a claim you would like to be true and might well cite when arguing for a common approach.

**Question 7 (moderate)** — Statement: "The review recommends introducing a common assurance approach." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The review recommends that the head of content design *consider whether* a common approach would be proportionate, which includes concluding it would not be. The statement asserts a recommendation to introduce, which was not made, so False rather than Cannot Say: the passage tells you what was recommended and it was something different. Content designers are unusually likely to be the person writing the record of what a review said, which makes reading this precisely a professional obligation rather than a test technique — and at your level you are likely to be the person the recommendation is addressed to, which means misreading it commits you to something nobody asked for.

**Question 8 (harder)** — Statement: "Individual sign-off is a weaker form of assurance than peer review." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The passage describes two approaches and reports one difference in outcome while explicitly declining to assess accuracy. It makes no judgement about relative strength. A reader may reasonably infer that peer review is stronger, and the passage neither supports nor excludes that, so Cannot Say. What makes this harder than Question 6 is that the passage's structure leans towards peer review — mentioned first, associated with a favourable outcome — and structural lean is not content. Noticing when you are responding to a document's implied stance rather than its assertions is among the most transferable things this section trains, and it applies directly to review reports, which routinely imply more than they will state.

*Passage C — for Questions 9 to 12*

"Content must be written so that it can be understood by users with a reading age of 9 where the subject matter allows. Where technical or legal terms cannot be avoided, they must be explained at first use. Content must not rely on colour alone to convey meaning. Headings must describe the content that follows and be used in order without skipping levels. These requirements apply to public-facing content and to internal guidance used by more than fifty staff."

**Question 9 (easy)** — Statement: "All content must be understandable by users with a reading age of 9." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The requirement is qualified: "where the subject matter allows". The statement's "all" drops that, and the passage explicitly contemplates subject matter where the standard cannot be met, so False. This qualifier is frequently misquoted in both directions. It is not a general excuse — most content claiming difficult subject matter could be far simpler — and it does acknowledge genuine cases. At your level you will be the person adjudicating whether a team's claim of difficult subject matter is honest, which requires reading the rule exactly rather than as a slogan in either direction.

**Question 10 (moderate)** — Statement: "Technical terms must be avoided." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The passage says that *where technical or legal terms cannot be avoided*, they must be explained at first use. That wording presupposes such cases exist and permits the terms, subject to explanation. The statement asserts an absolute prohibition the passage does not impose, so False. A rule addressing what to do in a situation implicitly accepts the situation occurs. Reading it as a prohibition produces content designers arguing that a term must be removed when the actual requirement is that it must be explained — and losing the argument, because the term is often genuinely necessary and the stakeholder knows it.

**Question 11 (moderate)** — Statement: "These requirements apply to internal guidance used by twenty staff." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The final sentence extends the requirements to internal guidance used by more than fifty staff. Guidance used by twenty does not meet that threshold, so the extension does not apply — but the passage does not say the requirements are *excluded* for smaller audiences, only where they do apply. Silence about the twenty-user case leaves it undetermined, so Cannot Say rather than False. This is a fine and common distinction in scoping clauses, and one you should expect to have to explain: stating where a rule applies is not stating where it does not, and a lead who tells a small team they are exempt when nobody has said so will eventually have to withdraw it.

**Question 12 (harder) — Main point**

Which best captures the main point of Passage C?

- A) Content must be written for a reading age of 9.
- B) Public-facing content and widely used internal guidance must be comprehensible and navigable without specialist knowledge or reliance on visual cues — through plain language where the subject allows, explanation where terms are unavoidable, meaning carried in more than colour, and headings that describe and are correctly ordered.
- C) Headings must be used in order.
- D) Technical terms must be explained at first use.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The main point must cover the whole passage without adding anything. Option A drops the qualifier and ignores four other elements. Options C and D are each true of one sentence and silent on the rest. Option B captures all four requirements, the scope clause, and the logic joining them: each exists so content can be understood and navigated by someone who does not share the author's knowledge and may not perceive the page as the author does. Note that B preserves both qualifiers that A discarded and is the only option mentioning scope, which matters because a summary omitting it implies the requirements are universal. Writing a summary that keeps every qualifier and adds nothing is, more or less exactly, the skill you assure in others.

### Preparation tips

- **Point at the words.** Before marking True, identify the phrase that makes it true. Experience makes this harder.
- **Trust Cannot Say.** Especially where the claim is one you would like to be true.
- **Circle every qualifier.** All, some, may, must, should, unless, provided that, where the subject matter allows, more than.
- **Separate an exemption from a deferral.** "Reviewed within five days" is not "exempt from review".
- **Separate a conditional prohibition from an absolute one.** "Not without agreement" means you can ask.
- **Distinguish stating where a rule applies from stating where it does not.**
- **Separate "consider whether X" from "recommend X".** You may be the person it is addressed to.
- **Practise on real governance prose.** Write three statements about a page — one True, one False, one Cannot Say.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Answering from professional knowledge.** The dominant error at your level.
- **Marking True on a claim you would like to be true.**
- **Dropping a qualifier.** Citing a rule without it is how a good argument gets corrected in public.
- **Reading a deferral as an exemption.** It produces a layer of content nobody ever reviews.
- **Reading a conditional rule as a prohibition.**
- **Converting a scope statement into an exemption.**
- **Responding to implied stance.** Review reports imply more than they assert.
- **Treating a proxy outcome as the thing itself.** Fewer corrections is not greater accuracy.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment presents realistic workplace scenarios and asks what you would do. There is no passage to parse and no arithmetic. You get a dilemma of the kind your role produces, four or five candidate responses, and a question asking which is most effective and often which is least.

This section measures whether your judgement matches what the profession considers good practice at your level. Your role places you as an expert practitioner and leader who directs a team of content designers, assures quality across teams, aligns content to strategy, works with service managers and programme directors to resource teams and resolve problems, and promotes the discipline. The strongest answers involve acting on systems rather than instances, developing your team rather than doing their work, absorbing pressure on their behalf, and using influence where you hold no formal authority.

Three features distinguish your level.

The first is that most of what you achieve happens through other people. An option that solves a problem through your personal intervention is usually second-best, because it does not survive your absence.

The second is that you absorb pressure downwards. When a programme director leans on a content designer to publish something they should not, the content designer should not be holding that line alone.

The third is that content quality is unusually hard to argue about, because everybody reads. That makes evidence your primary instrument and makes options that bring evidence systematically stronger than options that assert quality.

Four principles carry most scenarios. Users come first, and content failures are quiet — nobody rings to say they misunderstood and gave up — which puts a particular obligation on you to represent people who are not in the room. Protect the accuracy of what is published. Ask what a request is for. And fix the source, not only the instance.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The judgement dimensions map onto your named skills:

- **Assuring quality across teams** maps to your role level's core accountability.
- **Directing and developing a team** maps to **Agile working (content design)** and to leading content designers.
- **Resourcing and resolving problems with senior stakeholders** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management (content design)** and to your role level's named work with service managers and programme directors.
- **Aligning content to strategy** maps to **Strategic thinking (content design)**.
- **Protecting users** maps to **User focus (content design)** and **User-centred content design**.
- **Promoting the discipline** maps to your role level's named engagement with the cross-government community.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — A content designer under pressure**

A content designer tells you a programme director has asked them to publish a page before the plain-language review, because a launch is tomorrow. They are unsure what to do. What is the most effective response?

- A) Tell them to refuse and cite your instruction.
- B) Work out with them what is actually possible — whether the review can happen in the next hour — and take the conversation with the programme director yourself, since a director pressing a content designer to skip assurance is a matter at your level.
- C) Approve the exception.
- D) Tell them to publish and review afterwards.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Two things must happen: the content gets reviewed or a knowing decision is taken not to, and the content designer is not left negotiating with a director. Option B does both, and the practical half matters as much as the protective half — a plain-language review of one page takes half an hour, and establishing that usually dissolves the conflict entirely. Taking the conversation yourself is your job, because a power differential of that size is what a lead exists to absorb. Option A is least effective: it leaves them refusing a director by proxy, teaches nothing, and frames assurance as hierarchy rather than as something with a purpose. Option C grants an exception without establishing whether one is needed. Option D instructs them to do the thing they were uncomfortable with, which is worse than A because it also removes the review.

**Question 2 (easy) — A standard nobody follows**

Your organisation has a content standard that teams routinely ignore. They describe it as unrealistic. What is the strongest approach?

- A) Reissue it with a message from you.
- B) Review recent content with the teams to establish which requirements are genuinely unworkable and which merely unclear, revise it with them, and pair it with a lightweight routine — such as reading drafts aloud before sign-off — that gives compliance a moment of application and produces something the teams want anyway.
- C) Introduce mandatory compliance checks.
- D) Run training on the standard.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A standard everybody ignores has already failed, and reissuing changes only the date. Option B treats the objection as data — some requirements probably are unworkable and the rest usually unclear — and revising with the teams produces authorship and therefore defenders, which is the only mechanism by which a standard becomes self-sustaining rather than dependent on your attention. The routine named is the right one for this discipline: reading drafts aloud exposes exactly the failures a style guide describes abstractly, costs nothing, and teams tend to keep doing it because it visibly improves the work. Option A is least effective, the purest form of appearing to act. Option C enforces a standard people have told you is unrealistic. Option D is useful and insufficient alone.

**Question 3 (easy) — Resourcing a team**

Your content designers are spread one per service across seven services, are pulled into delivery firefighting, have no time for critique, and rarely see each other. Two have left in six months. What is the strongest response?

- A) Discuss workload with each delivery manager.
- B) Treat it as structural: establish a protected routine bringing content designers together, negotiate the time with delivery leadership by showing what the current pattern costs in rework and attrition, and change how people are deployed if single embedding is the cause.
- C) Introduce a wellbeing initiative.
- D) Recruit replacements quickly.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Two departures in six months alongside no critique and no professional contact is a structural pattern, not a series of individual circumstances. Option B addresses it on three fronts, and the deployment clause matters particularly for content design: a content designer embedded alone has nobody to read their drafts, which removes the single most effective quality mechanism the discipline has, so single embedding damages the work as directly as it damages the people. The negotiation detail matters too — protected time not paid for in delivery's currency gets cancelled by the third sprint. Option D is least effective, reproducing the attrition on a lag in a competitive market where your reputation as a place to work travels quickly. Option A addresses symptoms individually. Option C treats a design-of-work problem as a morale problem.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Assuring another team's content**

A team you do not manage has published a page whose central claim about eligibility is wrong. It is live. What is most effective?

- A) Correct it yourself.
- B) Contact the team's content designer and lead immediately with the specific error and the evidence, agree who corrects it and by when, and check whether the same claim appears elsewhere in their service or in yours — while making clear that a live eligibility error needs fixing today rather than in a sprint.
- C) Raise it at the next content community session.
- D) Report it to the service owner.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** A wrong eligibility claim may cause people to apply when they cannot or not apply when they can, so speed matters — and option B moves quickly while doing the things that distinguish a lead's response from a proofreader's: going to the people who can fix it, agreeing ownership and a deadline rather than leaving it as a report, and checking where else the claim appears, because an eligibility statement is almost never made once. Option C is least effective by a distance: deferring a live factual error to a community session is a category error about what that forum is for, and it also broadcasts a team's mistake before they have had a chance to fix it. Option A bypasses the team and may be outside your permissions. Option D escalates before asking, which will be resented and is slower.

**Question 5 (moderate) — A stakeholder who will not accept evidence**

A programme director dismisses research showing users do not understand a key term, saying the participants were unrepresentative and the term is standard in the sector. What is most effective?

- A) Concede and keep the term.
- B) Acknowledge the sample's limits honestly, point out that a term being standard among practitioners says nothing about the public and is if anything weak evidence the other way, and propose a cheap check the director will accept — the site search log, which shows whether users search for that term or a different one.
- C) Insist on the change.
- D) Escalate to your head of profession.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** The director has made two claims of very different quality, and separating them is the whole move. The sample objection is partly fair and worth conceding, which costs nothing and buys credibility for the part that matters. The sector-standard claim is a category error — terms become standard among specialists by being used among specialists, which is weak evidence that the public shares them. Option B says both and then proposes evidence the director is likely to accept, using data the organisation already holds, which converts a disagreement into a question with an answer. That last move is what makes it a lead's answer rather than a practitioner's. Option A is least effective, conceding to an objection that does not survive examination and teaching the director that this works. Option C wins nothing without new evidence. Option D escalates a conversation you are equipped to have.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Developing someone whose work is weak**

A content designer you lead consistently over-writes: long sentences, unnecessary qualifiers, information the user does not need. You have raised it twice in general terms and it continues. What is the most effective next step?

- A) Edit their work before it publishes.
- B) Move from general feedback to something structured: sit with them and read one of their own pages aloud, so they hear where a reader would stumble; then agree they will read every draft aloud before sign-off, and review the next one together.
- C) Raise it in their performance conversation.
- D) Move them to work that involves less writing.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Twice-repeated general feedback has not worked, which usually means the person agrees in principle and cannot see it in their own writing. Option B addresses that with the mechanism specific to this discipline: hearing your own sentence read aloud is a categorically different experience from being told it is long, and the failures become obvious — you run out of breath, you stumble on a qualifier, you have to re-read a clause. Agreeing they will do it themselves makes it portable, and reviewing the next draft closes the loop. Option A is least effective because it removes the problem from view while guaranteeing it persists, makes you a permanent bottleneck, and signals you do not think they can learn. Option C is legitimate eventually and premature. Option D writes off a content designer's core skill rather than developing it.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Content that conflicts with strategy**

A team has produced content that is clear, well tested and popular, and that describes the service in terms conflicting with the organisation's published strategy. What is most effective?

- A) Approve it; it works for users.
- B) Establish which description is right before deciding — if the strategy is aspirational and the content describes the service as it is, the content stands and somebody should know the strategy has drifted; if the content misdescribes what the service does, that is an accuracy problem regardless of how well it tests.
- C) Require the content to match the strategy.
- D) Escalate the discrepancy.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** Your role names making content align to strategy *while* meeting user needs, and this is the case where those pull apart. The resolution is not to pick a side but to establish what is true, because the conflict has two very different explanations. Strategies routinely describe services as they are intended to become, and content describing them as they are will conflict while being entirely correct. Alternatively the content may misdescribe the service in a way that happens to test well, which is a real risk — users can find a wrong description clear and reassuring. Option B distinguishes them. Option C is least effective: requiring content to match a document rather than reality means publishing something users will discover is untrue, which damages trust in the service and in the strategy. Option A treats user testing as settling a question of fact. Option D escalates before diagnosing.

**Question 8 (harder) — Content that may cause harm**

Two days before launch you learn a page omits that applying may reduce a benefit the user already receives. Adding it needs policy sign-off and may delay launch. What is most effective?

- A) Launch and add it in the next update.
- B) Raise it immediately with the service owner, stating specifically that users may reduce their income without knowing, ask for an explicit recorded decision, and have your team draft the wording for policy to approve so the sign-off is as fast as possible.
- C) Have your team add a sentence based on their understanding.
- D) Launch and log it.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** A page omitting a financial consequence may cause real harm, and weighing that against a launch date is a decision for someone with authority — though raising it precisely and now is entirely yours. Option B does everything in your remit, and two details make it a lead's answer: stating the consequence in terms of income prompts action in a way that "the page is incomplete" does not, and having your team draft the wording removes the commonest cause of sign-off delay, which is that nobody has written anything for policy to react to. Option C is least effective: publishing your team's own account of how two benefits interact, unapproved, risks telling people something wrong about their money, and authorising that is worse than an individual doing it because it becomes practice. Options A and D launch with a known harm.

**Question 9 (harder) — A recurring problem across teams**

Across four teams, content consistently omits timescales. Each has a plausible local reason. What is most effective?

- A) Add timescales to the content checklist.
- B) Establish the common cause — most likely that the standard is silent on what to publish when a timescale is uncertain — and fix it, most plausibly by agreeing a form of words such as "we usually reply within X and will tell you if it will take longer", then negotiating with policy once, on all four teams' behalf, for the figures.
- C) Remind the four teams of the requirement.
- D) Accept it; some timescales genuinely cannot be given.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** Four teams behaving identically for plausible reasons is one systemic cause with four rationalisations, and the plausibility is what makes case-by-case challenge ineffective. Option B identifies the likely cause and does the two things only a lead can: supplies the form of words that makes an uncertain timescale publishable, and negotiates once with policy rather than leaving four content designers to have the same losing conversation separately. That second element is the substance of leading a discipline. Option C is least effective and is the weakest available response to any compliance problem. Option A adds a checklist item to a system where teams already know the requirement and cannot meet it. Option D accepts the rationalisation without testing it — genuinely unknown timescales are rarer than claimed, and where they are real, silence is still the wrong answer.

**Question 10 (harder) — Promoting the discipline**

You are asked to explain to a programme board why content design needs three people rather than one. Board members believe content is writing and can be done by anyone. What is most effective?

- A) Explain the discipline and its methods.
- B) Lead with the evidence in their currency — the contact volume attributable to content gaps, the calls avoided by specific past changes, the cost per call — and then explain what three people would do that one cannot, tying each to a measurable outcome.
- C) Point to the service standard's requirements.
- D) Ask the head of profession to make the case.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A board that believes content is writing will not be moved by an explanation of the discipline, because they have already discounted the premise — and explaining methods to people who do not accept the problem exists reliably confirms their view that this is a craft preference. Option B inverts the order: establish the problem in numbers the board already uses, then explain what capacity buys. "Forty thousand calls a year ask when something will happen; the last time we fixed one of those it removed nine thousand calls; here is what three people could reach that one cannot" is an argument in their language. Option A is least effective for precisely the reason it feels natural. Option C invokes compliance, which wins a minimum and never wins resource. Option D forgoes the relationship and the standing that comes from making the case yourself.

**Question 11 (harder) — A failure in something you assured**

Six months ago you approved a form of words now used across five services. You realise it misstates a deadline in the two services with a different statutory period. What should you do?

- A) Note it for the next review.
- B) Notify all five services now, scope precisely which two are affected and which three are not, correct the two immediately, and examine why the approval did not check whether the deadline varied — then change what the approval process asks.
- C) Amend the wording quietly in the two affected services.
- D) Withdraw the agreed wording from all five.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** A shared form of words used in five services means a flaw is potentially five errors, and the scoping matters as much as the disclosure — three services are correct, and telling them otherwise creates unnecessary work and erodes confidence in shared wording generally, which is a cost you pay for years. Option B does all four necessary things: notifies, scopes, corrects, and asks the question that prevents the next one, which is why the approval did not ask whether the deadline varied by service. Option C is least effective: correcting quietly leaves three services unaware that a shared asset had a defect and, more importantly, leaves the approval process unchanged. Option A defers a live error about a deadline. Option D discards something working in three services because it failed in two.

**Question 12 (harder) — Cross-government contribution**

You have solved a content problem — explaining a common benefit rule — that you know several other departments face. Your team has an agreed, tested form of words. What is most effective?

- A) Use it in your services and move on.
- B) Share it through the cross-government content community with the evidence behind it and an honest note about what was and was not tested, and offer to work with anyone adapting it — accepting that others may improve it, which is the point.
- C) Publish it as a pattern for others to adopt.
- D) Offer it to the departments you know personally.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Your role names promoting the discipline and engaging the cross-government community, and this is the case where that obligation is concrete: a rule appearing in several departments explained several ways is a problem multiplied, and one tested form of words is worth considerably more than five untested ones. Option B does it well, and two details matter. Sharing the evidence lets others judge whether it transfers to their context rather than adopting it blindly, and the honest note about what was not tested prevents the wording acquiring more authority than it earned. Option A is least effective, keeping a solution that cost you effort while several departments repeat the work. Option C publishes without the evidence or the caveats, which is how patterns get adopted into contexts they do not fit. Option D helps two departments and misses the mechanism designed for this.

### Preparation tips

- **Absorb pressure downwards.** If a power differential is above the level of the person facing it, it is yours.
- **Ask what an option does to the system.** An intervention depending on your attention has not solved anything.
- **Lead with evidence in the other person's currency.** Calls, pounds and hours beat an explanation of the discipline.
- **Let people experience the failure.** Reading their own draft aloud teaches what feedback cannot.
- **Negotiate once on everyone's behalf.** Four content designers having the same losing conversation is a leadership failure.
- **Supply the wording that makes compliance possible.** Teams omit timescales because nobody has told them what to write when it is uncertain.
- **Scope your own errors precisely.** Three correct services should not be told they are wrong.
- **Share with the evidence and the caveats.** A form of words without them acquires unearned authority.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Reissuing a standard nobody follows.**
- **Reminding teams of rules they already know and cannot meet.**
- **Becoming the permanent editing bottleneck.**
- **Explaining the discipline to people who have not accepted the problem.**
- **Requiring content to match a strategy rather than reality.**
- **Deferring a live factual error to a scheduled forum.**
- **Conceding to an objection that does not survive examination.**
- **Keeping a solved problem to yourself while other departments repeat the work.**

## Conclusion

You have reached the end, and it is worth registering the scale: forty-eight questions across four assessment types, with the reasoning worked through on each. That is a substantial piece of professional preparation and more than most people at your level undertake.

Look back at the ground. In the cognitive section you traced a compliant, well-written page's call volume to something outside the page, diagnosed five teams' missing timescales as one systemic cause with five rationalisations, recognised a heavily used recovery page as a measure of a shared component's failure rate, separated a reaction to tone from evidence about need, and worked out what to do when good content conflicts with a strategy. In the numeric section you annualised before reporting, returned to counts before combining rates and recomputed when nothing matched, reconstructed an implied cost per call, recognised that a fall in views can be success, and declined to endorse a comparison that would set a precedent against your teams. In the verbal section you separated a deferral from an exemption, a conditional prohibition from an absolute one, and a scope statement from an exclusion, and you declined to mark True on a claim you would have liked to be true. And in the situational judgement section you took a director's pressure off a content designer, negotiated once with policy on four teams' behalf, made a resourcing case in a board's own currency, and shared a solved problem across government with its caveats intact.

Two threads run through it. The first is whether you know the limits of the evidence and will say so — including when the claim is one that would help you. The second is whether you act on mechanisms rather than instances. Between them those are most of what your role consists of. Teams can write without you. What you supply is content that can be trusted at the strength it is stated, and a practice around you that keeps producing it.

A word about the particular difficulty of your level. Content quality is hard to argue about because everybody reads, which means your authority rests on evidence far more than on craft judgement — and the errors that cost you most are no longer the ones you would notice. They are made fluently, in areas where your experience runs deep enough that checking feels unnecessary. The verbal reasoning section is the clearest demonstration, and the pattern generalises.

If you want to build on this, a few things repay effort more than rereading. Read your services' site search logs for a month; almost nobody does. Spend an hour listening to contact centre calls and find the pages that should have answered them. Read every letter and email one of your services sends, in the order a user receives them. Take a legal phrase your teams have accepted for a year and ask the legal team what the requirement actually is. And go and watch whatever your strongest team does that the others do not, before assuming you know what it is.

Finally, for the day itself. An assessment samples your reasoning on one morning under artificial conditions. It is not a verdict on your judgement or your career. Read the stem twice, find the denominator, trust Cannot Say, keep the qualifiers, and remember that at your level the strongest answer is almost always the one honest about uncertainty and aimed at the mechanism rather than the symptom.

Good luck. Go carefully, ask what things are for, and keep reading things aloud.
