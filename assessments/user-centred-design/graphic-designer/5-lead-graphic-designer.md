# Lead Graphic Designer - Psychometric Assessment Resources

## Introduction

Welcome. This guide is written for you as a lead graphic designer in the UK Government Digital and Data profession — an expert practitioner who influences and mentors others, works with service managers and programme directors to develop design concepts, sets direction and assures the quality of design delivery across teams, and leads multiple or highly complex services. Your role description is organisational: you gain support for design strategy from senior leaders, communicate it across organisational boundaries, coach others, measurably improve inclusive design practice across multiple teams, embed iterative working, and create the conditions in which designers can do good work.

You may be facing a formal assessment as part of a selection process, or you may want to re-examine reasoning that has become fluent enough to be worth checking. At your level the risk is not that you lack technique but that technique has become automatic, automatic reasoning feels certain, and few people around you are positioned to catch you.

Three features of the role shape everything that follows. First, you assure work you did not produce, on services you have not seen, judged from an output rather than from the thinking behind it. Second, you work at the level of shared assets: tokens, type scales, component styles, grids and standards. A decision at that level is paid for many times over, and its accessibility characteristics propagate to every place it is used — which is why the arithmetic of a colour value matters more when it enters a token set than when it appears on a page. Third, your authority is largely informal, and in a visual discipline that is a particular difficulty: everybody has opinions about colour and type in a way they do not about database schemas, which means your influence rests almost entirely on evidence and measurement rather than on craft authority.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the reasoning your role demands, presented through practical materials rather than abstract puzzles. At lead level the materials are organisational and the reasoning is diagnostic. You will meet work produced by other people, standards applied unevenly, evidence gathered under conditions nobody recorded, and prioritisation problems whose units are shared assets rather than screens.

The format is typically an online test of fifteen to thirty minutes covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective against a norm group of comparable seniority. Adaptive platforms raise difficulty after correct answers, and acuity reports distinguish speed from accuracy.

Three things characterise this section at your level.

The first is reasoning at a distance. You rarely see the file; you see a rendered page, a screenshot in a report, a claim in a board paper, and must judge from visible traces whether the thinking underneath holds.

The second is that the interesting questions concern mechanisms. Off-system values, contrast failures and inconsistent components are nearly always produced by something: a token that was unavailable in the tool teams actually use, a scale missing a step designs need, a review that happens too late to change anything, or a brand palette optimised for print. Fixing the instance leaves the mechanism intact.

The third is the multiplier. A token adopted by ten services carries its properties ten times and is far harder to change than to set. Several items ask you to weigh a decision by how many times it will be paid for.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The cognitive dimensions map onto the skills named in your role summary:

- **Auditing at a distance** maps to setting direction and assuring the quality of design delivery across teams.
- **Cross-service synthesis** maps to **Evidence-based design**, where you analyse, synthesise and use evidence to improve how the organisation works.
- **Systemic diagnosis** maps to **Iterative design**, where you iterate delivery processes or team structures to help teams work effectively.
- **Strategic prioritisation** maps to **Designing strategically**, where you use risks, opportunities and constraints to shape design.
- **Diagnosing structural exclusion** maps to **Designing for everyone**, where you measurably improve inclusive practice across multiple teams.
- **Reasoning about positions** maps to **Design communication**, where you gain support from senior leaders and communicate across boundaries.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Auditing against a standard**

Your standard requires every component entry to state: the problem it solves, when not to use it, its contrast values, its minimum target size, known limitations, and the date last reviewed. An entry states the problem, contrast values, minimum target size and date; describes limitations as "none known"; and has no "when not to use". How many requirements are unmet?

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Work through all six. Problem, contrast values, target size and date are present. "None known" is a statement about known limitations — thin, and it addresses the requirement, so it is met as written. "When not to use" is absent. One unmet, giving A. Option B typically comes from rejecting "none known", which is a defensible design judgement and the wrong audit judgement: the discipline is to apply the standard as written. At your level that produces the useful output. If "none known" satisfies your standard, the standard needs to require something more specific — a statement of what was tested and in what conditions — because "none known" is what an entry says when nobody looked, and a standard that cannot distinguish that from a genuine clean result is not doing its job.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction from a governance rule**

Your standard states: "A custom colour value may enter the token set only if no existing token is suitable, unless approved by the head of graphic design." A team reports adding a custom value last month with no such approval. Which must be true?

- A) The standard was breached.
- B) No existing token was suitable.
- C) Either no existing token was suitable, or the standard was breached.
- D) The value fails contrast.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A conditional with a stated exception, plus facts silent on the branch that applies, licenses only the disjunction. Options A and B each assume one. Option D imports a claim nothing supports. Only C holds. Note the structure carefully, because the exception here attaches to a rule that already has a condition: the general rule permits a custom value where no token is suitable, and the exception permits it anyway with approval. Since we know approval was not given, the only remaining route is the general one — so the disjunction is between "no token was suitable" and "the standard was breached". Reading the two conditions apart is what makes the deduction correct rather than merely plausible.

**Question 3 (moderate) — Reading conditions from an output**

A report you are assuring states: "Participants read the interface comfortably and distinguished all status colours easily." It notes sessions ran in a usability lab on a calibrated 27-inch monitor. What is the most important observation?

- A) The sample is too small to support the claim.
- B) A calibrated lab monitor at controlled brightness flatters exactly the two properties claimed — legibility and colour discrimination — so the finding describes performance under near-ideal display conditions rather than the conditions users have, and cannot support a general claim.
- C) "Comfortably" is not measurable.
- D) The study should have used a validated legibility scale.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The finding and the conditions match too neatly to be coincidence, and the strongest observation names why. A calibrated monitor at controlled brightness in a lit room makes low-contrast text look fine and makes similar hues look distinct; the same design on a dimmed laptop, a cheap monitor with a colour cast, or a phone in daylight may fail both tests. That is the display equivalent of a sampling frame problem, and it is the specific methodological trap of visual disciplines — testing on the best screen in the building is the equivalent of recruiting only confident users. Option C is a fair secondary point about measurement. Option A misdiagnoses validity as size. Option D is a technique fix leaving the conditions untouched, and a validated scale administered on the same monitor produces the same flattered result with more authority.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Strategic prioritisation**

You can direct substantial capacity to one of four things this quarter:

1. A colour and type token set is being fixed next month for adoption by seven services.
2. A live service has a known contrast failure on a secondary label.
3. A programme wants exploratory visual concepts for next year.
4. A directorate wants a brand refresh applied to existing services.

Which is strongest?

- A) Item 2, because it affects users now.
- B) Item 1, because the decisions are imminent, will be inherited by seven services, and a token set propagates its accessibility characteristics everywhere it is used — making it far harder to change after adoption than to set correctly now.
- C) Item 4, because brand consistency affects everything.
- D) Item 3, because early work has the highest leverage.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The right frame is what your input can still change and whether there is time, and item 1 carries a multiplier the others lack. A token set inherited by seven services propagates every property it has, and changing a colour token after adoption means coordinating seven teams and revisiting every place it appears. Item 2 matters and is one label with a known fix somebody can make in an hour, which is exactly why it does not need a quarter of your capacity. Item 3 has no deadline pressure. Item 4 is the interesting distractor at your level: a brand refresh applied across existing services is expensive, highly visible, and — unless the new palette has been checked for contrast across every context it will meet — is a substantial risk rather than an improvement, which makes it work you would want to shape rather than lead first.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Cross-service synthesis**

Three items reach you in a month. One team reports users missing a status change. A second service's analytics show high abandonment at a page whose only state indicator is colour. An accessibility audit of a third flags colour-only status labels. What is the strongest synthesis?

- A) Three teams have made the same mistake.
- B) The organisation has a systemic gap: colour-only status is appearing across services, which suggests the token set or component library offers colour states without a paired non-colour indicator — so the fix is in the shared asset rather than in three teams' pages.
- C) Teams should be reminded not to use colour alone.
- D) Each team should add an icon.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The synthesis that adds value asks why three teams independently made the same choice, and the usual answer is that the shared asset made it the easy one. If the component library offers a set of coloured status chips with no icon or text variant, three teams using it correctly will all produce colour-only status, and none of them has made a mistake exactly. Option B identifies that and locates the fix where it removes the problem for every service at once, including those that have not built the page yet. Option D fixes three instances and leaves the fourth team to repeat it. Option C is the weakest available response to any recurring problem — teams that used the component as supplied did not need reminding of a principle, they needed a variant. Option A misattributes a systemic gap to individual error.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Feedback on a token proposal**

A designer proposes a new secondary text grey at 4.6:1 against white for the token set. What is the most useful intervention?

- A) Approve it; it passes 4.5:1.
- B) Note that 4.6:1 leaves 0.1 of headroom and will fail against any tinted panel in the system, and that tokens get used wherever they look right rather than re-measured in context — so ask what backgrounds it will meet and propose a value with enough headroom to survive them.
- C) Reject it.
- D) Require it to be tested on every background.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The value passes and passes by nothing, and the strongest intervention names the mechanism rather than the measurement. Contrast falls as a background darkens towards the text, so a grey passing on white fails on a light grey panel — and because a token is available everywhere, nobody re-measures it in context, which is both the convenience of tokens and their risk. Option B reframes the decision around the backgrounds the token will meet, which is the question the proposal has not asked. Option C reaches a defensible conclusion without the reasoning, so it cannot survive being challenged. Option A treats the threshold as the whole question. Option D is not wrong and is impractical as a standing rule, because new panel colours appear faster than audits do — which is precisely the argument for headroom rather than for testing.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Diagnosing a systemic problem**

Six teams consistently use off-scale spacing values. Each has a plausible local reason. What is the strongest diagnosis and response?

- A) Remind teams to use the scale.
- B) Six teams doing the same thing for plausible reasons is one systemic cause with six rationalisations; establish which it is — the scale lacks a step designs genuinely need, the tokens are unavailable in the tool teams design in, or the component defaults do not sit correctly on the scale — and fix that, since the remedies differ entirely.
- C) Add spacing to the design review checklist.
- D) Accept it; some layouts need bespoke values.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Six teams behaving identically for individually plausible reasons is the signature of a systemic cause, and the plausibility is what makes case-by-case challenge exhausting and ineffective. Option B names the realistic candidates, and they imply entirely different remedies: adding a step is a decision you can take in a morning; making tokens available in the design tool is a tooling job; and component defaults sitting off-scale is a defect in the shared asset that will keep generating off-scale values no matter how carefully teams work. Diagnosing wrongly costs a quarter. Option A is the weakest available response. Option C adds a checklist item where teams already know the rule. Option D accepts the rationalisation without testing it, and "some layouts need bespoke values" is true occasionally and used constantly.

**Question 8 (harder) — Scoping a flaw**

A study you are assuring ran on a single calibrated monitor. Four findings: (i) participants could not find a secondary action; (ii) participants read body text comfortably; (iii) participants misinterpreted an icon; (iv) participants distinguished status colours easily. Which are most compromised?

- A) All four.
- B) None; display conditions are incidental.
- C) Findings (ii) and (iv) are most compromised, because a calibrated display flatters legibility and colour discrimination — while (i) and (iii) concern position and comprehension, which the display affects far less.
- D) Only (iv).

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The skill is scoping precisely rather than treating a condition as invalidating everything or nothing. A calibrated monitor makes low-contrast text look fine and similar hues look distinct, which is exactly what findings (ii) and (iv) claim. Findings (i) and (iii) concern where something is and what an icon means, which display quality barely touches. Option C says this. Option A discards two sound findings, and at your scale that does organisational damage: a lead who condemns wholesale teaches teams that declaring their test conditions invalidates their work, which is a direct incentive to stop declaring them — and you will then be assuring studies whose conditions are invisible, which is worse than the present situation because you no longer know. Option B is wrong on the facts.

**Question 9 (harder) — Apparent contradiction between teams**

Two teams reach opposite conclusions about the same grey: one finds it legible, the other that users struggle. Both studies look sound. What is strongest?

- A) Commission a third study.
- B) Establish what differs before treating it as a contradiction — most likely the background, since a grey legible on white fails on a tinted panel, or the type size and weight, since the threshold differs — and the synthesis is that the token is context-dependent and its documentation should say so.
- C) Report both and note the inconsistency.
- D) Treat the larger study as authoritative.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Apparent contradictions between sound studies are usually context differences in disguise, and in this discipline the candidates are unusually specific and checkable: background colour, type size, weight, and display conditions each change whether the same grey works. Option B reaches the synthesis, and the synthesis is more valuable than either finding — it tells every team in the organisation that this token has conditions attached, which neither study alone could, and it produces a concrete documentation change. Option A spends money on a question that dissolves on inspection. Option C abdicates the synthesis your role exists to produce, and "note the inconsistency" is how a usable finding gets filed as a caveat. Option D picks a winner on an irrelevant criterion.

**Question 10 (harder) — Reading the distribution of failures**

Your standard requires an accessibility check before release. Across the year, sixteen of twenty services complied; the four that did not were all in one directorate, all under ministerial deadline pressure, all by experienced designers. What is the strongest response?

- A) Report the four and require remediation.
- B) Treat the concentration as diagnostic: four failures in one directorate under identical pressure by people who know the requirement indicates a process that cannot be complied with when time is short — investigate what the check demands and how long it takes, build a fast route, and remediate the four alongside.
- C) Remind all designers of the requirement.
- D) Introduce a mandatory pre-release gate for every service.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The distribution is the most informative feature. Scattered breaches would indicate conduct or capability; four concentrated under identical pressure by experienced people indicate a process that does not survive a deadline. Option B investigates the mechanism and fixes it, while the remediation clause preserves the point that a systemic diagnosis is not a reason to leave individual failures unaddressed. Option A treats the instances and guarantees recurrence. Option C is the weakest available compliance response. Option D imposes friction on sixteen compliant services to catch four, which is how organisational burden accumulates — and you will be defending that gate in two years, having forgotten you introduced it.

**Question 11 (harder) — Evidence for a brand decision**

The organisation is considering adopting a refreshed brand palette. Available: a brand agency's assertion that the palette is "accessible"; an internal check finding two of eight colours fail 4.5:1 against white; two directorates reporting the palette looks better; and a cost model for applying it. What is your most rigorous contribution?

- A) Oppose the refresh on the contrast finding.
- B) Set out what each source establishes and its limits — including that "accessible" from an agency usually means the palette contains compliant combinations rather than that every colour is usable for body text, so the two findings are not in conflict — and name what would most change the decision, which is how the palette performs on the tinted backgrounds and at the text sizes the services actually use.
- C) Support the refresh on the aesthetic reports.
- D) Recommend a full audit before deciding.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Your contribution at a decision like this is neither advocacy nor delay; it is making the evidence legible. Option B does three things. It resolves an apparent contradiction that would otherwise consume the meeting — an agency's accessibility claim almost always means the palette *contains* compliant pairings, which is entirely consistent with two individual colours failing for body text, so both statements are true and neither is decisive. It identifies that the internal check tested against white only, which is the wrong question for a palette that will meet tinted panels. And it names the residual uncertainty. Option A opposes on partial evidence and will be read as the design profession obstructing a brand decision. Option C ignores the finding. Option D defers a decision the evidence may already inform.

**Question 12 (harder) — A pattern across your teams**

Reviewing a year of output, you find two teams consistently produce accessible, on-system work and five do not. Both strong teams have a designer who checks every screen in a contrast tool before handing over. What is the most useful inference and action?

- A) The five weaker teams need training.
- B) A pre-handover contrast check is a cheap, transferable routine that plausibly explains the difference — watch how they do it to establish what it actually catches, then build it into the organisation's expectations and, if possible, into tooling so it happens automatically.
- C) Recruit more designers like those two.
- D) Increase assurance scrutiny on the five weaker teams.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The pattern is an opportunity to identify a practice, not to grade people. A check performed before handover is among the most effective routines available in this discipline, because it catches defects when they cost minutes rather than after they have propagated into a build. Option B goes and watches first, which matters because the routine may be catching something other than contrast — often the real benefit is that somebody looks at every screen deliberately rather than at the ones they were working on. And it names the strongest form of propagation: automating a check removes reliance on discipline entirely. Option A posits a knowledge deficit without diagnosing one, and training unattached to a routine rarely changes practice. Option C misattributes a routine to individuals. Option D catches more defects and creates no capacity to prevent them.

### Preparation tips

- **Read outputs for the conditions behind them.** A finding matching its test conditions too neatly is telling you something.
- **Scope flaws precisely.** Wholesale condemnation teaches teams to stop declaring conditions.
- **Ask what background a value will meet.** Headroom, not the threshold, is the question for anything reusable.
- **Ask why three teams made the same choice.** Usually the shared asset made it the easy one.
- **Weight decisions by adoption.** A token inherited by seven services is worth seven times the attention.
- **Read the distribution of failures.** Concentrated non-compliance diagnoses a process.
- **Resolve apparent contradictions by definition.** An agency's "accessible" and your audit measure different things.
- **Prefer automation to discipline.** A check that happens by itself does not depend on anyone remembering.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Applying a standard as you would have written it.** If "none known" satisfies it, change the standard.
- **Approving values at the threshold.** They fail as soon as context changes.
- **Reminding teams of rules they followed correctly using a deficient asset.**
- **Adding friction for the compliant majority.**
- **Picking a winner between contradictory studies.** The insight is in the difference between contexts.
- **Testing on the best screen in the building.**
- **Reaching for training as the answer to a systemic problem.**
- **Opposing a brand decision on partial evidence.** It confirms every stereotype about design governance.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A numeric reasoning assessment measures how accurately you handle quantitative material. At lead level the emphasis is almost wholly on interrogation. You will need to compute correctly, but the questions that matter ask whether a figure means what somebody claims — and in most rooms you enter, you may be the only person who will ask.

The professional stake is unusually high for your discipline, and worth naming. Visual decisions are the ones colleagues feel most entitled to have opinions about, which leaves design leadership perpetually at risk of being treated as taste. A measured value is the only thing that reliably converts a matter of preference into a matter of fact, and a lead who can say "that token measures 4.4:1, and it is used on eleven pages" is exercising authority that a lead who says "that grey is too light" is not.

The format is typically a timed online test of fifteen to thirty minutes, each item presenting a table, chart or paragraph of figures with a multiple-choice question. Calculators are usually provided and the arithmetic is tractable.

Four habits carry the load. Establish what the question asks before looking at the data. Identify the denominator and the population it represents. Estimate, calculate, compare. And sanity-check against the world.

Distractors come from a predictable catalogue: wrong denominator, percentage confused with percentage point, new value as the base in a percentage change, off-by-one errors in grid arithmetic, thresholds read as approximate, and rates averaged without weighting.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The numerical skills tested map onto your named skills:

- **Interrogating quantitative claims** maps to assuring the quality of design delivery across teams.
- **Aggregating across services** maps to **Evidence-based design**, where you analyse and use evidence to improve how the organisation works.
- **Comparing groups fairly** maps to **Designing for everyone**, where you measurably improve inclusive practice — and "measurably" is the operative word.
- **Threshold and headroom reasoning** maps to **Designing strategically**, where shared assets carry their properties everywhere.
- **Presenting figures at defensible strength** maps to **Design communication**, where you gain support from senior leaders.
- **Detecting misleading metrics** maps to **Evidence-based design**, where a wrong measure misdirects design effort across several teams at once.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Rate from counts**

Of 7,200 users who reached a page, 5,400 completed it. What percentage?

- A) 70%
- B) 75%
- C) 80%
- D) 85%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 5,400 ÷ 7,200 = 0.75, so 75%, and the answer is B. Estimate first: 5,400 of 7,200 is three quarters. Options A and C come from dividing by 7,714 and 6,750. The three-second estimate before every calculation is the cheapest available error insurance and costs nothing under a clock.

**Question 2 (easy) — Thresholds at the boundary**

A standard requires 4.5:1 for text under 24 pixels and 3:1 for text 24 pixels or larger. Four cases: 14px at 4.4:1; 18px at 4.5:1; 24px at 2.9:1; 28px at 3.0:1. How many meet the standard?

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 14px needs 4.5:1 — 4.4 fails. 18px needs 4.5:1 — 4.5 meets it exactly, and "at least" is inclusive, so it passes. 24px falls into the *larger* category because the wording is "24 or larger" rather than "over 24", so it needs 3:1 — 2.9 fails. 28px needs 3:1 — 3.0 passes. Two pass, giving B. Three boundary readings decide this item, and each is a real distinction: 4.4 fails though it rounds to 4; "at least" includes the value; and "24 or larger" includes 24. Reading them loosely produces both false failures, which waste effort and make standards look unreasonable, and false passes, which ship.

**Question 3 (easy) — Grid arithmetic**

A 16-column grid spans 1,440 pixels with 24-pixel gutters between columns and no outer margin. What is one column's width?

- A) 67.5 pixels
- B) 72 pixels
- C) 76.5 pixels
- D) 90 pixels

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Sixteen columns have fifteen gutters between them, not sixteen. The gutters take 15 × 24 = 360 pixels, leaving 1,440 − 360 = 1,080 for the columns, and 1,080 ÷ 16 = 67.5. So A. Option B follows from assuming sixteen gutters: 1,440 − 384 = 1,056, and 1,056 ÷ 16 = 66 — which is not offered, so the more likely route to B is a different slip; and option D is 1,440 ÷ 16, ignoring gutters, the commonest error. The off-by-one is worth fixing permanently: for n columns there are n − 1 internal gutters. At your level the consequence is not one wrong sum but a system definition whose numbers never reconcile, which every designer using it will notice and nobody will be able to explain.

**Question 4 (easy) — Centre and spread**

Time to locate a control, in seconds, across eleven participants: 3, 4, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 11, 14 and 96. Which summary is most defensible?

- A) Mean 15.2 seconds
- B) Median 7 seconds, range 3 to 96, with the 96-second case described separately
- C) Median 7 seconds
- D) Mean 15.2 seconds, range 3 to 96

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The values sum to 167, and 167 ÷ 11 = 15.2, so the mean is correct and exceeds ten of the eleven observations. The median is the sixth value, 7 seconds, which describes the group well. But the median alone conceals a session lasting more than thirteen times the typical duration, which is the most informative observation — something about the design defeated that person completely, and in a visual discipline that usually means a contrast, size or hierarchy failure the other ten worked around without noticing. Option B supplies centre, spread and explicit treatment of the outlier. Option D pairs the range with a misleading centre. The rule to write into your reporting conventions: a central figure never travels alone.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Weighted aggregation**

Four services report accessibility defect rates per hundred components: 2 on 500 components, 8 on 100, 4 on 200, and 6 on 200. What is the overall rate per hundred across all 1,000 components?

- A) 3.8
- B) 4.0
- C) 4.4
- D) 5.0

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Convert to counts. Service one: 2 per hundred on 500 components = 10 defects. Service two: 8 per hundred on 100 = 8. Service three: 4 per hundred on 200 = 8. Service four: 6 per hundred on 200 = 12. Total 38 defects across 1,000 components, so 3.8 per hundred, giving A. Option D is the unweighted mean of 2, 8, 4 and 6, which is exactly 5 and over-weights the 100-component service against the 500-component one that should dominate. Always return to counts. The organisational point matters more than the arithmetic: the unweighted figure would tell you the average *service* has 5 defects per hundred, while the weighted figure tells you the average *component* in the estate carries 3.8 — and only the second describes what users encounter.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Comparing groups**

Of 1,200 users on wide screens, 1,080 completed. Of 800 on narrow screens, 400 completed. What is the gap in percentage points?

- A) 30
- B) 40
- C) 50
- D) 90

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Wide: 1,080 ÷ 1,200 = 90%. Narrow: 400 ÷ 800 = 50%. The gap is 40 percentage points, giving B. Options C and D are the narrow rate and the wide rate offered as if they were the gap — a distractor pattern worth recognising, since figures from your working frequently appear among the options. A 40 point gap is enormous, and at your level the useful follow-up is not to investigate this service but to ask whether the same gap appears in others using the same component library, because a reflow failure in a shared component produces the same pattern everywhere it is used.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Percentage change**

Reported accessibility defects across the estate fell from 480 to 360 a quarter after a change to the review process. What is the percentage decrease?

- A) 20%
- B) 25%
- C) 33%
- D) 75%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The difference is 120, and 120 ÷ 480 = 0.25, a 25% decrease, giving B. Option C is 120 ÷ 360, dividing by the new figure. Option D is the new figure as a proportion of the old, a true statement answering a different question. The rule: for percentage change, the denominator is where you started. Worth adding a caution beyond the arithmetic, because this particular figure is treacherous: reported defects can fall because there are fewer defects or because fewer are being reported, and a review process change plausibly affects both. Before claiming a 25% improvement you would want to know whether the same people are looking as hard.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Quantifying impact across an estate**

Eleven services use a component whose focus indicator fails the contrast requirement. Combined annual sessions across those services are 4.2 million, and roughly 3% of sessions involve keyboard-only navigation. Roughly how many sessions a year are affected?

- A) 12,600
- B) 126,000
- C) 1,260,000
- D) 42,000

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 3% of 4,200,000 is 0.03 × 4,200,000 = 126,000, giving B. Option A is 3% of 420,000; option D is 1%. The reason this calculation belongs in a design guide is what it does to a conversation: "the focus indicator on this component is below 3:1" is a technical remark that competes for attention with everything else in a backlog, and "126,000 sessions a year in which the user cannot see where they are on the page" is not. Converting a shared-component defect into sessions is among the most effective moves available to you, precisely because the multiplier is what makes shared assets worth your attention in the first place.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Choosing the denominator**

A journey shows 60,000 start, 48,000 reach a form, 36,000 reach review, 34,200 submit. What proportion of those reaching review submitted?

- A) 57%
- B) 60%
- C) 71%
- D) 95%

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** The stem names users who reached review, so the denominator is 36,000 and the part is 34,200: 34,200 ÷ 36,000 = 0.95, giving D. Option A uses 60,000, answering a whole-journey question nobody asked. Strategically the funnel shows where effort belongs: 12,000 lost between start and form and 12,000 between form and review, against 1,800 at submission. A team proposing to redesign the review page would need a strong reason, and establishing that in fifteen seconds is what lets you redirect a plan in the meeting rather than after a sprint.

**Question 10 (harder) — A value with no headroom**

A proposed token measures 4.6:1 against white. The system contains six panel tints. What is the most useful observation?

- A) It passes, so it is fine.
- B) It passes against white by 0.1 and will fail against every tint, since contrast falls as the background moves towards the text — and because tokens are used wherever they look right rather than re-measured, a boundary value becomes a defect discovered on a page nobody audited.
- C) It should be tested on each of the six tints.
- D) The tints should be removed.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The strongest observation names the mechanism rather than the measurement. A value at 4.6:1 against white has 0.1 of margin, and any tint reduces the luminance difference and therefore the ratio. Because a token is available everywhere, nobody re-measures it in context — the convenience and the risk are the same property. Option B draws the practical conclusion, which is to require headroom in anything reusable, and that is a standard you can write rather than a check you must repeat. Option C is not wrong and is impractical as a standing rule, because new tints appear faster than audits do, which is exactly the argument for headroom over testing. Option A treats the threshold as the whole question. Option D removes the design to solve the problem.

**Question 11 (harder) — Percentage points and relative change**

Completion on narrow screens rose from 50% to 62% after a component fix. Which statement is accurate?

- A) A 12% increase.
- B) A 12 percentage point increase, equivalent to a 24% relative increase.
- C) A 24 percentage point increase.
- D) A 19.4% increase.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The arithmetic gap is 62 − 50 = 12 percentage points. The relative increase is 12 ÷ 50 = 0.24, or 24%. Option B labels both. Option A is ambiguous and generally read as relative. Option C misapplies the label. Option D divides by the new value. The professional stake is real: both descriptions describe one change, and one sounds twice as impressive. At your level the useful move is to make giving both a standing convention rather than a case-by-case judgement, because it removes the temptation entirely and protects your teams from any suggestion that the flattering figure was selected — which, in a discipline already suspected of advocacy, is worth more than the accuracy alone.

**Question 12 (harder) — A directionless metric**

After a type size increase across a service, time on page rose 18% and completion was unchanged. A director concludes the change slowed users down. What is the strongest analysis?

- A) Agree; larger text means more scrolling.
- B) Time on page has no direction — it rises when people struggle and when they read more of the content — so unchanged completion with longer dwell is as consistent with people reading text they previously skipped as with being slowed, and the way to distinguish them is whether errors or contact volume moved.
- C) Revert the change.
- D) The change failed.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Time on page is among the most misused metrics in interface work because it has no direction: a page people cannot read produces long dwell, and so does a page people can finally read. Unchanged completion rules out the most alarming interpretation. Option B names the ambiguity and identifies what resolves it — errors and contact volume, both directional. Option C reverts on an ambiguous measure, which is a specific hazard for legibility improvements because their benefits appear in comprehension and error rates rather than in speed, so a service that judges them on dwell time will systematically undo them. Option A adopts one interpretation without evidence. Option D declares failure on a metric that cannot support it, and at your level the important thing is to say so to the director before the conclusion becomes a decision.

### Preparation tips

- **Read thresholds at the boundary.** "At least 4.5:1" means 4.4 fails; "24 or larger" includes 24.
- **Remember n − 1 gutters for n columns.**
- **Return to counts before combining rates.** The weighted figure describes what users encounter.
- **Convert shared-component defects into sessions.** The multiplier is the argument.
- **Require headroom in reusable values.** It is a standard you can write rather than a check you must repeat.
- **Ask whether a fall in reported defects means fewer defects or less looking.**
- **Distrust directionless metrics.** Time on page rises for opposite reasons.
- **Make giving both framings a convention.** It removes the temptation and the suspicion.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Rounding a contrast ratio.**
- **Ignoring gutters, or counting one too many.**
- **Averaging service-level rates without weighting.**
- **Approving values at the threshold.**
- **Reverting a legibility improvement on dwell time.**
- **Confusing percentages with percentage points.**
- **Reporting a number from your working as though it answered the question.**
- **Claiming an improvement from reported-defect counts without asking who was looking.**

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately you read. At your level the documents that matter are governance frameworks, organisational standards, accessibility regulations, audit reports, brand guidelines and contracts. A misread exception clause becomes a compliance failure across several teams; a misread audit finding becomes advice given confidently to a director.

The classic format presents a short passage of dense workplace prose, typically three to six sentences, followed by statements to classify as True, False or Cannot Say. Alongside these come comprehension items, main-point items, and questions asking which of four summaries is most accurate.

The governing rule is that you answer from the passage alone, and the part specific to your level is this: expertise actively costs marks. The more accessibility criteria you have applied, the more readily you supply the clause you expect. Candidates at your level underperform on verbal reasoning at rates that surprise them, and this is nearly always the mechanism. The counter-habit must be mechanical: before marking anything True, put your finger on the words.

Cannot Say is the answer experienced practitioners avoid most, because forming a view under incomplete information is what the job consists of.

Small words carry the load, and one matters more in your discipline than in most: "or". Accessibility criteria routinely offer alternative routes separated by "or", and reading them as conjunctions produces needlessly dark palettes, unnecessary work, and arguments with whoever chose the brand — which costs credibility you will need for the requirements that are genuinely non-negotiable.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The verbal skills tested map onto your named skills:

- **Reading precisely** maps to setting direction and assuring the quality of design delivery across teams.
- **Separating statement from inference** maps to **Evidence-based design**.
- **Identifying the main point** maps to **Design communication**, where you gain support from senior leaders and communicate across boundaries.
- **Handling qualified language** maps to **Designing strategically**, where knowing which constraints are hard tells you where design can move.
- **Reading for absence** maps to **Designing for everyone**.
- **Reading governance prose accurately** maps to assuring across teams, where a misreading propagates.

### Practice questions

*Passage A — for Questions 1 to 4*

"Text smaller than 24 pixels must have a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 against its background. Text 24 pixels or larger, or 19 pixels or larger and bold, must have a contrast ratio of at least 3:1. Non-text elements that convey information, including icons and the boundaries of form fields, must have a contrast ratio of at least 3:1. Logotypes are exempt, as is text that is part of an inactive user interface component."

**Question 1 (easy)** — Statement: "All text must have a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The passage sets 3:1 for large and bold text and exempts logotypes and text in inactive components. The statement's "all" contradicts all three, so False. This is the most common real misquotation in the field — "everything needs 4.5:1" is the version most people carry — and at your level correcting it matters in both directions, because a team applying it to a large heading produces a needlessly dark palette and an avoidable argument with communications, which then makes the necessary arguments harder.

**Question 2 (easy)** — Statement: "Bold text at 20 pixels needs 4.5:1." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The second sentence sets 3:1 for text "24 pixels or larger, **or** 19 pixels or larger and bold". Bold text at 20 pixels satisfies the second route, so the threshold is 3:1 and the statement is contradicted. False. This is the "or" trap and the most consequential misreading in applying contrast criteria. Read as "and", the clause would require text to be both 24 pixels and bold, which would make it nearly useless. Read as written, it recognises that size and weight each aid legibility, and either qualifies.

**Question 3 (moderate)** — Statement: "The boundary of a disabled form field must meet 3:1." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** Two provisions bear on this and reading them together settles it. Non-text elements that convey information, expressly including the boundaries of form fields, must meet 3:1. The final sentence exempts *text* that is part of an inactive user interface component — and a field boundary is not text. So the exemption does not reach it, the 3:1 requirement stands, and the statement follows. True. The item rewards noticing exactly what an exemption is drafted for: this one covers the label of a disabled control, not the control's visible boundary, and a reader who generalises "inactive components are exempt" from the presence of the word "inactive" will answer False. In practice the distinction is consequential, because a disabled field whose boundary disappears is a field users cannot see at all, which is a different problem from a label they do not need to read.

**Question 4 (harder)** — Statement: "A decorative icon must meet 3:1." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The third sentence requires 3:1 for non-text elements *that convey information*, naming icons as an example of those. A decorative icon conveys no information, so it falls outside the stated requirement — but the passage does not say decorative elements are exempt, only where the requirement applies. Silence about the decorative case leaves it undetermined, so Cannot Say rather than False. The distinction between stating where a rule applies and stating where it does not recurs constantly in standards. In practice the useful move is to ask whether the icon is genuinely decorative, because designers describe icons as decorative considerably more often than users treat them that way.

*Passage B — for Questions 5 to 8*

"The audit of design practice found considerable variation between teams. Four teams run a contrast check before handover; the remainder check at review. Teams checking before handover reported fewer defects reaching live, although the audit did not examine whether their designs were more accessible overall. The audit recommends that the head of graphic design consider whether a common check point would be proportionate."

**Question 5 (easy)** — Statement: "Some teams do not run a contrast check before handover." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The passage states that four teams check before handover and the remainder check at review, establishing directly that some do not check before handover. True. Answer quickly. The only conceivable ambiguity — whether "the remainder" might be empty — is closed by the passage describing what the remainder does.

**Question 6 (moderate)** — Statement: "Checking before handover produces more accessible designs." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The passage reports fewer defects reaching live and then states explicitly that overall accessibility was not examined. Those are different things, and the gap is instructive: an earlier check might produce better designs, or might catch the same defects sooner — which produces the same statistic and is a genuine benefit — or those teams might differ in other ways entirely. The passage flags its own silence, so Cannot Say. This is the specific trap for someone in your position, because "checking earlier improves accessibility" is a claim you would like to be true and would probably cite when proposing a common check point. Citing it and being asked for the evidence is how a good proposal loses.

**Question 7 (moderate)** — Statement: "The audit recommends introducing a common check point." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The audit recommends that the head of graphic design *consider whether* a common check point would be proportionate, which includes concluding it would not be. The statement asserts a recommendation to introduce, which was not made, so False rather than Cannot Say. This pervades audit prose, and at your level you are likely to be advising the person the recommendation is addressed to — so misreading it commits your organisation to something nobody asked for and then reports delivery against it.

**Question 8 (harder)** — Statement: "Checking at review is a weaker practice than checking before handover." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The passage describes two practices and reports one difference in outcome while declining to assess overall accessibility. It makes no judgement about relative strength. A reader may reasonably infer that the earlier check is stronger, and the passage neither supports nor excludes that, so Cannot Say. What makes this harder than Question 6 is that the passage's structure leans towards the earlier check — mentioned first, associated with a favourable outcome — and structural lean is not content. Noticing when you are responding to a document's implied stance rather than its assertions is among the most transferable things this section trains, and audit reports routinely imply more than they will state.

*Passage C — for Questions 9 to 12*

"Interactive elements must have a visible focus indicator with a contrast ratio of at least 3:1 against adjacent colours. Touch targets must be at least 24 by 24 pixels, unless an equivalent target of at least 24 by 24 pixels is available elsewhere on the same screen. Where a component is provided in the design system, services must use it unless an exemption has been agreed with the head of graphic design. Components added to the system must be documented before use."

**Question 9 (easy)** — Statement: "Every touch target must be at least 24 by 24 pixels." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The requirement carries an exception: a smaller target is permitted where an equivalent target of at least 24 by 24 pixels is available elsewhere on the same screen. The statement's "every" drops that, so False. The exception exists for a practical reason: an inline link inside a paragraph cannot always be enlarged without breaking the text, and the requirement accepts that provided the same action is reachable from a properly sized control nearby. Knowing the exception is what stops your teams fighting an argument they should not be having.

**Question 10 (moderate)** — Statement: "A service using a custom component has breached the requirements." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The third sentence requires services to use the system component *unless an exemption has been agreed with the head of graphic design*. Whether an exemption was agreed is not stated, so the case is undetermined. Cannot Say. This is the rule-plus-exception structure appearing in reading form, and it rewards the same discipline as its logical counterpart — resisting the conclusion that feels obvious from the general rule when the facts are silent on the exception. At your level it has an additional edge: you are likely to be the person who would have granted the exemption, so the question of whether you did is one you can settle rather than infer, which is worth remembering before an accusation you have to withdraw.

**Question 11 (harder)** — Statement: "An undocumented component in the system has breached the requirements." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The final sentence requires components added to the system to be documented *before use*. A component that is in the system but undocumented has breached the requirement only if it has been used; if it has been added and not yet used, the deadline has not arrived. The statement does not say whether it is in use, so the case is undetermined. Cannot Say. This is a deadline-trigger item of the kind that appears throughout this section, and the specific reading matters: "before use" locates the obligation at the point of use rather than at the point of addition, which permits a component to sit in the system awaiting documentation without anyone being in breach.

**Question 12 (harder) — Main point**

Which best captures the main point of Passage C?

- A) Services must use design system components.
- B) Interactive elements must be perceivable and operable regardless of how a user perceives or points, and services should draw on documented shared components unless an exemption is agreed — with visible focus, adequately sized targets or equivalents, and documentation preceding use.
- C) Touch targets must be at least 24 by 24 pixels.
- D) Components must be documented before use.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The main point must cover the whole passage while adding nothing. Option A is one clause and drops its exemption. Options C and D are each true of one sentence, and C also drops the equivalence exception. Option B captures all four requirements and the logic joining them: two concern making elements usable by people who cannot see or point precisely, and two ensure those properties are inherited and understood rather than reinvented. Note that B preserves both the equivalence exception and the exemption — a summary dropping an exception is how a standard acquires a reputation for being unreasonable, and at your level you are the person who will have to defend it.

### Preparation tips

- **Point at the words.** Before marking True, identify the phrase that makes it true.
- **Read "or" as "or".** In accessibility criteria it offers alternative routes.
- **Check what an exemption actually covers.** One drafted for text does not extend to non-text elements.
- **Establish what a deadline is triggered by.** "Before use" is not "on addition".
- **Trust Cannot Say.** Especially where the claim would support a proposal you favour.
- **Distinguish stating where a rule applies from stating where it does not.**
- **Keep exceptions in summaries.**
- **Notice structural lean.** Order and favourable association are not assertions.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Answering from professional knowledge.** The dominant error at your level.
- **Reading "or" as "and".**
- **Extending an exemption beyond its stated subject.**
- **Marking True on a claim that would help your case.**
- **Treating a proxy outcome as the thing itself.** Fewer defects reaching live is not greater accessibility.
- **Converting a scope statement into an exemption.**
- **Forgetting the rule-plus-exception structure.**
- **Dropping an exception when summarising.**

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment presents realistic workplace scenarios and asks what you would do. There is no passage to parse and no arithmetic. You get a dilemma of the kind your role produces, four or five candidate responses, and a question asking which is most effective and often which is least.

This section measures whether your judgement matches what the profession considers good practice at your level. Your role places you as an expert practitioner who sets direction and assures design quality across teams, leads multiple or highly complex services, gains support for design strategy from senior leaders, coaches others, measurably improves inclusive practice, and creates the conditions in which designers can work well.

Three features distinguish your level.

The first is that you act on systems rather than instances. A graphic designer who fixes a failing colour has done their job; a lead who fixes it without asking why the token allowed it has done half of theirs.

The second is that your authority is largely informal, and in a visual discipline that is a specific difficulty. Everybody has opinions about colour and type in a way they do not about infrastructure, which means options relying on assertion of craft authority score poorly and options bringing measurement score well.

The third is that you absorb pressure downwards. When a director tells a designer their brand colour is non-negotiable, the designer should not be holding that line.

Four principles carry most scenarios. Users who cannot see, read or operate something come first. Bring the value, not the opinion. Fix the shared asset, not the instance. And calibrate escalation to severity.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The judgement dimensions map onto your named skills:

- **Assuring across teams** maps to setting direction and assuring the quality of design delivery.
- **Coaching and capability** map to **Leading design**, where you mentor and coach and create an inclusive, productive environment.
- **Influence with senior leaders** maps to **Design communication**, where you gain support for design decisions and strategy.
- **Organisational inclusion** maps to **Designing for everyone**, where you measurably improve inclusive practice across multiple teams.
- **Embedding practice** maps to **Iterative design**, where you iterate delivery processes and team structures.
- **Working across boundaries** maps to **Designing together**.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — A designer under pressure from a director**

A designer tells you a director has instructed them to use the brand's light grey for body text, which measures 3.2:1, and has said the brand is non-negotiable. What is the most effective response?

- A) Tell the designer to refuse and cite your instruction.
- B) Work out with them the alternative from inside the brand — a darker variant of the same hue — so they have something to offer rather than a refusal, and take the conversation with the director yourself.
- C) Contact the director and cite the accessibility standard.
- D) Approve the light grey.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Two things must happen: the text becomes readable, and the designer is not left arguing with a director about a brand. Option B does both, and the alternative matters — arriving with a darker variant of the same hue turns the conversation from a refusal into a substitution, and most brand palettes already contain one because the primary is optimised for impact rather than reading. Taking the conversation yourself is your job: a power differential of that size is what your role absorbs. Option D is least effective, publishing text some people cannot read because of who asked, and establishing that the argument works. Option A leaves a designer refusing a director by proxy. Option C leads with a standard rather than a solution, which is the version of this conversation that confirms every stereotype about design governance.

**Question 2 (easy) — A standard nobody follows**

Teams routinely use off-scale spacing despite a documented system. They describe the scale as too coarse. What is the strongest approach?

- A) Reissue the scale with a message from you.
- B) Review recent work with the teams to establish whether the scale genuinely lacks steps designs need or whether the tokens are simply unavailable in the tool they design in, then fix whichever it is — adding steps, or getting the tokens into the tool — and revise the scale with them.
- C) Introduce compliance checks.
- D) Run training on the scale.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A standard teams route around has already failed, and reissuing changes only the date. Option B treats the objection as data and names the two realistic causes, which imply completely different remedies: a genuinely coarse scale needs steps added, and unavailable tokens need a tooling fix. Diagnosing wrongly wastes a quarter and the credibility of the next attempt. Revising with the teams also produces authorship, which is the only mechanism by which a standard becomes self-sustaining rather than dependent on your attention. Option A is least effective, the purest form of appearing to act. Option C enforces a standard people have told you does not work. Option D is useful and insufficient alone.

**Question 3 (easy) — Conditions for the team**

Your graphic designers are embedded one per service across eight services, are pulled into delivery work, have no time for critique, and rarely see each other. Two have left in six months. What is the strongest response?

- A) Discuss workload with each delivery manager.
- B) Treat it as structural: establish a protected routine bringing designers together for critique, negotiate the time with delivery leadership by showing what the pattern costs in rework and attrition, and change how designers are deployed if single embedding is the cause.
- C) Introduce a wellbeing initiative.
- D) Recruit replacements quickly.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Two departures in six months alongside no critique and no professional contact is a structural pattern. Option B addresses it on three fronts, and the deployment clause matters particularly for this discipline: a graphic designer working alone has nobody to look at their work, and visual judgement degrades faster than most when unchecked — you stop seeing a design you have looked at for three days. So single embedding damages the work as directly as the people, and your assurance burden rises to compensate. The negotiation detail matters too: protected time not paid for in delivery's currency gets cancelled by the third sprint. Option D is least effective, reproducing the attrition on a lag in a small market where your reputation travels. Option A addresses symptoms individually. Option C treats a design-of-work problem as morale.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Assuring across a boundary**

A team you do not manage has shipped a component whose focus indicator measures 1.8:1. It is now in three services. What is most effective?

- A) Advise the three services to stop using it.
- B) Contact the designer and their lead with the measured value and a reproduction, agree who fixes it and by when, and check whether the component's focus style came from the system default or replaced it — while making clear this is live in three services.
- C) Raise it at the design community.
- D) Report it to the service owners.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** A focus indicator at 1.8:1 means keyboard users cannot see where they are in three services, so speed matters. Option B moves quickly while doing what distinguishes a lead's response: going to the people who can fix it, with a measured value rather than an impression, and asking the diagnostic question — because a failing custom focus style has usually replaced a compliant system default, in which case reinstating it fixes everything in an afternoon and the same substitution has probably happened elsewhere. Option C is least effective, deferring a live accessibility defect to a scheduled forum and broadcasting a team's error before they can fix it. Option A bypasses the people who can fix the component. Option D escalates before asking.

**Question 5 (moderate) — A recurring problem across teams**

Six teams keep producing colour-only status indicators. Each says the component library offers coloured chips and nothing else. What is most effective?

- A) Remind teams that meaning must not be conveyed by colour alone.
- B) Accept the diagnosis they have given you — the library offers no non-colour variant — and fix the library by adding chips with icons or text, since six teams using the supplied component correctly are not the problem.
- C) Add colour-only status to the review checklist.
- D) Require each team to add an icon.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Six teams giving the same explanation, and a plausible one, is unusually good diagnostic information — they have told you where the problem is. Option B acts on it, and the framing matters: teams using a supplied component as supplied have not made a mistake, and treating them as though they had is both unfair and ineffective, because they will keep using what the library gives them. Fixing the library removes the problem for every service including those that have not built the page yet. Option A is least effective and is the weakest available response to any recurring problem, telling six teams a principle they already know and cannot act on. Option D fixes six instances and leaves the seventh team to repeat it. Option C adds a check for a defect the system generates.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Competing directorates**

Two directors want your design capacity in the same quarter. One has a failing live service; the other a ministerially visible launch. Both have raised it with your director. What is most effective?

- A) Split capacity.
- B) Set out for each what design work would deliver, what decision it informs, and the cost of deferral; recommend a sequence with reasoning; and put that recommendation to both directors and your director together rather than negotiating separately.
- C) Prioritise the visible launch.
- D) Ask your director to decide.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A prioritisation problem is being conducted as a political one, and the way through is to make the trade-off explicit and simultaneously visible. Option B does the analytical work and handles the politics correctly: putting the recommendation to both at once prevents the parallel-negotiation dynamic in which each director hears something slightly different and trust collapses when they compare notes. Making a recommendation rather than presenting options is essential; you are the expert on what design can deliver. Option A is least effective — splitting constrained design capacity between two substantial needs produces two efforts too thin to inform either decision, and in a visual discipline it produces something worse than that, because half-finished design work looks finished and gets built. Option C ranks by visibility. Option D discards your judgement.

**Question 7 (moderate) — A brand decision already made**

A brand refresh has been approved. Two of the eight new colours fail 4.5:1 against white, and the palette has not been checked against the tinted panels in your system. What is most effective?

- A) Oppose the refresh.
- B) Accept the decision and make it workable: establish exactly which colours fail in which contexts, propose a usage specification saying where each colour may be used — headings, non-text elements, panels — and identify the darker variants needed for body text, framing it as completing the palette rather than correcting it.
- C) Require the palette to be re-designed.
- D) Document the risk and proceed.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** The decision is made and reopening it will fail, so the useful work is making it workable. Option B does that, and the framing is what makes it succeed: a usage specification is a normal part of any brand palette, so proposing one is completing the work rather than correcting somebody's judgement, and it lets you say where each colour *can* be used rather than only where it cannot. Most palettes need this anyway. Option D is least effective — documenting a risk with nothing following it is how an exclusion becomes permanent while everyone feels they have handled it, and your name is on the register entry. Option A opposes a decision already taken and spends standing for nothing. Option C asks for a redesign that will be refused.

**Question 8 (harder) — Inclusion practice that is not improving**

After a year of guidance, templates and talks, components still ship with contrast failures. What is the strongest response?

- A) Produce clearer guidance and repeat the training.
- B) Stop adding guidance and find out what is preventing it — most likely that nobody checks before handover, or that checking is manual and easy to skip, or that failures are found only in an audit months later — then put a check where the work already flows, ideally automated so it does not depend on anyone remembering.
- C) Introduce a mandatory accessibility checklist.
- D) Report the failure to your director.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A year of guidance that has not changed practice is evidence that the barrier is not knowledge, and continuing to supply knowledge is the characteristic failure of well-intentioned improvement programmes. Option B does the diagnostic work first and names the realistic blockers, all concerning *when and how* checking happens rather than whether people know it should. The remedy it points at is the strongest available in this discipline: contrast is one of the few design properties that can be checked automatically, so a check in the pipeline removes reliance on discipline entirely and catches defects when they cost minutes. Option A is least effective, repeating an intervention that demonstrably did not work and spending the credibility of the next one. Option C adds a checklist to a system already full of guidance. Option D reports a failure you are accountable for solving.

**Question 9 (harder) — Consensus that would exclude people**

A team you advise has converged on a status system using colour alone. Everyone likes it; your evidence indicates it will fail users with colour vision deficiency. What is most effective?

- A) Let it go.
- B) Raise it once with the arithmetic — that across this service the affected group runs into tens of thousands a year — propose the minimal mitigation of a paired icon or text, and if the team proceeds ensure the risk is documented and propose measuring the outcome.
- C) Escalate to the service owner.
- D) Keep raising it.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** You have an obligation to say the thing and you also have to keep advising these people. Option B threads that, and two details matter: leading with arithmetic rather than principle makes the point unarguable, and proposing the minimal mitigation makes it acceptable — "add an icon" is a change a team will accept where "rethink the status system" is one they will resist. If the decision stands, documenting the risk makes it knowing, and measuring gives reality a vote. Option A is least effective, because a consensus excluding people who already have fewer options is not a value. Option C escalates before the direct route. Option D substitutes persistence for persuasion, and an adviser who relitigates everything stops being heard.

**Question 10 (harder) — A token you approved**

Nine months ago you approved a token now used in four services. You realise it measures 4.4:1 rather than the 4.6:1 recorded. What should you do?

- A) Note it for the next token review.
- B) Notify all four services now, state the measured value and where the token is used, correct it, and check whether other tokens approved in the same session were measured the same way — since the error suggests a method problem rather than a slip.
- C) Correct the token quietly.
- D) Leave it; 4.4 is close.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** A token is used everywhere it is available, so 4.4:1 is a failure across four services. Option B does the four things that matter — notifies, states the value rather than describing the problem, corrects it, and asks the diagnostic question that distinguishes a lead's response from a fix. A mis-measured token usually means it was checked against the wrong background or with the wrong tool, and if so everything approved alongside it is suspect. Option D is least defensible: "close" applied once to a threshold becomes the standard for every future judgement, and thresholds work precisely because they have no discretionary zone. Option C leaves both published work and method unexamined. Option A defers a live failure.

**Question 11 (harder) — Working across a boundary**

A technology directorate has adopted a component library whose focus styles fail your standard. Five services already use it. What is strongest?

- A) Require them to stop using the library.
- B) Establish precisely which components fail and how, propose a shortlist to override with compliant styles — which for focus indicators is usually a single global change — and a route to remediate the five services, framing it as making their choice workable.
- C) Escalate to your director.
- D) Document the risk and proceed.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** A library is almost never uniformly bad, and focus styles in particular are usually set once globally rather than per component — which makes this a considerably cheaper fix than it first appears and is exactly the detail worth establishing before the conversation. Option B does that, converting an unwinnable argument about a decision already made into a tractable and small piece of work. Option D is least effective: documenting a risk with nothing following is how an exclusion becomes permanent. Option A asks for reversal without a route, and five services cannot start again. Option C escalates a solvable disagreement across a boundary you will need repeatedly.

**Question 12 (harder) — A standard that will be resisted**

You intend to require that every token entry state its contrast against every background in the system. You anticipate resistance: teams will say the combinations are too numerous. What is strongest?

- A) Introduce it and handle objections as they arise.
- B) Engage the objection before introducing it, because it is partly right: the full matrix is large. Frame the requirement as stating contrast against the backgrounds the token is *approved for*, with a rule that using it elsewhere requires a check — and pair it with a tool or script that generates the matrix, so the work is automated rather than manual.
- C) Introduce it as guidance.
- D) Require it only for text tokens.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** The objection is real: eight tokens against six backgrounds is forty-eight combinations, and requiring each to be documented manually is a job nobody will do twice. Option B resolves the tension by changing what is required — approved backgrounds rather than all backgrounds — which is both feasible and more useful, because it tells the next designer where the token may be used rather than burying that in a matrix. The automation clause is the other half, since contrast is one of the few design properties a script can compute, and generating the matrix removes the objection entirely. Option A is least effective: introducing a requirement you know to be contestable and arguing it case by case spends enormous credibility for a worse outcome. Option C removes the force. Option D exempts non-text tokens, where the 3:1 requirement applies and is most often forgotten.

### Preparation tips

- **Bring the value, not the opinion.** Your authority in a visual discipline rests on measurement.
- **Absorb pressure downwards.** A designer should not be arguing with a director about a brand.
- **Arrive with the alternative from inside the constraint.** A darker variant beats a refusal.
- **Take a team's diagnosis seriously when six of them give the same one.**
- **Ask whether a failing style replaced a compliant default.** It is frequently a one-line fix.
- **Automate what can be automated.** Contrast is one of the few design properties a script can check.
- **Propose the minimal mitigation.** "Add an icon" is accepted where "redesign it" is not.
- **Frame corrections as completing work rather than correcting judgement.**

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Reissuing a standard teams route around.**
- **Reminding teams of a principle they already know and cannot act on.**
- **Documenting a risk and calling it management.**
- **Opposing a decision already taken.** It spends standing and confirms a stereotype.
- **Splitting design capacity.** Half-finished visual work looks finished and gets built.
- **Treating "close enough" as a threshold.**
- **Leading with a standard rather than a solution.**
- **Adding a check for a defect the system generates.**

## Conclusion

You have reached the end, and it is worth registering the scale: forty-eight questions across four assessment types, with the reasoning worked through on each. That is a substantial piece of professional preparation and more than most people at your level undertake.

Look back at the ground. In the cognitive section you read a finding that matched its test conditions too neatly, weighted a token decision by the seven services that would inherit it, asked why six teams independently made the same choice, resolved an apparent contradiction between two studies of the same grey, and treated a pre-handover check as a transferable routine rather than a story about talent. In the numeric section you read thresholds at their boundaries, remembered that sixteen columns have fifteen gutters, weighted rather than averaged, converted a shared-component defect into 126,000 sessions a year, and declined to let a directionless metric undo a legibility improvement. In the verbal section you read "or" as offering alternative routes, established that an exemption drafted for text does not reach a field boundary, noticed that "before use" locates an obligation at use rather than addition, and declined to mark True on a claim that would have supported your own proposal. And in the situational judgement section you absorbed a director's pressure, fixed a component library rather than reminding six teams of a principle, made a brand decision workable rather than opposing it, and designed a documentation requirement around its strongest and truest objection.

Two threads run through it. The first is whether you know the limits of the evidence and will say so — including, and especially, when the claim would support something you want. The second is whether you act on shared assets rather than instances. Between them those are most of what your role consists of. Teams can produce screens without you. What you supply is a set of shared properties that can be trusted wherever they are used, and conditions in which that is the normal result.

A word about the particular difficulty of your level. Visual authority is the weakest kind, because everybody has eyes and therefore opinions. That is why measurement matters disproportionately in your role and why the numeric section of this guide is not an aside. It is also why the errors that cost you most are the confident ones: a design you have looked at for three days stops being something you can see, and a value you measured once stops being something you re-check.

If you want to build on this, a few things repay effort more than rereading. Measure every token in your system against every background it will meet, and see how many have no headroom. Complete a service you are responsible for using only a keyboard and watch the focus indicator. View it at 400% zoom, at 375 pixels, and on a phone outdoors. Ask whether a contrast check could be automated in your pipeline, and if the answer is yes, do that before anything else in this list. And go and watch what your strongest team does before assuming you know what it is.

Finally, for the day itself. An assessment samples your reasoning on one morning under artificial conditions. It is not a verdict on your judgement or your career. Read the stem twice, read thresholds at the boundary, find the denominator, trust Cannot Say, and remember that the strongest answer is almost always the one honest about uncertainty and aimed at the shared asset rather than the instance.

Good luck. Go carefully, measure things, and keep looking at the edges.
