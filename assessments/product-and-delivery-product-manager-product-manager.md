# Product Manager - Psychometric Assessment Resources

## Introduction

Welcome! This guide has been created for you as a product manager working within the UK Government Digital and Data profession. Whether you are preparing for an internal assessment, applying for a new post, benchmarking your own capability, or simply curious about how psychometric assessments relate to your day-to-day work, this document will help you practise, reflect, and build confidence.

Psychometric assessments are structured, standardised exercises that measure the mental capabilities and behavioural tendencies that predict success in a role. For a product manager, that means far more than abstract puzzles. The assessments described here are job-specific: they use the artefacts you genuinely handle — prioritised roadmaps, key performance indicators, benefit analyses, user research findings, service standard assessments, stakeholder communications, and sprint data — and the decisions you genuinely make, such as prioritising work for your teams, deciding when a product should move to the next life cycle phase, escalating risks to value, and mediating between stakeholders whose needs pull in different directions.

Why do these assessments matter for your role in particular? As a product manager, you are responsible for product quality and value. You lead a product through the full life cycle, from discovery through development, delivery, and continuous improvement, to retirement. You balance user needs and business needs, you use trusted data to make decisions, and you are open about how you make them. Assessments of cognitive ability, numeric reasoning, verbal reasoning, and situational judgement map directly onto those demands. Practising them sharpens exactly the capabilities your role depends on: spotting logical inconsistencies in a roadmap, interpreting a KPI dashboard accurately under time pressure, extracting precise meaning from a policy document or stakeholder email, and choosing the most effective course of action when the team, the data, and the stakeholders point in different directions.

This document is organised into four main assessment sections, each following the same pattern: an overview of what the assessment measures and why employers use it for product roles, a mapping of the assessment's dimensions to the specific named skills in your role — from Agile and Lean practices to Strategic ownership — a substantial set of practice questions progressing from easy to challenging with full worked explanations, practical preparation tips, and common pitfalls to avoid.

Fourth, use the preparation tips and pitfalls for self-reflection: they connect assessment performance to your professional development as someone who prioritises with evidence, works in the open, and protects both your users and your team.

One reassurance before you begin: nothing here requires knowledge beyond your role. If you can reason clearly about a roadmap, a completion rate, a research summary, and a difficult stakeholder meeting — and you do all of those things already — then you can do well on these assessments. Practice converts capability into confidence.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive ability assessment measures the exact mental processes your role demands, using practical workplace scenarios rather than abstract puzzles. Instead of shape sequences, it presents the everyday materials of product management — roadmaps, backlogs, KPI definitions, life cycle plans, dependency maps, benefit chains — and asks you to reason quickly and accurately about them.

The typical format is an online, timed test lasting 15 to 30 minutes, with 15 to 30 questions covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective, comparing your responses against a norm group at a comparable level, and many modern platforms adapt question difficulty to your performance as you go. Employers usually receive a breakdown of speed versus accuracy rather than a single score, so a steady, careful pace beats a frantic one. Most platforms provide short, ungraded practice questions before the real test so you can settle into the interface without pressure.

For a product manager, cognitive assessment is particularly relevant because your core professional value is structured decision-making amid competing signals. You address uncertainty while managing scope, ensuring critical deadlines are met and value delivered. That requires holding several moving parts in mind at once: which backlog items serve which outcomes, which dependencies constrain which releases, where a stakeholder claim contradicts the analytics, and what follows for the roadmap when one assumption changes. You also carry a quality-assurance responsibility — ensuring teams comply with appropriate standards, and ensuring the product is developed in line with the right service standards for its phase — which is fundamentally an error-checking discipline. A well-designed cognitive assessment simulates precisely those demands in miniature, which is why performance on it predicts performance in the role.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The assessment dimensions map directly to the named skills in your role summary:

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **Agile and Lean practices** and **Managing product outcomes**: helping teams measure, evaluate, and visualise outcomes means spotting recurring signals in sprint data, retrospective themes, and KPI movements — and distinguishing genuine trends from noise.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Product management** and **Creating value for money**: addressing uncertainty while meeting critical deadlines, and analysing the benefit of investment against anticipated outcomes, both require reasoning validly from evidence and constraints to their consequences — and no further.
- **Error checking** maps to **Life cycle management** and your duty to **ensure teams comply with appropriate standards**: verifying that a product is developed in line with the right service standards for its phase, and that roadmaps, KPIs, and benefit cases are internally consistent, is systematic inconsistency-hunting.
- **Prioritisation** maps to **Creating value for money** and your duty to **make decisions on priorities for your teams**: comparing effort to value, sequencing under constraint, and deciding when to escalate risk are ranking problems solved under time pressure.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Applying user-centred insights** and **Product management**: investigating and validating what adds value now and in future means decomposing messy, partly contradictory information into a decision the team can act on.

Every question below uses artefacts from this list, so practising the assessment technique doubles as rehearsal for the job itself.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern recognition in sprint data**

Your team's last six sprints completed the following story points: 20, 21, 19, 20, 12, 20. During one of those sprints, two developers were seconded to a departmental incident response for a week. Which sprint most plausibly corresponds to the secondment, and what does the pattern suggest about the team's underlying velocity?

- A) Sprint 5; underlying velocity is around 20 points, with sprint 5 an explained outlier.
- B) Sprint 5; underlying velocity is around 18.7 points, the average of all six sprints.
- C) Sprint 3; velocity is declining steadily.
- D) No sprint stands out; the data is too noisy to interpret.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Five of the six sprints cluster tightly between 19 and 21 points; sprint 5's 12 points sits far outside that cluster and coincides naturally with the explained capacity loss. The underlying velocity — what the team delivers at normal capacity — is therefore around 20 points. Option B makes a subtle but important error: including a known, explained outlier in a planning average drags the figure down to 18.7 and misrepresents the team's real capacity. When you help teams measure, evaluate, and visualise outcomes, distinguishing signal (the stable cluster) from explained noise (the secondment) is exactly this judgement. Option C invents a decline the data does not show, and option D gives up on data that is actually unusually clean. Assessment technique: before computing anything, look at the shape of the data — clusters and outliers first, arithmetic second.

**Question 2 (easy) — Logical deduction from a phase gate rule**

Your governance framework states: "A service may move from private beta to public beta only if it has passed a service standard assessment and its accessibility audit has no outstanding high-severity issues." Your service has passed its service standard assessment. Which one of the following must be true?

- A) The service may move to public beta.
- B) The service may not move to public beta.
- C) The service may move to public beta if and only if its accessibility audit has no outstanding high-severity issues.
- D) The accessibility audit has been completed.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The rule sets two jointly necessary conditions for the phase move. One condition (the assessment) is confirmed; the other (the accessibility audit position) is unknown. So you cannot conclude the move is permitted (option A) or forbidden (option B) — what remains is precisely that the second condition is now decisive, which is option C. Option D confuses "no outstanding high-severity issues" being required with the audit having occurred; the passage says nothing about whether it has. This reasoning pattern is the daily texture of **Life cycle management**: identifying if the product should move to the next phase means checking every gate condition explicitly, not stopping at the first favourable fact. In assessments as in governance, the discipline is the same — enumerate the conditions, mark each as satisfied, failed, or unknown, and conclude only what the marks support.

**Question 3 (easy) — Error checking a KPI definition**

Your team drafts four KPIs for the renewals service. Which one is not actually measurable as written?

- A) "Percentage of renewals completed online without assisted digital support, reported monthly."
- B) "Median time from starting a renewal to receiving confirmation, reported monthly."
- C) "Users feel more confident about the renewals process."
- D) "Number of support calls per 1,000 completed renewals, reported monthly."

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Options A, B, and D each define a quantity (a percentage, a median duration, a rate), a population, and a reporting cadence — you could hand any of them to an analyst today. Option C names a worthy outcome but no measure: "feel more confident" specifies no instrument (survey question? scale?), no baseline, no population, and no cadence. It is an outcome statement wearing a KPI's clothes. The fix is not to discard it — confidence may genuinely matter — but to operationalise it, for example "percentage of post-completion survey respondents rating confidence 4 or 5 out of 5, versus a pre-launch baseline". Working with others to define KPIs that assess performance, value, and outcomes is a named part of your **Managing product outcomes** skill, and the most common failure you will police is exactly this one: aspirations recorded where measures should be. In error-checking questions, test each item against a concrete question — "could an analyst compute this tomorrow?" — rather than judging plausibility.

**Question 4 (easy) — Prioritisation by value and effort**

You have four candidate roadmap items with team estimates:

- Item 1: value 8, effort 2
- Item 2: value 8, effort 8
- Item 3: value 3, effort 2
- Item 4: value 5, effort 5

Using value-to-effort ratio as the ordering rule, what is the correct priority order?

- A) 1, 2, 3, 4
- B) 1, 3, 4, 2
- C) 2, 1, 4, 3
- D) 1, 4, 3, 2

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Compute each ratio. Item 1: 8 ÷ 2 = 4.0. Item 2: 8 ÷ 8 = 1.0. Item 3: 3 ÷ 2 = 1.5. Item 4: 5 ÷ 5 = 1.0. Ordering by ratio gives Item 1 (4.0) first, Item 3 (1.5) second, then Items 4 and 2 tied at 1.0. The tie is broken sensibly by preferring the smaller commitment first (Item 4 at effort 5 before Item 2 at effort 8), since smaller items deliver feedback and value sooner for the same ratio — giving 1, 3, 4, 2, option B. Option A ranks by value alone; option D places Item 4 above Item 3 despite the lower ratio. Two professional notes travel with this: first, comparing effort to value when deciding priorities is named in your **Creating value for money** skill, and ratio thinking is its arithmetic core; second, in real life the ratio is an input to judgement, not a substitute for it — dependencies, deadlines, and risk can all justify departing from pure ratio order, but you should know what the ratio order is before you depart from it.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Deduction across roadmap dependencies**

Your roadmap constraints are: (i) the identity integration must be live before the save-and-return feature; (ii) save-and-return and the redesigned dashboard must launch in the same quarter, because both change the account area and must be tested together; (iii) the identity integration cannot go live before Q2, when the supplier contract starts; (iv) the dashboard redesign needs one full quarter of design work, which can begin no earlier than Q1. What is the earliest quarter in which save-and-return can launch?

- A) Q1
- B) Q2
- C) Q3
- D) Q4

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Chain the constraints forward. Identity cannot be live before Q2 — earliest, live during Q2. Save-and-return requires identity live first, so save-and-return cannot launch before Q3. Check the dashboard constraint does not push later: design work can start in Q1, takes one quarter, so the dashboard can be ready to launch from Q2 onwards — and can therefore launch alongside save-and-return in Q3, satisfying the same-quarter rule. Nothing forces Q4. The earliest launch is Q3, option C. The common errors: choosing Q2 by treating "identity live in Q2" as compatible with "save-and-return launches in Q2", when rule (i) requires identity before save-and-return; or choosing Q4 by serialising the dashboard work after identity unnecessarily — the two streams run in parallel. This is precisely the reasoning behind creating a prioritised roadmap you can defend to stakeholders: the sequence is not a preference, it is the logical consequence of dependencies, and being able to walk a stakeholder through that chain is what makes the roadmap persuasive.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Error checking a benefits chain**

A business case for automating document checks claims: "Automation will process checks faster → caseworkers will spend less time on routine checks → caseworker time will be redirected to complex cases → complex-case backlogs will fall → citizen waiting times for complex decisions will improve." A colleague asks you to identify the weakest link before the case goes to the investment board. Which link deserves the most scrutiny?

- A) Automation → faster processing of routine checks
- B) Faster routine processing → less caseworker time on routine checks
- C) Less routine time → time redirected to complex cases
- D) Falling complex backlogs → improved citizen waiting times

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Links A and B are close to mechanical: if automation works, routine checks consume less caseworker time. Link D is near-definitional: complex-case waiting times are largely a function of the complex backlog. Link C is where benefit cases habitually fail, because it assumes freed time automatically converts into redirected productive time. In practice, freed capacity can be absorbed by vacancies, leak into other duties, be removed as a headcount saving, or require complex-case training the caseworkers do not yet have. Without a management commitment and a mechanism — named teams, training plans, workforce decisions — the redirection is a hope, not a benefit. Analysing the benefit of investment against anticipated outcomes, part of your **Creating value for money** skill, means finding exactly this kind of category jump: the chain moves from a technical effect to an organisational choice while presenting both as automatic. In the assessment, scan causal chains for the link where a different kind of actor must act; that is almost always the weak one.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Prioritisation under competing pressures**

It is Monday morning. Four items compete for your attention:

1. The analytics dashboard shows completions of your service fell 30% over the weekend, cause unknown.
2. A service standard assessment for your beta is scheduled Thursday; your team's evidence pack needs your final review, which takes about two hours.
3. A senior stakeholder has asked for a meeting "this week" to discuss adding a new feature to the roadmap.
4. Your fortnightly team retrospective is tomorrow morning, and you usually prepare discussion prompts.

Which sequencing is most defensible?

- A) 2, 3, 1, 4 — governance and stakeholders before operational noise
- B) 1, 2, 4, 3 — investigate the completion drop first, then the Thursday assessment pack, then tomorrow's retro prep, then schedule the stakeholder meeting
- C) 3, 2, 1, 4 — senior stakeholders always come first
- D) 4, 1, 2, 3 — team rituals protect everything else

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Rank by impact-weighted urgency. Item 1 is a live service signal: a 30% completion drop could mean users are failing to access a government service right now, and the cause is unknown — triage cannot wait, even if the investigation itself is delegated to the team within the hour. Item 2 has a hard Thursday deadline and a known two-hour cost; it comes next, comfortably schedulable once the incident is triaged. Item 4 is tomorrow morning, small, and follows. Item 3 says "this week" — the right Monday action is a scheduling reply, which takes two minutes and can happen at any point, but as a block of work it ranks last. Option A treats a possible live user-facing failure as "operational noise" below an undated meeting; option C ranks by seniority rather than impact; option D protects ritual over users. The professional principle is one your role summary names twice over: outcomes for users come first, and protecting the team includes protecting them from a Thursday assessment arriving unprepared — both are served by sequence B.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Pattern recognition across user feedback channels**

In the same fortnight, you observe: (a) support calls mentioning "code not arriving" rose from ~40 to ~130 per week; (b) app-store-style feedback on the service's sign-in page shows new one-star comments mentioning text messages; (c) analytics show sign-in success down 9 percentage points; (d) your SMS provider's status page reports "degraded delivery in some regions" starting twelve days ago. What is the most reasonable synthesis?

- A) Four unrelated issues that should each be logged separately.
- B) The evidence converges on degraded SMS delivery harming sign-in: the provider incident is the plausible common cause, and the team should verify SMS delivery rates, engage the supplier, and consider a fallback channel.
- C) Users have become less patient; no product action is needed.
- D) The analytics drop proves the sign-in redesign failed and should be rolled back.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This is triangulation — the same skill as reading research, applied to operational signals. Each channel is individually ambiguous: calls could be seasonal, comments could be a vocal minority, a 9-point analytics drop could have many causes. But all four point at one mechanism (SMS codes not arriving), and signal (d) supplies a common cause with matching timing. The synthesis in option B also does what good product reasoning must: it states the hypothesis, then names the verification step (measure actual SMS delivery rates) before committing to remedies — conclusions drive checks, checks drive action. Option A wastes the convergence; treating correlated symptoms as independent tickets is how outages persist for weeks. Option C explains away quantitative movement with an evidence-free story. Option D attributes the drop to a redesign the scenario never mentions — imported assumptions are as dangerous in assessments as in incident reviews. Using trusted data to make decisions, and using insights to represent users in internal discussions, both begin with this: assemble the signals, find the mechanism that explains them all, verify, then act.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Applied problem solving on reuse**

Your team needs a booking component. Three options emerge: build bespoke (estimated 60 developer-days); adopt a cross-government component used by eleven services (10 days integration, but it does not support one of your requirements — recurring appointments — which its roadmap schedules for next quarter); or reuse code from a neighbouring team's abandoned alpha (25 days, unmaintained, no accessibility testing history). Recurring appointments affect about 15% of your bookings and are not needed until your public beta, two quarters away. What is the strongest recommendation?

- A) Build bespoke — full control, no compromises.
- B) Adopt the cross-government component now, plan for recurring appointments when its next-quarter roadmap delivers, and agree a fallback with the component team in case that slips.
- C) Reuse the abandoned alpha code — 25 days is cheaper than 60.
- D) Delay the decision until recurring appointments are needed.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Work the options against the constraints. The bespoke build costs six times the integration effort and forgoes the maintenance, accessibility assurance, and shared improvement that eleven live services fund — option A pays a large premium for a requirement gap that is, on the facts, temporary and two quarters away. The abandoned alpha (option C) is the classic false economy: "unmaintained, no accessibility testing history" means the 25 days is a down payment, with the team inheriting sole responsibility for a codebase of unknown quality — deciding when it is not appropriate to reuse something is as much part of your **Creating value for money** skill as spotting reuse opportunities. Option D confuses deferring a feature with deferring the component decision the whole booking journey needs now. Option B fits the timeline: the gap affects 15% of bookings, is not needed until public beta, and the component's roadmap covers it a quarter early — while the agreed fallback converts the residual risk (their roadmap slips) into a managed contingency rather than a surprise. That final move is the mark of a strong recommendation: name the risk you are accepting and pre-agree what happens if it lands.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Multi-constraint scheduling**

You must schedule four activities in the next six weeks (weeks 1–6): a discovery review (D), a stakeholder workshop (W), a service assessment dry run (R), and the service assessment itself (A). Constraints: (i) A is fixed in week 6; (ii) R must happen exactly two weeks before A; (iii) W needs the discovery review's outputs, which take one full week to write up after D; (iv) the room and facilitator for W are only available in weeks 2 and 5; (v) at most one activity per week. In which week must the stakeholder workshop take place?

- A) Week 2
- B) Week 3
- C) Week 5
- D) Either week 2 or week 5

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Fix the anchors first. A is in week 6, so R is in week 4 (exactly two weeks before). W must be in week 2 or week 5 (constraint iv). Test week 2: W in week 2 requires D's outputs, written up over one full week after D — so D would need to happen in week 1 at the latest, with the write-up occupying... but check: if D is week 1, the write-up takes the following full week (week 2), meaning outputs are ready at the end of week 2, after the workshop has already happened. Week 2 therefore fails: there is no week available for D early enough. Test week 5: D can be in week 1, 2, or 3 (weeks 4 and 6 are taken by R and A), the write-up completes a week later (end of week 2, 3, or 4 respectively), all before week 5 — and one-activity-per-week holds, e.g. D week 1, W week 5. So W must be in week 5, option C. The method — anchor the fixed events, propagate the relative constraints, then test each candidate slot for feasibility rather than plausibility — is exactly how you pressure-test a delivery plan before promising dates to stakeholders. Option D is the trap for solvers who check that week 2 is on the permitted list but not whether it is achievable.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Spotting the flaw in a metrics argument**

At a quarterly review, a colleague argues: "Since we introduced the new onboarding flow, average session duration has increased 40%. Users are clearly more engaged, so the flow is a success and we should extend the same design to the renewals journey." Which is the best critique of this argument?

- A) The argument is sound: a 40% increase is too large to be chance.
- B) Session duration is ambiguous for a government service — longer sessions may mean engagement or may mean users are stuck; the argument also assumes causation from timing alone, and "success" should be tested against task-completion and user-feedback measures before the design is extended.
- C) The argument is wrong because session duration always indicates confusion.
- D) The argument is irrelevant because engagement does not matter for government services.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** There are two stacked flaws, and option B names both. First, metric validity: for transactional government services, users succeed by finishing, not by lingering — so rising duration is directionally ambiguous; it could signal richer engagement or a flow that traps people. The disambiguating evidence is exactly what B prescribes: completion rates, error loops, drop-off points, and what users say. Second, causal inference: "since we introduced X, Y rose" is sequence, not causation — seasonality, a policy change driving new user types, or an unrelated content change could each explain the shift. Options C and D make the mirror-image errors of the original: C replaces one absolute reading of an ambiguous metric with another, and D throws out measurement entirely. This question rehearses a duty your role summary makes explicit: analyse and use trusted data — where "trusted" means validated for the decision at hand — and identify success metrics that genuinely track outcomes. Before any metric drives a scaling decision, ask the two questions in order: does this metric mean what the argument needs it to mean, and did our change actually move it?

**Question 12 (challenging) — Reconciling three sources under a deadline**

Thursday's investment board will decide whether your product proceeds to public beta. You hold three artefacts. The performance report says: "All four KPIs met their private beta targets in each of the last two months." The user research summary says: "Participants using screen readers could not complete the eligibility step in three of four recent sessions." The service assessment report, from five weeks ago, rated the service "met" on all standards, noting "accessibility testing in progress; results to be confirmed." A colleague drafts the board paper stating: "All quality gates are green; no outstanding concerns." What is the most accurate characterisation of that draft?

- A) Accurate — the KPIs are met and the assessment was passed.
- B) Inaccurate — the research reveals a serious unresolved accessibility failure that postdates the assessment's "to be confirmed" caveat, so a known concern is outstanding and the paper must disclose it with a remediation plan.
- C) Inaccurate — because KPI reports can never be trusted.
- D) Accurate for the board's purposes — accessibility detail is too granular for an investment decision.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Reconcile the three sources by date and scope. The KPI report covers quantitative targets and is genuinely green. The assessment is five weeks old and explicitly flagged accessibility results as unconfirmed — a caveat, not a clearance. The research is the most recent evidence on exactly the caveated question, and it shows screen reader users failing the eligibility step in three of four sessions: a serious, current, unresolved defect that likely also bears on legal accessibility obligations. "All quality gates are green; no outstanding concerns" is therefore false on the evidence its own authors hold — the caveat plus the newer contradicting evidence means at least one gate is amber at best. Option D fails on both accuracy and governance: excluding a known access barrier from an investment decision is not summarisation, it is omission of material information, and boards exist to weigh precisely such risks. Option C rejects a source wholesale instead of reconciling scopes. The professional behaviours here are named in your skills: be open about your decision making, and ensure the product is developed in line with the appropriate service standards for its phase. In the assessment, when sources conflict, rank them by recency and relevance to the disputed point — and remember that a caveat in an old document is an open item until newer evidence closes it, not a formality that lapses with time.

### Preparation tips

- Practise on your own artefacts. Take your current roadmap and walk its dependency chain out loud; take last quarter's KPI report and check each measure against "could an analyst compute this?"; take a benefits case and hunt the link where a different actor must act. This is free, doubly useful practice.
- For deduction questions, enumerate conditions explicitly — satisfied, failed, unknown — before concluding. Nearly every wrong answer in gate and rule questions comes from treating "unknown" as "satisfied".
- Write out working for scheduling and dependency questions. Anchor fixed events first, propagate relative constraints, then feasibility-test each candidate. Ten seconds of scribbled weeks prevents the most common hard-question error: confirming an option is permitted without checking it is achievable.
- Interrogate metrics before trusting them: what exactly is the numerator, the denominator, and the period — and does the metric mean what the argument needs it to mean? This single habit covers a large share of hard cognitive and numeric questions alike.
- Time-box your attention: bank the easy questions accurately, and if a hard question stalls, eliminate what you can, choose, flag, and move on. Speed-accuracy reporting rewards composure, not heroics.
- Arrive rested. Cognitive performance tracks alertness closely; a good night's sleep outperforms a late-night cramming session every time.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- Importing assumptions the scenario never states — a redesign that "must have" caused the drop, a stakeholder who "probably" agreed. Assessments test reasoning from the given facts; so do incident reviews.
- Treating explained outliers as part of the trend, or trends as part of the noise. Look at the shape of data before computing on it, and ask whether any point has a known cause.
- Concluding from one satisfied condition that a multi-condition gate is passed. Phase gates, definitions of done, and standards checklists all fail this way — in tests and in governance.
- Ranking priorities by seniority of the requester rather than impact and urgency. A director's undated request rarely outranks a live user-facing failure or a fixed governance deadline.
- Accepting causal chains that quietly switch actors — where a technical effect is assumed to become an organisational outcome without anyone deciding it. Name the missing decision.
- Rushing artefact-comparison questions. When two documents conflict, the answer lives in their dates, scopes, and caveats; skimming reads "met" and misses "results to be confirmed".

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures how accurately and confidently you work with the kinds of numbers your role genuinely produces and consumes. The mathematics involved is deliberately everyday — percentages, ratios, averages, rates, and trends — but applied to product data with realistic wrinkles: KPI tables, completion funnels, cost-per-transaction figures, uptake trends, benefit estimates, and sprint metrics.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 20 to 40 minutes with 15 to 25 questions. Each question presents a small dataset — a table, a chart description, or a short scenario with figures — and asks you to extract, calculate, compare, or project. A calculator is usually permitted, rough paper is essential, and scoring is objective, often adaptive, with employers seeing both speed and accuracy.

For a product manager, numeric fluency is not an optional analytical garnish; it is the language in which your central responsibilities are transacted. You estimate the value of each item on your roadmap. You compare effort to value when deciding priorities. You analyse the benefit of investment against anticipated outcomes, and you discuss estimates with stakeholders to validate understanding — which means defending your arithmetic against challenge in real time. You define KPIs with your team and then read them monthly, distinguishing genuine movement from noise, unit-cost changes from volume changes, and percentage points from percentages. A product manager who misreads a funnel or blends unequal groups into a misleading average will prioritise the wrong work with complete confidence — which is why employers test this. The reassuring news: a small set of techniques, each shown step by step below, covers nearly everything the assessment can throw at you.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Percentages, rates, and funnels** map to **Managing product outcomes**: completion rates, drop-off analysis, and KPI movements against baseline are the core arithmetic of analysing trusted data to enable continuous improvement.
- **Averages, weighted averages, and trends** map to **Agile and Lean practices** and **Life cycle management**: velocity planning, satisfaction reporting across user groups, and reading uptake trends across life cycle phases all depend on choosing and computing the right average.
- **Ratios and unit economics** map to **Creating value for money**: cost per transaction, value-to-effort ratios, and benefit-per-pound comparisons are how you estimate roadmap value and analyse investment against outcomes.
- **Projection and target arithmetic** maps to **Strategic ownership** and **Product management**: a strategy that "sets out the value, benefits and outcomes needed to justify investment" lives or dies by whether its projections are computed honestly, assumptions stated.
- **Reading tables and charts under pressure** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**: identifying and explaining success metrics to stakeholders starts with extracting the right figure from the right cell, quickly and correctly, in the meeting itself.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Completion rate from a funnel**

Last month, 8,000 users started your service's application journey and 6,200 completed it. What was the completion rate, and what was the drop-off rate?

- A) Completion 77.5%; drop-off 22.5%
- B) Completion 62%; drop-off 38%
- C) Completion 78.5%; drop-off 21.5%
- D) Completion 77.5%; drop-off 32.5%

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Completion rate = completers ÷ starters × 100. Step 1: 6,200 ÷ 8,000 = 0.775. Step 2: × 100 = 77.5%. Drop-off is the complement: 100% − 77.5% = 22.5%. So option A. Option D pairs the correct completion rate with an arithmetic slip on the complement — a reminder that assessments often make the second half of a two-part answer the discriminating half, so verify both figures. Option B divides the wrong way round conceptually (reporting completers as if they were a percentage of some other base). Funnel arithmetic is the bread and butter of outcome measurement for transactional services, and the completion/drop-off pair is usually the first number a stakeholder asks you for — worth being able to produce it flawlessly under meeting conditions.

**Question 2 (easy) — Percentage change in a KPI**

Support calls per week about your service fell from 480 in January to 384 in March. What is the percentage change?

- A) A fall of 24%
- B) A fall of 20%
- C) A fall of 25%
- D) A fall of 96%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Percentage change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100. Step 1: change = 384 − 480 = −96. Step 2: −96 ÷ 480 = −0.20. Step 3: × 100 = −20%, a 20% fall — option B. Option C (25%) is the classic anchor error: dividing by the new figure (96 ÷ 384 = 25%) instead of the original. Option D reports the absolute change (96 calls) as if it were the percentage. Always anchor on the starting value. Note the asymmetry this creates and why it matters in reporting: a 20% fall from 480 to 384 would require a 25% rise to return from 384 to 480 — so "calls fell 20% then rose 25%" describes a round trip, not a net worsening. Stakeholders are routinely confused by exactly this; being the person in the room who can untangle it builds the credibility your estimates depend on.

**Question 3 (easy) — Average from a KPI table**

Your service's monthly user satisfaction scores for the last quarter were: April 78%, May 82%, June 86%. What was the quarterly average, and what is the trend?

- A) 82% average; rising by 4 percentage points per month
- B) 82% average; stable
- C) 84% average; rising
- D) 78% average; rising by 4 percentage points per month

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Average = (78 + 82 + 86) ÷ 3. Step 1: 78 + 82 = 160; 160 + 86 = 246. Step 2: 246 ÷ 3 = 82. The trend: each month rises by exactly 4 percentage points (78 → 82 → 86). So option A. This question rewards reporting both the level and the direction — a habit worth carrying into your KPI reviews, because "82% average" and "86% and climbing" tell a stakeholder quite different stories, and the honest report gives both. Note also the correct unit: the monthly rise is 4 percentage points, not "4%" — a 4% rise from 78% would be 78 × 1.04 ≈ 81.1%. Precision with points versus percent is a small discipline that prevents large misunderstandings when your KPI report reaches an investment board.

**Question 4 (easy) — Cost per transaction**

The table shows two channels for the same service:

| Channel | Transactions per month | Monthly cost |
|---|---|---|
| Online | 30,000 | £36,000 |
| Phone | 5,000 | £40,000 |

What is the cost per transaction for each channel?

- A) Online £1.20; phone £8.00
- B) Online £1.20; phone £5.00
- C) Online £0.83; phone £8.00
- D) Online £1.00; phone £8.00

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Cost per transaction = monthly cost ÷ transactions. Online: £36,000 ÷ 30,000 = £1.20. Phone: £40,000 ÷ 5,000 = £8.00. Option A. Option C inverts the online division (30,000 ÷ 36,000); option B slips on the phone figure. The comparison this enables is the strategic point: each phone transaction costs 6.7 times its online equivalent, so every user successfully shifted online saves roughly £6.80 per transaction — the arithmetic seed of a channel-shift benefits case. But carry the product manager's caveat alongside the number: the remaining phone users may include people who cannot use the digital route, so the value case for channel shift must be built on making online genuinely usable for more people, not on degrading the phone service. Unit-cost arithmetic tells you the size of the prize; user-centred judgement tells you the legitimate way to win it.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Funnel drop-off localisation**

Your five-step journey recorded these completions of each step last month — Step 1: 10,000; Step 2: 9,200; Step 3: 6,900; Step 4: 6,555; Step 5: 6,424. Which step loses the greatest proportion of the users who reach it?

- A) Step 1 to Step 2
- B) Step 2 to Step 3
- C) Step 3 to Step 4
- D) Step 4 to Step 5

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Compute each step's loss as a percentage of those reaching the earlier step. Step 1→2: (10,000 − 9,200) ÷ 10,000 = 800 ÷ 10,000 = 8%. Step 2→3: (9,200 − 6,900) ÷ 9,200 = 2,300 ÷ 9,200 = 25%. Step 3→4: (6,900 − 6,555) ÷ 6,900 = 345 ÷ 6,900 = 5%. Step 4→5: (6,555 − 6,424) ÷ 6,555 = 131 ÷ 6,555 ≈ 2%. The largest proportional loss is Step 2→3 at 25%, option B. This calculation is how a product team converts a vague "conversion is poor" into a precise "Step 2's exit is the problem" — which is where you would point user research next, and a clean example of quantitative data telling you where and qualitative insight telling you why. Assessment tip: percentage-of-those-reaching-it is the correct denominator; a common error is dividing every step's loss by the original 10,000, which understates late-funnel problems and would here still give B, but on other data can change the answer entirely.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Weighted satisfaction across user groups**

Your product serves 1,800 citizens whose satisfaction is 84%, and 600 caseworkers whose satisfaction is 56%. A stakeholder's slide reports "average satisfaction: 70%". What is the correct overall figure, and what is wrong with the slide?

- A) 70% is correct: (84 + 56) ÷ 2.
- B) 77%: the groups must be weighted by size — and the slide's unweighted average conceals that the true blended figure is 7 points higher while caseworker satisfaction remains a serious outlier.
- C) 84%: the larger group's figure should be used.
- D) 66.5%: weight by the inverse of group size.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Weight by group size. Step 1: satisfied citizens ≈ 1,800 × 0.84 = 1,512. Step 2: satisfied caseworkers ≈ 600 × 0.56 = 336. Step 3: total satisfied = 1,512 + 336 = 1,848. Step 4: overall = 1,848 ÷ 2,400 = 0.77 = 77%, option B. The slide's 70% is the unweighted mean of two group percentages (option A's method), which is only valid when groups are equal in size; here it understates the blend by 7 points. But note what option B's wording adds: correcting the arithmetic is not the whole job. The blended 77% — however correctly computed — still masks a 28-point gap between groups, and the caseworkers, though fewer, may be the users whose daily work depends on your product. The product-manager-grade answer reports the correct blend and the split, because advocating for research with all types of users, and being open about your decision making, both require surfacing the unhappy group rather than averaging it away.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Value-to-effort with money attached**

Two roadmap items compete for the next quarter. Item A: estimated benefit £90,000 per year, effort 30 developer-days. Item B: estimated benefit £60,000 per year, effort 15 developer-days. A developer-day costs £500. Which statement is the strongest?

- A) Item A first: it has the larger total benefit.
- B) Item B first on benefit-per-day (£4,000 versus £3,000), and its £7,500 build cost is recovered in under seven weeks of its annual benefit — though with capacity for both across the quarter, sequencing B then A captures the better ratio first.
- C) Item A first: £90,000 minus £15,000 build cost beats £60,000 minus £7,500.
- D) They are equivalent, so the order does not matter.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Compute the ratios. Item A: £90,000 ÷ 30 days = £3,000 benefit per developer-day; build cost 30 × £500 = £15,000. Item B: £60,000 ÷ 15 = £4,000 per developer-day; build cost 15 × £500 = £7,500. B yields a third more benefit per unit of scarce capacity, so it leads. Payback check for B: £60,000 per year ≈ £1,154 per week, so £7,500 ÷ £1,154 ≈ 6.5 weeks — under seven weeks, as stated. Option A ranks by gross benefit, ignoring that effort is the scarce resource; option C nets off build cost but still ignores the ratio (net £75,000 for 30 days versus net £52,500 for 15 days is £2,500/day versus £3,500/day — B still wins). The deeper point option B captures: with a 45-day quarter of capacity, this is a sequencing decision, not a selection — do both, better ratio first, so value lands sooner. Estimating roadmap value, comparing effort to value, and discussing estimates with stakeholders are all named in your **Creating value for money** skill; per-unit reasoning with a payback cross-check is the arithmetic that makes those discussions concrete.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Reading a two-way table correctly**

Assessment digital uptake by age band:

| Age band | Online applications | Total applications |
|---|---|---|
| Under 35 | 5,400 | 6,000 |
| 35–64 | 6,300 | 9,000 |
| 65+ | 1,500 | 5,000 |

Which statement is correct?

- A) The 35–64 band has the highest online uptake rate, because it has the most online applications.
- B) Online uptake rates are: under 35: 90%; 35–64: 70%; 65+: 30% — so the under-35 band has the highest rate even though the 35–64 band has the highest count.
- C) The 65+ band accounts for most of the paper applications, so overall uptake is 30%.
- D) Overall uptake cannot be calculated from this table.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Rates first: under 35: 5,400 ÷ 6,000 = 90%. 35–64: 6,300 ÷ 9,000 = 70%. 65+: 1,500 ÷ 5,000 = 30%. So option B — and it correctly separates count (35–64 leads with 6,300) from rate (under 35 leads at 90%), the distinction option A collapses. Option C starts from a true observation (65+ contributes 3,500 of the 6,800 paper applications, the majority) but then leaps to a false conclusion: overall uptake is total online ÷ total applications = (5,400 + 6,300 + 1,500) ÷ (6,000 + 9,000 + 5,000) = 13,200 ÷ 20,000 = 66%, not 30%. Option D is defeatist — the table contains everything needed. For your role, the rate-by-band view is the actionable one: it locates the digital divide precisely (65+ at 30%) and quantifies the improvement opportunity, feeding both your uptake strategy and your duty to advocate for research with all types of users — including the 3,500 older applicants the aggregate 66% conveniently hides.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Benefit estimate with chained percentages**

Your service receives 2,400 support calls per month; call logs attribute 35% of them to users unable to track their application status. A proposed status-tracking feature is expected to deflect 60% of those calls. Each call costs £5.50 to handle. What is the expected annual saving?

- A) £2,772
- B) £33,264
- C) £55,440
- D) £16,632

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Chain each percentage to its correct base, then annualise. Step 1: status-related calls per month = 2,400 × 0.35 = 840. Step 2: calls deflected = 840 × 0.60 = 504. Step 3: monthly saving = 504 × £5.50 = £2,772. Step 4: annual saving = £2,772 × 12 = £33,264 — option B. Option A is the monthly figure presented as annual (the most common slip in benefits papers); option C assumes all 840 status calls disappear and annualises that (840 × £5.50 × 12); option D halves the correct answer, as if deflection ran for six months. When you analyse the benefit of investment against anticipated outcomes, this four-line calculation — with the 35% and 60% assumptions stated openly so stakeholders can challenge them — is precisely the format that survives scrutiny. And carry the operational caveat: the 60% deflection assumption depends on users finding and trusting the tracker, which is a design and content question your benefits realisation plan should test against actual call data after launch.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Projecting uptake against a strategy target**

Your strategy commits to 75% digital uptake by the end of Q6. Quarterly uptake so far: Q1: 48%; Q2: 54%; Q3: 60%; Q4: 66%. A stakeholder says: "We're growing 6 points a quarter — we'll hit 78% by Q6 and beat the target." Which is the most accurate assessment?

- A) The stakeholder is right, and no further analysis is needed.
- B) The straight-line projection does reach 78% by Q6 (66 + 6 + 6), but the claim rests on the assumption that growth stays linear; uptake curves typically flatten as the remaining non-digital users face higher barriers, so the team should treat 75% as at risk and monitor the quarterly increment for early signs of slowing.
- C) The projection is wrong: growth is 6% per quarter, so Q6 uptake will be 66 × 1.06 × 1.06 ≈ 74%, missing the target.
- D) Targets should not be projected; wait for Q6 actuals.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Verify the arithmetic first: increments are 54 − 48 = 6, 60 − 54 = 6, 66 − 60 = 6 points per quarter; continuing linearly gives Q5 = 72% and Q6 = 78%, above the 75% target. So the stakeholder's calculation is internally correct — and option C's compound-growth alternative misreads "6 points" as "6%", producing a different (and here inapplicable) model; the observed pattern is arithmetic, not geometric. The discriminating judgement is what option B adds: a projection is only as good as its assumption of continuation, and digital uptake is a canonical case of saturation — the users yet to switch increasingly include those with access needs, low digital confidence, or no connectivity, so late increments tend to shrink. The professional position is neither the stakeholder's certainty (A) nor analytical abstention (D), but a verified projection with its assumption named, a risk judgement attached, and a monitoring trigger defined (watch whether Q5's increment drops below 6). That is what regularly reviewing and iterating the strategy, and escalating risk to value at the right moment, look like in numeric form — and note the inclusion corollary: closing the last 9 points is likely to require assisted digital investment, not just momentum.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Unit cost versus total cost movement**

Your service's figures across two quarters:

| Quarter | Transactions | Total operating cost | Support calls |
|---|---|---|---|
| Q1 | 40,000 | £96,000 | 3,200 |
| Q2 | 55,000 | £121,000 | 3,850 |

A finance stakeholder warns: "Costs are up 26% and calls are up 20% — the service is deteriorating." What do the unit figures show?

- A) The stakeholder is right: both totals rose materially.
- B) Cost per transaction fell from £2.40 to £2.20, and calls per 1,000 transactions fell from 80 to 70 — both unit measures improved; totals rose because volume rose 37.5%, so the service became more efficient while serving many more people.
- C) Cost per transaction rose from £2.20 to £2.40, confirming deterioration.
- D) The data is contradictory and no conclusion is possible.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Compute the unit measures. Cost per transaction: Q1: £96,000 ÷ 40,000 = £2.40; Q2: £121,000 ÷ 55,000 = £2.20 — an 8.3% improvement. Calls per 1,000 transactions: Q1: 3,200 ÷ 40 = 80; Q2: 3,850 ÷ 55 = 70 — a 12.5% improvement. Meanwhile volume grew (55,000 − 40,000) ÷ 40,000 = 37.5%. So both totals rose (cost by 26%, calls by 20%) precisely because demand rose faster still, while every unit measure improved: option B, the exact reverse of the stakeholder's reading. Option C transposes the two unit costs — under time pressure, always recheck which quarter produced which figure. This question is a rehearsal for one of the most common stakeholder conversations a product manager has: totals alarm people, units inform them. Identifying and explaining success metrics to your stakeholders — a named element of **Managing product outcomes** — often consists of exactly this move: normalising by volume, showing the per-unit trend, and crediting the team for absorbing 37.5% more demand at lower unit cost, while conceding the genuine consequence that budgets track totals, so rising demand still needs funding even when efficiency improves.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Comparing an A/B test's results honestly**

You test a redesigned eligibility page. Over the same fortnight: variant A (current page) served 12,000 users with 8,640 completions; variant B (redesign) served 3,000 users with 2,250 completions. A colleague says: "A had 8,640 completions and B only 2,250 — the redesign failed." What do the rates show, and what is the sound conclusion?

- A) The colleague is right: 8,640 beats 2,250.
- B) Completion rates are A: 72%, B: 75% — the redesign performed 3 percentage points better per user served; the raw counts differ because A received four times the traffic, and the sound conclusion is that B is promising and the improvement should be checked for consistency across user groups before full rollout.
- C) The rates are A: 72%, B: 75%, so B should immediately replace A with no further checks.
- D) The test is invalid because the groups were different sizes.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Convert to rates before comparing. A: 8,640 ÷ 12,000 = 72%. B: 2,250 ÷ 3,000 = 75%. Per user served, the redesign performed 3 percentage points better; the colleague's comparison of raw counts (option A) merely restates the traffic split. Option D overcorrects: unequal group sizes are routine in A/B testing and are exactly why rates, not counts, are compared — the split affects precision, not validity. The judgement separating B from C is the professional one: a 3-point lift on one fortnight's data is promising, not proven. Before rollout, check the effect holds across segments that matter — device types, assisted digital users, screen reader users — because an average improvement can conceal a regression for a minority group, and your role's advocacy for all types of users applies to experiments too. Working with your team to investigate and validate what adds value — the first line of your **Product management** skill — is captured in that one word "validate": the arithmetic identifies the winner; the segmented check confirms it is a winner for everyone.

### Preparation tips

- Drill the five core recipes until automatic: part ÷ whole for rates; (new − old) ÷ old for change; total ÷ count for averages; weight-then-combine for mixed groups; and cost ÷ volume for unit economics. Every question above is one of these, sometimes chained — the skill is choosing which, and applying each percentage to its correct base.
- Write one line per step. The questions that punish mental arithmetic are exactly the chained ones (35% of calls, then 60% deflected, then annualised); intermediate figures on paper make each step checkable and each error findable.
- Normalise before comparing: rates before counts, units before totals, weighted before unweighted. When a question offers a dramatic raw-number contrast, the answer almost always lives in the normalised view.
- Check units and periods obsessively — percentage points versus percent, monthly versus annual, per transaction versus per 1,000. Assessments hide wrong answers inside correct arithmetic with the wrong unit.
- Practise on your own product's data: this month's funnel, last quarter's unit cost, your top KPI's trend. Twenty minutes with real numbers builds both assessment fluency and a better KPI review.
- Manage the clock calmly: bank accurate answers on easier questions, and on a stalled question eliminate the options with wrong denominators or wrong units — usually two go immediately — then choose and move on.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- Dividing by the new value when computing percentage change. Anchor on the original; and remember a 20% fall needs a 25% rise to recover — asymmetry that confuses slides and stakeholders alike.
- Comparing raw counts across unequal groups — test variants, age bands, channels. Convert to rates first, every time.
- Averaging group percentages without weighting by group size — and, just as importantly, reporting a correct blended average without the group split that gives it meaning.
- Presenting monthly figures as annual, or vice versa. Benefits cases fail audits over exactly this; multiply or divide by 12 deliberately and label the result.
- Reading totals as performance. Rising total cost with rising volume can mean improving efficiency; compute the unit measure before agreeing that anything is "deteriorating".
- Treating a straight-line projection as a commitment. State the assumption, name what would bend the line (saturation, seasonality, policy change), and define the early-warning check.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment measures how precisely you read, interpret, and draw conclusions from the written material your role runs on: service standards, policy extracts, governance papers, stakeholder emails, research summaries, supplier communications, and strategy documents.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 15 to 30 minutes. You read a short passage and answer questions about it, most commonly in "true / false / cannot say" form: True means the passage guarantees the statement; False means the passage contradicts it; Cannot say means the passage does neither. Other formats include best-summary selection, inference identification, assumption spotting, and clear-writing judgement. Scoring is objective, and the cardinal rule is to answer from the passage alone — your professional knowledge of how things usually work is deliberately irrelevant and frequently weaponised against you as a distractor.

For a product manager, verbal precision is a load-bearing skill. You ensure teams comply with appropriate standards, which means reading standards for what they actually require, permit, and omit. You consult and mediate with stakeholders, which means hearing the exact commitment, condition, or concern in an email rather than the one you expected. You work in the open and share your work, which means writing updates whose words survive being quoted out of context. And you use research summaries to represent users in internal discussions — where over-claiming what the evidence says is as damaging as ignoring it. Employers test verbal reasoning for product roles because the cost of imprecise reading compounds: a misread condition becomes a missed commitment, becomes a surprised stakeholder, becomes a governance escalation. The practice below trains the precision that prevents that chain from starting.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Comprehension of standards and policy** maps to your duties to **ensure teams comply with appropriate standards** and to ensure the product is developed **in line with the appropriate service standards for a phase**: exact reading of requirements, permissions, exceptions, and silences.
- **True/false/cannot-say discipline** maps to **Applying user-centred insights** and **Managing product outcomes**: representing users with insight means claiming exactly what the evidence supports — no more, no less — and being open about decision making includes being open about uncertainty.
- **Inference and assumption spotting** maps to **Creating value for money** and **Strategic ownership**: benefit cases and strategies are arguments in prose; validating them means finding the unstated assumptions on which they stand.
- **Reading stakeholder communications** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**: distinguishing a condition from a refusal, a concern from an instruction, and a hope from a commitment is how you identify and resolve issues and influence effectively.
- **Clear-writing judgement** maps to **Product management** and **Product leadership**: working in the open, sharing your work, and advocating for your product all depend on prose that says what changed, why, and what happens next.

### Practice questions

For questions 1 to 6, decide whether the statement is True (definitely follows from the passage), False (definitely contradicted by the passage), or Cannot say (the passage does not give enough information). A fourth option is included where relevant.

**Question 1 (easy) — Service standard extract**

Passage: "Before a public beta assessment, teams must show they have tested the service with users who have access needs, and that identified accessibility issues have either been fixed or have a documented remediation plan with dates. Teams should also show how assisted digital support will be provided."

Statement: "A team may pass the public beta assessment with unfixed accessibility issues, provided those issues have a documented, dated remediation plan."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, but only for low-severity issues

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The passage offers an explicit either/or: issues "have either been fixed or have a documented remediation plan with dates." The statement tracks the second branch precisely — unfixed issues are acceptable when a documented, dated plan exists — so it is True. Option D imports a severity restriction the passage never makes; assessments regularly test whether you will add qualifiers that feel prudent but are not in the text. The professional habit this trains is reading standards for their actual structure — requirement, alternative, condition — rather than a stricter or looser remembered version. When you prepare a team for an assessment, knowing that the standard permits a dated plan (and not a vague intention) is exactly the difference between a pass and an awkward Thursday.

**Question 2 (easy) — What silence means**

Passage: "The department's API strategy requires all new services to expose data through the central API gateway. Services must publish their API documentation in the developer hub. Rate limits are set by the gateway team."

Statement: "The API strategy requires existing services to migrate to the central gateway."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, within twelve months

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage's requirement is scoped to "all new services." About existing services it says nothing: it neither requires migration nor exempts them. Since silence is not contradiction, the answer is not False; since silence is not confirmation, it is not True. Cannot say — option C. Option D invents both the requirement and a deadline. The scope word "new" is doing all the work here, and one-word scope limiters ("new", "citizen-facing", "during beta") are the most common hinge in standards-reading questions — and in real compliance conversations: telling a legacy service team "the strategy requires you to migrate" when it does not would spend your credibility on a claim the document cannot back. Precise reading of scope is also self-protection: your duty is to ensure teams comply with what standards actually say.

**Question 3 (easy) — Research summary discipline**

Passage: "In eight moderated sessions on the redesigned eligibility checker, six participants completed the check unaided. Two participants, both using screen magnification, could not operate the postcode field. Participants who completed the check described the result page as clear."

Statement: "The redesigned checker works well for users with access needs."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) False, because two participants failed

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Tempting to answer False given the two failures — and option D packages that temptation attractively. But examine the statement's scope: "users with access needs" is a broad population spanning many access needs and assistive technologies; the passage documents difficulties for two screen-magnification users and says nothing about screen reader users, keyboard-only users, users with cognitive impairments, and so on. The evidence raises a serious concern about one interaction for one group, but it can neither confirm the broad claim (so not True) nor strictly contradict it as worded — a claim about a population is not falsified by two sessions, just as it is not proven by six. Cannot say. The product management lesson is double-edged: never let "cannot say" become an excuse for inaction — the magnification finding demands a fix and further testing — but never write "works well for users with access needs" in a board paper on this evidence either. Representing users with insight means matching your claims to your evidence's actual scope, in both directions.

**Question 4 (moderate) — A supplier's carefully worded email**

Passage (supplier email): "We anticipate the API rate-limit increase will be deployed in the June release, subject to change-board approval on 28 May. Should approval be delayed, the increase would move to the July release. We will confirm within two working days of the board's decision."

Statement: "The supplier has committed to deploying the rate-limit increase in June."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, because June is stated as the plan

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Parse the modal language. "We anticipate... subject to change-board approval" is an expectation with an explicit condition, and the email then names the fallback (July) if the condition fails. A commitment is an undertaking that something will happen; this email carefully avoids one — that is what "anticipate" and "subject to" exist to do. So the statement misdescribes the email: it claims a commitment where the passage shows a conditional expectation, which the passage's own structure contradicts. False. Option D mistakes a plan for a promise — the exact misreading that leads product managers to pass "the supplier has committed to June" up to their stakeholders, and then to own the surprise when 28 May goes badly. The transferable habit: when reading supplier and stakeholder correspondence, underline the verbs of commitment ("will", "commit", "guarantee") versus the verbs of expectation ("anticipate", "aim", "subject to"), and relay upward only the strength of claim the sender actually made. Your stakeholder communications strategy depends on that fidelity.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Governance minutes and what was decided**

Passage (board minutes extract): "The board reviewed the proposal to retire the legacy reporting module. Members noted the migration plan and the low remaining usage (4% of users). The board requested a data-retention impact note and agreed to take a decision at its July meeting. The product team was thanked for a clear paper."

Statement: "The board has approved the retirement of the legacy reporting module."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, effective July

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Follow the verbs of governance: "reviewed", "noted", "requested", "agreed to take a decision at its July meeting". Agreeing when to decide is explicitly not deciding — the passage states the decision has been deferred to July, which directly contradicts "has approved". False. Option D converts the July decision date into a July effective date for an approval that has not happened. This distinction is bread and butter in **Life cycle management**: retirement is a phase transition needing an actual authorisation, and reporting "the board approved it" after a meeting like this one would misinform your team, your stakeholders, and your plan — teams have decommissioned services on exactly this misreading. Minutes questions in assessments reward tracking who decided what, versus noted, discussed, requested, or deferred; minutes in real life reward it even more.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Policy with an exception and a condition**

Passage: "Teams must release changes through the standard deployment pipeline. Emergency fixes may bypass the pipeline where a live incident is in progress, provided the change is retrospectively reviewed within five working days. Bypasses without an active incident are not permitted under any circumstances."

Statement: "A team that bypassed the pipeline during a live incident, and completed the retrospective review seven working days later, has complied with the policy."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, because the incident justified the bypass

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The exception has two parts: a triggering condition (live incident in progress) and an obligation attached to using it (retrospective review within five working days). The team met the trigger but breached the attached obligation — seven working days exceeds five. A conditional permission is only fully exercised when its conditions are fully met, so the team has not complied: False. Option D reflects the most common real-world misreading of exception clauses — treating the trigger as the whole test and the proviso as administrative decoration. Assessment questions and audit findings alike turn on the word "provided". As the person accountable for ensuring your teams comply with appropriate standards, this is a reading habit to institutionalise: when a policy grants an exception, extract both the trigger and every proviso, and track the provisos to completion — a bypass log with review dates is precisely the artefact that keeps "we had an incident" from becoming "we breached the policy".

**Question 7 (moderate) — Best summary of a prioritisation note**

Passage: "We assessed four candidate features against user value, delivery effort, and strategic fit. The payment-reminder feature scored highest on value and fit, with moderate effort, and is recommended for the next quarter. The document-upload feature scored well on value but requires a security review before any commitment. The remaining two features scored low on value and are deferred to the backlog. We will revisit the deferred items if user research changes the value picture."

Which sentence best summarises the passage?

- A) Four features were assessed; payment reminders are recommended for next quarter, document upload awaits a security review before commitment, and two low-value features are deferred with a review trigger.
- B) Payment reminders and document upload are both committed for next quarter, with two features cancelled.
- C) The team assessed four features and deferred them all pending research.
- D) Payment reminders are recommended because they are the easiest to build.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Test each candidate against the passage's four moving parts: the recommendation (payment reminders, next quarter), the conditional item (document upload, security review before any commitment), the deferrals (two features, to the backlog not to oblivion), and the revisit trigger (new research on value). Option A preserves all four with correct strength — "recommended", "awaits... before commitment", "deferred with a review trigger". Option B upgrades a conditional into a commitment and a deferral into a cancellation — two strength errors that would each mislead a stakeholder differently. Option C demotes the recommendation into a deferral. Option D fabricates a rationale: the passage credits value and fit, with effort merely moderate. Summary questions are miniature stakeholder updates: the skill being scored is preserving the strength of each claim — recommended versus committed, deferred versus cancelled — which is exactly the precision your roadmap discussions with stakeholders require. Most roadmap misunderstandings are strength errors, not content errors.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Spotting the assumption in a benefits argument**

Passage (from a draft business case): "Introducing web chat will reduce telephone waiting times, because a proportion of callers will choose web chat instead, and each web chat agent can handle three conversations concurrently while a telephone agent can handle only one."

Which unstated assumption is most critical to this argument?

- A) That enough callers will actually switch to web chat, and that the staffing model converts the concurrency advantage into freed telephone capacity rather than absorbing it elsewhere.
- B) That web chat software is cheaper than telephone systems.
- C) That telephone waiting times are currently measured accurately.
- D) That users prefer speaking to typing.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Reduce the argument to its mechanism: some callers divert to chat; chat's 3:1 concurrency means the diverted demand needs fewer agents; therefore telephone queues shorten. Two load-bearing assumptions are unstated, and option A names both. First, uptake: if few callers switch, nothing changes regardless of concurrency. Second, capacity conversion: the queues only shorten if the staffing model actually reallocates the freed effort to telephones (or reduces telephone demand share) — if chat is staffed by new hires while telephone staffing is cut proportionally, or freed agents are absorbed by other work, the benefit evaporates. Option B concerns cost, which the argument never claims; option C would affect measuring the benefit, not producing it; option D, if anything, runs against the argument and is not required by it. This is the verbal twin of the benefits-chain question in the cognitive section, and the same professional muscle: analysing benefit against anticipated outcomes means finding where an argument needs the world to cooperate and asking what evidence — an uptake benchmark, a workforce plan — supports the cooperation.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Two documents in tension**

Passage 1 (strategy, published January): "Our priority for this year is consolidating our three case-handling products into a single platform. New feature development on the legacy products should be limited to essential compliance changes."

Passage 2 (email from an operations director, September): "The legacy casework team urgently needs a bulk-reassignment feature — their workaround costs hours each week. I understand the consolidation strategy, but this is a productivity issue affecting statutory timeliness targets."

Statement: "Approving the bulk-reassignment feature on the legacy product would be consistent with the strategy only if it qualifies as an essential compliance change."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) False, because the director's request overrides the strategy

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The strategy permits exactly one category of new development on legacy products: "essential compliance changes." Therefore any legacy feature approval is strategy-consistent if and only if it falls in that category — which is precisely what the statement asserts. True. The interesting texture is in the email: the director's argument gestures toward the category ("statutory timeliness targets" hints at compliance) without establishing membership — hours lost to a workaround is a productivity harm, and whether it genuinely threatens statutory compliance is exactly the question a product manager would need evidenced before deciding. But the statement does not ask you to decide the feature's fate; it asks about the logical structure of consistency with the strategy, and that structure is airtight. Option D confuses organisational reality (directors exert pressure) with textual logic (the strategy's words define consistency; pressure does not rewrite them). This mirrors real strategic ownership: when a request strains the strategy, the productive move is to test the request against the strategy's own exception category — and if genuinely compliance-critical work keeps arriving, that is evidence for the named review-and-iterate loop, not for quiet exceptions.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Clear-writing judgement for a difficult update**

Your show-and-tell follows a sprint in which a planned feature was pulled mid-sprint after user research raised concerns. Which opening sentence for your stakeholder update is best?

- A) "Despite headwinds, the team continued to drive velocity across multiple workstreams in line with evolving priorities."
- B) "We paused the document-sharing feature mid-sprint after research showed users misunderstood who could see their documents — a privacy risk we're redesigning to fix, with a revised plan by 14 June."
- C) "Owing to a research-driven reprioritisation event, deliverable timelines have been recalibrated pending stakeholder alignment."
- D) "The sprint didn't go to plan; details on request."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Score each against the three-part test for working in the open: what changed, why, what happens next. Option B delivers all three in one sentence — the specific feature, the specific evidence-based reason (users misunderstood visibility — a privacy risk, named as such), and a dated next step. It also models the harder virtue: it presents pausing on research findings as sound product practice rather than failure, which is precisely the story a user-centred team should tell in the open. Options A and C are professional-sounding vapour: no feature, no reason, no date — and "recalibrated" plus "alignment" are the vocabulary stakeholders have learned to distrust; such updates generate more anxiety than the bad news they conceal. Option D is honest but abdicates: it transfers the effort of discovery to every stakeholder individually. Note the deeper alignment with your skills: being open about decision making and sharing work in the open are named behaviours, and option B shows their practical grammar — concrete noun, causal clause, date. If a sentence in your own updates could describe any team in any sprint, it is not an update; rewrite it until it could only describe yours.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Inference across a research and analytics passage**

Passage: "Analytics show that 22% of started applications are abandoned at the evidence-upload step. In interviews, every participant who abandoned at this step said they did not have the required documents to hand; none mentioned difficulty operating the upload itself. Two participants said they returned and completed the application later that week. The upload component passed usability testing in March with no significant findings."

Statement: "Improving the usability of the upload component is unlikely to substantially reduce abandonment at the evidence-upload step."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say — the passage does not address usability improvements
- D) True, and abandonment is therefore not a problem

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** This is a genuine inference question: the conclusion is not stated verbatim but follows from converging evidence. The interviews attribute abandonment entirely to document availability ("did not have the required documents to hand"), with explicit absence of operational difficulty ("none mentioned difficulty operating the upload"); independently, the component passed usability testing without significant findings. If the cause of abandonment is not usability, then a usability fix does not address the cause — so a substantial reduction from that route is unlikely, and the hedged wording ("unlikely to substantially") matches the evidence's strength exactly. True. Option C is over-cautious: Cannot say is for absent evidence, and here two independent sources bear directly on the mechanism; declining to infer would itself be misreading. Option D bolts on an unsupported second claim — the passage even hints the real fix (some users return later; better upfront document checklists or save-and-return might help), so abandonment remains a problem with a different solution. The professional point is one of the most valuable in product work: the data tells you where users leave, the research tells you why, and interventions should target the why. Spending a sprint polishing an upload widget nobody struggles with is the cost of failing this inference in real life.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Precision about what a metric claim licenses**

Passage (from a performance report): "The new appointment-booking journey launched on 1 April. In April, 68% of bookings were made online, against 54% in March. The online booking KPI target for the year is 65%. April also saw the closure of two walk-in centres, which previously handled around 10% of monthly bookings."

Statement: "The new booking journey caused online bookings to exceed the KPI target."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, because the target was exceeded in the same month the journey launched

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Separate the factual components from the causal one. Factual: April's 68% exceeds the 65% target — verified from the passage. Causal: "the new booking journey caused" the rise. The passage itself supplies a rival explanation: two walk-in centres, handling roughly 10% of bookings, closed in the same month — users displaced from walk-in channels would raise the online share with no help from the redesign. With two candidate causes operating simultaneously and no evidence apportioning the effect between them, causation is neither established nor refuted: Cannot say. Option D is the post hoc trap with a KPI ribbon on it, and option B overshoots — the journey may well have contributed; the passage simply cannot show it. Notice how the passage's last sentence is doing the discriminating work: strong verbal reasoning means actively scanning for facts that complicate the headline claim, because report authors (and assessment writers) place them quietly. In your role this is the difference between telling stakeholders "online uptake exceeded target; the redesign and the centre closures both contributed and we're segmenting the data to size each effect" — trustworthy — and claiming the KPI scalp for your feature, only to have a finance colleague find the closures. Being open about decision making includes being open about what your metrics cannot yet prove.

### Preparation tips

- Internalise the three-way test and apply it mechanically: could the statement be false while the passage stays true? Then it is not True. Does the passage's own content rule the statement out? Only then is it False. Everything else is Cannot say.
- Underline scope words and modal verbs as you read: "new", "all", "only", "must", "may", "anticipate", "committed", "provided that". Nearly every hard question — and most stakeholder misunderstandings — hinge on one of them.
- For exception clauses, extract the trigger and every proviso separately, and check both. "May bypass, provided reviewed within five days" is two tests, not one.
- Track governance verbs precisely: reviewed, noted, requested, deferred, approved. Practise on real minutes from your own boards — write one True, one False, and one Cannot say statement about them, and you will start seeing the traps from the author's side.
- For summary and clear-writing questions, apply the strength test: does each candidate preserve recommended-versus-committed, deferred-versus-cancelled, expectation-versus-promise? Then apply the three-part update test: what changed, why, what next.
- When a passage ends with an oddly specific extra fact, assume it is load-bearing. Ask what claim it complicates before you answer.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- Marking plausible statements True. Your product experience makes many wrong answers feel right; the test measures fidelity to the passage, and so does accurate stakeholder reporting.
- Marking unmentioned things False. Silence is Cannot say; only contradiction is False. This single distinction decides more marks than any other.
- Upgrading claim strength while reading: anticipations into commitments, recommendations into decisions, plans into approvals. If you would not sign your name to the stronger version, do not select it.
- Treating an exception's trigger as the whole test and skipping its provisos. "Provided", "subject to", and "unless" always carry a second condition worth checking.
- Missing the scope limiter — "new services", "in the sessions", "screen-magnification users" — and answering about a wider population than the passage covers.
- Accepting causal claims because the timing fits. Same-month evidence plus a rival explanation in the passage is the assessment's favourite Cannot say — and the boardroom's favourite false victory.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment presents realistic workplace dilemmas and asks you to judge the effectiveness of possible responses. Formats vary: choose the most effective response; choose the most and least effective; rank all options; or rate each response independently. Scoring keys are built from the judgements of experienced practitioners and from the profession's behavioural expectations, so there is rarely a formula — but there are consistent principles, and the explanations below make them explicit.

The typical format is an online test of 20 to 40 minutes with 10 to 20 scenarios, often untimed or generously timed, because it measures judgement rather than speed. There are no trick questions, but the distractors are engineered from real failure modes: conflict avoidance dressed as diplomacy, unilateral heroics dressed as decisiveness, and maximal escalation dressed as integrity.

For a product manager, situational judgement is arguably the most job-relevant assessment of the four. Your role summary is dense with behavioural verbs: consult and mediate with stakeholders; protect the team from unnecessary distractions and requests to change direction; be open about your decision making; advocate for research with all types of users; decide how and when to escalate risk to value. Each of those is a judgement call made under pressure, with incomplete information, in front of people who disagree. At this level you are expected to handle most situations independently — the calibration being tested is no longer "do you know when to ask for help?" so much as "do you resolve the right things yourself, escalate the right things upward, and keep users, evidence, and honesty intact while doing both?" As you work through the scenarios, notice the recurring fingerprint of effective responses: they engage rather than avoid, they are proportionate rather than maximal, they convert conflict into evidence, and they leave every affected party knowing where they stand.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Stakeholder mediation scenarios** map to **Stakeholder relationship management** and **Product leadership**: consulting and mediating, resolving issues, and influencing effectively — including when stakeholders pull in opposite directions.
- **Team protection scenarios** map to **Product leadership**: protecting the team from unnecessary distractions and mid-sprint direction changes, without becoming a wall that blocks legitimate change.
- **Prioritisation and scope scenarios** map to **Product management** and **Creating value for money**: addressing uncertainty while managing scope, meeting critical deadlines, and escalating risk to value at the right moment.
- **Evidence and openness scenarios** map to **Managing product outcomes** and **Applying user-centred insights**: using trusted data honestly, being open about decisions, and representing users — including inconvenient findings — in internal discussions.
- **Standards and phase scenarios** map to **Life cycle management** and your duty to **ensure teams comply with appropriate standards**: holding the line on service standards, accessibility, and phase gates under delivery pressure.
- **People and community scenarios** map to the role's people dimension — supporting senior colleagues, developing team members, and participating in the product community.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — A mid-sprint change request**

Three days into a two-week sprint, a senior stakeholder emails you: "Please get the team to add a data-export option to this sprint — it came up in a directors' meeting and shouldn't take long." The sprint goal is completing the payment journey ahead of a fixed compliance deadline. What is the most effective response?

- A) Forward the email to the team and ask them to fit the export in.
- B) Reply that sprint scope is locked, decline the request, and copy the team so they see you defending them.
- C) Reply promptly: acknowledge the request, explain the sprint is protecting a compliance deadline, ask about the export's driver and urgency, and offer to assess it for the next sprint — bringing evidence of its value and effort to the conversation.
- D) Add the export to the backlog silently and say nothing.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** This is the signature product-leadership scenario — protect the team from mid-sprint direction changes — and option C shows what protection actually looks like: the team never sees the disruption, the stakeholder gets engagement rather than a wall, and the request enters the prioritisation process it skipped, with value and effort evidence rather than politics deciding. Asking about the driver matters: "came up in a directors' meeting" might conceal a genuine need or nothing at all, and finding out is mediation, not capitulation. Option A passes the disruption straight through to the team — the exact failure your role exists to prevent, and it silently deprioritises a compliance deadline. Option B protects the sprint but spends the relationship: a flat "scope is locked" with the team copied in reads as performance, teaches the stakeholder to escalate above you next time, and forecloses the possibility that the request is genuinely urgent. Option D avoids everyone: the stakeholder learns nothing, waits, and eventually escalates. The pattern to bank: protect the sprint, engage the requester, and route the request into evidence-based prioritisation — all three, not any one.

**Question 2 (easy) — Inconvenient research before a big demo**

Tomorrow you demo the new eligibility checker to a director's group that has championed it. Today your user researcher reports that in this week's testing, four of six participants misinterpreted the checker's "likely eligible" result as a guarantee, and two said they would have made financial decisions based on it. What is the most effective response?

- A) Proceed with the demo as planned and share the research afterwards, once fixes are designed.
- B) Include the finding in tomorrow's demo: show the checker, present the misinterpretation risk and its potential harm plainly, and set out the team's proposed response and timeline.
- C) Cancel the demo until the issue is fixed.
- D) Ask the researcher to soften the finding to "some users wanted clearer wording".

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The finding is material: users making financial decisions on a misread result is a harm risk, and the audience championing the product has the most right — and the most need — to hear it while shaping the response. Option B is openness as your role defines it: be open about your decision making, use insights to represent users in internal discussions, and pair every problem with a proposed response so the demo remains a working session rather than a confession. Champions who hear risks early from you become more committed, not less; champions who discover risks later, from others, become ex-champions. Option A gambles the product's credibility on a quiet fortnight — and if a citizen is harmed meanwhile, "we knew before the demo" is indefensible. Option C overcorrects: the demo is the ideal forum for exactly this conversation, and cancelling it forfeits both momentum and transparency. Option D corrupts the evidence chain — asking a researcher to dilute a harm finding is an integrity breach that also poisons your relationship with the discipline whose independence your decisions depend on. The rule: material user-harm findings travel to decision-makers at the first opportunity, undiluted, with a plan attached.

**Question 3 (easy) — Two teams, one component**

You discover that a team in a neighbouring directorate is three weeks into building a consent-management component nearly identical to one your team finished last month. You have never met their product manager. What is the most effective response?

- A) Do nothing — their duplication is their directorate's problem.
- B) Contact their product manager directly: share what your component does, offer a walkthrough with a view to them adopting or extending it, and if adoption looks viable, flag the reuse opportunity to both directorates' leads.
- C) Email their director stating that the team is wasting public money.
- D) Tell your own team lead and take no further action.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Seeking opportunities for reuse and learning in your area is a named element of your **Product leadership** skill, and option B executes it with the right social mechanics: peer-to-peer first (no one is embarrassed in front of their leadership), concrete and generous (a walkthrough, an offer, not a critique), and escalated only as a constructive reuse story once viability is established. Three weeks in, adoption may still save real money — and even partial convergence (shared roadmap, shared patterns) has value. Option A treats organisational boundaries as boundaries of professional responsibility; value for money in government does not stop at your directorate's edge. Option C leads with an accusation aimed above the people who can actually fix it — the surest way to convert a potential collaborator into a defensive opponent, and it spends trust you will want later. Option D discharges the letter of responsibility while ensuring nothing happens in time to matter. Note also the community thread: this is exactly what product management community participation is for — had either of you been sharing work in the open earlier, the duplication would have surfaced at week zero rather than week three.

**Question 4 (moderate) — The velocity conversation with a delivery deadline**

Your team's evidence-based forecast says the casework migration will complete in November. Your senior product manager, under pressure from the programme board, asks you to "commit to September — the team always sandbagging estimates anyway." You believe the November forecast is sound. What is the most effective response?

- A) Commit to September to support your senior colleague, and push the team to speed up.
- B) Walk your senior colleague through the forecast's basis, state that you cannot honestly commit to September, and offer alternatives with trade-offs made explicit: a smaller September scope, added capacity with its costs and risks, or the full scope in November — so they carry real options to the board.
- C) Refuse to discuss dates other than November.
- D) Agree to September in the meeting, then let the slip emerge gradually through sprint reports.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Supporting lead and senior product managers — named in your role summary — means equipping them with defensible positions, not echoing indefensible ones. Option B does the full job: it takes the challenge seriously (walking through the forecast basis answers the "sandbagging" suspicion with evidence rather than indignation), it holds the honesty line on the commitment itself, and — the step that separates strong from merely principled — it converts the confrontation into options with explicit trade-offs, which is what a board actually needs. Scope-for-date is usually the real negotiation hiding inside date pressure; naming a credible September slice may satisfy the board's underlying need entirely. Option A manufactures a commitment you believe to be false; when it fails, your senior colleague — who defended it upward — pays the highest price, which is the opposite of support. Option D is worse: deliberate ambush by drift, converting an honest forecasting disagreement into a trust collapse. Option C holds the line but refuses the mediation your role exists to provide. Addressing uncertainty while managing scope and ensuring critical deadlines are met is your **Product management** skill in action — and its first principle is that dates, scope, and capacity trade against each other in the open, or they trade against your credibility in the dark.

**Question 5 (moderate) — A KPI moving the wrong way before a board paper**

Your monthly board paper is due tomorrow. Three of four KPIs improved; the fourth — completion rate for assisted digital users — fell for the second consecutive month, and the team has not yet diagnosed why. A colleague suggests: "Lead with the three good ones and put the fourth in the annex — we'll have an explanation by next month." What is the most effective response?

- A) Take the suggestion — annexes exist for detail, and next month's explanation will be better.
- B) Report all four KPIs with equal prominence, state plainly that the assisted digital decline is undiagnosed, and set out the investigation under way with a date for findings.
- C) Delay the board paper until the diagnosis is complete.
- D) Report the fourth KPI but reframe it as "within normal variation" to buy time.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Two named behaviours collide with one temptation here. The behaviours: be open about your decision making, and identify and explain success metrics to your stakeholders — which includes when they move against you. The temptation: manage the narrative. Option B resists it correctly, and the detail matters: "undiagnosed, investigation under way, findings by [date]" is a complete and honest status — boards handle uncertainty well when it arrives labelled as uncertainty with a plan attached. Note also whose metric is declining: assisted digital users are among those least able to raise the alarm themselves, which makes burying their KPI (option A) not just a transparency failure but a user-advocacy one — annex placement is editorial de-prioritisation, and everyone in governance knows it. Option D is worse than omission: "within normal variation" is a factual claim you have no diagnosis to support — if the board later learns the decline was real and the phrase was cover, every future "normal variation" from you is discounted. Option C sacrifices the governance rhythm to perfectionism; boards need current truth on schedule, not complete truth late. The durable rule: bad news travels at the same speed and prominence as good news, carrying a diagnosis date.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Standards pressure at a phase boundary**

Your service's public beta assessment is in three weeks. The delivery manager proposes skipping the planned round of research with users of assistive technology to protect the date: "We passed accessibility automated checks; the assessment only needs us to show testing is planned." You know automated checks catch only a fraction of accessibility barriers. What is the most effective response?

- A) Agree — the assessment criteria will technically be satisfied.
- B) Insist the research happens as planned, explain that automated checks are not user testing and that barriers found post-assessment cost far more to fix, and work with the delivery manager to fit a proportionate round — even a reduced one — before the assessment, escalating the trade-off jointly if it truly cannot fit.
- C) Report the delivery manager to the assessment panel.
- D) Postpone the assessment by two months to allow exhaustive testing.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The proposal optimises for passing the assessment rather than for the users the assessment protects — the difference between complying with a standard and gaming it. Option B holds the substance while respecting the delivery constraint: it corrects the technical misconception (automated checks catch perhaps a third of barriers; real assistive-technology users find the rest), it makes the economic argument that lands with delivery managers (post-assessment fixes cost multiples), and it negotiates a proportionate round rather than a maximal one — three sessions before the assessment beat zero, and beat ten after it. Crucially, if the trade-off genuinely cannot fit, option B escalates it jointly and openly, making the risk acceptance a visible decision at the right level rather than a quiet operational default. That is "decide how and when to escalate risk" and "ensure the product is developed in line with the appropriate service standards for a phase" working together. Option A passes the assessment and ships the barriers. Option C converts a colleague's bad proposal — not yet a bad act — into a betrayal, skipping every collaborative rung. Option D swings to the opposite maximalism, sacrificing momentum that a proportionate plan preserves. Standards scenarios reward the same shape every time: hold the substance, offer proportionality, escalate residual risk visibly.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Mediating stakeholders who want opposite things**

Policy colleagues insist the new grant application must collect a full financial history "so decisions are robust"; operations colleagues insist it must be shorter "because abandonment is killing us"; both cite the service's success as their goal, and both have escalated to you. Your data shows abandonment concentrates on the financial-history pages; policy's legal advice says some financial information is statutorily required. What is the most effective response?

- A) Side with operations — the data on abandonment is decisive.
- B) Side with policy — statutory requirements always win.
- C) Convene both parties around the evidence: separate what is statutorily required from what is merely customary, test with users a journey that collects only the required minimum upfront with the remainder deferred or retrieved from existing government data, and agree success measures both sides own.
- D) Build both versions and let a senior board choose.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** "Consult and mediate with stakeholders" is named in your **Product leadership** skill, and this scenario tests whether you mediate by splitting the difference or by dissolving the false binary. The framing "full history versus short form" survives only while nobody separates the statutory core from the customary padding — the single analytical move (option C's first step) that both sides' own positions already support: policy's legal advice defines the true floor; operations' abandonment data motivates stripping everything above it. The design hypotheses that follow (progressive collection, reuse of data government already holds) are standard patterns for exactly this conflict, and shared success measures convert opponents into co-owners of the outcome. Options A and B each accept the binary and create a loser — and the losing side of a binary decision relitigates it forever, usually at your least convenient moment. Option D outsources your role's core work: boards adjudicate escalated trade-offs, but manufacturing two full builds to avoid a mediation is an expensive abdication, and the board would rightly ask what the product manager is for. The mediation fingerprint worth memorising: find the constraint that is real, evidence the cost of everything beyond it, and design so both parties' genuine needs — robust decisions, completed applications — are met by the same journey.

**Question 8 (moderate) — A team member's growth versus a safe pair of hands**

A high-visibility discovery is starting for a politically sensitive service area. Your usual approach would be to lead stakeholder engagement yourself. An associate product manager on your team — capable, but untested with senior stakeholders — asks to lead it as a development opportunity. Their last piece of work was strong; the stakeholders involved are known to be demanding. What is the most effective response?

- A) Decline — the sensitivity is too high for development experiments; they can lead something smaller later.
- B) Agree fully and step back — people learn by doing, and intervening would undermine them.
- C) Agree with scaffolding: they lead the engagement with defined scope, you agree the approach together beforehand, attend the first sessions in a supporting role, debrief after each, and set explicit points where you would step in.
- D) Split the role ambiguously — tell them they are leading, but attend everything and answer questions yourself when stakes rise.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Your role level "might recruit and manage people in the product profession, ensuring people with the right skills are hired, supported and developed" — and developing people is a risk-management discipline, not an act of faith. Option C is the textbook scaffold: real leadership (they own the room and the relationships), bounded risk (agreed approach, your presence early, explicit step-in triggers), and the debrief loop where the actual development happens. The step-in triggers matter most: agreed in advance, they turn any intervention into a planned support rather than a public overruling. Option A protects this quarter's optics at the cost of the pipeline — "something smaller later" is how capable people stay untested until they leave; note the evidence in the scenario points the other way (strong last delivery, self-nominated ambition). Option B confuses development with abandonment: senior-stakeholder skills are learned fastest with a safety net, and an unsupported failure in a sensitive area harms the associate, the product, and your stakeholders simultaneously. Option D is the worst of all: nominal authority with real authority quietly retained teaches the stakeholders to bypass the associate and teaches the associate that your delegation is theatre. Delegation scenarios score on one dimension above all: did you transfer genuine ownership while engineering the failure modes down to survivable size?

**Question 9 (challenging) — Pressure to reframe a benefits case**

Your product's business case promised £400,000 annual savings from reduced casework handling time. A year in, verified data shows £150,000 — handling time fell less than modelled. A programme lead preparing the annual review says: "Add the £180,000 of 'staff satisfaction and error-reduction benefits' from the wellbeing survey to close the gap — it's all benefit, after all." The survey is real but its monetisation method was explicitly flagged by your analysts as not robust. What is the most effective response?

- A) Include the £180,000 — benefits are benefits, and the review needs to land well.
- B) Report the £150,000 verified against the £400,000 promise, analyse why the model overestimated, present the wellbeing findings qualitatively (or with their monetisation caveats fully stated), and bring options for closing the genuine gap — further automation, scope change, or a revised benefits baseline.
- C) Report only the £150,000 with no explanation, and let the review draw its own conclusions.
- D) Ask the analysts to rework the monetisation method until it clears the robustness bar, then include whatever figure results, delaying the review if needed.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The pressure here is subtle because the proposed padding is not fabricated — the survey exists; only the monetisation is unsound, and "it's all benefit" is half true. That is exactly how benefits cases decay: each increment defensible-ish, the total indefensible. Option B holds the evidential line your **Creating value for money** skill draws — analyse the benefit of investment against anticipated outcomes means verified benefit against original promise, variance explained — while treating the wellbeing evidence honestly: qualitative findings presented as qualitative, or monetised with the analysts' caveats attached, never laundered into the headline. The options it brings (automation, scope, baseline revision) matter equally: a £250,000 variance is a product problem to manage, not just a number to confess, and reviews respect teams that arrive with the gap named and a plan. Option A launders a flagged-unsound figure into a governance document over your analysts' explicit objection — if audited, the finding is not "optimistic" but "knew and included anyway". Option C is honest but inert; unexplained variance invites the review to write its own story, usually a worse one. Option D sounds rigorous but reverses the method: commissioning analysis to reach a predetermined figure is the same integrity breach in slow motion, and delaying governance for it compounds the cost. The rule: the number the evidence supports, the variance explained, the plan attached — and soft benefits reported in their native currency.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Most and least effective: the failing experiment**

Six weeks ago, with stakeholder fanfare, your team launched an opt-in "smart reminders" pilot you championed. Data now shows negligible uptake (2%), no measurable effect on missed appointments among users, and two complaints about reminder timing. The team is divided between extending the pilot with heavier promotion and stopping it. Rank the responses: which is MOST effective and which is LEAST effective?

1. Run a structured review against the pilot's original success criteria, share the results and your recommendation openly with the team and stakeholders — including that you championed it — and either stop or run one bounded, hypothesis-driven iteration if the review identifies a specific fixable cause of low uptake.
2. Extend the pilot with a bigger promotion budget, since the idea deserves a fair chance.
3. Quietly wind the pilot down without a stakeholder announcement, folding its budget back into the roadmap.
4. Continue the pilot unchanged for another quarter to gather more data before any decision.

- A) Most effective: 1; least effective: 3
- B) Most effective: 2; least effective: 4
- C) Most effective: 1; least effective: 4
- D) Most effective: 3; least effective: 2

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Response 1 is the clear maximum: it judges the pilot against its own pre-agreed criteria (the discipline that makes experiments experiments), it is open about the outcome and about your own advocacy — which is what "be open about your decision making" costs when it matters — and it leaves one evidence-gated path to iteration, honouring "recognise, reflect and adapt when something does not work, encouraging experimentation" without honouring sunk costs. For least effective, compare 2, 3, and 4. Response 2 spends more to avoid updating on evidence — classic escalation of commitment — but it is at least visible and testable; if promotion fails too, the case closes. Response 4 is passive sunk-cost drift, wasting a quarter, but it deceives nobody. Response 3 is qualitatively different: it reaches the right operational outcome (stopping) by a method that corrodes the environment — stakeholders who celebrated the launch discover the disappearance later, learn that your team announces successes and buries failures, and discount your next launch accordingly; the team learns that failed experiments are shameful secrets, which kills the willingness to experiment at all. So most: 1, least: 3 — option A. The scoring insight for most/least formats: the least effective response is the one that damages trust or hides signal, not the one that merely wastes resources. Money returns; credibility compounds in whichever direction you last moved it.

**Question 11 (challenging) — A data request that feels wrong**

A policy team urgently drafting advice for ministers asks you to send over "the raw user feedback database, including the free-text comments" from your service. The free-text comments frequently contain personal details users volunteer — health conditions, family circumstances, occasionally names and addresses — despite the form's warnings. The request is legitimate in purpose and comes with genuine time pressure. What is the most effective response?

- A) Send the database — the purpose is legitimate, the requesters are civil servants, and the deadline is real.
- B) Refuse on data protection grounds and suggest they submit a formal request through information governance, which typically takes three weeks.
- C) Establish what question the policy team actually needs answered, and meet it with proportionate data — aggregated themes, redacted or sampled comments prepared with your analyst — while checking the sharing basis with your data protection colleagues in parallel, at the pace the deadline requires.
- D) Send the database but ask the policy team to promise to delete the personal details after use.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Both halves of this dilemma are real: ministers' advice deadlines are genuine, and free-text comments containing volunteered health and family details are personal data whose reuse for a different purpose needs a lawful basis and minimisation. Option C resolves it with the move that experienced practitioners recognise as the professional standard: interrogate the need behind the request. Policy teams almost never need raw databases; they need answers — "what are users telling us about X" — and aggregated themes with illustrative redacted quotes usually serve the purpose better than 40,000 raw rows, while transforming the data protection question from "can we ship the database?" (hard, slow, probably no) to "can we share this analysis?" (usually yes, fast). Running the governance check in parallel rather than in sequence respects the deadline honestly. Option A treats requester legitimacy as a lawful basis, which it is not — purpose limitation exists precisely for well-intentioned internal reuse. Option D acknowledges the problem and then contracts it away; a deletion promise neither creates a sharing basis nor un-shares anything. Option B is the process-shaped failure: technically defensible, practically a refusal to help, and it teaches urgent requesters to route around you — the worst long-term outcome for data protection, not the best. Effective compliance behaviour serves the need and the rules simultaneously; the skill is refusing the request's form while meeting its substance.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Your product should probably be retired**

You have managed the internal reporting product for two years. Usage data shows a 60% decline as teams migrate to the department's new analytics platform; your last user survey drew 31 responses, mostly from one directorate; keeping the product compliant with new security requirements will need roughly 40% of your team's capacity next year. Nobody senior has asked whether the product should continue — its funding renews by default, your team likes working on it, and its retirement would shrink your own portfolio. What is the most effective response?

- A) Continue delivering well — retirement decisions belong to whoever funds the product, and no one has raised it.
- B) Build the analysis yourself: usage trends, cost of continuation including the security work, the migration path for the remaining users (especially the dependent directorate), and bring a retirement-or-invest recommendation to your senior product manager and governance, even though it may argue your own product away.
- C) Quietly reduce effort on the product and let it degrade until retirement becomes inevitable.
- D) Commission a survey campaign to boost engagement and justify the product's continuation before anyone examines the numbers.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Leading a product through the life cycle "to retirement" is written into your role level, and this scenario tests whether you honour the clause that has no natural constituency — nobody lobbies for retirement, budgets renew by inertia, teams prefer building, and your own portfolio shrinks if you succeed. Option B is strategic ownership pointed at yourself: the same analysis you would demand for any investment (trend, cost, benefit, user impact) applied to continuation, which is, after all, a yearly re-investment decision whether or not anyone frames it that way. The migration-path work is where user-centredness shows: the dependent directorate's 31 respondents are exactly the users a lazy retirement would strand, and a good recommendation retires the product without retiring their capability. Option A hides behind formal accountability: funders decide, but deciding well requires the analysis only the product manager can assemble — withholding it by silence is a decision too, just an unexamined one. Option C reaches retirement by managed neglect, degrading service for remaining users while dodging the honest conversation — the passive-aggressive cousin of option 3 in the previous question. Option D is the darkest distractor: manufacturing engagement evidence to defend a product against scrutiny inverts your entire evidential duty. The deepest behaviour this assesses appears in your skills as one quiet line — decide when it's appropriate to reuse something, and when it's not; analyse benefit against outcomes — applied where it is hardest: a product manager's value is measured by outcomes delivered, not products retained, and the practitioners who recommend their own product's retirement when the evidence says so are the ones organisations learn to trust with bigger products.

### Preparation tips

- Extract the principles, not the answers. Across every scenario above, effective responses share a fingerprint: engage rather than avoid; be proportionate rather than maximal; convert conflict into evidence; keep bad news moving at the speed of good news; protect users who are not in the room; and leave every party knowing where they stand.
- Rehearse your escalation ladder: resolve directly → mediate with evidence → escalate jointly and openly → escalate alone as last resort. Most wrong options live at the ladder's extremes — quiet avoidance at one end, regulators and directors as a first move at the other.
- For most/least formats, find the most effective by asking "which response solves the problem and strengthens the system?" and the least effective by asking "which response hides a signal, deceives someone, or corrodes trust?" — not "which is most timid".
- Notice the recurring negotiables and non-negotiables: dates, scope, and capacity trade openly against each other; evidence integrity, user safety, accessibility, and data protection do not trade at all. Options that trade a non-negotiable are wrong regardless of how diplomatic they sound.
- Run your own week through the format: after each difficult moment, sketch the four responses you could have made and honestly rank them. This is the highest-yield practice available, and it doubles as reflective practice — a behaviour your Agile and Lean skill names explicitly.
- Before the assessment, reread the Civil Service values, the Service Standard, and your organisation's product principles. Scoring keys are drawn from those documents; fluency in their vocabulary steadies your instincts under time pressure.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- Choosing conflict avoidance dressed as diplomacy: quiet wind-downs, annexed bad news, softened findings, unanswered requests. If the mechanism is controlling what others know, it scores poorly — and works worse.
- Choosing unilateral heroics dressed as decisiveness: refusing all date conversations, cancelling demos, building both versions, absorbing impossible commitments personally. Product managers mediate and equip; they rarely win alone.
- Choosing maximal escalation as a first move: reporting colleagues to panels, directors, or regulators before any direct conversation. Proportionality is scored; the ladder has lower rungs for a reason.
- Confusing supporting seniors with echoing them. Support means evidence, options, and honest limits — the colleague who commits to a date they disbelieve is not supporting anyone, least of all the senior who repeats it.
- Forgetting sunk costs and self-interest as biases the assessment deliberately probes. Scenarios featuring your own championed pilot, your own portfolio, or your own estimate are testing whether evidence still wins when it costs you.
- Solving the request instead of the need. Raw-database requests, mid-sprint feature demands, and "make it £100,000" asks all have a legitimate need buried in an illegitimate form; the top-scoring response refuses the form and serves the need.
