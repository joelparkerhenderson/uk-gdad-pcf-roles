## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the thinking processes your role genuinely demands, using practical workplace materials rather than abstract shapes and number sequences. Rather than asking you to rotate a cube in your head, it puts in front of you the sorts of things an associate user researcher really handles — a recruitment screener, a session schedule, a consent checklist, a set of research notes, a short extract from a research plan — and asks you to reason about them accurately and at a reasonable pace.

The usual format is an online test lasting somewhere between fifteen and thirty minutes. The question types cluster into five families: pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective, and your performance is compared against a norm group of people applying for or working at a similar level, so you are being measured against realistic expectations rather than against senior specialists.

Two features of modern testing platforms are worth knowing about in advance, because both can be unsettling if they surprise you. The first is adaptive testing: some platforms adjust difficulty based on how you are doing, making questions harder after correct answers and easier after incorrect ones. If the test suddenly feels tough, that usually means you are performing well, so treat difficulty as encouragement rather than alarm. The second is the acuity report: employers often receive a breakdown of speed against accuracy rather than a single number. This matters because it means rushing is not rewarded. A steady, accurate performance generally reads better than a fast, error-strewn one.

For an associate user researcher specifically, cognitive assessment is relevant because so much of the early-career value you add comes from noticing and from carefulness. You notice that a participant's answer contradicts something they said ten minutes earlier. You notice that the session schedule has two participants booked into the same slot. You notice that a consent form has not been signed. You notice that the recruitment screener would exclude exactly the users the team most needs to hear from. Each of those is a small act of cognition — comparison, deduction, error detection — and each one protects the quality of the research.

There is one more thing worth saying clearly, because trainees often worry about it. At your level, nobody is testing whether you have memorised research methods, ethics codes or statistical formulae. Every rule you need in the questions below is written into the question. Your task is to apply given information carefully and consistently — which is precisely what you do at work when a senior researcher briefs you and you then carry the task out independently.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The cognitive dimensions map directly onto the skills named in your role summary:

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **Analysis and synthesis**, where you are asked to show an awareness of the need for careful analysis of research data to produce clear findings. Analysis begins with seeing that three participants stumbled at the same step, or that one page attracts a cluster of complaints. Before you can synthesise, you must notice.
- **Error checking** maps to **Research management, leadership and assurance**, where you must explain the importance of working to a user research plan and of following standards for user research. Standards only protect people if somebody actually checks that they have been met — that the consent was recorded, that the incentive was logged, that the plan was followed.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Analysis and synthesis** again, and to **User-centred practice and advocacy**, where you describe how services and technology affect how people behave. Sound deduction is what stops "two people said this" from becoming "users think this". Knowing exactly what your evidence does and does not license is a research skill before it is a test skill.
- **Prioritisation** maps to **Agile research practices**, where you describe agile working and the roles in a multidisciplinary digital team. Agile teams work in short cycles with competing demands, so deciding what to do first — and being able to explain why — is a daily requirement.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **User research methods** and to **Inclusive research**, where you must show awareness of the diversity of users of government services and the need to make services usable and accessible for everyone. When a participant cannot use the prototype in the way you planned, or when a recruitment approach is quietly excluding disabled users, you need to break the problem down and find a workable, inclusive next step.
- **Careful reading of instructions** cuts across all of these and maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**, where you describe who your stakeholders are and what they find important. Understanding precisely what somebody has asked for, before you act, is the foundation of a good working relationship.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Error checking a session schedule**

Your team's rule is that every usability session must have a fifteen-minute gap after it, to allow for notes and to absorb overruns. You are checking tomorrow's schedule:

| Session | Start | End |
|---|---|---|
| P1 | 09:00 | 10:00 |
| P2 | 10:15 | 11:15 |
| P3 | 11:25 | 12:25 |
| P4 | 13:00 | 14:00 |

How many gaps between consecutive sessions break the fifteen-minute rule?

- A) 0
- B) 1
- C) 2
- D) 3

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Work out each gap in turn rather than eyeballing the table. P1 ends at 10:00 and P2 starts at 10:15, which is a gap of fifteen minutes — that meets the rule, because the rule says fifteen minutes, not more than fifteen. P2 ends at 11:15 and P3 starts at 11:25, a gap of ten minutes — this breaks the rule. P3 ends at 12:25 and P4 starts at 13:00, a gap of thirty-five minutes, which comfortably satisfies it. So exactly one gap breaks the rule, and the answer is B. Two habits are being tested here. The first is calculating each interval separately instead of scanning for something that "looks tight". The second is reading the rule precisely: a rule stating fifteen minutes is met by exactly fifteen minutes, and candidates who assume it means "more than fifteen" will wrongly answer C. In real life this check matters, because a ten-minute gap is how a researcher ends up greeting a participant while still writing up the last one.

**Question 2 (easy) — Logical deduction from a consent rule**

Your team's research standard states: "A session recording may be shared with the wider team only if the participant has given recorded consent for sharing, unless the recording has been fully anonymised and approved by the lead user researcher." A colleague tells you: "I shared yesterday's recording with the whole team, and the participant did not give consent for sharing." Which one of the following must be true?

- A) Your colleague broke the research standard.
- B) The recording was fully anonymised and approved by the lead user researcher.
- C) Either the recording was anonymised and approved, or the standard was broken.
- D) The participant will complain.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** This is a conditional rule with a single stated exception, and the facts you have been given do not tell you whether the exception applies. Option A leaps to the conclusion that the standard was broken, but the exception route might have been used. Option B leaps the other way, assuming the exception was used, and you have no evidence for that either. Option D introduces something the question never mentions. Only option C is guaranteed in every possible version of events: either the exception applied, or the rule was broken, and one of those two things must be so. Assessments reward this discipline of concluding only what the information actually guarantees, and so does research. The workplace parallel is direct: before you suggest a colleague mishandled personal data, you would quietly check whether the anonymisation route was used — which is both better logic and better collegiality.

**Question 3 (easy) — Pattern recognition in session notes**

You are reading the observation notes from five usability sessions on the same prototype. The notes record where each participant hesitated for more than ten seconds:

- P1: the address entry screen
- P2: the address entry screen; the confirmation screen
- P3: the upload evidence screen
- P4: the address entry screen
- P5: the address entry screen; the upload evidence screen

Which screen shows the clearest pattern of difficulty across participants?

- A) The address entry screen
- B) The confirmation screen
- C) The upload evidence screen
- D) No pattern can be seen

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Count the participants affected by each screen rather than counting the total number of mentions. The address entry screen caused hesitation for P1, P2, P4 and P5, which is four of the five participants. The upload evidence screen affected P3 and P5, which is two. The confirmation screen affected P2 alone, which is one. Four out of five is the clearest pattern, so the answer is A. Notice the counting discipline here: what matters in this kind of analysis is how many different people hit a problem, not how many times the problem is written down. Notice too that identifying the pattern is not the same as explaining it — the address screen might be badly labelled, or it might simply be the first screen where users have to type something, and typing is slower than tapping. Your role summary asks you to show awareness of the need for careful analysis, and this is what that looks like in miniature: count properly first, interpret second.

**Question 4 (easy) — Prioritisation on a research day**

It is 8:45 am and your first session starts at 9:30. Four things need attention:

1. The participant for the 9:30 session has emailed to say they cannot find the video call link.
2. Your ticket on the team board has not been moved into "in progress" before the 9:15 stand-up.
3. A designer has asked whether you can review a new prototype screen at some point today.
4. The weekly user research community newsletter has arrived in your inbox.

What should you do first?

- A) Move your ticket so the board is accurate for stand-up.
- B) Send the participant the video call link.
- C) Review the designer's prototype screen while the day is quiet.
- D) Read the newsletter before things get busy.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Prioritisation questions ask you to weigh urgency against impact. Item 1 is both: a participant who cannot join is a session lost, a person's time wasted, and a gap in the evidence the team needs — and the deadline is forty-five minutes away and immovable. It also takes about two minutes to fix. Item 2 is genuinely worth doing and takes less than a minute, so it fits comfortably before the 9:15 stand-up but after the participant has been helped. Item 3 has no stated deadline beyond "today", so it can be scheduled around the sessions. Item 4 is the least urgent, although reading it later supports your connection to the wider research community. The general pattern to carry into the test: something that affects a real person on a fixed deadline outranks internal tidiness, and internal tidiness outranks undated requests.

**Question 5 (easy) — Error checking a consent checklist**

Before each session your team requires four things: (i) the consent form signed, (ii) the recording permission recorded verbally at the start, (iii) the participant's access needs noted, and (iv) the incentive payment logged. You review the tracker for four completed sessions and find: P1 has all four; P2 has the signed form, the verbal permission and the access needs, but no incentive logged; P3 has the signed form and the incentive logged only; P4 has all four. How many of the four required items are missing across the whole tracker?

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Count the missing items, not the number of participants with a problem, because the question asks how many items are missing across the tracker. P1 and P4 are complete, contributing nothing. P2 is missing one item, the incentive log. P3 has only two of the four, so it is missing the verbal recording permission and the access needs — that is two items. One plus two gives three, so the answer is C. The trap here is answering B, which is the number of participants with gaps rather than the number of gaps. Read the question stem with care: "how many of the four required items are missing across the whole tracker" is a count of items. This is not pedantry for its own sake; in the real job the distinction determines whether you tell your lead "two sessions have admin gaps" or "we are missing three records, one of which is a consent-related permission" — and the second is far more useful.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Deduction from a recruitment screener**

A screener for a study says participants must meet all of the following: they have used the service in the last six months; they are aged 25 or over; and they do not work for the department. Four people apply:

- A: used the service four months ago, aged 31, works for a different government department.
- B: used the service last week, aged 22, does not work for the department.
- C: used the service eight months ago, aged 44, does not work for the department.
- D: used the service two months ago, aged 25, works for the department.

How many applicants meet all three criteria?

- A) 0
- B) 1
- C) 2
- D) 3

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Test every applicant against every criterion, one at a time. Applicant A used the service four months ago (within six months, so pass), is 31 (25 or over, pass), and works for a different government department — the criterion excludes people who work for "the department", meaning the one running the study, so a different department does not disqualify them. A passes all three. Applicant B fails the age criterion at 22. Applicant C used the service eight months ago, outside the six-month window, so fails. Applicant D is exactly 25, which satisfies "25 or over", and used the service two months ago, but works for the department, so fails. Only A qualifies, giving B as the answer. Two boundary details are doing the work here: "25 or over" includes 25 exactly, and "the department" is narrower than "any government department". Screener criteria are read this literally in real recruitment too, which is why a well-written screener is worth the time it takes to draft.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Pattern recognition across accessibility data**

Your team runs the same five tasks with two groups: twelve participants who do not use assistive technology, and six participants who use a screen reader. The percentage of participants completing each task unaided is:

| Task | No assistive tech | Screen reader |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 92% | 83% |
| 2 | 83% | 17% |
| 3 | 100% | 100% |
| 4 | 75% | 67% |
| 5 | 92% | 83% |

Which task most clearly needs investigation for an accessibility problem?

- A) Task 1
- B) Task 2
- C) Task 4
- D) Task 5

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The signal you are looking for is not a low score on its own, but a large gap between the two groups, because that gap is what points to a barrier specific to assistive technology. Work out each difference: Task 1 is 9 percentage points, Task 2 is 66, Task 3 is 0, Task 4 is 8, and Task 5 is 9. Task 2's gap of 66 points dwarfs everything else, so the answer is B. Notice that Task 4 has the lowest overall completion rate, at 75% and 67%, which makes it tempting — but its gap is small, so it looks like a task that is hard for everybody rather than one that is inaccessible. That is still worth fixing, but it is a usability problem, not an accessibility barrier. Your role summary asks you to demonstrate awareness of the diversity of users and the need to make services usable and accessible for everyone, and reading a table this way is exactly how that awareness becomes practical.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Error checking against a note-taking standard**

Your team's note-taking standard says observation notes must: record what the participant did and said, avoid recording the participant's name, avoid the researcher's own interpretation, and include a timestamp for each note. You review a colleague's note: "10:14 — Sarah looked confused by the upload button and clearly doesn't understand file formats." How many of the four standards does this note break?

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Check the note against each standard in turn. It does include a timestamp, "10:14", so that standard is met. It records the participant's name, "Sarah", which breaks the second standard. "Looked confused" is the researcher's interpretation rather than an observation of what the participant did or said, and "clearly doesn't understand file formats" is a stronger interpretation still, so the third standard is broken. And because the note contains interpretation in place of behaviour, it fails to record what the participant actually did or said — we never learn whether they hovered, clicked, paused or asked a question — so the first standard is broken too. Three standards are broken, giving C. A better note would read: "10:14 — P3 hovered over the upload button for about twenty seconds, then said 'I'm not sure what it wants from me'." Notice how much more useful that is for analysis, and how it protects the participant's privacy at the same time.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Applied problem solving in a session**

You are five minutes into a remote usability session. The participant's audio is clear but their screen share has failed twice, and they sound increasingly flustered. You have fifty minutes of scheduled time left and a discussion guide with eight tasks. What is the most sensible way to think about this problem?

- A) Keep trying to fix the screen share until it works, because without it there is no usable data.
- B) Reassure the participant, stop troubleshooting, and adapt the session to what is still possible — for example, asking them to describe aloud what they see and do.
- C) End the session immediately and rebook, to avoid wasting the participant's time.
- D) Carry on with all eight tasks and hope the screen share recovers by itself.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Break the problem into what has failed and what still works. The screen share has failed; the audio, the participant's goodwill, and fifty minutes of their time have not. Option B protects the person first — a flustered participant is being made to feel that they are the problem, and reassurance costs nothing — and then salvages real value from what remains. A think-aloud description is genuinely usable data, even though it is not the data you planned for. Option A puts the tool ahead of the person and risks spending the whole session on troubleshooting. Option C throws away fifty minutes of a participant's time when a workable alternative exists, though it would become the right answer if the audio failed too. Option D ignores a live problem, which is the least defensible choice of the four. Afterwards, the genuinely valuable step is to tell your senior researcher what happened, so that the setup instructions can be improved for the next participant — adapting in the moment and improving the system afterwards are two halves of the same skill.

**Question 10 (moderate) — Deduction about what findings support**

In a study with eight participants, six said they would prefer to receive updates by text message rather than email. Which of the following conclusions is best supported by this evidence alone?

- A) Most users of the service prefer text messages to email.
- B) The service should switch all notifications to text messages.
- C) A clear majority of the participants in this study preferred text messages, which is worth exploring further.
- D) Email notifications are unpopular and should be removed.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Eight participants is a normal and useful sample for qualitative research, but it does not support a claim about a whole user population, so option A overreaches by turning "six of our eight participants" into "most users". Options B and D go further still, converting a preliminary finding into a service-wide decision, and D additionally invents a claim about email being unpopular that the evidence never establishes — preferring one channel is not the same as disliking the other. Option C describes exactly what the evidence shows and proposes a proportionate next step. This distinction between what participants said and what users in general do is one of the most valuable habits you can build early. Stating findings at the right strength protects your credibility, and it protects the service from decisions built on thinner evidence than they appear to have.

**Question 11 (harder) — Prioritisation with competing pressures**

You are supporting a study while your senior researcher is on leave for the day. At 11:00 the following are all true:

1. A participant due at 11:30 has replied to say they use a screen reader, which the team did not know when the session was set up.
2. A product manager has asked for the headline findings from last week's sessions by end of day.
3. Two of next week's five participants have dropped out and recruitment closes at 17:00 today.
4. Your notes from yesterday's third session are still unwritten.

What is the most sensible order of work?

- A) 4, 2, 3, 1 — clear the backlog, then deal with the future.
- B) 1, 3, 2, 4 — handle the imminent session, protect next week's recruitment, then the request, then notes.
- C) 2, 1, 3, 4 — the product manager is the most senior stakeholder.
- D) 3, 1, 4, 2 — recruitment first because it has a hard deadline.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Rank by deadline and by consequence. Item 1 is thirty minutes away and involves a participant's access needs, so it comes first: you need to check whether the prototype works with a screen reader and, if it does not, contact the participant honestly rather than putting them through a session that cannot work. Item 3 has a hard 17:00 deadline and, if missed, costs the team two participants that cannot be replaced next week, so it comes second. Item 2 has an end-of-day deadline and is within your control once the recruitment request is submitted. Item 4 matters, but a day-old set of notes is recoverable while a missed recruitment window is not. Option D is close and defensible, but it leaves the screen reader question until after a recruitment task, which risks the 11:30 session going ahead badly. Option C ranks by seniority rather than by consequence, which is the classic error in this question type — a senior stakeholder's undated-until-5pm request does not outrank a participant's access needs half an hour away.

**Question 12 (harder) — Applied problem solving about inclusion**

Your team recruits participants for a benefits service study through an online form shared on social media and through a research participant panel. After three rounds, every participant has been aged between 24 and 45, digitally confident, and in work. The service is used heavily by people who are out of work, and by people over 60. What is the strongest analysis of what has gone wrong?

- A) Nothing has gone wrong; the panel simply reflects who volunteers for research.
- B) The recruitment channels themselves select for digitally confident, socially connected, working-age people, so the sample systematically excludes key user groups and the findings cannot represent them.
- C) The team should run more sessions using the same channels until older and out-of-work participants appear.
- D) The team should stop doing research until a better panel is available.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The strongest analysis names the mechanism, not just the symptom. Both recruitment routes require someone to be online, comfortable with forms, and connected to social media or a research panel — so the channels are quietly filtering the sample before anyone assesses a single applicant. That is why three rounds have produced the same demographic profile: the bias sits upstream in the method, not downstream in chance. Option A mistakes a fixable design flaw for an immovable fact. Option C repeats the flawed method and expects a different result, which will produce more of the same sample and more delay. Option D overcorrects into paralysis when practical alternatives exist — recruiting through job centres, libraries, charities and community organisations, offering telephone or face-to-face sessions, and providing incentives that work for people without bank cards. Your role summary asks you to show awareness of the diversity of users and the need to make services usable for everyone, and recognising that recruitment method determines who gets heard is one of the most consequential forms that awareness takes.

### Preparation tips

- **Practise on real artefacts.** Ask to see an anonymised discussion guide, screener or session schedule from your team, and spend ten minutes checking it against the standards it is meant to meet. This exercises exactly the comparison muscle the assessment measures, and your team gets a free proofread.
- **Slow down to speed up.** Trainees lose more marks to rushing than to not knowing. Read each rule twice before applying it. Careful reading is quicker than re-reading after a wrong turn.
- **Write the rules down.** For deduction questions, jot each condition as a short note — "used service ≤ 6 months", "age ≥ 25", "not our dept". Getting rules out of your head and onto paper frees your attention for the reasoning.
- **Check every option.** These questions usually include one nearly-right distractor. Confirm your answer by quickly testing why each of the other three fails.
- **Count carefully, and count the right thing.** Ask yourself whether the question wants a number of people, a number of items, or a number of rule breaches. They are often different.
- **Do a short timed drill.** Once you feel comfortable, set a ten-minute timer and attempt four questions. The goal is calm familiarity with time pressure, not a speed record.
- **Settle yourself on the day.** Log in early, do the platform's ungraded practice questions, have paper and a pen ready, and treat the first question as a warm-up rather than a verdict.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Importing knowledge that is not in the question.** If the question states three screener criteria, apply those three, not the ones your team uses. Assessments test how you apply given information.
- **Stopping at the first problem you find.** Error-checking questions almost always ask "how many", so keep scanning systematically to the end of the table.
- **Confusing "could be true" with "must be true".** Deduction questions reward only conclusions that hold in every possible case. If you can imagine one scenario where an option fails, it is not the answer.
- **Missing boundary wording.** "25 or over" includes 25. "At least fifteen minutes" is satisfied by exactly fifteen. These single words decide questions.
- **Letting seniority drive prioritisation.** A director's end-of-day request does not outrank a participant's access needs thirty minutes away. Rank by consequence and deadline.
- **Overstating what small samples show.** "Six of our eight participants" is a finding. "Most users" is a claim you have not earned. The test rewards the first, and so will your colleagues.
- **Panicking when the questions get harder.** On an adaptive test, rising difficulty usually means you are doing well. Keep breathing and keep reasoning.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A numeric reasoning assessment measures how well you work with the numbers your job actually produces. This is the section that worries people most, so let us clear something up straight away: this is not a maths exam. You are not being asked to solve equations, remember formulae, or do anything you could not do with a calculator and a clear head. Most platforms let you use a calculator, and where they do not, the arithmetic is deliberately simple. What is being measured is whether you can read a table correctly, pick out the right numbers from a cluttered display, choose the right operation, and interpret the result sensibly.

The format is usually a timed online test of fifteen to thirty minutes, in which each question presents a small table, chart or short paragraph of figures, followed by a multiple-choice question. Time per question is tight — often around a minute — so the real skill is finding the relevant numbers quickly and ignoring the rest. Test designers routinely include figures you do not need, precisely to see whether you can tell the difference.

Some people arrive at this section convinced they are "not a numbers person", especially in a qualitative discipline like user research. That belief is worth challenging, because user research is full of numbers even when it is not statistical. You count participants. You track no-show rates. You report how many of eight people completed a task. You read analytics dashboards to decide where to focus a study. You calculate whether your recruitment is on track. You check that an incentive budget covers the sessions you have planned. None of this requires advanced mathematics, but all of it requires numerical care, and getting it wrong has consequences — a miscounted recruitment target means a session with nobody in it.

At associate level, the numbers you will meet in an assessment are deliberately modest: percentages, fractions, proportions, simple averages, ratios, and reading values from a table. The techniques that carry you through are equally modest. Work out what the question is actually asking before you look at the data. Identify the two or three numbers you need. Do the arithmetic in one clear step at a time. Then sanity-check the answer — if you have calculated that 3 out of 8 participants is 62%, something has gone wrong, and a two-second check catches it.

A final practical note. Wrong-answer options in numeric tests are rarely random. They are usually the results of specific, predictable mistakes: dividing by the wrong total, confusing a percentage with a percentage point, using last month's figure instead of this month's. That means finding your answer among the options is not proof that you are right. It also means that if you understand the common traps, you can often spot them coming.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The numerical skills tested map directly onto the skills named in your role summary:

- **Reading tables accurately** maps to **Analysis and synthesis**, where you show awareness of the need for careful analysis of research data to produce clear findings. Much of the data a research team holds arrives as a table — a participant tracker, a task completion grid, an analytics export — and misreading a row is how a wrong finding gets born.
- **Percentages and proportions** map to **Analysis and synthesis** and to **Inclusive research**, because comparing completion rates between user groups is how accessibility barriers become visible. Percentages let you compare groups of different sizes fairly, which is exactly what you need when you have twelve participants in one group and six in another.
- **Averages** map to **User research methods**, since summarising session length, task time or satisfaction scores is part of reporting a study honestly. Knowing when an average conceals more than it reveals is part of the same skill.
- **Simple budgeting and counting** map to **Research management, leadership and assurance**, where you must explain the importance of working to a research plan. A plan has numbers in it — participants, sessions, incentives, days — and following the plan means tracking them.
- **Interpreting figures cautiously** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**, where you explain what your stakeholders find important. Stakeholders often want a number, and part of your job is giving them one that is honest about its own limits.
- **Spotting when a number is implausible** maps to **User-centred practice and advocacy**. If an analytics figure suggests 40% of users complete a step in under two seconds, the most useful thing you can do is ask whether the measurement is right before anyone builds a story on it.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Percentage of participants**

You ran a study with 20 participants. 15 of them completed the main task without help. What percentage completed the task without help?

- A) 60%
- B) 66%
- C) 75%
- D) 80%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A percentage is the part divided by the whole, multiplied by 100. Here the part is 15 and the whole is 20, so the calculation is 15 ÷ 20 = 0.75, and 0.75 × 100 = 75%. The answer is C. It helps to sanity-check with a rough estimate before you calculate: 15 out of 20 is clearly more than half and clearly less than everyone, and it is three quarters of the way up, so an answer near 75% is expected. If you had calculated 60%, you would have divided 15 by 25; if you had reached 80%, you would have divided 16 by 20. Both are the kinds of small slips that time pressure produces, and both are caught by the estimate you made before you started.

**Question 2 (easy) — No-show rate**

Across a round of research you booked 24 sessions. 3 participants did not attend. What was the no-show rate, to the nearest whole percent?

- A) 8%
- B) 12%
- C) 13%
- D) 15%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The no-show rate is the number who did not attend divided by the number booked: 3 ÷ 24 = 0.125, which is 12.5%. The question asks for the nearest whole percent, and 12.5 rounds up to 13, so the answer is C. Option B is the trap for anyone who rounds down or truncates rather than rounding, and standard rounding takes .5 upwards. This is a genuinely useful figure to be able to produce, because no-show rates drive over-recruitment decisions: if you know roughly one in eight participants will not turn up, you know to book nine people to be reasonably confident of getting eight.

**Question 3 (easy) — Reading a table**

Your team tracked how many participants completed each of four tasks, out of 16 participants in total:

| Task | Completed | Did not complete |
|---|---|---|
| A | 14 | 2 |
| B | 10 | 6 |
| C | 16 | 0 |
| D | 12 | 4 |

Which task had a completion rate of exactly 75%?

- A) Task A
- B) Task B
- C) Task C
- D) Task D

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** You need three quarters of 16, which is 12, so you are looking for the task where 12 participants completed. That is Task D, making the answer D. You could also work through each rate: Task A is 14 ÷ 16 = 87.5%, Task B is 10 ÷ 16 = 62.5%, Task C is 16 ÷ 16 = 100%, and Task D is 12 ÷ 16 = 75%. Both routes work, but converting the target percentage into a number of people first is much faster, because it turns four calculations into one calculation and a glance down a column. Looking for the number rather than computing every rate is a habit worth building for timed tests.

**Question 4 (easy) — Simple average**

Five usability sessions lasted 45, 50, 60, 55 and 40 minutes. What was the mean session length?

- A) 48 minutes
- B) 50 minutes
- C) 52 minutes
- D) 55 minutes

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The mean is the total divided by the count. Add the values: 45 + 50 = 95, plus 60 = 155, plus 55 = 210, plus 40 = 250. Divide by 5 sessions: 250 ÷ 5 = 50 minutes, so the answer is B. A useful shortcut for a set of numbers clustered around a round figure is to work from the middle: guess 50, then note the differences from 50 as −5, 0, +10, +5 and −10, which sum to zero, confirming the mean is exactly 50. That method is quicker and less error-prone than long addition under time pressure, and it works whenever the numbers sit close together.

**Question 5 (easy) — Incentive budget**

Your study will run 12 sessions. Each participant receives a £40 incentive. Your budget for incentives is £600. How many sessions in total can the budget cover?

- A) 12
- B) 14
- C) 15
- D) 18

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Divide the budget by the cost per session: £600 ÷ £40 = 15, so the budget covers 15 sessions and the answer is C. If dividing by 40 feels awkward, simplify first by dividing both figures by 10, which turns the sum into 60 ÷ 4 = 15. Note what the question did not ask: it did not ask how much money is left over after the 12 planned sessions, which would be £600 − (12 × £40) = £600 − £480 = £120, and it did not ask how many extra sessions are affordable, which would be 3. All three figures — 15, £120 and 3 — are true statements about this budget, and a test will happily offer you the wrong true answer. The habit that protects you is to re-read the final sentence of the question after you calculate, and check that your number answers that exact sentence. Budget arithmetic like this is a normal part of working to a research plan, and getting it right is how you avoid promising a participant an incentive the study cannot pay.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Comparing two groups fairly**

In a study, 9 of 12 participants who do not use assistive technology completed a task, and 2 of 6 participants who use a screen reader completed it. Which statement is correct?

- A) Both groups had similar completion rates.
- B) The completion rate was 75% for the first group and about 33% for the second.
- C) The first group completed 7 more tasks, so the difference is small.
- D) The rates cannot be compared because the groups are different sizes.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Convert each to a rate so the different group sizes stop distorting the comparison. The first group is 9 ÷ 12 = 0.75, or 75%. The second is 2 ÷ 6 = 0.333, or about 33%. So option B states both correctly. Option A is simply wrong once the rates are calculated. Option C compares raw counts across groups of different sizes, which is the classic error this question is built to catch — 9 and 2 are not comparable when they come from 12 people and 6 people. Option D gets the situation backwards: converting to percentages is precisely how you compare groups of different sizes fairly. A gap of more than 40 percentage points is large enough to warrant investigation, which connects directly to the inclusive research awareness your role asks for.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Percentage change**

Last quarter your team completed 25 research sessions. This quarter it completed 32. What is the percentage increase, to the nearest whole percent?

- A) 7%
- B) 22%
- C) 28%
- D) 32%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Percentage change is the difference divided by the original value, not by the new one. The difference is 32 − 25 = 7. Divide by the original: 7 ÷ 25 = 0.28, which is 28%, so the answer is C. Option A mistakes the raw difference for a percentage. Option B is what you get by dividing by the new figure, 7 ÷ 32, which is the single most common error in percentage-change questions. Option D simply repeats a number from the question, which is a distractor type that catches people who are guessing. The rule worth memorising is short: for percentage change, the denominator is always where you started from.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Working backwards from a percentage**

In a survey, 30% of respondents said they had struggled to upload evidence. That was 45 people. How many people responded to the survey in total?

- A) 75
- B) 135
- C) 150
- D) 300

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** You know that 30% of the total equals 45, so the total is 45 ÷ 0.30 = 150, and the answer is C. If dividing by a decimal feels awkward, reason in steps instead: if 30% is 45, then 10% is 45 ÷ 3 = 15, and 100% is 15 × 10 = 150. That stepwise route is reliable under pressure and easy to check. You can verify the result quickly: 30% of 150 is 45, which matches. Option B is 45 × 3, which would be right only if 45 were one third rather than 30%. Working backwards from a percentage to a total comes up often in real reporting, particularly when a stakeholder quotes you a percentage and you need to know how many people it actually represents — the difference between 30% of 150 and 30% of 15 matters a great deal.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Ratio in recruitment**

Your research plan requires participants in a ratio of 2:1, meaning two people who have used the service before for every one person who has not. You need 18 participants in total. How many should be people who have not used the service before?

- A) 6
- B) 9
- C) 12
- D) 18

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** A ratio of 2:1 means the group is divided into 2 + 1 = 3 equal shares. With 18 participants, each share is 18 ÷ 3 = 6. Previous users take two shares, which is 12 people, and people who have not used the service take one share, which is 6. The question asks for the second group, so the answer is A. Option C answers the question the other way round, which is the trap for anyone who reads quickly and grabs the larger number. The discipline that prevents that error is to write down what each share represents before you calculate, and to re-read the final sentence of the question before choosing. Getting recruitment ratios right matters in practice, because a sample skewed towards experienced users will make a service look far easier to use than it is for newcomers.

**Question 10 (moderate) — Choosing the right total**

An analytics report for a service shows: 8,000 users started an application, 6,400 reached the evidence upload step, and 4,800 submitted a completed application. What percentage of the users who reached the evidence upload step went on to submit?

- A) 60%
- B) 68%
- C) 75%
- D) 80%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Identify the correct denominator before doing anything else. The question asks about users who reached the upload step, so the whole is 6,400, not 8,000. The part is 4,800. So 4,800 ÷ 6,400 = 0.75, which is 75%, giving C. Option A is 4,800 ÷ 8,000, which answers a different and unasked question about the whole funnel. This is the single most common trap in funnel data, and it is a trap with real consequences: telling a team that three quarters of people get through the upload step is a very different message from telling them that three fifths of all applicants finish. Read the question stem twice, underline the group it names, and only then look at the figures.

**Question 11 (harder) — Percentage points versus percentages**

Before a design change, 40% of participants completed a task. After the change, 50% completed it. Which statement is accurate?

- A) Completion rose by 10%.
- B) Completion rose by 10 percentage points, which is a 25% increase.
- C) Completion rose by 25 percentage points.
- D) Completion rose by 50%.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This distinction trips up experienced professionals, so it is worth learning properly. The arithmetic difference between 40% and 50% is 10 percentage points, which is the correct unit for the gap between two percentages. The relative increase is a different calculation: the rise of 10 divided by the starting value of 40 gives 0.25, which is a 25% increase. Option B states both correctly. Option A uses "10%" loosely, which is ambiguous at best and wrong at worst. Option C misapplies the percentage-point label to the relative figure. Option D appears to have used the new value, 50, as the increase. Why this matters at work: "completion rose 25%" sounds far more impressive than "completion rose 10 percentage points", and both describe the same change. Being precise about which you mean is part of reporting research honestly.

**Question 12 (harder) — Interpreting an average that hides something**

Six participants took the following times to complete a task, in minutes: 3, 4, 4, 5, 5, and 33. A colleague reports "the average task time was 9 minutes". What is the best comment on this report?

- A) The average is wrong; it should be 5 minutes.
- B) The average is correct, but it is misleading because one very long time pulls it well above what five of the six participants experienced.
- C) The average is fine and needs no comment.
- D) The 33-minute result should be deleted from the data.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** First check the arithmetic: 3 + 4 + 4 + 5 + 5 + 33 = 54, and 54 ÷ 6 = 9, so the mean of 9 minutes is calculated correctly, which rules out A. But no participant took anywhere near 9 minutes — five of them finished within 5 minutes, and one took 33. The mean has been dragged upward by a single extreme value, so it describes nobody's actual experience. Option B says exactly this. Option C lets a misleading summary stand. Option D is the worst answer of the four: deleting an inconvenient data point is a serious research failing, and in any case that 33-minute session is probably the most interesting thing in the dataset, because something went badly wrong for that person and finding out what is the whole point of the research. The better report would give the median of 4.5 minutes alongside the range, and then describe the outlier separately. Reporting a distribution rather than a single number is a habit that will serve you for your entire career.

### Preparation tips

- **Practise the four core operations only.** Percentages, percentage change, averages and ratios cover the great majority of questions at this level. Drill those four until they feel automatic, and ignore everything more advanced.
- **Estimate before you calculate.** A rough answer takes three seconds and catches most serious errors. If your estimate says "about three quarters" and your calculation says 62%, you know to look again.
- **Find the denominator first.** Before touching any figures, decide what the whole is. Most numeric errors in real research reporting are denominator errors, not arithmetic errors.
- **Say the units out loud in your head.** "Nine of twelve people" and "nine minutes" behave differently. Naming what you are counting prevents you from combining things that should not be combined.
- **Learn the percentage point distinction properly.** Write yourself one sentence about it and re-read it before any assessment. It comes up constantly and it is easy marks once it clicks.
- **Use your own team's data as practice material.** Take a real participant tracker and calculate the completion rate, the no-show rate and the average session length. It is more memorable than abstract drills, and it makes you useful.
- **Get comfortable with the calculator you will use.** If the platform provides an on-screen calculator, practise with one. Fumbling an unfamiliar interface wastes the time you need for thinking.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Dividing by the wrong total.** Funnel questions in particular are built around this. Underline the group the question names before you calculate.
- **Confusing percentages with percentage points.** A rise from 40% to 50% is 10 percentage points and a 25% increase. Both are correct; they are not interchangeable.
- **Comparing raw counts across groups of different sizes.** Nine out of twelve and two out of six cannot be compared as 9 versus 2. Convert to rates first, every time.
- **Trusting an answer because it appears in the options.** Distractors are built from the specific mistakes candidates make, so seeing your figure listed is not confirmation.
- **Rounding too early.** Carry your working to at least one decimal place and round only at the end, or small errors will compound.
- **Truncating instead of rounding.** 12.5% to the nearest whole percent is 13%, not 12%.
- **Reporting a mean without checking the spread.** If one value is wildly different from the rest, the mean will mislead. Glance at the range before you quote an average.
- **Assuming you are bad at this.** Numeric reasoning at this level is a set of learnable techniques, not a talent. Every trap described here is one you can learn to see coming.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately you read. That sounds almost too simple to test, but it turns out to be one of the strongest predictors of performance in roles that run on documents, and user research is such a role. You read research standards, ethics guidance, discussion guides, policy summaries, stakeholder emails, accessibility requirements and the words participants actually say. Getting any of those slightly wrong changes what you do next.

The classic format presents a short passage — typically three to six sentences of dense workplace prose — followed by statements you must classify as True, False or Cannot Say. True means the statement follows necessarily from the passage. False means the passage contradicts it. Cannot Say means the passage neither confirms nor contradicts it, so you would need information you have not been given. Alongside these, you will often meet straightforward comprehension questions, questions about the author's main point, and questions asking you to identify which of four summaries is most accurate.

The golden rule of this section is that you answer only from the passage. Everything you know about user research, everything you have read about GDPR, everything your team does in practice — all of it must be set aside. If the passage says something you believe to be wrong, the passage still wins. This feels unnatural, and it is the single biggest source of lost marks. Candidates read a statement, recognise it as true of the world, and mark it True even though the passage never said it. That is a Cannot Say.

Cannot Say deserves particular attention, because people avoid it. It feels like an admission of defeat, so candidates reach for True or False to seem decisive. In reality, Cannot Say is very often the correct answer, and choosing it confidently is a mark of good reading rather than weak judgement. Whenever you feel yourself supplying a plausible link that the passage does not actually make, you have found a Cannot Say.

Watch the small words, because they carry most of the meaning. "All" is not "most". "May" is not "must". "Some evidence suggests" is not "research shows". "Should" is not "will". A statement that swaps any of these for a stronger or weaker version is False, or Cannot Say, even though it reads almost identically to the passage. Test writers construct their distractors from exactly these swaps.

For an associate user researcher, this is a section where you can genuinely excel, because the discipline it demands is the same discipline that good research demands. Distinguishing what a participant said from what you inferred they meant is the same mental move as distinguishing what a passage states from what it implies. Practising one improves the other.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The verbal skills tested map onto your role's named skills:

- **Reading precisely** maps to **Research management, leadership and assurance**, where you must explain the importance of following standards for user research. Standards are written documents, and following one requires reading it as written rather than as remembered.
- **Distinguishing statement from inference** maps to **Analysis and synthesis**, where careful analysis of research data produces clear findings. A finding that quietly adds an inference the data does not support is the most common way research goes wrong, and it starts as a reading error.
- **Identifying the main point** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**, where you describe what your stakeholders find important and why. A long email from a policy colleague usually contains one thing they actually need; finding it is a service to both of you.
- **Understanding qualified language** maps to **User-centred practice and advocacy**, where you explain how user-centred practices build services that adapt to changing needs. Advocacy that overstates the evidence damages trust; advocacy pitched at the right strength builds it.
- **Reading for what is absent** maps to **Inclusive research**, where you show awareness of the diversity of users and the need for accessible services. Noticing that a plan never mentions users with access needs is a reading skill with a direct ethical consequence.
- **Following written instructions exactly** maps to **User research methods**, where you explain basic methods. A method described in a guide only works if it is carried out as described.

### Practice questions

*Passage A — for Questions 1 to 4*

"All user research involving members of the public must be recorded in the team's research log within two working days of the session. The log entry must state the date, the method used and whether consent was obtained. Recordings of sessions may be retained for up to twelve months, after which they must be deleted unless the lead user researcher has documented a specific reason for keeping them for longer. Anonymised notes are not subject to the twelve-month limit."

**Question 1 (easy)** — Statement: "A session held on a Monday must be logged by the following Wednesday, assuming no public holidays." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The passage requires logging within two working days of the session. Counting two working days from Monday gives Tuesday and Wednesday, so the deadline is Wednesday, and the statement adds the correct caveat about public holidays. The statement follows necessarily, so it is True. Note the care taken over "working days" rather than "days" — that distinction is exactly the sort of small word that decides these questions, and the statement respects it. If the statement had said "by the following Tuesday", it would have been False.

**Question 2 (easy)** — Statement: "Anonymised notes must be deleted after twelve months." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The final sentence of the passage says directly that anonymised notes are not subject to the twelve-month limit, so the statement contradicts the passage and is False. Be careful not to over-read that final sentence in the other direction, though: saying the twelve-month limit does not apply is not the same as saying the notes may be kept for ever, and a statement claiming they can be kept indefinitely would be a Cannot Say, because the passage simply does not address it. The passage tells you which rule does not apply, not which rule does.

**Question 3 (moderate)** — Statement: "A recording kept for fourteen months is a breach of the rules." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The passage sets a twelve-month limit but explicitly allows a longer retention period where the lead user researcher has documented a specific reason. You have not been told whether such a reason was documented for this recording. If it was, fourteen months is permitted; if it was not, fourteen months is a breach. Because both remain possible, the correct classification is Cannot Say. This is the archetypal Cannot Say structure: a rule with an exception, plus facts that do not tell you whether the exception applies. Candidates who mark this False are reasoning from the general rule and forgetting the exception clause they read three lines earlier.

**Question 4 (moderate)** — Statement: "The log entry must include the participant's name." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The passage lists three things the log entry must state — the date, the method used, and whether consent was obtained. The participant's name is not among them. But listing three required items does not tell you that nothing else is required, and it does not tell you that names are forbidden; the passage is simply silent on the point. So the answer is Cannot Say rather than False. This is a genuinely subtle distinction and it appears constantly in verbal reasoning tests. False would require the passage to say something incompatible with the statement, such as "the log must not contain names". A list of requirements is not automatically an exhaustive list.

*Passage B — for Questions 5 to 8*

"Recruitment for the pilot study was carried out through an online panel. The team acknowledges that this approach may under-represent people who are not confident using digital services. Two of the twelve participants used assistive technology. The team plans to supplement the pilot with face-to-face sessions in three community locations, subject to funding being confirmed in April."

**Question 5 (easy)** — Statement: "Twelve people took part in the pilot study." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The passage states that two of the twelve participants used assistive technology, which establishes that there were twelve participants in total. The statement restates that directly, so it is True. This is the most straightforward question type in the section — a fact stated plainly, tested plainly — and it is worth answering quickly and confidently so that you bank the time for the harder items. Resist the urge to look for a hidden trick in easy items; sometimes the passage simply says the thing.

**Question 6 (moderate)** — Statement: "The face-to-face sessions will take place in April." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The passage says the team plans face-to-face sessions subject to funding being confirmed in April. Two separate assumptions would be needed to reach the statement: that the funding will indeed be confirmed, and that the sessions themselves will happen in April rather than later. The passage supports neither. April is the month named for the funding decision, not for the sessions, and a plan subject to a condition is not a certainty. Cannot Say is correct. This question rewards reading the sentence structure carefully — the phrase "subject to" is a conditional flag, and conditional flags almost always point towards Cannot Say.

**Question 7 (moderate)** — Statement: "The team is aware that its recruitment method has a limitation." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The second sentence says the team acknowledges that the online panel approach may under-represent people who are not confident using digital services. Acknowledging a way in which a method may fall short is precisely being aware of a limitation, so the statement follows and is True. Notice that the passage's word is "may", which is appropriately cautious, and the statement's word is "a limitation", which is general enough to be supported by that cautious phrasing. Had the statement claimed "the team knows its recruitment method excluded digitally excluded users", that would be a stronger claim than "may under-represent", and the answer would shift to Cannot Say.

**Question 8 (harder)** — Statement: "Fewer than one in five pilot participants used assistive technology." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** This combines reading with a small calculation. Two of twelve participants used assistive technology, and one in five of twelve would be 2.4 participants. Two is fewer than 2.4, so the statement holds and is True. You can also work it as percentages: 2 ÷ 12 is about 16.7%, and one in five is 20%, so 16.7% is indeed fewer than one in five. Verbal reasoning sections do occasionally embed simple arithmetic like this, which catches candidates who have mentally switched off their numeric attention. The approach that works is the same as everywhere else in this guide: identify the two numbers you need, do one clear calculation, then check the comparison direction before answering.

*Passage C — for Questions 9 to 12*

"The service standard requires teams to conduct research with users at every stage of a service's life, including after it goes live. Teams should include participants with a range of access needs. Where a team cannot recruit participants with a particular access need, it should record this gap and explain how it will address it. Research findings should be shared openly across the organisation unless they contain personal data or commercially sensitive information."

**Question 9 (easy)** — Statement: "Research should continue after a service goes live." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The first sentence requires research at every stage of a service's life and adds "including after it goes live", which states the point explicitly. The statement is True. This is a good example of a passage anticipating the reader's likely assumption — many people assume research stops at launch, and the standard has been written to close that assumption off. In your own reading, treat an "including" clause as a signal that the author is deliberately covering a case people tend to miss.

**Question 10 (moderate)** — Statement: "A team that cannot recruit any blind participants has breached the service standard." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The passage anticipates exactly this situation and provides a route through it: where a team cannot recruit participants with a particular access need, it should record the gap and explain how it will address it. So failing to recruit blind participants is not in itself a breach; the standard is breached only if the team also fails to record and address the gap. The statement claims a breach has occurred purely from the failure to recruit, which contradicts the passage, making it False. This is worth internalising beyond the test, because it reflects how good standards are written — they set an expectation, then tell you what honesty looks like when the expectation cannot be met.

**Question 11 (harder)** — Statement: "All research findings must be shared openly across the organisation." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The passage says findings should be shared openly "unless they contain personal data or commercially sensitive information". The statement says all findings must be shared, dropping the exception entirely and hardening "should" into "must". Both changes break it. Because the passage explicitly names circumstances in which findings are not shared openly, the statement is contradicted, so it is False rather than Cannot Say. Two small words are carrying the whole question — "all" and "must" — which is exactly the pattern to watch for. Whenever a statement uses an absolute term, go back and check whether the passage contained a qualifier, an exception, or a softer verb.

**Question 12 (harder) — Main point**

Which of the following best captures the main point of Passage C?

- A) Research must be shared openly at all times.
- B) The service standard sets expectations for ongoing, inclusive research, while providing for honest handling of gaps and sensitive material.
- C) Teams should recruit participants with access needs.
- D) Research after a service goes live is the most important stage.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The main point must cover the whole passage without adding anything. Option A is contradicted by the exception clause. Option C is true of one sentence but ignores the other three, so it is a detail rather than the main point. Option D introduces a ranking of importance that the passage never makes — it says research should happen at every stage, not that one stage matters most. Option B captures all four elements: research throughout the life of the service, inclusion of varied access needs, honesty about gaps, and openness with defined limits. Main-point questions reward the answer that is broad enough to cover everything and disciplined enough to add nothing, and the wrong options are usually either a true detail promoted too far or a plausible claim the passage never made.

### Preparation tips

- **Answer only from the passage.** Cover your own knowledge deliberately. Before marking anything True, point to the words that make it true.
- **Get comfortable with Cannot Say.** It is a confident answer, not a hedge. If you are supplying a link the passage does not make, the answer is Cannot Say.
- **Circle the qualifiers.** All, some, most, may, must, should, unless, subject to, usually, at least. These decide more questions than anything else on the page.
- **Read the statement before re-reading the passage.** Knowing what you are checking makes the second read of the passage far faster and more targeted.
- **Distinguish "not required" from "forbidden".** A list of requirements does not rule out other requirements, and silence is not prohibition.
- **Practise on real documents.** Read a page of the GOV.UK Service Manual or your team's research standard, then write three statements about it — one True, one False, one Cannot Say. Making the questions teaches you how they are built.
- **Watch for arithmetic hiding in the prose.** Fractions, ratios and simple counts do appear in verbal sections. Keep your numeric attention switched on.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Answering from world knowledge.** The most frequent cause of lost marks. What you know about data protection is irrelevant if the passage does not say it.
- **Avoiding Cannot Say.** Reaching for True or False to look decisive converts correct readings into wrong answers.
- **Missing the exception clause.** Rules with "unless" or "subject to" almost always generate a Cannot Say or a False, and candidates forget the clause within a few lines of reading it.
- **Accepting hardened language.** "Should" becoming "must", or "may under-represent" becoming "excluded", changes the claim even when the sentence looks familiar.
- **Treating an absolute as harmless.** "All" and "every" are strong words. Verify them against the passage rather than skimming past them.
- **Confusing a true detail with the main point.** Main-point questions punish options that are accurate but partial.
- **Rushing the second half of a long passage.** Test writers know attention fades, and they place the exception clauses accordingly.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment presents realistic workplace scenarios and asks what you would do. There is no passage to decode and no arithmetic to perform. Instead you are shown a dilemma of the kind your role actually produces, given four or five possible responses, and asked to identify the most effective and sometimes the least effective one. Some versions ask you to rank all the options; others ask you to rate each response on a scale from "counterproductive" to "highly effective".

This section measures judgement rather than knowledge, and judgement in a specific sense: whether your instincts align with what the organisation considers good professional practice at your level. That last phrase matters enormously. The same scenario has different best answers for a trainee and for a head of profession. An associate user researcher who unilaterally redesigns a study is overstepping; a lead who escalates every small decision is under-performing. The assessment is calibrated to your level, so answer as the person your role description says you are — someone with real responsibility for doing the work well, working under guidance, who is expected to raise things rather than resolve everything alone.

Four principles will carry you through most scenarios in this section, and they are worth committing to memory because they generalise far beyond any single question.

The first is that participants and users come first. When an option protects a participant's wellbeing, dignity, consent or accessibility, it will almost always outrank an option that protects the schedule, the budget or somebody's convenience. Research ethics is not a box-ticking layer on top of the work; it is the work.

The second is that you should act within your remit and be transparent about the edges of it. At associate level, the best answers usually involve doing what you can competently do, and promptly telling the right person about what you cannot. Neither silent struggling nor reflexive escalation of everything scores well. The sweet spot is: attempt, then tell.

The third is that you address problems directly and early, with the person involved, rather than going around them or letting things drift. Options that involve talking to a colleague honestly and privately tend to beat options that involve complaining to someone else, saying nothing, or firing off an accusatory message.

The fourth is that you fix the instance and then improve the system. The strongest options often do both: they solve today's problem and they feed something back so the problem is less likely to recur. This is how a trainee starts becoming a professional.

One caution about a common trap. Some options are attractive because they are pleasant — agreeing with everyone, avoiding awkwardness, being maximally helpful to whoever asked most recently. Others are attractive because they are decisive — taking charge, making the call, sorting it out yourself. Neither pleasantness nor decisiveness is the criterion. The criterion is what genuinely produces the best outcome for users, for the research, and for the team, given your level of experience.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The judgement dimensions map onto your named skills:

- **Ethical judgement and participant care** map to **Inclusive research**, where you demonstrate awareness of the diversity of users and the need to make services usable and accessible for everyone, and to the ethical core of **User research methods**.
- **Working within a plan and raising deviations** maps to **Research management, leadership and assurance**, where you explain the importance of working to a user research plan and following standards.
- **Collaborating in a multidisciplinary team** maps to **Agile research practices**, where you describe agile working and the roles in a multidisciplinary digital team.
- **Handling requests, pressure and disagreement from stakeholders** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**, where you describe who your stakeholders are, what they find important, and why managing those relationships matters.
- **Representing findings honestly** maps to **Analysis and synthesis**, where careful analysis produces clear findings, and to **User-centred practice and advocacy**, where you explain how user-centred practices build services that adapt to user needs.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — A participant becomes distressed**

You are observing a session, taking notes, while a senior researcher facilitates. The participant is describing a benefits application they made after a bereavement, and becomes tearful. What is the most effective response from you?

- A) Keep taking notes as normal, since the senior researcher is facilitating and it is not your session.
- B) Interrupt to say you are sorry and ask whether they would like to stop.
- C) Stop typing, stay quiet and attentive, and follow the facilitator's lead — being ready to help if asked.
- D) Leave the call quietly so the participant has more privacy.

**Correct answer: C. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Two things are true at once here: the participant's wellbeing matters most, and you are not the facilitator. Option C honours both. Stopping typing is a small act of respect that participants notice — the sound of continued note-taking while someone cries is jarring. Staying attentive and following the facilitator's lead avoids two people trying to manage one moment, which would fluster the participant further. Option A treats note-taking as more important than the person in front of you. Option B is well-meant but cuts across the facilitator mid-moment; the senior researcher may be about to offer exactly that, and a double intervention is disorientating. Option D is the least effective because a participant who has just become upset will experience someone silently leaving as a reaction to them. Afterwards, the valuable things you can do are check in with the facilitator, note that this topic carries emotional weight so future sessions can be designed with more care, and be honest with yourself if the session affected you too.

**Question 2 (easy) — You do not understand the task you have been given**

Your senior researcher asks you to "do a quick affinity map of the last three sessions before Thursday". You have heard of affinity mapping but have never done one. What is the most effective response?

- A) Say yes, then search online and attempt it alone, hoping it comes out right.
- B) Say yes, and add that you have not done one before and would find fifteen minutes of guidance really useful before you start.
- C) Say you cannot do it because you have not been trained.
- D) Say yes, and quietly ask another associate to do it with you instead of telling your senior.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** Your role description says explicitly that you need guidance and training to produce good work, so asking for it is you doing your job correctly rather than falling short. Option B combines willingness with honesty and makes a specific, small, easy-to-grant request — fifteen minutes is far cheaper for your senior than reviewing a misconceived map on Wednesday night. Option A risks producing something unusable and, worse, hides the gap so it never gets filled. Option C is the least effective because it declines the growth opportunity entirely and puts the work back on someone else without offering anything. Option D avoids the awkwardness but leaves your senior with an inaccurate picture of your skills, and it quietly pulls a colleague off their own work. The underlying principle for this whole section: at your level, "yes, and here is what I need to do it well" is almost always the strongest possible answer.

**Question 3 (easy) — A participant has an access need you did not know about**

A participant emails an hour before their remote session to say they use a screen magnifier and may need longer than usual. Your discussion guide has six tasks and a sixty-minute slot. What should you do?

- A) Run all six tasks faster to fit them in.
- B) Reply to thank them for telling you, confirm that the session will go at their pace, and plan to cover fewer tasks if needed.
- C) Cancel and rebook once you have worked out how to adapt.
- D) Run the session as planned and note afterwards that it overran.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A participant who volunteers information about their access needs is doing you a favour and taking a small social risk. The first move is to make that feel welcome, which option B does. Confirming that the session goes at their pace removes the pressure they may already be feeling, and planning to cover fewer tasks accepts the real trade-off honestly: depth on four tasks with this participant is worth more than a rushed sprint through six. Option A is the least effective because rushing a participant who has just told you they need more time actively harms them and produces poor data as well. Option C wastes their time and may read as though their access need is a problem. Option D drifts into an overrun without agreeing it, which is discourteous when someone has given you a fixed hour. Afterwards, mention to your team that the recruitment process is not capturing access needs early enough — that systemic fix is worth more than any single adaptation.

**Question 4 (easy) — Conflicting instructions**

Your senior researcher has asked you to write up yesterday's notes this morning. The delivery manager then asks you to spend the morning helping recruit for next week instead, saying it is urgent. What is the most effective response?

- A) Do what the delivery manager asked, because they are more senior.
- B) Do what your senior researcher asked and tell the delivery manager you are busy.
- C) Tell the delivery manager about the existing task, and ask the two of them to agree the priority — offering your own view of what is achievable.
- D) Try to do both by working through lunch.

**Correct answer: C. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** You have been given two legitimate instructions and you cannot serve both. The problem is not yours to resolve unilaterally, but it is yours to surface immediately and helpfully. Option C does that, and the phrase "offering your own view of what is achievable" is what raises it above simple escalation — saying "I could do the recruitment this morning and the write-up straight after lunch, if that works" makes you part of the solution. Option A ranks by seniority, which is the trap; the delivery manager may not know about the write-up. Option B is abrupt and leaves an urgent recruitment need unaddressed. Option D is the least effective, because working through lunch hides the conflict, sets an unsustainable expectation, and means nobody learns that the team is over-committing you. Making competing demands visible is a genuine contribution in an agile team, not a complaint.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Consent has not been recorded**

You are about to start a remote session. You realise the participant's consent form is not in the shared folder, and you cannot reach your senior researcher, who is in another session. The participant is already on the call. What should you do?

- A) Proceed, and sort out the paperwork afterwards.
- B) Explain the consent points to the participant, ask for their verbal consent, record that clearly at the start of the session, and follow up with the written form afterwards.
- C) Cancel the session and rebook.
- D) Proceed without recording, so there is no data to worry about.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Consent is not paperwork; it is the participant's informed agreement, and the form is only evidence of it. Option B secures the substance — the participant genuinely understands what will happen and agrees to it — documents it in the way your team's standard would recognise, and repairs the record afterwards. Option A is the least effective because it proceeds without consent at all and treats a serious obligation as an administrative afterthought. Option C is over-cautious and costs the participant their time when a proper solution takes ninety seconds, although it would become correct if the participant hesitated or if your team's standard specifically prohibited verbal consent. Option D misunderstands the issue: consent covers taking part, not just recording, so removing the recording does not remove the requirement. The wider lesson is that when a standard cannot be met in the form you expected, ask what the standard is protecting and find another route to protect it.

**Question 6 (moderate) — A stakeholder wants a stronger finding than you have**

You share a short summary of five sessions with a product manager. Three participants struggled to find the "save and return" link. The product manager replies asking you to write in the summary that "users cannot find the save function", so it will be taken seriously in a prioritisation meeting. What should you do?

- A) Make the change, since the product manager knows how to get things prioritised.
- B) Refuse and send the summary unchanged without further comment.
- C) Keep the finding accurate — "three of five participants did not find the save and return link" — and offer to add a line on the potential impact if the pattern holds.
- D) Ask your senior researcher to have the conversation for you.

**Correct answer: C. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** The product manager's underlying goal is legitimate: they want a real problem to get attention. The proposed method is not, because "users cannot find the save function" claims something about all users that five sessions cannot establish. Option C keeps your credibility intact while genuinely helping — adding impact framing is usually what actually moves a prioritisation meeting, and it is honest. Option A is the least effective because once you inflate a finding, every other finding you report becomes negotiable, and the day someone checks the underlying data, the whole study's reputation goes with it. Option B protects accuracy but throws away a chance to help a colleague achieve a reasonable aim. Option D is not wrong — this is a good thing to mention to your senior — but handing over a conversation you are capable of having yourself skips a development opportunity. Do C, and tell your senior about it afterwards.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Recruitment is excluding people**

You notice that every participant recruited so far for a study on a benefits service is in work and confident online, because recruitment has run entirely through an online panel. You are the most junior person on the team. What is the most effective action?

- A) Say nothing, since recruitment is not your responsibility.
- B) Raise it at the next team stand-up or with your senior researcher, describing the pattern you have noticed and asking whether other recruitment routes are possible.
- C) Start contacting community organisations yourself to recruit different participants.
- D) Add a note to the final report saying the sample was unrepresentative.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** You have spotted something important, and spotting it is exactly the "awareness of the diversity of users" your role asks for. Option B converts the observation into action through the right channel, at the right time — early enough that the study can still be changed. Describing the pattern rather than accusing anyone makes it easy for colleagues to engage with. Option A is the least effective: staying quiet because of your grade means a study about a benefits service will systematically miss the people who most rely on it, and juniors noticing things is one of the ways teams stay honest. Option C acts beyond your remit and could cut across existing arrangements, incentive processes and safeguarding considerations, though the instinct behind it is right. Option D is better than nothing but comes far too late to fix anything. Raise problems while they are still cheap to solve.

**Question 8 (moderate) — A colleague's notes contain personal data**

You are reviewing shared session notes and see that a colleague has recorded a participant's full name, their employer, and a detail about their health. Your team's standard says notes must be anonymised. What should you do?

- A) Post in the team channel that the notes breach the standard.
- B) Edit the notes to remove the identifying details, and tell your colleague privately what you changed and why.
- C) Delete the notes entirely.
- D) Leave them, since it was not your session and your colleague is more experienced.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Personal data sitting in a shared folder is a live problem that gets worse the longer it sits there, so acting quickly matters. Option B does both halves of the job: it reduces the exposure now, and it tells the colleague directly and privately so they can correct their practice and check their other notes. Telling them what you changed is important — silently editing someone's work is unsettling and stops them learning. Option A is the least effective because it publicly criticises a colleague, and it also broadcasts the existence of the personal data to a wider audience. Option D lets a data protection issue stand out of deference, which is never the right trade. Option C destroys legitimate research data along with the problem, and deleting a colleague's work without discussion is a significant step. If your colleague responds badly, or if the health detail is particularly sensitive, that is the point to involve your senior researcher.

**Question 9 (harder) — Pressure to change how a session runs**

A policy stakeholder asks to observe a session. Ten minutes in, they unmute and start asking the participant leading questions about a policy option, in a way that suggests the desired answer. You are facilitating and your senior researcher is not present. What should you do?

- A) Let them continue, since they are a senior stakeholder and it is their policy.
- B) Politely intervene straight away — thank them, explain that you need to keep questions neutral, ask them to hold further questions for the debrief, and return to the guide.
- C) End the session.
- D) Message them privately during the call and hope they stop.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Leading questions contaminate the data in real time, and every minute you wait costs you more of the session. Option B is firm and courteous at once, and the structure of it matters: thanking them acknowledges their interest, explaining the reason gives them a principle rather than a telling-off, and offering the debrief gives their genuine curiosity somewhere to go. Most stakeholders comply immediately and are slightly embarrassed, which is why the graciousness is worth the words. Option A is the least effective because it sacrifices the validity of the session, and the participant is now being steered by someone with authority, which is uncomfortable for them too. Option C is disproportionate when a sentence would fix it. Option D is too slow and too quiet, and the participant meanwhile sits watching you type. Beforehand, an observer briefing prevents almost all of this; afterwards, mention what happened to your senior so the briefing can be strengthened.

**Question 10 (harder) — Findings that nobody wants to hear**

Your team has spent four months building a feature. Five of six participants in your first round of testing could not complete the core journey with it. At the show and tell, you have five minutes. What is the most effective approach?

- A) Present the positives and mention the difficulties briefly, to keep morale up.
- B) Present what happened clearly and without blame — what participants tried, where they got stuck, what they said — and frame the next step as finding out why.
- C) Present the failure rate starkly to make sure the team takes it seriously.
- D) Ask your senior researcher to present it instead, since it is bad news.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Difficult findings are the most valuable findings, and how you deliver them determines whether they get acted on or defended against. Option B works because it keeps the focus on observed behaviour rather than on the quality of anyone's work, which lets the team engage with the problem instead of their feelings about it. Ending on a question — why did this happen? — invites the designers and developers into the analysis, and they will often see causes you cannot. Option A is the least effective: softening a five-in-six failure rate means four months of effort continues down the same path, which serves nobody, least of all the people who built it. Option C is accurate but adversarial, and a defensive room learns nothing. Option D is understandable at your level, and it is fine to ask your senior to be present, but handing over the whole message deprives you of the experience and slightly undermines your standing as the person who ran the research.

**Question 11 (harder) — Something goes wrong and it was your mistake**

You realise you sent a recruitment email to twelve prospective participants with all their email addresses in the "To" field rather than "Bcc". What should you do?

- A) Send a follow-up asking everyone to delete the email, and say nothing further.
- B) Tell your senior researcher immediately, describe exactly what happened, and ask what your organisation's process is for reporting a personal data incident.
- C) Wait to see whether anyone complains.
- D) Quietly correct your process for next time and move on.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** This is a personal data breach, small but real, and organisations have defined processes for handling them that may include notifying a data protection officer within a set timeframe. Option B is correct because speed matters, because the decision about what to do next is not yours to make alone, and because being straightforward about your own mistake is the single fastest way to build trust with a senior colleague. Option C is the least effective: waiting for complaints means the clock runs down on any reporting obligation and turns a manageable error into a mishandled one. Option A takes a reasonable step but hides the incident from the people who need to know. Option D learns the lesson while leaving the incident unreported. Everyone makes mistakes like this; what distinguishes people is what they do in the following ten minutes. Report early, report fully, and accept that the discomfort of doing so is much smaller than the discomfort of it emerging later.

**Question 12 (harder) — Being asked to skip research**

With two weeks until a deadline, a delivery manager says there is no time for research on a new screen and asks you to "just give us your opinion as a user researcher instead". What is the most effective response?

- A) Give your opinion, since it is better than nothing and it is what has been asked for.
- B) Explain that your opinion is not research, and propose something small that is genuinely feasible — such as three short sessions or a quick round of guerrilla testing — while being clear about what that will and will not tell them.
- C) Refuse, and explain that proper research takes longer than two weeks.
- D) Agree, and label your opinion as findings so it carries weight.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** The delivery manager has a real constraint and is trying to solve it with the resource in front of them. Option B respects the constraint while protecting the distinction that makes your role worth having: three sessions is not a robust study, but three sessions will reliably catch the worst problems on a single screen, and two weeks is comfortably enough time for that. Being explicit about the limits — "this will find obvious blockers, it will not tell us about people using assistive technology" — lets the team make an informed choice. Option A blurs professional judgement into evidence, and once your opinion is treated as research, the team stops being able to tell the difference. Option D is the least effective because deliberately mislabelling opinion as findings is a straightforward integrity failure. Option C is technically defensible but unhelpfully absolute; small research is real research, and "no" without an alternative teaches the team that research is an obstacle. Do mention the conversation to your senior researcher, both for support and because repeated squeezes on research time are a pattern worth someone tracking.

### Preparation tips

- **Answer as an associate, not as a hero or a bystander.** The best options at your level usually combine competent action with prompt, honest communication upwards.
- **Put the participant first, every time.** When one option protects a participant's wellbeing, consent or access needs, start by assuming it is the strongest and only reject it for a good reason.
- **Look for the option that does two things.** Fixing today's instance and improving tomorrow's system is a recognisable signature of a strong answer.
- **Beware the pleasant option and the decisive option.** Neither agreeableness nor boldness is the criterion. Effectiveness is.
- **Talk to the person involved.** Direct, private, non-accusatory conversation beats public criticism, silence, and going over someone's head.
- **Read the least effective question separately.** The worst option is not simply the opposite of the best one — it is usually the one that causes active harm, conceals something, or lets a problem grow.
- **Discuss real scenarios with your team.** Ask colleagues what they would have done in a tricky session. You will learn your organisation's actual norms, which is what this assessment measures.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Ranking by seniority.** Who asked is far less important than what the consequences are. A senior stakeholder's request does not outrank a participant's access needs or a data protection duty.
- **Escalating everything, or escalating nothing.** Both extremes score poorly. Attempt what is within your competence, then tell the right person about what is not.
- **Choosing silence out of deference.** "It was not my session" and "they are more experienced than me" are the two most common reasons good observations go unshared.
- **Delaying bad news.** Every scenario involving a mistake rewards speed. Problems reported early are cheap; problems discovered late are expensive.
- **Inflating findings to be helpful.** Overstating evidence is the fastest way to lose the credibility that makes your role useful.
- **Solving the immediate problem while ignoring the cause.** If the recruitment process is not capturing access needs, adapting one session is only half an answer.
- **Assuming the awkward option is wrong.** Politely interrupting a senior stakeholder mid-session feels uncomfortable and is frequently correct.
- **Forgetting that you are allowed to ask.** Your role description says you work with guidance. Using it well is competence, not dependency.

## Conclusion

You have reached the end, and that is worth pausing on for a moment. You have just worked through forty-eight practice questions across four different assessment types, and read the reasoning behind every one of them. That is a substantial piece of preparation, and considerably more than most people do.

Look back at what you have covered. In the cognitive section you practised spotting patterns across sessions, checking schedules and consent records against the rules that govern them, deducing only what the evidence guarantees, prioritising a crowded morning, and thinking your way through problems like a failed screen share or a recruitment approach that quietly excludes people. In the numeric section you worked with percentages, no-show rates, averages, ratios and funnel data, and — probably more valuable than any single calculation — you learned to find the right denominator, to tell a percentage from a percentage point, and to notice when a mean is hiding an outlier. In the verbal section you practised the discipline of reading exactly what is written: distinguishing True from Cannot Say, catching the exception clause, and refusing to let "should" quietly become "must". And in the situational judgement section you thought through distressed participants, missing consent, conflicting instructions, a stakeholder who wanted a stronger finding than the evidence supported, and a mistake of your own that needed reporting straight away.

Notice how much of that was not really about tests. Every one of those skills is a working skill. The researcher who counts participants correctly, reads the standard as written, states findings at the strength the evidence supports, and tells their senior about a problem within ten minutes of finding it — that is simply a good researcher. Preparing for these assessments and getting better at your job turn out to be very nearly the same activity, which is the most encouraging thing about the whole exercise.

A word about where you are in your career. You are an associate user researcher: an entry-level, trainee role, working alongside more experienced colleagues, expected to show potential and to need guidance while you build your skills. Please read that as the invitation it is. Nobody is expecting you to have the judgement of someone with ten years behind them. What they are looking for is care, curiosity, honesty about what you do not yet know, and steady improvement — and every one of those is a choice available to you today.

If you want to keep going from here, a few things will help more than re-reading this guide. Ask to observe sessions run by researchers whose work you admire, and pay attention to how they handle the awkward moments rather than the smooth ones. Volunteer to check a screener or a consent tracker, because the checking is where the noticing muscle gets built. Ask your senior researcher what they would have done in the situations from the last section, and expect some of their answers to differ from these ones — real judgement is contextual, and hearing why is how you develop your own. Read your organisation's research standard properly, once, all the way through. And when you get something wrong, say so quickly; you will be surprised how much goodwill that buys.

Finally, the thing worth remembering when the nerves arrive. An assessment is a snapshot taken on one particular morning. It is not a verdict on your ability, your potential, or your future in this profession. You will have good days and less good ones, and the researchers you look up to have all had both. What actually determines how far you go is the quality of your attention to the people who use these services, and your willingness to keep learning — and the fact that you have read this far suggests you have plenty of both.

Good luck. The users of government services are lucky to have people who care enough to prepare like this.
