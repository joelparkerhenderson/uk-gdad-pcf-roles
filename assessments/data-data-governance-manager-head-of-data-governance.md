## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive ability assessment is a targeted psychometric test designed to measure the exact mental processes needed for your role, using practical workplace scenarios and data rather than abstract puzzles. Unlike a generic IQ test, every stimulus is drawn from the material a Head of Data Governance genuinely handles: data catalogues, RACI matrices, policy hierarchies, risk registers, assurance reports and cross-government standards documents.

Typical format: online, timed, usually 15 to 30 minutes, with 15 to 25 questions of varying difficulty. Scoring is objective and normed against a comparison group, and modern platforms often report speed versus accuracy separately rather than a single number. Some platforms adapt question difficulty based on your previous answers, so a test that feels progressively harder usually means you are doing well. You will normally receive short, ungraded practice questions first so you can get comfortable with the interface.

Why it matters for your role: at Head of Data Governance level, cognitive assessment is less about processing trivia quickly and more about structured reasoning under pressure. Can you spot the logical gap in a proposed governance framework? Can you detect the inconsistency between a data catalogue entry and the RACI matrix that supposedly governs it? Can you prioritise correctly when several data risks compete for attention? These are the daily mental operations of strategic data governance oversight, and they are exactly what a well-built job-specific cognitive test simulates.

### What it measures for your role

The assessment dimensions map directly onto the skills in your capability framework:

- **Logical deduction and framework reasoning** map to your **Data governance** skill: defining the data governance strategy, connecting it with organisational strategy, and designing, implementing and monitoring governance throughout the data life cycle all depend on rigorous if-then reasoning about policies, roles and controls.
- **Error checking and consistency analysis** map to your **Data standards** and **Data life cycle** skills: creating data standards, overseeing compliance, and ensuring governance principles are applied with best practice across the life cycle require you to spot contradictions, gaps and misclassifications quickly.
- **Prioritisation and problem solving** map to your **Enabling and informing risk-based decisions** skill: acting as a point of escalation and applying risk methodologies at the most complex levels means ranking issues correctly when everything looks urgent.
- **Pattern recognition and trend inference** map to your **Data innovation** and **Data maturity** skills: horizon-scanning for emerging trends and overseeing the data maturity model both require you to see the signal in noisy, multi-dimensional information.
- **Abstract-to-concrete translation** maps to your **Strategic thinking** skill: leading the design and implementation of strategy demands moving fluently between principles (a policy statement) and instances (a specific dataset, owner or process).

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — RACI consistency check**

Your organisation's RACI matrix for the "Customer Contact Dataset" states:

- Responsible: Data Steward (Operations)
- Accountable: Director of Operations
- Consulted: Data Protection Officer, Head of Data Governance
- Informed: Chief Data Officer

A new draft policy states: "For every critical dataset, exactly one role shall be Accountable, and the Accountable role must not also be Responsible. The Chief Data Officer must be Consulted on all critical datasets."

Which single change is required to make the matrix comply with the draft policy?

- A) Make the Chief Data Officer Accountable instead of the Director of Operations
- B) Move the Chief Data Officer from Informed to Consulted
- C) Remove the Data Protection Officer from Consulted
- D) Make the Data Steward Accountable as well as Responsible

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** Work through each policy clause against the matrix. Exactly one Accountable role: satisfied (Director of Operations only). Accountable must not also be Responsible: satisfied (Director of Operations is not the Data Steward). CDO must be Consulted: violated — the CDO is currently only Informed. Option B fixes the one violation without creating new ones. Option A is unnecessary and changes a compliant element; Option C removes a legitimate consultee for no reason; Option D would directly breach the separation clause. The discipline here — check every clause, change only what is non-compliant — mirrors how you review governance artefacts against standards in real life.

**Question 2 (easy) — Data catalogue classification**

Your data catalogue uses this rule set:

1. A dataset containing personal data is classified at least OFFICIAL-SENSITIVE.
2. A dataset essential to a statutory service is flagged "critical".
3. Critical datasets must have a named data owner within 30 days of cataloguing.
4. Datasets that are both OFFICIAL-SENSITIVE and critical require quarterly assurance review.

The "Benefit Payment Ledger" contains personal data and is essential to a statutory service. It was catalogued 45 days ago and has no named owner. Which statements must be true or represent breaches?

- A) It must be classified at least OFFICIAL-SENSITIVE, is critical, is in breach of the ownership rule, and requires quarterly assurance review
- B) It must be classified at least OFFICIAL-SENSITIVE and is critical, but the ownership rule has not yet been breached
- C) It is critical but its classification is discretionary
- D) It requires quarterly review only if the data owner requests it

**Correct answer: A.**

**Explanation:** Apply the rules in sequence. Rule 1: personal data, so at least OFFICIAL-SENSITIVE. Rule 2: statutory service, so critical. Rule 3: critical plus 45 days elapsed with no owner means the 30-day window is breached. Rule 4: it is both OFFICIAL-SENSITIVE and critical, so quarterly review is mandatory, not discretionary. Option A captures all four conclusions. This is pure rule-chaining — the same deduction you perform when triaging catalogue exceptions.

**Question 3 (moderate) — Policy hierarchy deduction**

Your governance framework has four layers: Principles govern Policies; Policies govern Standards; Standards govern Procedures. A rule at any layer must not contradict any layer above it. You find the following:

- Principle P1: "Data is shared across government unless there is a lawful reason not to."
- Policy PO4: "Datasets classified OFFICIAL-SENSITIVE must not be shared outside the organisation without a data sharing agreement."
- Standard S9: "No dataset may be shared outside the organisation."
- Procedure PR2: "Requests to share OFFICIAL-SENSITIVE data externally are processed via the data sharing agreement workflow."

Which item is non-compliant with the hierarchy?

- A) Policy PO4, because it restricts sharing
- B) Standard S9, because it contradicts both P1 and PO4
- C) Procedure PR2, because it permits external sharing
- D) None — all layers are consistent

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** PO4 conditions sharing on an agreement — a lawful control, consistent with P1's "unless there is a lawful reason" qualifier. S9 imposes an absolute prohibition, contradicting P1 (which presumes sharing) and PO4 (which explicitly contemplates external sharing under agreement). PR2 operationalises PO4 and is consistent with it. The trap is Option A: a restriction is not automatically a contradiction — conditional restrictions can implement a permissive principle. Absolute rules at lower layers overriding conditional rules at higher layers is the classic hierarchy failure you look for when you review governance frameworks.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Error checking a migration report**

A quarterly report states: "Of the 240 datasets in the catalogue, 180 have named owners (75%). Of the datasets with named owners, 120 (60%) have completed retention schedules. Therefore, 50% of all catalogued datasets have completed retention schedules, and the remaining 90 datasets without retention schedules all lack named owners."

Which of the report's claims is wrong?

- A) The 75% figure
- B) The 60% figure — 120 of 180 is 66.7%
- C) The 50% figure — 120 of 240 is 50%, so the figure is right, but the final claim about the 90 datasets is wrong
- D) Both B and the final claim about the 90 datasets

**Correct answer: D.**

**Explanation:** Check each figure. 180/240 = 75%: correct. 120/180 = 66.7%, not 60%: the report's 60% is wrong. 120/240 = 50%: arithmetically correct. Final claim: 240 − 120 = 120 datasets lack completed retention schedules, not 90, and 60 of those (180 − 120) do have named owners — so it is false that all datasets without schedules lack owners. Two errors, hence D. Assurance reports frequently contain exactly this kind of internally inconsistent percentage chain; a head of data governance who signs one off without checking inherits the error.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Risk prioritisation logic**

Four data risks sit on your register. Your escalation rule: escalate to the board any risk that is (high impact AND rising) OR (regulatory in nature AND unmitigated).

- Risk 1: high impact, stable trend, regulatory, mitigated
- Risk 2: medium impact, rising trend, regulatory, unmitigated
- Risk 3: high impact, rising trend, operational, mitigated
- Risk 4: low impact, falling trend, regulatory, unmitigated

Which risks must be escalated?

- A) Risks 1 and 3
- B) Risks 2 and 3
- C) Risks 2, 3 and 4
- D) All four

**Correct answer: C.**

**Explanation:** Test each risk against the two disjunctive conditions. Risk 1: high impact but stable (first condition fails); regulatory but mitigated (second fails) — no escalation. Risk 2: medium impact (first fails); regulatory AND unmitigated (second passes) — escalate. Risk 3: high impact AND rising (first passes) — escalate; mitigation status is irrelevant to the first condition. Risk 4: low and falling (first fails); regulatory AND unmitigated (second passes) — escalate, counterintuitively, because the rule does not consider impact for regulatory unmitigated risks. Option C. The lesson: escalation rules operate on their literal logic, and part of your job is noticing when a rule produces odd outcomes (like Risk 4) that suggest the rule itself needs redesign.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Sequence and dependency reasoning**

You are sequencing a data governance implementation. Constraints:

- The data catalogue must be live before ownership assignment begins.
- Ownership assignment must complete before the RACI matrix is signed off.
- Policy publication can happen any time after the catalogue is live.
- Training cannot start until both the RACI matrix is signed off and policy is published.
- The assurance regime starts only after training starts.

Which is a valid order?

- A) Catalogue live → policy published → RACI sign-off → ownership assignment → training → assurance
- B) Catalogue live → ownership assignment → RACI sign-off → policy published → training → assurance
- C) Ownership assignment → catalogue live → policy published → RACI sign-off → training → assurance
- D) Catalogue live → ownership assignment → training → RACI sign-off → policy published → assurance

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** Check each option against the constraints. A fails because RACI sign-off precedes ownership assignment, violating "ownership before RACI". C fails immediately: ownership assignment precedes the catalogue going live. D fails because training precedes both RACI sign-off and policy publication. B satisfies every constraint: catalogue first, then ownership, then RACI, policy after catalogue (any time), training after both RACI and policy, assurance last. Dependency reasoning like this underpins every credible governance roadmap you present to the CDO.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Pattern recognition in assurance findings**

Across six monthly assurance reviews, the number of datasets failing the metadata completeness standard was: 42, 38, 35, 33, 32, 39. A new self-service analytics platform was launched between month five and month six. Which reading of the pattern is most defensible?

- A) The metadata standard is failing and should be withdrawn
- B) A steady improvement trend was interrupted in month six, plausibly linked to new datasets registered via the new platform; investigate month six's failures by source before concluding anything
- C) The month six figure is a random error and should be excluded
- D) Compliance is improving overall, so no action is needed

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** Months one to five show consistent improvement (42 → 32, roughly 2 to 4 fewer failures per month). Month six breaks the trend at exactly the point a known change occurred — a plausible causal candidate, but only a hypothesis until you segment the month six failures by origin. Option A over-reacts to one data point. Option C assumes error without evidence — you cannot exclude data because it is inconvenient. Option D ignores the reversal. B combines correct pattern reading with the correct epistemic discipline: correlation with a known change justifies targeted investigation, not conclusion. This mirrors how you triage data maturity metrics after any organisational change.

**Question 8 (hard) — Detecting the flawed inference**

A data owner argues: "Every dataset that failed last year's audit lacked a retention schedule. My dataset has a retention schedule. Therefore my dataset will pass this year's audit."

What is the logical flaw?

- A) There is no flaw; the argument is valid
- B) It affirms the consequent: lacking a schedule was common to failures, but having a schedule does not guarantee passing, because other failure causes may exist
- C) It is circular reasoning
- D) It relies on an unstated minor premise about audit frequency

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** Formally: "All failures lacked X" means lacking X may be necessary for failure in last year's sample, but it does not make having X sufficient for passing. Datasets can fail for reasons other than retention schedules — ownership gaps, classification errors, quality issues. The argument treats one shared characteristic of past failures as the sole cause of failure. It is not circular (C) — the conclusion is not assumed in the premises — and audit frequency (D) is irrelevant. Heads of data governance hear this argument shape constantly from data owners seeking assurance sign-off: "we fixed the thing that failed last time, so we're fine." Recognising it instantly is a core professional reflex.

**Question 9 (hard) — Multi-constraint prioritisation**

You have one senior governance analyst available for three days. Four tasks compete:

- Task W: complete the regulator's information request (statutory deadline in 4 days; takes 2 days; only this analyst can do it)
- Task X: fix catalogue errors blocking a major programme (programme escalated to the CDO; takes 1 day; another team member could do it at 80% quality in 2 days)
- Task Y: draft the data ethics framework update (board wants it in 3 weeks; takes 3 days)
- Task Z: quarterly literacy training refresh (due in 2 weeks; takes 1 day; can be delegated fully)

What is the optimal allocation of the analyst's three days?

- A) W (2 days) + X (1 day); delegate Z; defer Y
- B) Y (3 days); delegate W, X and Z
- C) W (2 days) + Z (1 day); delegate X; defer Y
- D) X (1 day) + Y's first 2 days; delegate Z; defer W

**Correct answer: A.**

**Explanation:** Start with hard constraints. W is statutory, imminent, and cannot be delegated — it must take 2 of the 3 days; this eliminates B and D immediately. That leaves 1 day for X or Z. X is escalated and time-critical to a major programme; delegation costs quality (80%) and doubles duration (2 days) on an escalated issue. Z, by contrast, delegates fully with no stated quality loss and has a comfortable 2-week deadline. So the analyst's remaining day goes to X, and Z is delegated — Option A, not C. Y's 3-week horizon makes deferral safe. The method — fix non-negotiables first, then allocate the remainder by delegation cost — is exactly how you protect scarce specialist capacity as a point of escalation.

**Question 10 (hard) — Framework gap analysis**

Your end-to-end data governance framework claims coverage of the full data life cycle: creation, storage, use, sharing, archival, destruction. The control inventory shows:

- Creation: classification-at-source control, ownership-at-registration control
- Storage: encryption standard, access control standard
- Use: purpose limitation policy, quality monitoring
- Sharing: data sharing agreement workflow
- Archival: retention schedule standard
- Destruction: (no controls listed)

An incident occurs: a decommissioned system's database, containing personal data, was found intact on a resold server. Separately, an internal report notes that datasets shared under agreements are never re-reviewed after signature. Which two framework gaps do these facts evidence?

- A) Missing destruction controls; missing ongoing assurance of sharing agreements
- B) Weak encryption; weak classification at source
- C) Missing archival controls; weak access control
- D) Missing creation controls; missing use controls

**Correct answer: A.**

**Explanation:** Map each fact to the life cycle stage it implicates. The resold-server incident is a destruction failure: data survived decommissioning, and the inventory shows destruction has no controls at all — the incident is the predictable consequence of the visible gap. The never-re-reviewed agreements show that the sharing stage has an entry control (the agreement workflow) but no ongoing compliance or assurance control — a one-time gate rather than life-cycle governance. Options B, C and D name stages where controls exist and where no evidence of failure is presented. The skill tested is precise mapping of incidents to control gaps — the heart of directing continuous improvement across the data life cycle.

**Question 11 (hard) — Conditional reasoning across policies**

Three rules apply to a proposed cross-government data share:

1. If the data includes special category data, ethics review is mandatory.
2. If ethics review is mandatory and the receiving body is outside central government, the CDO must personally approve.
3. If the CDO must personally approve, the share cannot proceed under the fast-track route.

The proposed share includes health data (special category) with a local authority (outside central government), and the programme team has submitted it via the fast-track route. Which conclusion follows necessarily?

- A) The share is prohibited entirely
- B) The share may proceed via fast-track if the ethics review passes
- C) The fast-track submission is invalid; the share requires ethics review and personal CDO approval via the standard route
- D) The local authority must conduct the ethics review

**Correct answer: C.**

**Explanation:** Chain the conditionals. Special category data → ethics review mandatory (Rule 1). Ethics review mandatory AND receiver outside central government → CDO personal approval (Rule 2). CDO personal approval → no fast-track (Rule 3). So fast-track is excluded by necessity, but nothing in the rules prohibits the share itself — it simply must travel the standard route with ethics review and CDO approval. A overstates (prohibition was never derived); B contradicts Rule 3's conclusion; D invents an obligation found in no rule. Multi-step conditional chaining without adding or dropping conclusions is precisely the reasoning you apply when advising programmes on which governance route applies.

**Question 12 (hard) — Odd one out: governance metadata**

Four catalogue entries each record: classification, owner status, life cycle stage, review cadence.

- Entry 1: OFFICIAL, owned, in use, annual review
- Entry 2: OFFICIAL-SENSITIVE, owned, in use, quarterly review
- Entry 3: OFFICIAL-SENSITIVE, unowned, archived, quarterly review
- Entry 4: OFFICIAL, owned, archived, annual review

Your standards require: OFFICIAL-SENSITIVE data is reviewed quarterly; archived data may be reviewed annually regardless of classification; all datasets must be owned. Which entry contains an internal contradiction rather than a simple standards breach, and which contains the standards breach?

- A) Entry 3 breaches the ownership standard; no entry is internally contradictory
- B) Entry 2 is contradictory; Entry 4 breaches standards
- C) Entry 3 is both contradictory and a breach
- D) Entry 4 breaches the review cadence standard

**Correct answer: A.**

**Explanation:** Test each entry. Entry 1: consistent and compliant. Entry 2: OFFICIAL-SENSITIVE with quarterly review — compliant. Entry 3: unowned breaches "all datasets must be owned"; its quarterly review is permitted (archived data may drop to annual, but quarterly is not forbidden — "may" grants an option, not an obligation). Entry 4: archived OFFICIAL data on annual review — explicitly permitted. So the only defect anywhere is Entry 3's ownership breach, and nothing is self-contradictory: A. The traps are reading "may be reviewed annually" as "must", and assuming an archived-but-quarterly combination is inconsistent. Distinguishing permissive from mandatory language is a daily necessity when you set and monitor standards.

**Question 13 (moderate) — Root cause isolation**

Data quality incidents rose sharply in one directorate. You establish: (a) the rise began the week a legacy system was migrated; (b) directorates using the same new system without a migration show no rise; (c) the migrated data was not validated against the catalogue's quality rules before load. What is the most logically supported root cause hypothesis?

- A) The new system is defective
- B) The directorate's staff lack data literacy
- C) The migration loaded unvalidated legacy data, introducing quality defects
- D) The catalogue's quality rules are too strict

**Correct answer: C.**

**Explanation:** Fact (b) is the control group: same system, no migration, no rise — this eliminates A (a defective system would affect all users). Nothing in the evidence mentions staff behaviour, eliminating B. Fact (a) establishes temporal coincidence with the migration, and fact (c) supplies the mechanism: unvalidated legacy data. D fails because the rules did not change and other directorates pass them. C is the only hypothesis consistent with all three facts. This is the method of difference — the comparative reasoning you apply whenever you co-ordinate with data owners to isolate and resolve organisational data issues.

### Preparation tips

- **Practise with your own artefacts.** Take a real (suitably anonymised) RACI matrix, policy hierarchy or risk register and interrogate it: what does each rule strictly imply? Where are the contradictions? You will be rehearsing the exact cognition the test samples.
- **Slow down on the logic, not the reading.** At your level the questions are rarely hard to read but often hard to reason about precisely. Budget your time so you can check each condition individually rather than pattern-matching to what "usually" happens.
- **Watch for permissive versus mandatory language.** "May", "must", "should" and "unless" carry the whole logical load in governance-flavoured questions, just as they do in real standards.
- **Do a timed rehearsal.** Simulate the 15 to 30 minute window with 10 to 15 questions. Senior professionals are often out of practice at timed conditions, not at reasoning.
- **Review errors by type.** After practice, classify your mistakes: misread rule, dropped condition, added assumption, arithmetic slip. Fixing the category fixes many future questions at once.
- **Rest and set-up matter.** Take the live test fresh, in a quiet space, with the interface practice questions completed unhurriedly.

### Common pitfalls

- **Answering from experience instead of from the stimulus.** You know how governance "should" work; the test asks what these rules imply. Seasoned leaders lose more marks to imported knowledge than to reasoning failures.
- **Treating one shared feature as the cause.** As in Question 8, resist inferring sufficiency from a common characteristic.
- **Changing compliant elements.** When asked for the minimal fix, alter only what breaches the rule (Question 1's trap).
- **Time-sink questions.** One multi-constraint prioritisation item can absorb five minutes. Flag it, move on, return — the marks are equal across questions.
- **Ignoring adaptive difficulty.** If questions get harder, that usually signals success; do not let perceived difficulty rattle your confidence mid-test.
- **Skipping the ungraded practice items.** They exist to eliminate interface surprises; rushing them wastes a free advantage.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment evaluates your ability to analyse, interpret and make sound decisions using numerical data drawn from the real data-handling demands of your role. It focuses on applied business mathematics — percentages, ratios, rates of change, budget arithmetic and cost-benefit comparison — rather than abstract formulae. Inputs arrive as tables, dashboards, budget lines and metric summaries, exactly the shapes in which data governance evidence reaches you.

Typical format: online, strictly timed (commonly around one minute per question, 15 to 25 questions), with an onscreen calculator usually permitted or provided. The emphasis is on interpretation and accurate extraction under time pressure, not mental arithmetic heroics. Many questions can be answered faster with sensible estimation than with full calculation.

Why it matters for your role: as Head of Data Governance you are accountable for understanding and managing organisational data risk, overseeing the data maturity model, reporting assessment findings to boards, and directing continuous improvement — every one of which arrives wrapped in numbers. Compliance percentages, risk exposure estimates, maturity scores, training completion rates, remediation budgets: your credibility with the chief data officer, with boards, and with senior stakeholders across government depends on reading these figures accurately, spotting when they mislead, and converting them into defensible recommendations. A numeric reasoning assessment for this role simulates precisely that: extracting the true signal from governance metrics quickly and without error.

### What it measures for your role

- **Interpreting compliance and assurance metrics** maps to your **Data governance** and **Data standards** skills: monitoring adherence to policies, frameworks and standards means reading percentage compliance, trend deltas and exception counts correctly.
- **Risk quantification** maps to your **Enabling and informing risk-based decisions** skill: applying risk methodologies at the most complex levels includes expected-loss arithmetic, likelihood-impact products and comparing mitigation costs against reduced exposure.
- **Maturity and improvement measurement** maps to your **Data maturity** skill: overseeing the maturity model, reporting findings to boards and communicating improvement plans require you to handle weighted scores, baselines and target trajectories.
- **Budget and resource arithmetic** maps to your **Strategic thinking** and **Data management** skills: leading strategy implementation and defining tools and training requirements means allocating budgets, comparing unit costs and evidencing value.
- **Training and adoption statistics** map to your **Data literacy improvement** skill: persuading senior decision-makers that literacy investment pays requires completion rates, cohort comparisons and cost-per-learner figures presented accurately.
- **Presenting numbers persuasively and honestly** maps to your **Communicating analysis and insight** skill: compelling stories at strategic level rest on arithmetic that survives scrutiny.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Compliance percentage**

Your catalogue holds 640 datasets. 512 meet the ownership standard. What is the compliance rate, and how many additional datasets must comply to reach a 90% target?

- A) 80%; 64 more datasets
- B) 80%; 76 more datasets
- C) 78%; 64 more datasets
- D) 82%; 52 more datasets

**Correct answer: A.**

**Explanation:** Compliance: 512 ÷ 640 = 0.80 = 80%. Target: 90% of 640 = 576 datasets. Additional needed: 576 − 512 = 64. Option B's 76 comes from mistakenly taking 90% of the non-compliant figure; C and D miscalculate the base rate. Always compute the target headcount from the whole population, then subtract current compliance — the pattern behind every "distance to target" figure you put in a board paper.

**Question 2 (easy) — Percentage change in incidents**

Data quality incidents fell from 125 in Q1 to 95 in Q3. What is the percentage decrease?

- A) 30%
- B) 24%
- C) 31.6%
- D) 20%

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** Decrease = 125 − 95 = 30. Percentage decrease = 30 ÷ 125 = 0.24 = 24%. The classic error is dividing by the new value (30 ÷ 95 = 31.6%, Option C) — percentage change is always measured against the starting value. When you report incident reductions to a board, this distinction is the difference between an accurate claim and an inflated one.

**Question 3 (moderate) — Weighted data maturity score**

Your data maturity model has four dimensions with weights: Governance 40%, Quality 25%, Literacy 20%, Architecture 15%. Current scores (out of 5): Governance 3.5, Quality 2.8, Literacy 2.0, Architecture 4.0. What is the weighted overall maturity score?

- A) 3.08
- B) 3.10
- C) 2.98
- D) 3.20

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** Multiply each score by its weight and sum: Governance 3.5 × 0.40 = 1.40; Quality 2.8 × 0.25 = 0.70; Literacy 2.0 × 0.20 = 0.40; Architecture 4.0 × 0.15 = 0.60. Total: 1.40 + 0.70 + 0.40 + 0.60 = 3.10. Option A is the unweighted mean (3.575 rounded wrongly) territory — actually the simple mean is 3.075, which rounds to 3.08, the precise trap in Option A. Boards see weighted scores; knowing whether a movement in one dimension can shift the headline number (a 0.5 rise in Literacy adds only 0.10) is essential when you communicate improvement plans.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Risk exposure arithmetic**

Your risk methodology values a risk as likelihood × impact. Risk R has a 20% annual likelihood of a data breach with an estimated impact of £2,400,000. A proposed control costs £180,000 per year and reduces likelihood to 8%. What is the annual expected exposure before and after, and is the control cost-justified on expected value?

- A) £480,000 before; £192,000 after; yes — exposure falls by £288,000, exceeding the £180,000 cost
- B) £480,000 before; £192,000 after; no — the control costs more than the residual exposure
- C) £2,400,000 before; £192,000 after; yes
- D) £480,000 before; £288,000 after; no

**Correct answer: A.**

**Explanation:** Before: 0.20 × £2,400,000 = £480,000. After: 0.08 × £2,400,000 = £192,000. Reduction: £480,000 − £192,000 = £288,000 per year. Control cost £180,000 < £288,000 benefit, so on expected value the control pays for itself with £108,000 of net annual benefit. Option B misapplies the test (the comparison is cost versus reduction, not cost versus residual). Option C confuses impact with exposure. This is the exact calculation you rehearse when advising senior risk owners whether to accept or treat a data risk.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Training completion table**

Data literacy training completion by directorate:

| Directorate | Staff | Completed |
|---|---|---|
| Operations | 1,200 | 780 |
| Policy | 400 | 340 |
| Digital | 600 | 480 |
| Finance | 300 | 195 |

Which directorate has the lowest completion rate, and what is the organisation-wide rate?

- A) Operations lowest (65%); overall 71.8%
- B) Finance lowest (65%); overall 71.8%
- C) Operations and Finance tied lowest (65%); overall 71.8%
- D) Operations lowest (65%); overall 72.5%

**Correct answer: C.**

**Explanation:** Rates: Operations 780/1,200 = 65%; Policy 340/400 = 85%; Digital 480/600 = 80%; Finance 195/300 = 65%. Operations and Finance tie at 65%. Overall: completions 780 + 340 + 480 + 195 = 1,795; staff 1,200 + 400 + 600 + 300 = 2,500; 1,795 ÷ 2,500 = 71.8%. Note that the overall rate is not the average of the four rates ((65 + 85 + 80 + 65) ÷ 4 = 73.75%) because directorates differ in size — the weighted reality sits below the naive mean, pulled down by large Operations. Distinguishing pooled rates from averaged rates protects you from a very common dashboard error when you report literacy progress.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Budget allocation**

Your annual data governance budget is £1,500,000, allocated: 40% staff, 25% tooling, 20% training, 15% assurance. Next year the total budget rises by 8%, but tooling costs are contracted to rise by 20%. If tooling absorbs its full increase and staff spending is protected at this year's cash value, how much remains for training and assurance combined?

- A) £529,500
- B) £570,000
- C) £495,000
- D) £525,000

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** This year's lines: staff £600,000; tooling £375,000; training £300,000; assurance £225,000. Next year's total: £1,500,000 × 1.08 = £1,620,000. Tooling with its contracted rise: £375,000 × 1.20 = £450,000. Staff protected at cash value: £600,000. Residual for training and assurance: £1,620,000 − £450,000 − £600,000 = £570,000. If you selected A or D you probably applied the 8% uplift to individual lines rather than the total, or inflated staff costs that the question explicitly froze. In budget questions, always establish the new total first, then apply the line-level constraints exactly as stated, then compute the residual. Notice also the strategic reading: training and assurance combined currently receive £525,000, so despite an 8% richer budget they gain only £45,000 — the tooling contract is quietly consuming the uplift. Spotting that kind of squeeze is what turns arithmetic into strategic advice.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Ratio of stewards to datasets**

Best practice in your framework targets one data steward per 40 critical datasets. You have 9 stewards and 460 critical datasets, with 60 more critical datasets expected after a machine-learning programme onboards next quarter. How many additional stewards must you recruit to meet the target after onboarding?

- A) 3
- B) 4
- C) 2
- D) 5

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** Future critical datasets: 460 + 60 = 520. Required stewards: 520 ÷ 40 = 13. Current: 9. Additional: 13 − 9 = 4. A common slip is computing on the current 460 (460 ÷ 40 = 11.5, round up to 12, giving 3 more — Option A) and forgetting the pipeline. Capacity questions in your role are almost always forward-looking: govern for the estate you are about to have, not the one you have today.

**Question 8 (hard) — Interpreting a compliance dashboard with scale qualifiers**

A dashboard shows records processed per month (in thousands): April 820, May 940, June 1,150. Records failing validation (actual counts): April 12,300, May 14,100, June 16,100. Is the validation failure *rate* rising or falling?

- A) Rising — failures grew from 12,300 to 16,100
- B) Falling — the rate went from 1.50% to 1.40%
- C) Flat at 1.5% throughout
- D) Cannot be determined from the data

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** The volumes are in thousands; the failures are actual counts — the scale qualifier is the whole question. April: 12,300 ÷ 820,000 = 1.50%. May: 14,100 ÷ 940,000 = 1.50%. June: 16,100 ÷ 1,150,000 = 1.40%. Absolute failures rose (Option A's bait) while the rate held steady then improved. Option B is correct: the trend in the rate is downward by June. Mixing units in a single dashboard is one of the most common ways governance reporting misleads boards; catching the "in thousands" label under time pressure is a genuinely job-relevant reflex, and the difference between reporting "failures up 31%" and "failure rate improving" is reputationally enormous.

**Question 9 (hard) — Cost-benefit of a metadata remediation programme**

A remediation programme costs £240,000 and will bring 1,600 datasets to metadata compliance. Analysts currently spend an average of 25 minutes per data request locating context that complete metadata would provide instantly; the organisation handles 36,000 such requests per year, and 40% touch the datasets in scope. Analyst time is costed at £45 per hour. What is the approximate annual saving, and the simple payback period?

- A) £270,000 per year; about 11 months
- B) £675,000 per year; about 4 months
- C) £270,000 per year; about 14 months
- D) £108,000 per year; about 27 months

**Correct answer: A.**

**Explanation:** Requests affected: 36,000 × 0.40 = 14,400 per year. Time saved per request: 25 minutes = 25/60 hours. Hours saved: 14,400 × 25/60 = 6,000 hours. Value: 6,000 × £45 = £270,000 per year. Payback: £240,000 ÷ £270,000 ≈ 0.89 years ≈ 10.7 months. Option B forgets the 40% scoping; Option D applies the 40% twice. Multi-stage calculations like this reward writing the chain down: population → affected fraction → unit saving → annual value → payback. This is the arithmetic backbone of every "invest in governance" business case you will put to the CDO or a spending review.

**Question 10 (hard) — Comparing risk treatment options**

Three treatments for a data risk with current expected annual loss of £900,000:

- Option 1: cost £150,000/year; reduces expected loss by 55%
- Option 2: cost £300,000/year; reduces expected loss by 80%
- Option 3: cost £60,000/year; reduces expected loss by 30%

Which option gives the greatest *net* annual benefit, and which gives the best benefit-to-cost *ratio*?

- A) Net: Option 2; Ratio: Option 3
- B) Net: Option 2; Ratio: Option 2
- C) Net: Option 1; Ratio: Option 3
- D) Net: Option 3; Ratio: Option 1

**Correct answer: A.**

**Explanation:** Benefits: Option 1: £900,000 × 0.55 = £495,000; net £495,000 − £150,000 = £345,000; ratio 3.3. Option 2: £900,000 × 0.80 = £720,000; net £720,000 − £300,000 = £420,000; ratio 2.4. Option 3: £900,000 × 0.30 = £270,000; net £270,000 − £60,000 = £210,000; ratio 4.5. Greatest net benefit: Option 2 (£420,000). Best ratio: Option 3 (4.5). The two criteria genuinely diverge — a scarce-budget context favours the ratio, an absolute-risk-reduction mandate favours net benefit. Being able to say to a board "the answer depends on whether we are optimising limited funds or minimising residual exposure, and here is the arithmetic" is exactly the expert escalation-point judgement your framework demands.

**Question 11 (hard) — Trend projection with a changing base**

Catalogue coverage grew: month 1: 2,000 datasets catalogued; months 2 to 4 each grew coverage by 10% on the previous month. The total estate is estimated at 3,200 datasets and static. What percentage of the estate is catalogued by month 4, and roughly how many more whole months of 10% growth are needed to exceed 95% coverage?

- A) 83.2%; 2 more months
- B) 83.2%; 1 more month
- C) 80.0%; 2 more months
- D) 75.6%; 3 more months

**Correct answer: A.**

**Explanation:** Month 4 coverage: 2,000 × 1.1³ = 2,000 × 1.331 = 2,662 datasets. Percentage: 2,662 ÷ 3,200 = 83.2%. Target: 95% of 3,200 = 3,040. Month 5: 2,662 × 1.1 = 2,928 (91.5% — not enough). Month 6: 2,928 × 1.1 = 3,221, but capped at the 3,200 estate — exceeds 3,040, so coverage passes 95% in month 6: two more months. Option B under-projects by assuming one compounding step suffices. Compound growth is deceptive near a ceiling; when you communicate maturity trajectories, showing the month-by-month compounding (and the cap) prevents overpromising to boards.

**Question 12 (hard) — Data sharing agreement review backlog**

You hold 84 active data sharing agreements. Policy requires each to be reviewed every 24 months. Reviews take 1.5 analyst-days each; you have 0.5 FTE (a full-time analyst works 220 days/year) assigned. Is the assigned capacity sufficient for steady-state compliance, and what is the shortfall or surplus in analyst-days per year?

- A) Insufficient; shortfall of 8 days/year
- B) Sufficient; surplus of 47 days/year
- C) Insufficient; shortfall of 16 days/year
- D) Sufficient; surplus of 4 days/year

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** Steady-state demand: 84 agreements every 24 months = 42 reviews per year. Effort: 42 × 1.5 = 63 analyst-days per year. Capacity: 0.5 × 220 = 110 analyst-days per year. Surplus: 110 − 63 = 47 days. Option B. The distractors come from halving the wrong quantity (treating all 84 as annual gives 126 days demand, a 16-day shortfall — Option C's logic). Converting a review cadence into an annualised workload, then netting against FTE capacity, is the core sum behind every assurance resourcing decision you make — and behind spotting when a policy cadence is unaffordable before you set it.

**Question 13 (moderate) — Literacy programme cost per learner**

Two literacy course options: Option A costs £96,000 flat for up to 800 learners; Option B costs £150 per learner. This year you expect 550 learners; next year 900 (Option A would require a second £96,000 licence beyond 800 learners). Which option is cheaper each year?

- A) Option A is cheaper both years
- B) Option B is cheaper both years
- C) Option B is cheaper this year; Option A is cheaper next year
- D) Option A is cheaper this year; Option B is cheaper next year

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** This year: Option A £96,000; Option B 550 × £150 = £82,500 — B is cheaper by £13,500. Next year: Option B 900 × £150 = £135,000; Option A needs two licences because 900 exceeds the 800-learner cap: 2 × £96,000 = £192,000 — B is cheaper again, by £57,000. Option C is the tempting error: at 900 learners a single flat fee of £96,000 would indeed beat £135,000, but the licence cap forbids it. The substantive lesson: flat-fee versus per-unit pricing flips at a break-even volume (£96,000 ÷ £150 = 640 learners per licence block), and threshold effects like the 800-learner cap can prevent the flip you expected. Always find the break-even point and check the thresholds before recommending a tooling or training contract.

**Question 14 (easy) — Reading a maturity movement**

Your organisation's governance maturity score moved from 2.4 to 3.0 over a year against a target of 3.2. What percentage of the planned improvement was achieved?

- A) 75%
- B) 60%
- C) 80%
- D) 94%

**Correct answer: A.**

**Explanation:** Planned improvement: 3.2 − 2.4 = 0.8. Achieved: 3.0 − 2.4 = 0.6. Proportion: 0.6 ÷ 0.8 = 75%. Option D (3.0 ÷ 3.2) measures level attained, not improvement achieved — a subtly different claim. When you report maturity progress to a committee, be precise about which of the two you are asserting; auditors notice.

**Question 15 (hard) — Subject access request performance**

Your organisation must answer subject access requests within one calendar month. Last quarter's figures: 1,800 requests received; 1,539 answered in time; 171 answered late; the remainder still open but within deadline at quarter end. New tooling is forecast to raise the in-time proportion of *closed* requests by 5 percentage points. If next quarter sees 2,000 requests and the same proportion remain open at quarter end as last quarter, how many requests would be answered late under the forecast?

- A) 100
- B) 190
- C) 95
- D) 105

**Correct answer: C.**

**Explanation:** First establish last quarter's structure. Open at quarter end: 1,800 − 1,539 − 171 = 90, which is 5% of received requests. Closed requests: 1,539 + 171 = 1,710. In-time proportion of closed: 1,539 ÷ 1,710 = 90%, so 10% of closed requests were late. Forecast: in-time share of closed rises 5 points to 95%, leaving 5% late. Next quarter: 2,000 received; 5% (100) still open at quarter end; closed = 1,900. Late: 1,900 × 0.05 = 95 — Option C. Option A applies the 5% late share to all 2,000 requests, ignoring that the improvement is defined on closed requests; Option B applies the old 10% late share to the wrong base; Option D mixes the bases. The real-world lesson is that percentage-point improvements are only meaningful relative to their stated denominator — "closed", "received" and "in scope" are different populations, and regulators will hold you to whichever one your report actually claims.

**Question 16 (moderate) — Horizon-scanning adoption figures**

A benchmarking survey you commissioned reports that 18 of 45 comparable public bodies (40%) have adopted automated data lineage tooling, up from 12 of 40 (30%) two years ago. Your CDO asks: "If adoption keeps growing by the same number of percentage points per two-year cycle, what share of bodies will have adopted it in four years, and when does adoption pass half?"

- A) 60% in four years; adoption passes half during the next two-year cycle
- B) 50% in four years; adoption passes half exactly in four years
- C) 60% in four years; adoption passes half in the cycle after next
- D) 70% in four years; adoption has already passed half

**Correct answer: A.**

**Explanation:** Growth per cycle: 40% − 30% = 10 percentage points. In two years: 40% + 10 = 50%; in four years: 50% + 10 = 60%. Passing half: adoption reaches exactly 50% at the two-year mark, so it exceeds 50% during the following period — but at the level of whole cycles, the honest phrasing is that it reaches half in the next cycle and passes it thereafter; Option A captures the four-year figure (60%) and locates the crossing in the next cycle, which is the intended reading. Note the professional caveats you would attach in real life: the survey base changed (40 versus 45 bodies), linear percentage-point extrapolation is an assumption rather than a law, and two data points make a fragile trend. Horizon-scanning arithmetic is easy; the senior skill is presenting it with its uncertainty attached, so the CDO hears a scenario rather than a promise.

### Preparation tips

- **Rehearse the role's core sums until they are automatic**: percentage of a population, percentage change against the original value, weighted averages, expected loss (likelihood × impact), cost-benefit netting, cadence-to-annual-workload conversion, and break-even volumes. Perhaps 80% of role-relevant numeric questions are one of these seven in costume.
- **Write the chain, not the answer.** For multi-stage questions, jot the sequence (population → fraction → unit value → total) before touching the calculator. It prevents double-applying a percentage — the most common senior-level slip.
- **Use estimation to eliminate options first.** In Question 9, a rough 14,000 × 0.4 hours × £45 ≈ £250,000-ish kills two options instantly. Estimation is a legitimate senior skill, not a shortcut.
- **Check units and qualifiers before calculating.** "In thousands", "per quarter", "per 0.5 FTE" — read the table header twice; it is where the test hides its teeth.
- **Practise with your own dashboards.** Take last quarter's compliance or maturity pack and re-derive every headline figure yourself. Any number you cannot reproduce is both practice material and a live assurance question.
- **Manage the clock deliberately.** At about a minute per question, a two-minute item must earn its time. Answer the six easy questions first if navigation allows.

### Common pitfalls

- **Misreading chart and table labels** — confusing monthly with annual figures, or ignoring scale qualifiers like "in thousands", as Question 8 punished.
- **Dividing by the wrong base** — percentage change uses the original value; pooled rates are not averages of subgroup rates (Question 5).
- **Over-calculating** — computing exact figures when estimation would eliminate three options faster; the test rewards accurate sufficiency, not precision theatre.
- **Time-sink questions** — one hard compound-growth item can cost you three easy marks; flag and return.
- **Importing outside knowledge** — use only the numbers given, even where you know your organisation's real figures differ.
- **Ignoring stated constraints** — protected budget lines, licence caps and FTE fractions change answers; the question text is a specification, and partial reading produces confident wrong answers.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment evaluates how well you understand, analyse and draw correct conclusions from written business information, mirroring the exact reading demands of your role. Passages use the terminology and document types you handle daily — data policies, standards, regulatory guidance, board papers, cross-government correspondence and assurance reports — and the questions test whether you can determine precisely what the text does and does not establish.

Typical format: online and time-pressured, usually less than a minute per question. The dominant format presents a dense passage followed by statements you must judge **True** (the passage establishes it), **False** (the passage contradicts it) or **Cannot Say** (the passage provides insufficient information either way), supplemented by multiple-choice comprehension items. The cardinal rule: answer strictly from the text provided, ignoring your outside knowledge — even where you know the real-world position.

Why it matters for your role: a Head of Data Governance lives or dies by textual precision. You set and monitor adherence to policies, frameworks and standards — documents whose force depends on exact wording. You oversee compliance with data ethics standards and legislation, where the difference between "must", "should" and "may" is the difference between a breach and a choice. You mediate between technical and non-technical audiences, which requires you to notice when a summary quietly changes what a source document says. And you act as a senior advisor across government, where misquoting a standard in front of another department's board is a credibility event. Verbal reasoning assessment simulates all of this: fast, exact reading with zero tolerance for imported assumptions.

### What it measures for your role

- **Precise extraction of obligations** maps to your **Data governance** and **Data standards** skills: setting the strategy for frameworks, processes, operational policies, standards, compliance and assurance requires reading normative text exactly — who must do what, when, subject to which exceptions.
- **Distinguishing established fact from inference** maps to your **Data ethics and privacy** skill: overseeing compliance with ethics standards and legislation across the organisation means never asserting more than the instrument states.
- **Detecting contradiction and consistency** maps to your **Data life cycle** and **Data management** skills: policies, retention schedules and process documents must agree with each other, and you are the person who notices when they do not.
- **Comprehending and re-expressing complex argument** maps to your **Communicating analysis and insight** and **Communicating between the technical and non-technical** skills: making complex recommendations at strategic level, and mediating high-risk discussions, depend on accurately grasping the argument in front of you before you translate it.
- **Reading stakeholder correspondence for position and commitment** maps to your **Stakeholder relationship management** skill: influencing and negotiating with senior stakeholders starts with reading exactly what they have and have not agreed to.

### Practice questions

**Passage A — Data retention policy extract**

*"All datasets containing personal data must be assigned a retention schedule within 60 days of first registration in the data catalogue. Retention schedules for datasets supporting statutory functions may only be varied with the written approval of the relevant data owner and the Head of Data Governance. Where a dataset supports more than one statutory function, the longest applicable retention period applies. Datasets that no longer serve a business purpose should be reviewed for disposal at the next quarterly assurance cycle; however, no dataset subject to a live litigation hold may be disposed of under any circumstances."*

**Question 1 (easy) — True / False / Cannot Say**

Statement: "A dataset containing personal data registered 90 days ago without a retention schedule breaches the policy."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A — True.**

**Explanation:** The passage says such datasets "must be assigned a retention schedule within 60 days of first registration". Ninety days without one is a direct breach of an unambiguous obligation ("must", not "should"). No exception in the passage rescues it. This is the baseline verbal skill: matching a fact pattern against a clear normative sentence.

**Question 2 (easy) — True / False / Cannot Say**

Statement: "The Head of Data Governance can unilaterally vary the retention schedule of a dataset supporting a statutory function."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B — False.**

**Explanation:** The passage requires "the written approval of the relevant data owner *and* the Head of Data Governance" — a joint condition. "Unilaterally" contradicts the conjunction: your approval is necessary but not sufficient. Reading "and" as "or" is one of the smallest and most expensive errors in governance documents, and test writers exploit it constantly.

**Question 3 (moderate) — True / False / Cannot Say**

Statement: "A dataset with no business purpose that is subject to a live litigation hold will be disposed of at the next quarterly assurance cycle."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B — False.**

**Explanation:** Two clauses interact. The disposal-review clause is only a "should... be reviewed" — and even that is trumped by the absolute prohibition: "no dataset subject to a live litigation hold may be disposed of under any circumstances." The statement asserts disposal *will* happen; the passage's explicit exception contradicts it. Note the double precision required: "reviewed for disposal" is not "disposed of", and the litigation-hold clause overrides everything. Layered exceptions are exactly how real retention policies work.

**Question 4 (moderate) — True / False / Cannot Say**

Statement: "Most datasets in the catalogue support statutory functions."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C — Cannot Say.**

**Explanation:** The passage regulates datasets that support statutory functions but says nothing about how many there are relative to the whole catalogue. You may privately know your own catalogue's composition; the test asks only what this text establishes, and it establishes nothing quantitative. Cannot Say is the correct discipline whenever a statement introduces information — proportions, motives, timelines — that the passage neither asserts nor denies.

**Passage B — Cross-government advisory letter**

*"Dear colleagues, Following the review of data security development practices across participating departments, we recommend that departments align their access control standards with the enterprise data architecture principles published in March. Departments that have already implemented role-based access controls need take no further action at this stage, although they are encouraged to participate in the peer assurance exercise scheduled for the autumn. Participation in the exercise is voluntary. We expect, however, that departments handling special category data at scale will find participation particularly valuable, and the Central Digital and Data Office will ask such departments to explain any decision not to participate."*

**Question 5 (moderate) — True / False / Cannot Say**

Statement: "Departments that have implemented role-based access controls are required to join the autumn peer assurance exercise."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B — False.**

**Explanation:** The letter states such departments "need take no further action at this stage" and are merely "encouraged" to participate, and it says flatly that "participation in the exercise is voluntary". The statement's "required" contradicts three separate textual signals. Encouragement plus an expectation of explanation is political pressure, not obligation — a distinction you navigate weekly as a senior advisor across government, and one verbal tests probe deliberately.

**Question 6 (hard) — Multiple choice: authorial position**

Which statement best captures the letter's stance towards departments handling special category data at scale?

- A) They are exempt from the exercise
- B) They are legally obliged to participate
- C) Participation remains voluntary for them, but non-participation carries an accountability cost: they will be asked to justify it
- D) They must implement role-based access controls before autumn

**Correct answer: C.**

**Explanation:** The letter never removes the voluntary status ("participation... is voluntary" is unqualified), but it attaches a consequence to declining: CDDO "will ask such departments to explain any decision not to participate". That is soft governance — voluntary in form, accountable in practice. Option B overstates (no legal obligation appears); A and D invent provisions. Recognising this "voluntary but answerable" register is essential to reading cross-government instruments correctly, where much coordination happens through expectation rather than mandate.

**Passage C — Data ethics framework consultation response**

*"The majority of respondents supported the proposed ethical review gateway for new data-sharing initiatives, with 62% agreeing that the gateway should apply to all initiatives involving special category data. A significant minority, however, argued that applying the gateway to research uses would duplicate existing academic ethics processes and could delay time-critical public health work. The consultation team therefore proposes that research uses already subject to an approved academic ethics process be exempt from the gateway, provided the data governance team receives a copy of the academic approval before processing begins. This proposal does not extend to operational pilots conducted jointly with academic partners, which remain in scope for the gateway."*

**Question 7 (moderate) — True / False / Cannot Say**

Statement: "Under the proposal, a university research project with approved academic ethics clearance is exempt from the gateway if the approval is copied to the data governance team before processing begins."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A — True.**

**Explanation:** The proposal exempts "research uses already subject to an approved academic ethics process... provided the data governance team receives a copy of the academic approval before processing begins". The statement reproduces both the condition and the timing requirement accurately. Check conditional statements clause by clause: exemption class (research use), qualifying condition (approved academic process), and procedural trigger (copy received before processing). All three match.

**Question 8 (hard) — True / False / Cannot Say**

Statement: "An operational pilot run jointly with a university, where the university holds academic ethics approval, is exempt from the gateway."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B — False.**

**Explanation:** The final sentence is decisive: the exemption "does not extend to operational pilots conducted jointly with academic partners, which remain in scope". The academic approval is a red herring — the passage carves operational pilots out of the exemption regardless. This question rewards reading to the end: the qualifying sentence that reverses the apparent answer is the last one, exactly where skimmers stop. Real policy documents bury their scope exclusions the same way.

**Question 9 (moderate) — True / False / Cannot Say**

Statement: "62% of respondents supported applying the gateway to research uses."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C — Cannot Say.**

**Explanation:** The 62% figure attaches to a different proposition: agreement "that the gateway should apply to all initiatives involving special category data". Support for applying it to *research uses* specifically is never quantified — we know only that "a significant minority" objected. The statement transplants a real number onto a claim the passage never measures. Number-transplanting is among the most common distortions in summaries of consultations, and catching it is precisely the assurance you provide when reviewing papers before they reach a board.

**Passage D — Board paper on data maturity**

*"The annual data maturity assessment scored the organisation at 3.1 against a target of 3.4, an improvement of 0.4 on last year. Governance and quality dimensions improved markedly; the literacy dimension declined slightly, which the assessment attributes primarily to the onboarding of two large delivery directorates whose staff have not yet completed foundation data training. The board is asked to note that, if the literacy dimension is excluded, the organisation would have exceeded its overall target. The assessment team cautions, however, that excluding dimensions retrospectively would undermine the comparability of year-on-year results, and recommends against it. The committee is invited to approve an accelerated literacy programme for the two new directorates, at a cost to be met from the existing transformation reserve."*

**Question 10 (moderate) — True / False / Cannot Say**

Statement: "The organisation missed its overall data maturity target this year."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A — True.**

**Explanation:** Scored 3.1 against a target of 3.4 — the target was missed on the actual, unadjusted measure. The hypothetical ("if the literacy dimension is excluded... would have exceeded") does not change the fact of the miss; it is explicitly counterfactual, and the assessment team recommends against the exclusion. Do not let a flattering hypothetical displace the stated result — a distortion you will meet in real board papers as often as in tests.

**Question 11 (hard) — True / False / Cannot Say**

Statement: "The decline in the literacy dimension was caused by the two new directorates."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C — Cannot Say.**

**Explanation:** The passage says the assessment "attributes" the decline "primarily" to the onboarding of the two directorates. An attribution is a judgement, not an established fact, and "primarily" concedes other contributing factors. The statement asserts causation flatly and exclusively. The passage neither proves that causal claim (so not True) nor contradicts it (so not False). This is the finest of the three distinctions — reported attribution versus established fact — and it matters enormously when you report assessment findings to boards or committees: you must know which of your sentences carry evidence and which carry opinion.

**Question 12 (moderate) — Multiple choice: what is the board actually being asked to decide?**

- A) To exclude the literacy dimension from the maturity score
- B) To approve an accelerated literacy programme funded from the transformation reserve
- C) To increase the maturity target to 3.4
- D) To commission next year's maturity assessment

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** Separate the paper's verbs: the board is asked to *note* the results and the hypothetical, and is *invited to approve* the literacy programme "at a cost to be met from the existing transformation reserve". Approval of the programme is the only decision sought. Option A is explicitly recommended against; C and D appear nowhere. Distinguishing "to note" from "to approve" is bread-and-butter committee craft — papers pass or fail on whether the ask is understood, and so do verbal reasoning questions about them.

**Passage E — Email from a delivery director**

*"Thanks for the session yesterday. My team accept the principle of registering all new datasets in the catalogue, and we will comply for everything created from next month. I must flag, though, that retro-registering our legacy estate — around 300 datasets — is not something we have budget or people for this financial year. If your team can fund a contractor, we could start in Q3. Meanwhile I'd ask that our programme not be flagged 'non-compliant' in the quarterly report, since the policy as written only mandates registration of new datasets, and we are meeting that."*

**Question 13 (moderate) — True / False / Cannot Say**

Statement: "The delivery director has agreed to register the legacy estate in Q3."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B — False.**

**Explanation:** The email offers a conditional: "*If* your team can fund a contractor, we *could* start in Q3." A conditional offer with an unresolved condition and a modal "could" is not an agreement. The statement converts possibility into commitment — precisely the misreading that later produces "but you agreed" disputes with senior stakeholders. In negotiation correspondence, catalogue exactly what is committed, what is conditional and what is merely floated; this question tests whether you do that under time pressure.

**Question 14 (hard) — Multiple choice: evaluating the compliance claim**

The director claims the programme should not be flagged non-compliant because "the policy as written only mandates registration of new datasets". Based solely on the email, which is the most accurate assessment of this claim?

- A) The claim is false — all policies cover legacy data
- B) The claim's validity cannot be verified from the email alone, since the policy text is not quoted; the email only reports the director's characterisation of it
- C) The claim is true — the director states it, and the director is senior
- D) The claim is irrelevant to the quarterly report

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** The email contains the director's *characterisation* of the policy ("as written only mandates registration of new datasets") but not the policy text itself. From this document alone you can verify neither the characterisation nor the compliance position; you would need the policy wording. Option A imports outside assumptions; Option C confuses seniority with accuracy; Option D dodges the substance. The professional reflex being tested — "check the instrument, not the paraphrase" — is the one that protects you every time a stakeholder tells you what your own policy says.

**Question 15 (easy) — True / False / Cannot Say**

Statement (on Passage E): "The delivery director's team will register all newly created datasets from next month."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A — True.**

**Explanation:** Stated directly: "we will comply for everything created from next month". An unconditional, unmodalised commitment in the director's own words. After the conditional traps of Questions 13 and 14, this item checks that you can also recognise a genuine commitment when one is made — over-scepticism is as much an error as over-reading.

**Passage F — Draft data standards bulletin**

*"From 1 April, the reference data standard for organisational identifiers becomes mandatory for all new systems procured or built by the department. Existing systems are expected to adopt the standard at their next major upgrade, and in any case no later than three years from the effective date. Systems scheduled for decommissioning within the three-year window are exempt from adoption, provided the decommissioning date is recorded in the technology roadmap and confirmed by the relevant service owner. Where a system exchanges organisational identifier data with another department, adoption may be accelerated by mutual agreement, and the Head of Data Governance should be notified of any such agreement within 20 working days. Failure to notify does not invalidate the agreement, but repeated failures will be reported to the data governance board."*

**Question 16 (moderate) — True / False / Cannot Say**

Statement: "An existing system with no major upgrade planned must adopt the identifier standard within three years of 1 April."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A — True.**

**Explanation:** The bulletin sets a two-part rule for existing systems: adoption "at their next major upgrade, and in any case no later than three years from the effective date". The "in any case" backstop operates independently of upgrade plans, so a system with no upgrade planned still hits the three-year longstop — unless it qualifies for the decommissioning exemption, which the statement's fact pattern does not raise. When two triggers are joined by "and in any case", the deadline is whichever comes first; reading the backstop as contingent on the upgrade is the designed error.

**Question 17 (hard) — True / False / Cannot Say**

Statement: "A cross-department acceleration agreement that was never notified to the Head of Data Governance is invalid."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B — False.**

**Explanation:** The passage addresses this exactly: "Failure to notify does not invalidate the agreement." The consequence of repeated failures is escalation — reporting to the data governance board — not invalidity. The statement asserts the one outcome the text expressly rules out, so it is False rather than Cannot Say. Notice the drafting pattern, common in real standards: a procedural obligation (notify within 20 working days), an express saving provision (non-compliance does not void the substantive act), and a separate escalation consequence. Disentangling procedural breach from substantive invalidity is a distinction you will use whenever you decide how hard to enforce a standard.

**Question 18 (moderate) — Multiple choice: exemption conditions**

Under the bulletin, which system is validly exempt from adopting the standard?

- A) A system decommissioning in two years, with the date recorded in the technology roadmap and confirmed by the service owner
- B) A system decommissioning in two years, with the date agreed informally at a team meeting
- C) A system decommissioning in four years, recorded and confirmed
- D) Any system the data owner considers low priority

**Correct answer: A.**

**Explanation:** The exemption has three cumulative conditions: decommissioning within the three-year window, the date recorded in the technology roadmap, and confirmation by the relevant service owner. Option A satisfies all three. Option B fails the recording and confirmation conditions; Option C fails the window (four years exceeds three); Option D invents a discretion the text nowhere grants. "Provided that" clauses are conjunctive checklists — every limb must be satisfied, and testing each limb separately is precisely how you will assess exemption requests when this kind of standard is yours to oversee.

### Preparation tips

- **Train the True / False / Cannot Say discipline explicitly.** The three verdicts have precise meanings: True means the passage establishes it; False means the passage contradicts it; Cannot Say means neither. Practise saying, for each statement, *which sentence* of the passage does the establishing or contradicting — if you cannot point to one, the answer is probably Cannot Say.
- **Read normative words as terms of art.** "Must", "should", "may", "and", "or", "unless", "provided that" carry the legal weight in governance text. Underline them mentally; most wrong answers flow from flattening one of them.
- **Read to the last sentence.** Scope exclusions and reversals cluster at the ends of paragraphs (as in Question 8). Budget your reading so the final clause gets full attention.
- **Suppress your expertise deliberately.** You know data protection law and government policy better than the passage does. The test — like an audit — cares only what the document in front of you supports. Practise answering as if you knew nothing beyond the text.
- **Use your own document diet as training material.** Take a policy, a board paper and a stakeholder email from your week, write three statements about each, and classify them True / False / Cannot Say against the text alone. Ten minutes of this daily sharpens live performance quickly.
- **Watch the clock but not anxiously.** At under a minute per question, one strategy works: skim the passage for structure, read the statement, then hunt the relevant sentence and read *it* with full care.

### Common pitfalls

- **Making assumptions** — importing personal or professional knowledge instead of relying only on the provided text; the more senior and knowledgeable you are, the stronger this pull, which is why senior candidates paradoxically drop marks here.
- **Misinterpreting nuance** — confusing a likely outcome, an attribution or a proposal with an established fact (Question 11's trap), or a conditional offer with a commitment (Question 13's).
- **Number transplanting** — attaching a genuine figure to a claim the passage never quantified (Question 9).
- **Treating encouragement as obligation** — "encouraged", "expected" and "invited" are not "required" (Question 5).
- **Stopping reading too early** — missing the final-clause exclusion that reverses the answer.
- **Poor time management** — reading every passage exhaustively before seeing the questions; scan for structure first, then read the load-bearing sentence precisely when a statement directs you to it.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment evaluates your decision-making, interpersonal effectiveness and professional conduct by presenting realistic workplace dilemmas and asking how you would respond. Unlike cognitive or numeric tests there is rarely a single objectively right answer: several responses may seem reasonable, but one aligns best with the values of the organisation and the demands of the role. For your role those values are the UK public sector's: meeting user needs, transparency, collaboration, accessibility, inclusion, and the responsible, ethical use of public data.

Typical format: a series of short scenarios, each followed by four or five possible actions. You are asked either to pick the **most effective** and **least effective** actions, or to rank or rate every option. Timing is usually generous or absent — the test measures natural judgement, not speed.

Why it matters for your role: judgement is the substance of the Head of Data Governance job. You act as the point of escalation for the organisation's hardest data questions; you moderate difficult discussions about high-risk topics within constrained timescales; you maintain long-term relationships with senior decision-makers while sometimes telling them things they do not want to hear; you balance enforcement of standards against the collaborative culture that makes governance sustainable; and you must weigh transparency, ethics and user needs when they pull against delivery pressure. A situational judgement test compresses years of these trade-offs into an hour, and — used developmentally — it is one of the best mirrors available for your leadership instincts.

### What it measures for your role

- **Escalation judgement and risk ownership** map to your **Enabling and informing risk-based decisions** skill: knowing when to escalate, when to hold, and how to be trusted by senior risk owners as the expert voice.
- **Difficult conversations and expectation management** map to your **Communicating between the technical and non-technical** skill: mediating between people, moderating high-risk discussions under time pressure, and representing the data community to large audiences.
- **Influence without authority** maps to your **Stakeholder relationship management** and **Data literacy improvement** skills: directing the relationship strategy, negotiating with senior stakeholders, and persuading decision-makers that data literacy underpins a data-driven culture.
- **Ethical backbone** maps to your **Data ethics and privacy** skill: overseeing compliance with ethics standards, building an ethics culture, and influencing others to act on it even when it is inconvenient.
- **Strategic patience versus decisive intervention** maps to your **Strategic thinking** and **Data governance** skills: choosing when a problem needs a framework fix and when it needs a personal intervention today.
- **Championing improvement** maps to your **Data management** and **Data maturity** skills: leading governance processes across the organisation and keeping improvement plans credible with boards.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (moderate) — The minister's deadline versus the ethics gateway**

A high-profile programme, personally sponsored by a minister, wants to launch a data-matching pilot in ten days. Your ethics review gateway normally takes four weeks. The programme director asks you to waive the review "just this once", noting that the CDO "is relaxed about it". You have not heard this from the CDO directly.

- A) Waive the review; the political cost of delay outweighs process purity, and the CDO is reportedly content
- B) Refuse outright and inform the programme director that launch must move to accommodate the standard four-week review
- C) Contact the CDO directly to verify their position, and in parallel commission an expedited, proportionate ethics review focused on the pilot's highest risks, making clear what a ten-day review can and cannot assure
- D) Escalate immediately to the permanent secretary that the programme is attempting to bypass governance
- E) Agree to the waiver but document your objection in an email for the record

**Most effective: C. Least effective: E.**

**Explanation:** Option C does three senior things at once: it verifies a second-hand claim rather than acting on it (never let "X is relaxed" substitute for X's actual view), it looks for a proportionate path that serves both the delivery need and the ethical obligation, and it is transparent about the residual risk a compressed review leaves. Option B is defensible but rigid — it treats the gateway's timetable as the value, when the value is the ethical assurance the gateway exists to provide. Option A acts on hearsay and sets a precedent that gateways bend to seniority. Option D escalates before verifying facts, spending relationship capital wildly. Option E is the worst: it permits the risk while creating a paper trail that protects only you — governance as self-defence rather than as public protection, and corrosive to the trust your role depends on. The pattern to internalise: verify, then find the proportionate route, and be honest about what assurance you can and cannot give in the time available.

**Question 2 (moderate) — The data owner who will not engage**

A director who owns several critical datasets has ignored three invitations to agree their risk acceptances, and their directorate's catalogue entries are six months out of date. The quarterly assurance report is due in two weeks, and their non-engagement will show as red.

- A) Report the red status factually in the assurance report without further contact
- B) Ask a member of your team to keep chasing the director's office weekly
- C) Request a short one-to-one with the director before the report closes, framing it around the risks to their own services and the practical support your team can offer, while being clear the report will reflect whatever position stands at the deadline
- D) Reassign ownership of the datasets to a more responsive deputy without the director's involvement
- E) Mark the datasets amber instead of red, noting "engagement in progress", to preserve the relationship

**Most effective: C. Least effective: E.**

**Explanation:** Option C combines relationship-building with honest reporting: it approaches the director as an owner of service risk (their language) rather than a governance defaulter (your language), offers help, and preserves the integrity of the report by being transparent that the deadline stands. This is textbook "influence and negotiate with senior stakeholders to resolve issues and enable progress". Option A is honest but abandons the relationship and the chance to fix the underlying problem. Option B delegates upwards pressure that has already failed three times — more of the same. Option D violates your own RACI framework: ownership assignment without the accountable party's involvement undermines the very structure you enforce. Option E is least effective because it falsifies an assurance product to avoid an awkward conversation — the one thing a Head of Data Governance can never do, since every future report inherits the doubt. Transparency in assurance reporting is a core public sector value precisely because it is tested at moments like this.

**Question 3 (hard) — The breach that might not be reportable**

At 16:40 on a Friday, an analyst tells you they may have emailed an unredacted extract of a sensitive dataset to an external mailing list. The extract's exact contents are unclear; the analyst thinks "it was probably the anonymised version". Your incident process requires initial assessment within 24 hours and, if personal data is involved, consideration of regulatory notification within 72 hours of awareness.

- A) Thank the analyst, note that it was probably anonymised, and pick it up on Monday
- B) Trigger the incident process now: secure the evidence, establish exactly what was sent, start the assessment clock, inform the data protection officer, and thank the analyst explicitly for reporting promptly
- C) Ask the analyst to quietly recall the email and confirm on Monday whether it worked
- D) Immediately notify the regulator to be safe
- E) Instruct the analyst's line manager to begin a disciplinary investigation into the error

**Most effective: B. Least effective: E.**

**Explanation:** Option B does everything the moment demands: the 72-hour regulatory window runs from awareness, and you are now aware — "probably anonymised" is a hypothesis, not an assessment. Securing facts first, engaging the DPO, and starting the clock is exactly the disciplined incident response your framework prescribes. Just as important is thanking the analyst: a reporting culture is the single most valuable data-risk asset you have, and how you treat this Friday-afternoon confession will be retold across the organisation. Option A gambles the organisation's regulatory position on an unverified "probably" and burns most of the window. Option C compounds the incident with concealment behaviour. Option D notifies before assessment — regulators expect considered notification based on established facts, and crying wolf erodes credibility. Option E is least effective: leading with blame on an honest self-report teaches the organisation to hide the next incident, which is catastrophic for risk management. Accountability questions can follow later, through fair process, once facts are known.

**Question 4 (moderate) — The literacy programme nobody funds**

Your data literacy programme bid was cut in this year's settlement. The CDO supports literacy in principle but has other priorities. Meanwhile, assurance findings show basic data-handling errors rising in two directorates. How do you advance the literacy agenda?

- A) Accept the decision and shelve the programme until next year's settlement
- B) Build a short evidence-based case linking the rising error findings to concrete service and financial impacts, propose a small targeted pilot in the two affected directorates funded from existing budgets, and use its measured results to bid for full funding
- C) Circulate the assurance findings widely to embarrass the directorates into funding their own training
- D) Ask the CDO to overrule the settlement decision
- E) Redesignate part of your assurance team as trainers to run the full programme anyway

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C.**

**Explanation:** Option B is how literacy actually gets funded: it converts a values argument ("literacy matters") into a decision-maker's argument (errors → service impact → cost), starts small where the evidence is strongest, and creates the measured results that make next year's bid unanswerable. This is your framework skill verbatim: "persuade senior decision-makers that data literacy is a vital part of a data-driven culture" — persuasion through evidence, not assertion. Option A abandons a live and worsening problem for a year. Option D spends CDO capital on a fight the evidence could win by itself, and overruling settlements is rarely within the CDO's gift anyway. Option E quietly de-staffs assurance — trading one control failure for another. Option C is least effective: public embarrassment poisons the long-term senior relationships your role depends on, and directorates shamed into training attend it resentfully. Influence, in the public sector, is a repeated game; never win a quarter at the cost of the relationship.

**Question 5 (hard) — Cross-government advice you disagree with**

You sit on a cross-government working group. Another department's head of security proposes that all departments adopt their access control standard, which you believe is weaker than the enterprise data architecture requires in two specific respects. The proposal has momentum, the chair wants consensus today, and your CDO could not brief you before the meeting.

- A) Go along with consensus to protect the relationship, and raise concerns informally later
- B) State your two specific technical concerns in the meeting, propose that the standard be adopted with those two areas strengthened or explicitly reserved for further analysis, and offer your team's help to close the gap within a fixed timescale
- C) Veto the proposal outright, stating your department will never adopt it
- D) Say nothing in the meeting, then advise your department to quietly ignore the standard
- E) Ask the chair to defer the entire item until your CDO can attend

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D.**

**Explanation:** Option B is what "act as a senior advisor across government on the best practice of data security development" looks like in a live meeting: specific, technical, constructive, and solution-shaped — consensus is preserved on everything except the two named weaknesses, a path and timescale are offered, and your objection is on the record where it can do good. Option A trades public assurance for private honesty — the standard ships weak, and your later concerns look like second-guessing. Option C wins the point and loses the network; vetoes without offered alternatives read as obstruction. Option E defers a whole item over two fixable points, spending the group's patience. Option D is least effective by a distance: public silence plus private defection is the behaviour that destroys cross-government trust permanently — transparency exists precisely so that departments' positions are what they appear to be. Disagree openly, specifically, and helpfully; that is what being "trusted by senior risk owners as an expert" means in practice.

**Question 6 (moderate) — The vendor dashboard that flatters**

A supplier implementing your data catalogue presents a dashboard to the data governance board claiming "94% metadata compliance". Your own team's sampling suggests the true figure is nearer 70%, because the supplier counts a field as complete if it contains any text, including "TBC". You discover this the day before the board meeting.

- A) Let the dashboard be presented, then correct the record at the following board once your team's figures are fully validated
- B) Contact the supplier immediately, explain the counting flaw, and require the board pack to carry either corrected figures or an explicit note on the measurement issue with both numbers shown; brief the chair beforehand
- C) Publicly challenge the supplier's figures at the board without warning them
- D) Present your team's 70% figure as the official number and drop the supplier's slide
- E) Cancel the supplier's slot at the board

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A.**

**Explanation:** Option B protects the one thing a governance board cannot function without: accurate information — while treating the supplier fairly (they may have made an honest definitional error) and preparing the chair so the board can handle the correction in an orderly way. Showing both figures with the measurement explanation is transparency done properly, and it turns the episode into a lesson about definitions rather than a scandal about spin. Option C achieves accuracy through ambush — theatrical, and it teaches suppliers to hide problems from you. Option D replaces one unvalidated number with another and conceals the discrepancy. Option E hides the issue instead of resolving it. Option A is least effective: knowingly letting a misleading figure inform a board decision — even temporarily — is a direct failure of your assurance accountability; boards make resource decisions on those numbers, and "we knew but corrected it next quarter" is indefensible. The deeper lesson: metadata compliance metrics are themselves data, and you are their governor too.

**Question 7 (hard) — Innovation versus governance**

A talented analytics team has built, without telling your team, a prototype that combines three datasets in ways the current sharing agreements do not cover. It demonstrably improves service outcomes for vulnerable users. The team lead says governance "would have taken months and users needed this now", and the prototype is already informing frontline decisions.

- A) Order the prototype switched off today and require the team to restart through the proper gateway
- B) Praise the outcome and retrospectively paper over the agreements
- C) Contain first, then fix and learn: assess with the DPO whether live use must pause given the uncovered data sharing, bring the prototype into an expedited compliance review, and separately commission work on a faster "innovation pathway" so that governance is no longer the reason teams route around it
- D) Report the team lead for a deliberate governance breach
- E) Allow the prototype to continue while a review proceeds at normal pace, since outcomes for vulnerable users take priority

**Most effective: C. Least effective: B.**

**Explanation:** This is the hardest genuine dilemma in data governance leadership: the breach is real, and so is the user benefit. Option C holds both truths. The immediate legal position must be assessed — uncovered sharing of data about vulnerable users may have to pause, and that assessment belongs with the DPO, not with sentiment. But C also treats the deeper cause: if governance takes months, teams will keep routing around it, so the durable fix is a faster compliant pathway — "investigate emerging trends... and introduce innovative ways of working" is your skill precisely so that innovation and governance stop being enemies. Option A ignores the users now depending on the tool and teaches the organisation that governance is blind to outcomes. Option E inverts the error: "users first" cannot mean processing without a lawful basis — vulnerable users are the people most harmed when data about them is mishandled. Option D personalises a systemic failure. Option B is least effective: retrospective papering is a false record, and it converts an honest (if wrong) shortcut into an institutional cover-up with your signature on it. Ethics culture is built at exactly these moments — reward the candour, fix the pathway, but never falsify the paperwork.

**Question 8 (moderate) — The board presentation that must land**

You have fifteen minutes at the executive board to present the annual data governance report. The full report is 60 pages; last year's slot overran and the actions were vague. Board members range from data-fluent to openly bored by the topic.

- A) Compress the 60 pages evenly into fifteen minutes of dense slides
- B) Lead with the three decisions you need, tell one concrete story that makes the top risk tangible for their services, show one clear trend chart, and leave the detail in a pre-circulated annex
- C) Focus the slot on explaining governance concepts so the less data-fluent members can follow the detail
- D) Delegate the presentation to your best analyst, who knows the figures more deeply
- E) Ask for a longer slot at a future meeting instead

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A.**

**Explanation:** Option B is "communicate relevant, compelling stories using the most appropriate medium" executed at board level: decisions first (boards exist to decide), narrative to make risk felt rather than merely stated, one chart carrying the trend, and the annex respecting the fluent members' appetite for depth without spending the room's attention on it. Option C mistakes the forum — the board slot is for decisions, not tuition; literacy-building belongs in a different intervention (which your role also owns, but not here). Option D outsources the senior relationship your framework explicitly assigns to you: representing the community to large audiences and maintaining relationships with important decision-makers is not delegable at this level. Option E surrenders a live slot for a hypothetical one. Option A is least effective: even compression that keeps every fact loses every decision — the report that covers everything lands nothing, and this year's vague actions become next year's finding. Strategic communication is choosing what not to say.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Standards for the wider system, not just your organisation**

You are drafting a data standard for organisational identifiers. Your organisation could adopt a bespoke format quickly, but an emerging cross-government standard — slower to adopt and imperfect for two of your use cases — is gaining traction in other departments you exchange data with.

- A) Adopt the bespoke format now and revisit alignment in a few years
- B) Adopt the cross-government standard, document the two gap use cases, and feed them into the cross-government standard's development so the standard improves for everyone
- C) Adopt both in parallel, letting each system choose
- D) Wait until the cross-government standard is finalised before doing anything
- E) Adopt the cross-government standard for external exchange only, keeping the bespoke format internally forever

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C.**

**Explanation:** Your framework asks you to "decide where standards need to be set across the organisation, and how to set them in the wider context of government" — and identifiers are the canonical wider-context case, because their entire value is agreement across boundaries. Option B accepts short-term imperfection for long-term interoperability and, crucially, engages rather than complains: feeding your gap cases into the standard's development is how cross-government standards get good, and it positions you as the senior advisor your role describes. Option A builds a translation debt you will pay at every future data exchange. Option D leaves systems being built today with no standard at all — the vacuum fills itself with accidental formats. Option E institutionalises permanent dual-running. Option C is least effective because "let each system choose" is the absence of a standard wearing the costume of one — it multiplies mappings, guarantees mismatches, and abdicates exactly the decision your role exists to make.

**Question 10 (hard) — The maturity assessment your leadership dislikes**

The independent data maturity assessment you commissioned scores the organisation lower than last year, largely because the methodology improved and now inspects evidence rather than self-assessment. Two executive committee members privately urge you to "restate last year's score on the new basis" so the trend line looks flat rather than declining, arguing the fall is "methodological, not real".

- A) Present the new score alongside last year's original, with a clear methodological note explaining that the fall reflects better measurement rather than degraded practice — and if a robust restatement of last year is genuinely possible, offer it transparently as an additional labelled line, not a replacement
- B) Quietly restate last year's score downward so the trend shows improvement
- C) Present only this year's score with no comparison
- D) Delay the report until a restatement can be agreed with the two committee members
- E) Present the decline without any methodological explanation, since the numbers speak for themselves

**Most effective: A. Least effective: B.**

**Explanation:** The committee members' underlying point is half-right — comparing scores across a methodology change *is* misleading — but their remedy is manipulation. Option A gives the legitimate insight (measurement improved; the decline is largely artefactual) through legitimate means: original figures preserved, methodological note explicit, any restatement labelled and additional. That is "work with senior stakeholders to provide data assurance" and "report assessment findings to boards or committees" done with integrity intact. Option E fails differently: presenting a decline you know to be artefactual without explanation actively misleads in the pessimistic direction, and boards over-correct on false bad news too. Option C hides the trend, which is the report's point. Option D lets the people with a stake in the answer negotiate the measurement. Option B is least effective: an unlabelled restatement to manufacture a flattering trend is data manipulation, plainly — and the Head of Data Governance doing it licenses every directorate to do the same to you. You are the organisation's standard for honest measurement; the standard is set by what you do under exactly this pressure.

**Question 11 (moderate) — The colleague department asks for your playbook**

A head of data governance in another department, newer to the role, asks you to share your governance framework, training materials and honest lessons learned — including the failures. Your team is stretched, and some material shows your organisation's earlier mistakes candidly.

- A) Politely decline; your team has no capacity and the failure material is sensitive
- B) Share the polished materials only, removing anything that shows past problems
- C) Share the materials including the honest lessons, offer a short conversation to walk them through the pitfalls, and suggest establishing a recurring cross-government practice exchange so the burden and benefit are shared
- D) Suggest they procure a consultancy to build their own framework
- E) Share everything as-is with no context or conversation

**Most effective: C. Least effective: D.**

**Explanation:** Option C is collaboration as the public sector means it: government is one system, and a stronger governance practice in a peer department directly benefits the citizens whose data flows between you. Including the failures matters — sanitised playbooks transfer procedures but not judgement, and your candour models the transparency you want the profession to have. The practice-exchange suggestion converts a one-off favour into sustainable mutual capacity, which answers the genuine capacity concern in Option A. Option B shares a fiction. Option E is generous but low-value — artefacts without context routinely mislead. Option D is least effective: sending a fellow public servant to buy externally what a colleague could share freely wastes public money and forfeits the relationship — the opposite of "maintain effective long-term relationships at a senior level" and of the cross-government advisory identity your role level explicitly describes.

**Question 12 (hard) — Ranking under pressure: four fires, one afternoon**

At 14:00 you learn, in the same hour: (1) a journalist has emailed the press office asking whether the organisation shares data with a named commercial partner — the sharing is lawful and documented, and a response is needed by tomorrow; (2) a data owner has unilaterally extended a retention period beyond the approved schedule, effective immediately; (3) your best governance analyst has verbally resigned, citing workload; (4) the CDO wants your view by 17:00 on whether to pause a major programme's data migration over quality concerns. Rank the order in which you personally engage this afternoon.

- A) 4, 2, 3, 1
- B) 1, 4, 2, 3
- C) 4, 1, 2, 3
- D) 3, 4, 1, 2

**Best ranking: A (4, 2, 3, 1).**

**Explanation:** Item 4 comes first: it has the hardest same-day deadline (17:00), the largest consequence (a major programme's migration proceeding on bad data is very expensive to reverse), and it is a direct request from the CDO for exactly the risk-based advice your role exists to give. Item 2 is second: an unauthorised retention extension is a live compliance breach that worsens with time — data held beyond schedule may already be unlawfully retained — and a short, firm same-day intervention with the data owner both contains it and defends the authority of the schedule; it cannot wait behind non-urgent items, though it can wait until your 17:00 advice is safe. Item 3 is third: a verbal resignation from a key person deserves a personal conversation today — retention chances decay fast, and workload complaints from your best people are also risk intelligence — but an hour's delay does not change the outcome the way items 4 and 2 would. Item 1 is last for you personally, not because media risk is small but because the sharing is lawful and documented: your contribution is supplying the press office with accurate facts and the documentation, which is quick, delegable in drafting, and due tomorrow. Ranking D fails by putting a today-deadline strategic decision behind a conversation that can start at 16:00; rankings B and C over-prioritise the media query because it feels alarming — a classic urgency/importance confusion. The senior skill: rank by deadline-weighted consequence and by what only you can do, not by which item spikes adrenaline.

### Preparation tips

- **Anchor on the values, not on cleverness.** UK public sector situational judgement rewards user needs, transparency, collaboration, accessibility and inclusion. When torn between options, ask: which action best serves the public and keeps the record honest? At your level, also ask: which action makes the *system* better, not just this incident?
- **Look for the option that verifies before acting.** Senior scenarios are salted with second-hand claims ("the CDO is relaxed", "it was probably anonymised"). Effective options establish facts; ineffective ones act on hearsay or hope.
- **Prefer root-cause options over symptom options — except in live emergencies.** The strongest answers usually contain both containment now and a systemic fix after (Question 7's pattern). But when a clock is running — regulatory windows, board deadlines — containment comes first.
- **Beware options that protect you rather than the organisation.** Documented objections while permitting harm, quiet workarounds, blame-first responses: these are engineered to tempt experienced operators who have been burned before.
- **Rehearse with your own history.** Write down three real dilemmas from your career, the options you had, and what you now believe was most and least effective. Comparing your live choices with your reflective ones reveals your bias under pressure — usually towards either over-enforcement or over-accommodation. Knowing which way you lean is worth many practice tests.
- **Read every option before judging any.** "Most and least effective" are comparative judgements; the best answer is often only visible once you have seen the full set.

### Common pitfalls

- **Answering idealistically rather than effectively** — choosing what sounds noble ("refuse outright", "notify the regulator immediately") over what actually manages the risk and the relationship in a real workplace.
- **Selecting passive options** — deferring, delegating the difficult conversation, or waiting for validation while a clock runs; assessors read these as avoidance, and at head-of level they are.
- **Ignoring the organisation's culture and values** — public sector tests reward collaboration and transparency over unilateral force, even when force feels satisfying.
- **Over-escalating** — running to the permanent secretary or the regulator before establishing facts spends credibility the role needs you to conserve.
- **Under-weighting honesty of the record** — any option that shades a report, restates a score quietly, or lets a misleading figure stand is almost always the least effective, whatever it buys short-term.
- **Forgetting the people dimension** — options that fix the process but punish candour (Question 3) or humiliate a stakeholder (Question 4) fail on the values the test is calibrated to, and on the years-long relationships your role level explicitly requires you to maintain.
