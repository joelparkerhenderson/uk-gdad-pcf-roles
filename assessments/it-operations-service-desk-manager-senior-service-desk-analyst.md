## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the exact mental processes your role demands, using practical scenarios rather than abstract puzzles. For a senior service desk analyst, that means questions built from call monitoring data, script logic, rota constraints, escalation rules, and problem records — the materials of a working desk rather than shapes and number sequences.

The typical format is an online, timed test lasting 15 to 30 minutes, with 15 to 30 questions covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective, comparing your responses against a norm group at a similar level, and many platforms adapt question difficulty to your performance — so a test that suddenly feels harder is often a test you are doing well on. Employers typically receive a profile of speed against accuracy rather than a single mark, which means careful work is rewarded, not just fast work. Expect a few ungraded warm-up questions before scoring begins.

Cognitive assessment is particularly relevant at your level because your role adds a supervisory layer to first-line thinking. An analyst reasons about one call at a time; you reason about the pattern across calls — which operator is stuck, which symptom is recurring, which queue is drifting — while also owning artefacts, like the analyst script, whose internal logic must stay consistent as you update them. You also contribute to problem management, which is applied deduction: distinguishing strategic, tactical, and operational levels of a problem, ruling causes in and out, and designing preventative measures that actually address the mechanism of failure. A well-designed cognitive test simulates precisely these demands in miniature.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Pattern recognition** maps to your core duty of monitoring inbound calls and to your **Problem management** skill: investigating "patterns and trends" begins with seeing them — clusters by site, by symptom, by time of day, or by analyst.
- **Logical deduction** maps to your ownership of the analyst script: a script is a decision tree, and updating it without breaking its branch logic is deduction in earnest. It also maps to **Service management framework knowledge**, since applying framework processes correctly means reasoning from their rules.
- **Error checking** maps to **Asset and configuration management** ("track, log and correct information") and to your quality role: reviewing analysts' tickets, records, and the script itself for inconsistencies before they propagate.
- **Prioritisation** maps to your duties of supporting operators, intervening in difficult calls, and managing availability: your attention is the desk's scarcest resource, and allocating it well is the job.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to your **Problem management** skill — investigating problems in systems, processes, and services, and contributing to remedies and preventative measures — and to your **Service focus** skill of taking inputs and establishing "coherent frameworks that work".

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern recognition in a monitoring dashboard**

Your call monitoring dashboard shows each analyst's current state. The convention is: green = available; amber = on a call under 10 minutes; red = on a call over 10 minutes; grey = logged out. At 10:30 the dashboard shows: Amir — amber, 4 minutes; Beth — red, 14 minutes; Carl — amber, 12 minutes; Dee — grey. Which entry is internally inconsistent?

- A) Amir
- B) Beth
- C) Carl
- D) Dee

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Test each entry against the colour rules. Amir: amber at 4 minutes — consistent, since amber means under 10 minutes. Beth: red at 14 minutes — consistent, since red means over 10. Carl: amber at 12 minutes — inconsistent, because a 12-minute call should show red. Dee: grey with no call — consistent. Carl's entry is the anomaly, and on a real desk it would matter: either the dashboard has a refresh fault, or the timer reset mid-call (perhaps a transfer), and in the meantime your at-a-glance view of who needs intervention support is wrong. Monitoring is only as good as the display's integrity, so a senior analyst checks the instrument as well as the team. The technique — verify each record against the stated rule, one field at a time — is the same one you apply to ticket audits and script reviews.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction from an intervention rule**

Your desk's guidance says: "A senior analyst should intervene in a live call if the call exceeds 15 minutes AND the analyst has flagged for help, OR if the caller asks for a supervisor." You observe: Elena's call is at 18 minutes and she has not flagged for help; the caller has not asked for a supervisor. According to the guidance, must you intervene?

- A) Yes — the call is over 15 minutes.
- B) Yes — long calls always require intervention.
- C) No — neither condition for mandatory intervention is met.
- D) No — senior analysts never intervene unless asked by the caller.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Parse the rule's logic carefully. Intervention is required when (call over 15 minutes AND analyst flagged) OR (caller asks for supervisor). Elena's call is over 15 minutes, but she has not flagged, so the first compound condition fails — the AND requires both parts. The caller has not asked for a supervisor, so the second condition fails too. Neither trigger fires: intervention is not mandatory, so C is correct. Option A treats the AND as an OR — the most common logical slip in rule application. Option D overcorrects into a rule that does not exist: the guidance describes when you must intervene, not when you may; your judgement to check in gently on an 18-minute call remains available and often wise. Assessments reward reading compound conditions exactly, and so does script ownership — the difference between AND and OR in a script branch sends every analyst down a different path.

**Question 3 (easy) — Error checking a script update**

You are updating the analyst script for password resets. The draft reads: "Step 1: Verify identity using two approved checks. Step 2: If verification fails, proceed to reset the password. Step 3: If verification succeeds, advise the caller that no reset is possible and close the call. Step 4: Log the contact." What is wrong with the draft?

- A) Step 1 should use three checks, not two.
- B) Steps 2 and 3 have their outcomes reversed.
- C) Step 4 is unnecessary.
- D) Nothing is wrong.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Trace the logic as an analyst would follow it. As drafted, a caller who fails identity verification gets their password reset, while a caller who proves their identity is refused — the exact inversion of safe practice, and a security incident waiting to happen. The correct flow is: verification succeeds → reset; verification fails → no reset (with an appropriate escalation or alternative route). Option A invents a requirement the scenario never states. Option C is wrong because logging every contact is standard. The deeper point is why this question belongs in your assessment: you own and update the script, which means an error you miss is executed faithfully by every analyst who trusts it — errors in instructions multiply by the number of people who follow them. Reviewing a script draft by role-playing it line by line, as a naive reader rather than its author, is the reliable defence.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Prioritisation across supervisory demands**

It is 11:00 on a busy Wednesday. Four things compete for your attention:

1. The dashboard shows a new analyst has been on a call for 22 minutes and has just flagged for help.
2. Your manager has asked for the weekly service report by 15:00 today.
3. An analyst has emailed asking to swap their shift next Tuesday.
4. You notice the queue's average wait has crept from 2 to 6 minutes over the past hour.

Which order of attention is most defensible?

- A) 2, 1, 4, 3 — the manager's deadline outranks live calls
- B) 1, 4, 2, 3 — live intervention first, then investigate the queue drift, then the report, then the swap
- C) 4, 1, 2, 3 — queue metrics before individual calls
- D) 1, 2, 3, 4 — handle requests in the order people asked

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Rank by impact-weighted urgency. Item 1 is both urgent and squarely your role: a struggling analyst has used the flag mechanism, a caller is 22 minutes deep, and intervention is a duty you cannot batch for later. Item 4 comes next: a tripling of average wait in an hour is a live service signal that may indicate short staffing, a developing incident, or several long calls — a quick diagnosis now may prevent an afternoon of failed service levels; it outranks the report because the report's 15:00 deadline has slack while the queue is degrading in real time. Item 2 follows with time still in hand. Item 3 is genuinely important to the analyst but concerns next Tuesday. Option A defers a flagged live call to paperwork; option C investigates the aggregate while a specific person waits with a flag raised — the flag is the sharper signal; option D mistakes politeness for prioritisation. The senior analyst's pattern: live people first, degrading trends second, deadlines with slack third, future logistics fourth.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Classifying a problem's level**

Your role asks you to understand whether a problem is strategic, tactical, or operational. Consider: (i) the ticket tool crashes for all users every time a specific report is run — a workaround exists but wastes 20 minutes daily; (ii) the department's five-year contract for the ticket tool expires next year and the market has changed substantially; (iii) this morning's ticket tool slowness turned out to be one analyst's browser needing a restart. Which classification is most accurate?

- A) (i) operational, (ii) strategic, (iii) not a problem at all — it was an incident, resolved
- B) (i) strategic, (ii) tactical, (iii) operational
- C) (i) tactical, (ii) strategic, (iii) operational
- D) (i) operational, (ii) operational, (iii) tactical

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Work the definitions. Item (i) is a recurring fault with a known workaround affecting daily operations — an operational problem: real, ongoing, and worth a permanent fix, but contained in scope. Item (ii) concerns a multi-year contract and a changing market — strategic: it shapes the desk's tooling for years and belongs in planning conversations, not the incident queue. Item (iii) is the subtle one: a single occurrence, resolved by a restart, with no evidence of recurrence — that is an incident, now closed, not a problem at all. Problem management deals with underlying causes of actual or potential recurring incidents; promoting every resolved incident into a "problem" clogs the problem register and dilutes attention from genuine patterns. Option A gets all three right. The senior analyst's contribution to problem management starts with exactly this triage: knowing what deserves investigation, at what altitude, and what is simply done.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Pattern recognition in coaching data**

You review first-contact resolution (FCR) rates for your four analysts over three months. Amir: 68%, 70%, 71%. Beth: 74%, 73%, 74%. Carl: 71%, 62%, 54%. Dee: 58%, 63%, 67%. Which reading of the data is most accurate?

- A) Carl is the weakest analyst and Dee the strongest.
- B) Beth is coasting and needs a stretch target.
- C) Carl shows a marked decline that needs a supportive conversation soon; Dee shows strong improvement worth recognising; Amir and Beth are stable.
- D) The differences are noise; no action is needed.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Read trends, not snapshots. Carl's trajectory — 71 to 62 to 54 — is a 17-point fall in two months; his latest figure is the lowest in the team, but the trend matters more than the level: something changed, and possibilities range from harder ticket mix to training gaps to personal circumstances, which is why the right response is a supportive conversation, not a verdict. Dee's trajectory is the mirror image: from the team's lowest starting point, up 9 points — improvement worth recognising explicitly, because reinforced progress tends to continue. Amir and Beth are stable within normal variation. Option A judges on the latest month alone and misses that Dee's 67% is still below Amir and Beth — "strongest" is simply wrong, and labelling people from snapshots is exactly the habit coaching data exists to prevent. Option D ignores a 17-point slide. Your coaching duty runs on this skill: trends identify who needs help and who deserves credit, before the monthly averages bury both.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Deduction across linked tickets**

Escalation guidance: (i) incidents caused by a change in the last five working days go to the change implementer's team; (ii) recurring incidents with no linked change go to problem investigation; (iii) single incidents with no linked change stay in the normal queue. Today is Thursday. The email client has produced the same error for six users this week; the change log shows the email client was last changed nine working days ago; the network was changed on Monday, three working days ago, but network diagnostics are clean. Where should this cluster go?

- A) To the email change implementer's team, under rule (i)
- B) To the network change implementer's team, under rule (i)
- C) To problem investigation, under rule (ii)
- D) To the normal queue, under rule (iii)

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Apply each rule against the facts. Rule (i) needs a causal change within five working days. The email change was nine working days ago — outside the window — so option A fails. The network change is within the window, but diagnostics are clean and nothing links the email error to the network, so treating it as the cause is assumption, not deduction: option B fails. That leaves the choice between (ii) and (iii), which turns on recurrence: six users with the same error in one week is clearly recurring, so rule (ii) applies and the cluster goes to problem investigation — option C. Note the two disciplines at work: check the time window arithmetic exactly (nine is not five), and refuse to promote a coincidental change into a cause without a connecting mechanism. Both mirror your problem management skill: investigation follows evidence, and the routing rules only work when their conditions are tested honestly.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Error checking a rota**

Next week's rota must satisfy: at least three analysts on phones at all times between 08:00 and 18:00; every analyst gets a lunch break; you (the senior) count as cover only during breaks. The draft rota for Monday shows four analysts: Amir 08:00-16:00 (lunch 12:00-13:00), Beth 09:00-17:00 (lunch 12:00-13:00), Carl 10:00-18:00 (lunch 13:00-14:00), Dee 08:00-16:00 (lunch 13:00-14:00). At which time does the rota first break the three-on-phones rule, assuming you provide cover for at most one absent analyst at a time?

- A) 08:00
- B) 09:00
- C) 12:00
- D) 17:00

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Check coverage hour by hour. At 08:00, only Amir and Dee have started (Beth starts 09:00, Carl 10:00): two analysts on phones. You may cover during breaks, but 08:00 is not a break — it is a staffing gap, and even counting your cover generously, two plus you is three only if senior cover applied, which the rule restricts to breaks. So the rota breaks the rule at 08:00, before the day has properly begun. It is worth continuing the check as practice: 12:00-13:00 has Amir and Beth both at lunch — Carl and Dee remain (two) plus you covering one absence makes three, which just passes; 13:00-14:00 has Carl and Dee at lunch — Amir and Beth remain plus your single cover makes three, which passes; from 17:00 only Carl remains, which fails again. The first break, though, is 08:00. The general lesson for rota checking: scan the boundaries — start times, end times, and overlapping breaks — because coverage failures live at the edges, not in the comfortable middle of the day. Managing availability is your named duty, and this bounded, systematic scan is how a draft rota becomes a dependable one.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Applied problem solving from mixed evidence**

Since Monday, analysts report the ticket tool "freezes randomly". You gather facts: (i) freezes affect analysts on both sites; (ii) freezes happen only when attaching files to tickets; (iii) the attachment storage service was migrated at the weekend; (iv) two analysts who claim freezes "all the time" show freezes only during attachment upload in their activity logs; (v) browsing and updating tickets without attachments never freezes. What is the strongest supported conclusion?

- A) The ticket tool needs to be replaced — it is unreliable.
- B) The freezes are specific to attachment upload and began after the storage migration, making the migrated storage service the leading suspect for investigation.
- C) The two analysts are exaggerating and the issue can be deprioritised.
- D) Both sites' networks developed simultaneous faults.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Converge on the mechanism. Facts (ii) and (v) isolate the failing operation precisely: attachment upload freezes; everything else works. Fact (i) rules out anything site-local, eliminating option D — simultaneous independent network faults at two sites is far less plausible than one shared cause. Fact (iii) supplies a recent change to exactly the component involved in the failing operation — the strongest kind of suspect: right place, right time. Fact (iv) is about evidence quality, not blame: the analysts' experience is genuine, their generalisation ("all the time") simply reflects how disruptive the freezes feel, and their logs actually strengthen the attachment-specific pattern — which is why option C is both unkind and analytically wrong. Option A leaps from symptom to procurement without a diagnosis. B states what the evidence supports and no more: a leading suspect for investigation, not a verdict. This is your problem management skill end to end — isolate the operation, correlate with change, weigh testimony against logs, and hand investigators a crisp, evidenced hypothesis.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Multi-constraint coaching schedule**

You must run four one-hour coaching sessions this week — Amir (call control), Beth (complaint handling), Carl (FCR recovery), Dee (progression discussion) — at most one session per day, Monday to Friday. Constraints: (i) Carl's session must happen before the team meeting on Wednesday morning, where new quality standards are announced; (ii) Beth is on leave Monday and Tuesday; (iii) Dee's progression discussion must follow the Wednesday morning meeting, since progression criteria change there; (iv) you are on desk cover all Friday and cannot coach. Which schedule works?

- A) Carl Monday, Amir Tuesday, Beth Wednesday afternoon, Dee Thursday
- B) Carl Monday, Beth Tuesday, Amir Wednesday afternoon, Dee Thursday
- C) Amir Monday, Carl Wednesday afternoon, Beth Thursday, Dee Friday
- D) Amir Monday, Carl Tuesday, Dee Wednesday afternoon, Beth Friday

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Eliminate against each constraint in turn. Option B places Beth on Tuesday, during her leave — fails constraint (ii). Option C fails twice: Carl's session on Wednesday afternoon comes after the Wednesday morning meeting, breaking constraint (i), and Dee on Friday breaks constraint (iv). Option D places Beth on Friday, breaking constraint (iv). Option A survives every test: Carl on Monday is before the Wednesday meeting (i); Beth on Wednesday afternoon is after her Monday-Tuesday leave (ii); Dee on Thursday follows the Wednesday meeting (iii); nothing falls on Friday (iv); and no day carries two sessions. The efficient method is to start from the most restrictive constraints — here, the two people tied to the Wednesday meeting in opposite directions: Carl must land in the Monday-Tuesday window and Dee in the Wednesday-afternoon-onwards window, with Friday closed. Once those anchors are placed, Beth's leave forces her into Wednesday afternoon or Thursday, and the remaining pieces fall in around them. This is precisely the reasoning behind your availability-management duty: anchor the immovable commitments first, schedule the flexible ones last, and check every plan at its edges before publishing it.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Trend versus artefact in monitoring data**

Average call duration on your desk rose from 6.0 to 7.5 minutes between April and May. You find: (i) in May, the new starter Dee began taking live calls, averaging 11 minutes; (ii) excluding Dee, the team's May average was 6.1 minutes; (iii) April had no equivalent new starter; (iv) call volumes were similar in both months. Your manager asks: "Has the desk slowed down?" What is the most accurate answer?

- A) Yes — duration rose 25%, the desk is slower.
- B) No — excluding the expected effect of a new starter learning the role, the established team's performance is essentially unchanged (6.0 to 6.1); the rise is a composition effect, and Dee's duration should be tracked as she develops.
- C) No — the data must be wrong.
- D) Yes — and Dee should be taken off the phones until she is faster.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Decompose before concluding. The headline rise (6.0 to 7.5, a 25% increase) is real arithmetic but misleading interpretation: adding one new starter averaging 11 minutes to a team averaging around 6 mechanically lifts the blend, even though nobody slowed down. Fact (ii) is the decisive test — the established team moved only 6.0 to 6.1, within noise. So the honest answer distinguishes the composition effect (new team member, expected) from a performance change (absent), and adds the forward-looking element: track Dee's trajectory, because a new starter's duration should fall steadily over her first months, and coaching exists to help it fall. Option A reports the number without the mechanism. Option C rejects data because it surprises — the opposite of interpretation. Option D punishes learning: taking a new starter off the phones for being new destroys the development your role exists to provide. This is the reporting skill at senior level: the story under the number, told accurately, with the action attached.

### Preparation tips

- Practise decomposing aggregate metrics: whenever a team number moves, ask "did the mix change or did the people change?" before explaining it. This single habit powers several question types — and most monthly reports.
- Rehearse compound conditions aloud: AND means both, OR means either, "unless" means an exception. Script logic, escalation rules, and test questions all turn on these small words.
- Review a real (anonymised) week of your desk's data and invent three questions from it — one pattern, one calculation, one anomaly. Writing questions teaches the patterns faster than answering them.
- Time-box your practice: accuracy first untimed, then add gentle time pressure. Senior-level tests reward the person who is quick because they are methodical, not quick because they gamble.
- Check boundaries systematically in any schedule or rota question — start times, end times, overlaps. Failures live at the edges.
- Before the real test: rest, a quiet room, and the platform's practice questions. Treat it like preparing an analyst for a difficult call — conditions matter.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Reading AND as OR.** The most common error in rule questions, and in script writing. Both conditions of an AND must hold before the rule fires.
- **Judging people from snapshots.** A single month's figure ranks; a trend explains. Assessments and coaching both reward the trend reading.
- **Promoting coincidence to cause.** A change near in time to a fault is a suspect, not a culprit — demand a connecting mechanism before routing blame.
- **Missing the composition effect.** Team averages move when membership changes. Decompose before declaring improvement or decline.
- **Forcing an answer when the options all fail.** Occasionally the disciplined conclusion is "as constrained, it cannot be done" — in tests, re-check once and trust your method; at work, renegotiate a constraint openly.
- **Treating your own artefacts as already correct.** The script you wrote is the hardest document for you to proofread. Review it as its most literal-minded reader, not as its author.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures how confidently and accurately you work with the numbers your role genuinely produces and consumes: team resolution rates, SLA performance, call volumes and durations, availability and rota coverage, complaint counts, and trends across weeks and months. It is not an advanced mathematics exam — the operations are percentages, ratios, weighted averages, rates, and careful table reading — but at senior level the questions increasingly mirror your supervisory reality: combining figures across analysts and sites, working backwards from targets, and judging whether a headline number tells the truth.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 20 to 30 minutes with 15 to 20 questions, each based on a small table or chart of workplace data. An on-screen calculator is usually available, and rough working is expected. Scoring compares accuracy and speed against a norm group; some platforms adapt difficulty as you progress. Employers read the results as evidence of whether you can be trusted with the desk's data — to produce reports, to challenge figures that look wrong, and to brief managers without introducing errors.

For a senior service desk analyst, numeric fluency has a distinctive purpose: you are frequently the first interpreter of team data. Your role summary asks you to produce relevant reports in agreed formats and timeframes, to interpret a data set and communicate it to others, and — through your problem management skill — to investigate patterns and trends. You also manage availability, which is rota arithmetic, and you coach analysts, which means reading their performance data fairly. The questions below practise all of these, using the desk's own units: calls, minutes, percentages, and people.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Percentages and SLA arithmetic** map to your **Customer service management** skill: maintaining a high level of customer service is monitored through percentage measures, and dealing with complex complaints often means checking the numbers behind a user's grievance.
- **Weighted averages and aggregation** map to your **IT service reporting** skill: interpreting a data set that spans analysts, shifts, or sites requires combining by totals, not averaging averages — the difference between a fair report and a misleading one.
- **Rates and coverage calculations** map to your duty to manage availability: converting expected demand into required analysts, and understanding what a gap in the rota costs in waiting time.
- **Trend measurement** maps to your **Problem management** skill: "investigate patterns and trends" begins with measuring week-on-week changes accurately before explaining them.
- **Fair comparison of individuals** maps to your coaching duty: comparing analysts who handle different volumes and ticket mixes demands rate-based, not raw-count, comparison — the numeric foundation of fair development conversations.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Team resolution percentage**

Yesterday your team of four analysts resolved 126 of the 140 tickets they closed within SLA. What percentage of closed tickets met the SLA?

- A) 86%
- B) 88%
- C) 90%
- D) 92%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Percentage within SLA = met ÷ total × 100. Step 1: 126 ÷ 140 = 0.9. Step 2: 0.9 × 100 = 90%. A no-calculator check: 10% of 140 is 14, so 90% of 140 is 140 − 14 = 126 — exactly the number met, confirming 90%. This is the desk's most frequently quoted figure, and at senior level you will both produce it and be asked to explain it. Knowing the complement view is equally useful: 14 tickets missed SLA, which is the "miss budget" framing you will use in later questions when working backwards from targets.

**Question 2 (easy) — Availability percentage**

An analyst is contracted for 37.5 hours this week. They spend 4.5 hours in training and 3 hours in team meetings; the rest is phone availability. What percentage of their contracted hours are phone-available?

- A) 75%
- B) 80%
- C) 82%
- D) 85%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Step 1: non-phone hours = 4.5 + 3 = 7.5. Step 2: phone-available hours = 37.5 − 7.5 = 30. Step 3: percentage = 30 ÷ 37.5 = 0.8 = 80%. A tidy check: 7.5 is exactly one fifth of 37.5, so non-phone time is 20%, leaving 80%. Availability percentages are the currency of rota planning — when you promise your manager "the team averages 80% phone availability", this is the calculation underneath, and knowing that each analyst contributes 30 phone-hours per week lets you convert staffing questions into coverage questions instantly.

**Question 3 (easy) — Reading a team performance table**

The weekly summary shows:

| Analyst | Calls handled | Resolved at first contact |
|---|---|---|
| Amir | 90 | 63 |
| Beth | 110 | 77 |
| Carl | 60 | 36 |
| Dee | 40 | 30 |

Who had the highest first-contact resolution (FCR) rate?

- A) Amir
- B) Beth
- C) Carl
- D) Dee

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Compute each rate. Amir: 63 ÷ 90 = 70%. Beth: 77 ÷ 110 = 70%. Carl: 36 ÷ 60 = 60%. Dee: 30 ÷ 40 = 75%. Dee's 75% is the highest, despite her handling the fewest calls. The trap is ranking by the raw resolved count — Beth's 77 is the biggest number in the column, but she also handled the most calls, and her rate matches Amir's exactly. Fair comparison of analysts always divides by each person's own volume first. This is the numeric backbone of fair coaching: praise and concern should attach to rates and trends, not to raw counts that mostly reflect shift patterns and call routing.

**Question 4 (easy) — Complaint rate per thousand contacts**

Last month the desk handled 4,000 contacts and received 6 complaints. What was the complaint rate per 1,000 contacts?

- A) 0.67 per 1,000
- B) 1.5 per 1,000
- C) 6 per 1,000
- D) 15 per 1,000

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Rate per 1,000 = complaints ÷ contacts × 1,000. Step 1: 6 ÷ 4,000 = 0.0015. Step 2: 0.0015 × 1,000 = 1.5 complaints per 1,000 contacts. Equivalent shortcut: 4,000 contacts is 4 thousands, so 6 complaints spread across 4 thousands is 6 ÷ 4 = 1.5 per thousand. Rates per thousand exist to make small numbers comparable across months of different sizes: 6 complaints in a 4,000-contact month is actually a better rate than 5 complaints in a 3,000-contact month (1.67 per 1,000). When you deal with complaints — a named part of your customer service skill — this normalisation is what lets you say whether complaint pressure is genuinely rising or the desk is simply busier.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Weighted average across the team**

Using the table in Question 3 (Amir 90 calls, Beth 110, Carl 60, Dee 40; FCR 70%, 70%, 60%, 75% respectively), what was the team's overall FCR rate?

- A) 68.7%
- B) 68.75%
- C) 69.75%
- D) 70%

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Combine by totals, not by averaging the four rates. Step 1: total calls = 90 + 110 + 60 + 40 = 300. Step 2: total first-contact resolutions = 63 + 77 + 36 + 30 = 206. Step 3: team FCR = 206 ÷ 300 = 0.6867 ≈ 68.7%. Option B, 68.75%, is the trap laid one decimal place away: it is the unweighted average of the four rates ((70 + 70 + 60 + 75) ÷ 4), which looks authoritative because it comes from a tidy calculation — but it is the wrong calculation for workloads of different sizes. The weighted truth is slightly lower because Carl's weaker 60% carries 60 calls of weight while Dee's excellent 75% carries only 40. When two candidate answers sit this close together, one is usually the weighted result and the other the unweighted one — recompute from totals and choose the weighted figure. In your reports, always aggregate from raw counts; never average the analysts' percentages.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Percentage change in backlog**

The desk's open-ticket backlog fell from 240 at the start of the month to 180 at the end. What was the percentage reduction, and if the same percentage reduction is achieved next month, what will the backlog be?

- A) 25% reduction; 135 next month
- B) 25% reduction; 120 next month
- C) 33% reduction; 120 next month
- D) 60% reduction; 72 next month

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Step 1: the fall is 240 − 180 = 60 tickets. Step 2: percentage reduction = 60 ÷ 240 = 0.25 = 25% (measured against the starting value). Step 3: applying 25% to the new starting point of 180: reduction = 180 × 0.25 = 45, so next month ends at 180 − 45 = 135. Option B applies the same absolute fall (60) rather than the same percentage — a subtle but important difference, because percentage-based projections shrink in absolute terms as the base shrinks. Option C divides by the wrong base (60 ÷ 180). Understanding that "same percentage" and "same amount" diverge is essential when you brief a manager on backlog trajectories: a desk clearing 25% of its backlog monthly approaches zero asymptotically, never linearly, and setting expectations accordingly is part of communicating a data set honestly.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Coverage arithmetic for the rota**

Your desk must answer an expected 540 calls next Monday. A full-time analyst on phones all day handles about 45 calls; an analyst on the phones for a half day handles about 22. Thirteen analysts are rostered: 10 will be on phones all day, and 3 will attend a half-day training session and work the phones for the other half. Roughly how many calls of capacity do you have, and is it enough?

- A) 450 capacity — not enough
- B) 471 capacity — not enough
- C) 516 capacity — not enough
- D) 561 capacity — enough

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Step 1: the 10 full-day analysts contribute 10 × 45 = 450 calls of capacity. Step 2: the 3 half-day analysts contribute 3 × 22 = 66. Step 3: total capacity = 450 + 66 = 516 against a forecast of 540 — a shortfall of 24 calls, roughly half an analyst-day. So the honest answer is "about 516, and not quite enough". Option A forgets the trainees' half days entirely; option D adds a phantom half-day block (450 + 66 + 45) that nobody is working; option B splits the difference plausibly but corresponds to no actual staffing arithmetic. Two working lessons follow. First, capacity sums are built person by person from what each is genuinely available to do — mixed days must be decomposed, not rounded up to full days. Second, a 24-call shortfall found on Thursday is a solvable problem (shift the training, borrow cover, pre-agree overflow) while the same shortfall discovered live on Monday is a queue of frustrated callers. Managing availability, your named duty, is exactly this: doing the multiplication before the day does it for you.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Average wait time and staffing gaps**

Between 09:00 and 10:00, 60 calls arrived and the average wait was 90 seconds. Between 10:00 and 11:00, after two analysts went into an incident review, 60 calls arrived and the average wait was 4 minutes 30 seconds. By what factor did the average wait increase, and what was the total extra waiting time experienced by the 10:00-11:00 callers compared with the earlier hour's average?

- A) Factor of 3; 180 extra minutes in total
- B) Factor of 3; 60 extra minutes in total
- C) Factor of 4.5; 270 extra minutes in total
- D) Factor of 2; 90 extra minutes in total

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Step 1: convert to common units — 4 minutes 30 seconds = 270 seconds; the earlier average was 90 seconds. Step 2: factor = 270 ÷ 90 = 3. Step 3: extra wait per caller = 270 − 90 = 180 seconds = 3 minutes. Step 4: total extra waiting across 60 callers = 60 × 3 = 180 minutes. So waits tripled, and the callers collectively spent three extra hours waiting. Option C misreads the factor as the new wait in minutes. The two-step insight — per-caller impact, then population impact — is what turns a metric into a message: "losing two analysts for an hour cost our users three hours of waiting" is the sentence that gets incident reviews scheduled outside peak times, and it is exactly the kind of interpretation your reporting skill calls for.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Working backwards from a team target**

Next month the desk must achieve 92% of incidents resolved within SLA. Volume is forecast at 1,150 incidents. Mid-month check: 620 incidents have arrived, of which 545 met SLA. How many of the remaining 530 forecast incidents can miss SLA while still achieving the monthly target?

- A) 17
- B) 33
- C) 47
- D) 92

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Step 1: the monthly target allows misses of at most 8% of 1,150 = 0.08 × 1,150 = 92 incidents. Step 2: misses so far = 620 − 545 = 75. Step 3: remaining miss budget = 92 − 75 = 17. So only 17 of the remaining 530 incidents may miss — a rate of 17 ÷ 530 ≈ 3.2%, far tighter than the 8% headline target, because the first half of the month burned 75 of the 92-miss budget (a 12.1% miss rate so far). Option D quotes the whole-month budget as if none were spent. This working-backwards pattern is one of the most valuable numeric habits at your level: converting a percentage target into a live "budget remaining" tells the team exactly how much headroom is left, and telling them early — while 530 incidents remain — is what makes recovery possible rather than merely reportable.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Problem fix effectiveness**

A recurring printing fault generated these incident counts in the four weeks before a preventative fix: 48, 52, 46, 54. In the four weeks after the fix: 30, 24, 27, 19. By approximately what percentage did the average weekly incident count fall after the fix?

- A) 40%
- B) 50%
- C) 60%
- D) 75%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Step 1: pre-fix average = (48 + 52 + 46 + 54) ÷ 4 = 200 ÷ 4 = 50 per week. Step 2: post-fix average = (30 + 24 + 27 + 19) ÷ 4 = 100 ÷ 4 = 25 per week. Step 3: reduction = (50 − 25) ÷ 50 = 0.5 = 50%. The averages make the comparison fair despite week-to-week noise (individual weeks ranged from 19 to 54). Two further readings a senior analyst should offer: first, the post-fix trend is still downward (30 → 19), so the full benefit may exceed 50% once transition effects clear; second, the fault has not reached zero, so either the fix is partial or a second cause exists — a candidate for further problem investigation. Measuring remedy effectiveness with before/after averages, then reading the residual trend, is precisely how your problem management skill "contributes to the implementation of remedies and preventative measures" with evidence rather than impressions.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Fair comparison with different ticket mixes**

Two analysts' monthly figures: Amir — 80 password tickets (average 6 minutes each) and 20 hardware tickets (average 30 minutes each); Beth — 20 password tickets (average 6 minutes each) and 80 hardware tickets (average 24 minutes each). Their manager notes Beth's average ticket time (20.4 minutes) is much worse than Amir's (10.8 minutes). What is the fairest reading?

- A) Amir is nearly twice as efficient as Beth.
- B) Beth is slower at both ticket types.
- C) Beth's higher overall average is driven by her hardware-heavy mix; on the shared ticket types she matches Amir on passwords (6 minutes) and is actually faster on hardware (24 versus 30 minutes).
- D) The data is insufficient to compare them in any way.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Verify the headline averages first. Amir: (80 × 6 + 20 × 30) ÷ 100 = (480 + 600) ÷ 100 = 10.8 minutes. Beth: (20 × 6 + 80 × 24) ÷ 100 = (120 + 1,920) ÷ 100 = 20.4 minutes. The headline is arithmetically correct — and interpretively misleading. Compare like with like: on password tickets both average 6 minutes; on hardware tickets Beth averages 24 minutes against Amir's 30 — Beth is 20% faster at the harder work. Her overall average is higher purely because 80% of her workload is the long ticket type. This is a workplace case of a famous statistical trap (an aggregate reversing the within-category truth), and it appears constantly in desk data because routing rarely deals identical mixes. Option A rewards the mix, not the person; option B is flatly contradicted by the per-type figures; option D gives up when the data actually supports a clear, fair comparison. For coaching and any performance conversation: always split by ticket type before comparing people — it is the difference between analytics and injustice.

### Preparation tips

- Master the weighted average until it is reflex: totals first, divide once. Nearly every team-level figure you will ever report is a weighted average in disguise.
- Practise the "miss budget" transformation: turn every percentage target into an allowable number of failures, then track spend against it. It makes SLA questions faster and monthly management genuinely easier.
- Convert units before calculating — seconds versus minutes, half days versus full days. Most wrong answers in timed tests are unit slips, not method errors.
- When comparing people or sites, compute rates per person or per contact, and where mixes differ, split by category before judging. Fairness is a calculation habit.
- Sense-check with 10% anchors: 10% of any figure is one decimal shift, and 5%, 20%, 90% follow quickly from it.
- If your computed answer is not among the options, re-read the stem for an alternative interpretation before abandoning your method — and in real work, write your assumption down.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Averaging averages.** The unweighted mean of analyst percentages is almost always wrong and often flattering. Aggregate from raw counts.
- **Dividing by the wrong base.** Percentage change uses the starting value; SLA performance uses total volume; complaint rates use total contacts.
- **Confusing "same percentage" with "same amount".** A 25% monthly backlog reduction shrinks in absolute terms every month; projecting the raw amount forward overstates progress.
- **Ranking people by raw counts.** Call counts reflect shifts and routing as much as effort. Rates, trends, and mix-adjusted comparisons are the fair tools.
- **Ignoring the spent budget.** Mid-period, the question is never "what does the target allow?" but "what remains of what it allows?"
- **Trusting a headline average across different mixes.** When workloads differ in composition, split by category first — aggregates can reverse the truth.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment measures how precisely you read, interpret, and communicate written material of the kind your role produces and consumes: service management framework extracts, policy notices, complaint letters, user research summaries, the analyst script itself, and reports for stakeholders. The dominant format presents a passage followed by statements to judge as **True** (supported by the passage), **False** (contradicted by it), or **Cannot say** (the passage provides insufficient information). Supporting formats include best-summary questions, inference questions, and clear-writing judgements — choosing the sentence or reply that communicates most accurately for its audience.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 15 to 25 minutes covering 20 to 30 statements across several passages. The governing rule: answer from the passage alone, setting aside your professional knowledge however relevant it feels. For experienced practitioners this restraint is the hardest and most valuable part of the practice.

Verbal precision carries particular weight at your level for a structural reason: you write instructions that others execute. The analyst script you own is read literally by every analyst on every call — an ambiguous conditional or a misplaced "only" propagates into hundreds of user interactions. You also handle complex complaints, where reading exactly what the complainant asserts (and did not assert) determines whether your response resolves or inflames; you digest framework documentation, where definitions are load-bearing; and you turn user research and desk data into reports, where faithful summary is the whole job. Employers test verbal reasoning at senior-analyst level because your words are operational infrastructure.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Precise comprehension of framework text** maps to your **Service management framework knowledge** skill: a Level 3 qualification is evidenced by exactly the kind of definitional care these questions demand.
- **True/False/Cannot-say discipline** maps to your **Problem management** skill: investigation records must distinguish what is established, what is contradicted, and what remains unknown — the three verdicts of the test, applied to real evidence.
- **Reading complaints accurately** maps to your **Customer service management** skill: dealing with complex complaints starts with an exact inventory of what is claimed, what is asked for, and what would count as resolution.
- **Faithful summary and inference** maps to your **IT service reporting** and **User focus** skills: collating user needs evidence and communicating data sets to others requires compressing without distorting.
- **Clear-writing judgement** maps to your ownership of the analyst script and your coaching duty: choosing the clearest formulation is the skill you exercise every time you update the script or model a good ticket note for your team.

### Practice questions

**Passage A (for Questions 1-3):** "Under the desk's problem management procedure, a problem record must be raised when three or more incidents with matching symptoms occur within any rolling ten-working-day window, unless the incidents are already linked to an open problem record. A problem record may also be raised at a senior analyst's discretion for fewer incidents where potential impact is high. Raising a problem record does not pause incident resolution: analysts continue to resolve or work around individual incidents while investigation proceeds. Problem records are reviewed fortnightly, and records with no progress for two consecutive reviews are escalated to the service desk manager."

**Question 1 (easy) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "A senior analyst may raise a problem record after two high-impact incidents with matching symptoms."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The passage states a mandatory trigger (three or more matching incidents in ten working days) and then adds a discretionary route: "may also be raised at a senior analyst's discretion for fewer incidents where potential impact is high." Two incidents is "fewer incidents", and the statement specifies high impact, so the discretionary route covers it exactly: True. Notice the structure the question tests — mandatory rule plus discretionary extension — which is common in service management documentation. A reader who anchors on "three or more" and stops reading would wrongly answer False; the second sentence changes the answer. This mirrors your working reality: the discretion is yours by role, and knowing precisely where mandatory ends and discretionary begins is what lets you use it confidently and defensibly.

**Question 2 (easy) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "While a problem is under investigation, analysts should stop resolving the related individual incidents to avoid disturbing the evidence."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage is explicit: "Raising a problem record does not pause incident resolution: analysts continue to resolve or work around individual incidents while investigation proceeds." The statement asserts the opposite — that resolution should stop — so it is directly contradicted: False. The distractor's appeal is its plausible-sounding rationale ("avoid disturbing the evidence"), which sounds like something a careful procedure might say; but the test is what this passage says, and an invented rationale cannot overturn an explicit sentence. Operationally, the passage reflects the standard separation between incident management (restore service now) and problem management (remove the cause) — a distinction your framework qualification formalises, and one worth reinforcing with analysts who assume a problem record means "stop fixing".

**Question 3 (easy) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "Most problem records at this desk are escalated to the service desk manager."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage defines when escalation happens — no progress for two consecutive fortnightly reviews — but gives no information about how often that condition occurs. "Most records are escalated" is a claim about frequency, and the passage contains no frequency data at all. It neither supports nor contradicts the statement: Cannot say. The trap is the familiarity effect — escalation is genuinely discussed, so the statement feels anchored in the text — but discussing a mechanism is not evidence about how often it fires. The same discipline applies to your investigation notes: "an escalation route exists" and "escalation is common" are different claims requiring different evidence, and senior analysts who keep them separate write records their managers can trust.

**Passage B (for Questions 4-6):** A complaint letter: "Dear Service Desk, I am writing to complain formally about the handling of my fault, reference INC-2026-01488. I reported on 3 June that my case management application crashes when I open documents. I was promised a call-back within one working day; the call came four days later. The workaround I was given — reopening the application each time — wastes my time and, more importantly, twice caused me to lose notes from client meetings. I accept that complex faults take time to fix. What I cannot accept is being left uninformed. I would like an explanation of the delay, a realistic timescale for a proper fix, and some assurance that lost work will not recur. I have copied my line manager. — R. Okafor, Casework"

**Question 4 (easy) — What is the complainant actually asking for?**

Which best captures what R. Okafor is requesting?

- A) Immediate replacement of the case management application.
- B) Disciplinary action against the analyst who promised the call-back.
- C) An explanation of the delay, a realistic fix timescale, and assurance against further lost work.
- D) Compensation for the lost meeting notes.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The letter states its requests explicitly in its penultimate sentence: an explanation, a realistic timescale, and assurance that lost work will not recur — option C, verbatim in structure. Options A, B, and D are all things an annoyed complainant might ask for, which is precisely why they are offered as distractors; none appears in the letter. Notice also what the complainant concedes — "I accept that complex faults take time to fix" — which narrows the complaint to communication failure and consequences, not fix speed. Reading a complaint this precisely is the foundation of your complex-complaints duty: responses that address the stated requests, acknowledge the stated concession, and avoid answering unmade demands resolve complaints; responses built on a skimmed impression ("user is angry about the slow fix") miss the target and generate a second letter. An accurate inventory — claims, concessions, requests — is the first paragraph of your case notes.

**Question 5 (moderate) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "The complainant lost client meeting notes on more than one occasion."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The letter says the workaround "twice caused me to lose notes from client meetings". "Twice" is more than one occasion, so the statement is directly supported: True. This looks trivial, but it rehearses an important verification habit: quantifiers in the source ("twice", "four days", "one working day") are checkable facts, and your complaint response should reflect them exactly — acknowledging "the two occasions on which notes were lost" demonstrates the letter was read, while a vague "any inconvenience caused" signals it was not. Precision in restating the complainant's facts is one of the cheapest and most powerful de-escalation tools available to a senior analyst, and assessments reward the same exactness in miniature.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Inference and tone judgement**

You are drafting the desk's reply. Which opening sentence best matches the complaint's substance and tone?

- A) "We apologise that your application keeps crashing; software faults are unfortunately common and fixes take time."
- B) "Thank you for your letter. You are right that we did not keep you informed as promised: the call-back that should have come within one working day took four, and we are sorry — both for that and for the notes you lost on two occasions."
- C) "We have investigated and found that the delay was caused by unusually high call volumes, for which we cannot be held responsible."
- D) "Your complaint has been logged with reference CMP-2026-0092 and will be processed according to our complaints procedure."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The complainant's own framing — fix delays are acceptable, silence is not — dictates the reply's centre of gravity. Option B opens by validating exactly the failure the complainant identified (the broken call-back promise, with the specific numbers), apologises for it and for the concrete harm (notes lost twice), and thereby demonstrates accurate reading. Option A apologises for the thing the complainant explicitly accepted, proving the letter was skimmed — a reply that misidentifies the grievance typically escalates it. Option C leads with self-exoneration; even where workload contributed, an explanation offered as a defence before any acknowledgement reads as excuse. Option D is procedurally correct and humanly empty — acceptable as a second paragraph, alienating as a first. The tested skill is matching response to substance: identify what the writer actually holds against you, and address that first, specifically. This is also the model you coach analysts toward when you review their draft complaint replies.

**Passage C (for Questions 7-8):** An extract from a script update you are reviewing: "If the caller reports a suspected phishing email, the analyst must not ask the caller to forward the email. The analyst should instruct the caller to leave the email unopened where possible and must raise a security incident within 15 minutes of the call ending. Callers who have already clicked a link or entered credentials must be transferred directly to the security team's hotline during its staffed hours (08:00-18:00). Outside those hours, the analyst must follow the out-of-hours security escalation instead, and must not advise the caller that no action is needed."

**Question 7 (moderate) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "A caller who clicked a phishing link at 19:30 should be told that no action is needed until the security hotline opens at 08:00."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Two clauses govern the out-of-hours case: the analyst "must follow the out-of-hours security escalation instead", and "must not advise the caller that no action is needed". The statement proposes telling the caller precisely what the script forbids, and substitutes waiting for the mandated out-of-hours escalation: contradicted twice over, so False. The question rehearses the exact reading skill script ownership demands — negative obligations ("must not advise...") are easy to skim past because they describe words rather than actions, yet they are among the most safety-critical lines in any script: a caller told "nothing to do until morning" may take no protective steps while credentials are actively misused. When you review script drafts, give "must not" sentences the same weight as "must" sentences; when you test analysts' script knowledge, quiz the negatives.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Clear-writing judgement on a script line**

A draft script line reads: "Analysts should try to make sure that callers are ideally encouraged, where feasible, to consider not opening emails that may potentially be suspicious." Which revision is best for a script analysts must follow under pressure?

- A) "Tell the caller not to open suspicious emails."
- B) "Analysts are encouraged to advise callers regarding email-opening best practice."
- C) "Where operationally viable, callers should be guided toward suspicious-email avoidance behaviours."
- D) Leave the line as drafted — it is thorough.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** A script line is an instruction executed mid-call; its virtues are the imperative mood, one action, and zero hedges. Option A delivers exactly that: who acts is clear from context (the analyst speaking), what to say is unambiguous, and nothing invites case-by-case interpretation. The draft buries the instruction under five hedges ("try to", "ideally", "where feasible", "consider", "potentially") — each one added, probably, to feel careful, and each one making the analyst's real-time decision harder; hedged instructions produce inconsistent calls, which is the exact failure script standardisation exists to prevent. Options B and C replace hedging with abstraction ("best practice", "avoidance behaviours"), which is no better: analysts cannot say an abstraction to a caller. The general test for any script line you write or approve: could a nervous analyst read it aloud, verbatim, on a difficult call, and would the caller know what to do? If not, shorten it until they could. Clear writing here is not style — it is operational reliability.

**Passage D (for Questions 9-11):** An extract from a user research summary: "We interviewed 12 caseworkers and surveyed 180 desk users about the new self-service portal. Interviewees valued the password reset feature but seven of the 12 said they could not find the 'report a fault' option without help. Survey respondents rated the portal 3.9 out of 5 on average; users aged over 55 rated it 3.1. Fault reports submitted via the portal contain, on average, 40% fewer of the diagnostic details analysts need than fault reports taken by phone, and portal-submitted faults take 1.3 contacts on average to resolve, against 1.1 for phone-reported faults. The research did not examine why portal fault reports contain less detail."

**Question 9 (moderate) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "The majority of interviewed caseworkers struggled to locate the fault-reporting option unaided."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Seven of the 12 interviewees "said they could not find the 'report a fault' option without help". Seven out of twelve is more than half — a majority — and "could not find without help" matches "struggled to locate unaided". The statement is a faithful restatement in different words, so: True. The verification habit worth naming: convert the claim's quantifier ("majority") into arithmetic (7 ÷ 12 = 58%) and check it against the passage's numbers rather than its adjectives. Also note the summary's own care — it reports what interviewees said, and the statement's "struggled" stays within that evidence. This is the collating-user-needs-evidence skill from your role summary operating at sentence level: your reports will be trusted precisely to the degree that their words track their numbers.

**Question 10 (challenging) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "Older users rate the portal lower because they find the interface harder to navigate."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage establishes the rating gap — 3.1 for users over 55 against 3.9 overall — so the "rate it lower" half is supported. But the statement goes further and asserts a cause: "because they find the interface harder to navigate." The passage offers no evidence about why older users rate it lower; navigation difficulty is one plausible explanation among several (feature relevance, channel preference, accessibility, prior expectations), and the research as summarised did not investigate causes for this gap at all. A claim whose factual half is supported but whose causal half is unevidenced must be judged as a whole: Cannot say. This cause-versus-correlation discipline is central to your user focus skill — "use quantitative and qualitative data about users to turn user focus into outcomes" works only if the quantitative gap prompts qualitative investigation rather than a guessed explanation. In reports, write "older users rate the portal lower; reasons not yet researched" and let the follow-up research earn the "because".

**Question 11 (challenging) — Best summary for a stakeholder report**

You must summarise Passage D in one sentence for the service desk manager. Which is best?

- A) "Users love the portal, though older users are unhappy with it."
- B) "The portal rates well overall (3.9/5), but fault reporting shows friction: most interviewees couldn't find it unaided, portal reports carry about 40% less diagnostic detail, and portal-reported faults take slightly more contacts to resolve — why detail is lower hasn't yet been researched."
- C) "The portal is failing and fault reporting should be withdrawn until it is fixed."
- D) "Research shows the portal reduces fault resolution efficiency by 40%."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Test each candidate against the passage. Option B carries the headline rating with its number, the three fault-reporting findings (findability, detail deficit, resolution contacts), and — crucially — the summary's own stated limit ("did not examine why"). It compresses without distorting, and the "why" flag tells the manager what decision-grade evidence is still missing. Option A converts 3.9 into "love" and 3.1 into "unhappy" — emotional inflation of moderate scores — and drops the fault-reporting findings entirely, which are the actionable part. Option C is a recommendation the evidence does not make; summaries that smuggle decisions in as findings misuse the reader's trust. Option D garbles two different statistics: the 40% figure describes missing diagnostic detail, not a 40% efficiency loss — the efficiency signal is the modest 1.3 versus 1.1 contacts. Mixing a big number with the wrong noun is one of the commonest ways service reports mislead, and catching it — in your own drafts and others' — is exactly the verbal precision this assessment measures.

### Preparation tips

- Practise the three-verdict discipline on real documents: take a policy notice or framework extract, write four statements about it (one true, one false, two cannot-say), and test a colleague. Writing cannot-say statements teaches the boundary faster than answering them.
- Split compound statements before judging: a claim with a supported half and an unevidenced half fails as a whole. Look especially for "because", which smuggles causes into factual claims.
- Inventory complaints before responding: list claims, concessions, and requests in the complainant's own quantities. Your reply — and your test answers — should track that inventory exactly.
- Read negatives with full attention: "must not", "does not pause", "did not examine" are load-bearing and skim-resistant. Slow down at every negation.
- For clear-writing questions, apply the read-aloud test: could a nervous analyst say this line verbatim on a difficult call? Prefer imperative, single-action, hedge-free formulations.
- Check every number-noun pairing in summaries: 40% of what, exactly? The commonest distortion in service reporting is a correct figure attached to the wrong quantity.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Importing professional knowledge.** Your framework training tells you what procedures usually say; the test asks what this passage says. Text only.
- **Anchoring on the first rule.** Passages often state a general rule and then extend or qualify it ("may also...", "unless...", "instead"). The later sentence frequently decides the answer.
- **Judging compound claims by their true half.** "Lower ratings because of navigation" fails on the "because" even though the ratings gap is real.
- **Mistaking familiarity for support.** A statement about a mechanism the passage discusses still needs the passage to assert that statement.
- **Inflating moderate findings.** 3.9/5 is not "love"; four days late is not "never contacted". Faithful summary preserves magnitude.
- **Answering unmade demands.** In complaint questions and complaint handling alike, respond to what was requested — addressing imagined demands signals the letter was not read.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement test (SJT) presents realistic workplace dilemmas and asks you to judge the effectiveness of possible responses. Rather than testing facts, it measures professional judgement: how you balance users, your analysts, procedure, honesty, and organisational interests when they pull in different directions. Common formats include selecting the most effective response, identifying the most and least effective from a set, ranking all options, or rating each option on a scale.

The typical format is an online test of 20 to 40 minutes with 10 to 20 scenarios, usually generously timed because judgement, not speed, is the object. Scoring compares your choices against a key calibrated by experienced practitioners and aligned to organisational values — in UK government, behaviours such as user focus, taking ownership, honesty, developing others, inclusion, and escalating well.

At senior-analyst level, the SJT shifts its centre of gravity in a way worth understanding before you start. First-line scenarios ask "what do you do for this caller?"; senior scenarios add a second person to almost every dilemma — the analyst you coach, the operator you support, the colleague whose call you might take over. The recurring tension is between doing and developing: stepping in rescues today's call but can hollow out tomorrow's analyst, while standing back can sacrifice today's user to a learning experience. Your role summary names both sides — intervene in difficult calls, and coach and develop analysts — so the strong answers usually find the move that serves the caller now while leaving the analyst stronger, not smaller. A second recurring theme is stewardship of standards: you own the script, contribute to problem management, and model record quality, so several scenarios test whether you protect those quietly, consistently, and without theatre. ### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — The struggling analyst on a live call**

Monitoring the dashboard, you see a newer analyst 20 minutes into a call, visibly stressed. Listening in, you realise they have misdiagnosed the fault and are leading the caller through irrelevant steps. The caller is patient but time is being wasted. What is the most effective response?

- A) Let the call run — analysts learn best from their own mistakes.
- B) Message the analyst a concise steer ("Ask whether this started after Tuesday's update — likely profile issue"), stay listening, and step in warmly only if they cannot recover; afterwards, debrief the diagnosis privately.
- C) Take over the call immediately, announcing to the caller that a senior is now handling it.
- D) Wait until the call ends, then tell the analyst everything they did wrong.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B serves both people in the scenario. The caller gets a corrected diagnosis within a minute; the analyst gets to complete their own rescue, which builds exactly the capability your coaching duty exists to build — and the private debrief converts the incident into learning while it is fresh. Option A abandons the caller to a learning exercise they never volunteered for; "learn from mistakes" does not justify spending a user's afternoon on a misdiagnosis you can see from your chair. Option C fixes the call at the analyst's expense: an announced takeover tells the caller the analyst was failing, and tells the analyst that struggle triggers replacement — after which they will hide struggles, which is the most dangerous habit a desk can teach. Option D lets the waste continue and then delivers feedback at maximum comprehensiveness and minimum usefulness. The senior pattern: smallest effective intervention now, full learning conversation after.

**Question 2 (easy) — Credit and correction in public**

In the team channel, an analyst posts a workaround for a common fault, clearly proud of it. You notice the workaround works but skips a mandatory logging step, meaning affected assets will not be tracked. Several analysts have already reacted positively. What is the most effective response?

- A) Reply in the channel: "This misses the mandatory logging step — please do not use it."
- B) Delete the post and share the correct procedure yourself.
- C) Reply in the channel crediting the workaround and adding the missing step ("Nice find — one addition: log the asset each time, or we lose track of affected machines"), then update the script so the complete version is the official one.
- D) Say nothing publicly but mention the logging step at the next team meeting.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C does four jobs in two sentences: it keeps the analyst's initiative rewarded (public credit sustains the exact behaviour — sharing solutions — a desk needs), it corrects the record where everyone who saw the original will see the correction, it explains why the step matters rather than merely citing the rule, and it routes the improvement into the script you own, making the complete version canonical. Option A is accurate but framed as pure rebuke; the analyst learns that sharing invites public criticism. Option B is worse: deletion plus replacement transfers credit to you and humiliates by erasure. Option D leaves a flawed workaround circulating for days while reactions accumulate — corrections must travel at least as fast and as far as the error. The general principle the key rewards: praise publicly, correct precisely, and institutionalise the fix through the proper artefact.

**Question 3 (easy) — The availability squeeze**

Two analysts ask for the same Friday afternoon off — one for a medical appointment booked weeks ago, one (your strongest performer) for an event, mentioning they "always cover when needed". Minimum staffing allows only one absence. What is the most effective response?

- A) Approve the strongest performer — retaining top talent matters most.
- B) Approve the medical appointment; explain the staffing constraint honestly to the other analyst, acknowledge their record of flexibility, and actively explore alternatives with them — a swap with another site's cover, a half-day, or priority for the next request.
- C) Refuse both to be fair.
- D) Approve both and cover the desk yourself all afternoon.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B applies a defensible principle — a long-booked medical appointment is a need; an event is a preference — and then does the senior-level work that softens the cost: honesty about why, explicit recognition of the analyst's flexibility (so goodwill is banked, not spent), and genuine effort on alternatives, which frequently dissolves the conflict entirely. Option A converts performance into privilege; the message received by everyone else is that reliability earns you less, which corrodes the team you are meant to develop. Option C manufactures equal unhappiness and calls it fairness while failing the medical need. Option D fails your own duties: you are not free cover — your monitoring, intervention, and coaching duties are the desk's safety net, and a rota that only works by consuming its supervisor is a rota that has already failed. Managing availability, named in your role summary, means making the constraint visible and the decision principled — not making the constraint disappear by absorbing it personally.

**Question 4 (moderate) — The complaint about your own intervention**

A caller complains formally: during a difficult call last week, "a supervisor barged in, talked over the analyst, and made me repeat everything". You recognise the call — you intervened because the analyst was struggling. You recall it as helpful; the recording shows you did interrupt the analyst mid-sentence and did re-ask two questions the caller had already answered. What is the most effective response?

- A) Reply defending the intervention: it was necessary and resolved the fault.
- B) Apologise for the caller's experience, acknowledging specifically what the recording confirms — the interruption and the repeated questions — while briefly explaining the purpose of intervention; then adjust your own handover practice, and share the learning with other seniors.
- C) Ask the analyst to reply, since it was their call.
- D) Apologise fully and commit to never intervening in live calls again.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The recording settles the facts: the caller's account is substantially accurate, so the response must start from acknowledgement — specific acknowledgement, because "sorry for any inconvenience" after a caller cites concrete behaviours reads as dismissal. Option B pairs that with proportionate context (intervention exists to help callers) and, critically, converts the complaint into practice improvement: a better intervention includes a warm handover ("Maya has brought me up to speed, so no need to repeat anything") — a technique worth propagating to every senior. Option A defends the decision to intervene, which was sound, but the complaint is about how, and defending the "what" against a complaint about the "how" misses the target — the same reading error the verbal section warned about, now in behavioural form. Option C delegates accountability downward for your own action — the inverse of ownership. Option D over-corrects: abandoning a core duty because one execution was flawed trades a fixable technique problem for a permanent capability loss. The key rewards accountability with learning, not defensiveness and not capitulation.

**Question 5 (moderate) — The script shortcut under pressure**

A major incident is generating heavy call volume. An experienced analyst suggests: "Skip the script's identity verification for callers who just want the outage status — it would halve our queue." Verification exists primarily to protect account actions; a status update reveals no personal data. What is the most effective response?

- A) Agree informally — common sense should prevail during a crisis.
- B) Refuse — the script must be followed exactly at all times, regardless of circumstances.
- C) Recognise the suggestion's merit, check whether the script or incident procedure already provides for it, and if not, get a rapid decision from the service desk manager to authorise a temporary, defined change — then update the script and tell the whole team at once, including exactly what remains verification-mandatory.
- D) Let each analyst use their own judgement about when verification is needed today.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The suggestion is operationally sensible — and that is precisely why it must be handled through the artefact, not around it. Option C treats the script as what it is: a controlled instrument you own. It captures the improvement, routes the authorisation to the right level (changing a security-relevant control during an incident is a manager-level decision, and "know when to escalate" applies to process changes as much as tickets), and — the step most often missed — publishes the change to everyone simultaneously with its boundaries stated, so no analyst extends "skip verification for status" into "skip verification generally". Option A creates an unauthorised, undocumented fork of the script known to whoever happened to be nearby; option D multiplies that fork by the number of analysts, producing exactly the inconsistency scripts exist to prevent — and both leave no record of who was told what. Option B protects the letter of the script while burning its purpose (serving users), and teaches analysts that suggestions are pointless. Stewardship means the script bends quickly and formally — never silently.

**Question 6 (moderate) — The pattern nobody has time for**

Reviewing yesterday's tickets, you notice five separate incidents at different sites, all closed with different causes, that share an odd detail: each user had recently returned from leave. You suspect a dormant-account process is breaking accounts after 30 days of inactivity. The desk is busy, the tickets are all closed, and the users are all working again. What is the most effective response?

- A) Nothing — five resolved tickets are five satisfied users.
- B) Reopen all five tickets and investigate each fully yourself today.
- C) Raise a problem record linking the five incidents, note the returned-from-leave pattern and your hypothesis, and flag it to the service desk manager — then monitor for recurrence while the desk continues normal work.
- D) Email all staff warning them not to take more than 30 days of leave.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C is problem management working exactly as designed and exactly at your level: the incidents are resolved (incident management succeeded), but the pattern predicts future incidents — every employee returning from long leave — and the pattern is invisible to anyone not looking across tickets, which is precisely the senior analyst's vantage point. Raising a linked problem record with the hypothesis preserved gives investigators a running start; flagging it routes resource decisions to your manager; monitoring closes the loop. Option A mistakes closed tickets for a closed cause — the five satisfied users will be joined by every future returner. Option B does the right investigation with the wrong resourcing: your duties (monitoring, intervention, coaching) do not pause for a personal research project, and problem investigation is a coordinated activity, not a solo one. Option D acts on an unconfirmed hypothesis, at absurd cost, through a channel you do not own. Your role summary's words are literal here: "investigate problems... contribute to the implementation of remedies" — contribute, with evidence, through the record.

**Question 7 (moderate) — The coaching conversation that isn't working**

For six weeks you have coached an analyst whose FCR has kept falling. Sessions are pleasant; they agree with every suggestion, then nothing changes. Today they cancel a session for the second time, citing call volume. You privately suspect something beyond skill is involved. What is the most effective response?

- A) Continue the fortnightly sessions — persistence will pay off eventually.
- B) Escalate to the service desk manager that the analyst is uncoachable.
- C) Change the conversation: arrange a protected, unhurried one-to-one, share the trend data honestly, say plainly that the current approach isn't working, and ask open questions about what is actually getting in the way — workload, wellbeing, role fit, or something at home — while making clear your intent is support.
- D) Sit beside them and re-teach the FCR techniques step by step.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Read the evidence like the diagnostician you are: agreeable sessions plus zero behaviour change plus a worsening trend plus emerging avoidance is not a skills signature — skills gaps produce effortful, partial improvement, not smooth agreement and decline. Option C responds to the actual pattern: it replaces technique-transmission with honest inquiry, protects time so "call volume" cannot cancel it, uses the data candidly (people trust coaches who name reality), and opens the door to causes coaching cannot fix but managers and support services can. Options A and D double the dose of a treatment that six weeks of evidence says is not working — D additionally infantilises an analyst who likely already knows the techniques. Option B skips a step and poisons the escalation: "uncoachable" is a verdict, and the honest escalation — if one follows — is "coaching hasn't moved the trend and I believe something else is going on", made after the conversation C describes, ideally with the analyst's knowledge. Developing people, your named duty, sometimes means noticing when development is not the need.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Most and least effective: the failed call-back promise**

You discover that a complaint is arriving: an analyst promised a vulnerable user a call-back "within the hour" three days ago, then went on leave; the promise was never logged, so nobody called. The user has now called back, upset, and is on hold for you. Four possible responses:

1. Take the call, apologise specifically, resolve or firmly schedule the resolution now, log everything — then, separately, raise the systemic gap (promises made near shift-end aren't captured) for a script and handover fix.
2. Take the call and explain that the analyst is on leave, so the delay was unavoidable.
3. Have the next available analyst take the call, since you are due in a problem review.
4. Take the call, apologise, and offer a goodwill gesture, but leave the process gap unexamined since the analyst returns next week.

Which is the MOST effective and which is the LEAST effective response?

- A) Most: 1; Least: 2
- B) Most: 1; Least: 3
- C) Most: 4; Least: 2
- D) Most: 1; Least: 4

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Option 1 is clearly most effective: it repairs the individual harm with specificity and immediate action, restores the record (the unlogged promise is exactly how the failure became invisible), and — the senior signature — treats the incident as a process specimen: if one promise evaporated at shift-end, others are evaporating, and the fix belongs in the script and handover procedure you steward. For least effective, compare 2, 3, and 4. Option 3 is poor — it deprioritises a harmed, waiting user for a meeting — but the user does get a competent analyst. Option 4 helps the user generously and merely misses the systemic lesson. Option 2 is the worst available: it converts an apology into an excuse, implies to a vulnerable user that the desk's internal leave arrangements are their problem, and is also false — the delay was caused by the unlogged promise, not the leave. Explanations that relocate fault onto circumstances are the complaint-handling equivalent of a misdiagnosis: they guarantee the next escalation. The ranking logic: repair, record, then re-engineer — and never explain where you should apologise.

**Question 9 (challenging) — The metric, the manager, and the truth**

Preparing the weekly report, you find FCR has dropped four points, driven by the new starters you are coaching. Your manager, under pressure from their own leadership, says: "Present it excluding the new starters — that's the 'underlying' figure, and it looks fine." There is a legitimate analytical case for showing an established-team figure; presented alone, it would hide the real service users received. What is the most effective response?

- A) Do as asked — your manager owns the report and the context.
- B) Refuse and submit the unadjusted figure alone.
- C) Propose presenting both figures with one line of explanation — headline FCR including everyone, and the established-team figure showing the trend is developmental, not systemic — and note that showing the drop alongside its benign cause is a stronger story than hiding it.
- D) Present the excluded figure as asked, but keep a private note that you disagreed.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The manager's instinct contains real analysis — the composition insight from your numeric practice — attached to a presentation choice that would mislead. Option C separates the two: both figures, honestly labelled, actually serve the manager better, because "FCR dipped four points; the dip is entirely new-starter onboarding and the established team is stable" is a defensible, even reassuring narrative, while a quietly filtered number is a time bomb — the moment anyone reconciles it against raw data, the report's author and approver both lose credibility. This is how you disagree upward at your level: not refusal (option B discards the legitimate insight and the relationship, presenting a scary number with no explanation), not compliance (option A makes you the instrument of a misleading report you drafted — "interpret a data set and communicate this to others" is your named skill and your professional signature is on the interpretation), and not documented acquiescence (option D protects you while the organisation is still misled, which mistakes self-defence for integrity). The key rewards the move that keeps the report true and gives the manager a better version of what they actually need.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Inclusion in development opportunities**

A prestigious project needs an analyst seconded to give "technical advice to the project team" two days a week. Your instinct is to nominate your strongest analyst — articulate, confident, always volunteers. You then notice you have made the last three such nominations on the same instinct, choosing the same person twice, and that a quieter analyst has equivalent technical knowledge, has expressed interest in project work at their last review, but has never been put forward. What is the most effective response?

- A) Nominate the strongest analyst — the project deserves the best, and visibility is earned.
- B) Nominate the quieter analyst without discussion — it is their turn.
- C) Treat it as the development decision it is: revisit what the project actually needs, tell both analysts about the opportunity, and weigh recorded interest and growth against the pattern you have noticed — with support available if the quieter analyst takes it on; separately, commit to advertising future opportunities to the whole team rather than nominating by instinct.
- D) Ask the two analysts to decide between themselves.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The scenario hands you the diagnostic gift of your own pattern: three consecutive instinct-based nominations concentrating opportunity on the most visible person. That is how development gaps compound — confidence attracts opportunity, opportunity builds confidence — and the quieter analyst's combination of equivalent knowledge plus recorded interest plus zero nominations is the signature of someone being skipped, not someone lacking. Option C fixes both the instance (an open, criteria-based choice with support attached) and the mechanism (advertising opportunities henceforth), which is what "coach and develop one or more analysts" means when it meets inclusion: development is distributed by need and potential, not by volume. Option A restates the instinct as a principle — "visibility is earned" ignores that visibility is also allocated, partly by you. Option B corrects the outcome while keeping the arbitrary method, and sets the quieter analyst up as a token rather than a choice. Option D delegates a development decision to the power dynamics that created the imbalance. Note the transferable move the key rewards: when you catch a pattern in your own decisions, fix the system that generates them, not just today's output.

**Question 11 (challenging) — The incident during the handover**

At 17:50, ten minutes before your desk's evening handover to the out-of-hours provider, an analyst shows you a ticket: a hospital-linked user reports their clinical-adjacent application is erroring intermittently. It is not yet a major incident — one user, intermittent — but the application's SLA class is critical, the error is unfamiliar, and the out-of-hours provider has a thinner knowledge base. The analyst asks: "Just hand it over as normal?" What is the most effective response?

- A) Yes — the out-of-hours process exists precisely for tickets that cross the boundary.
- B) No — stay late and resolve it yourself before leaving.
- C) Treat it as a high-risk handover: spend the ten minutes enriching the ticket (symptoms, timeline, SLA class, what has been tried, escalation triggers and contacts), speak directly to the out-of-hours lead rather than relying on the queue, agree the conditions under which they should escalate overnight, and flag it to the on-call manager so nobody is surprised at 02:00.
- D) Ask the user to call back in the morning when the full desk is available.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The scenario sits deliberately between "routine handover" and "major incident", and the senior skill is recognising that the risk lives in the transition itself: out-of-hours failures overwhelmingly happen when context fails to cross the boundary — the provider sees "one user, intermittent" without seeing "critical SLA class, unfamiliar error, clinical-adjacent". Option C spends the available ten minutes exactly where they buy most risk reduction: written context (survives shift changes), verbal handover (surfaces questions the ticket doesn't answer), pre-agreed escalation triggers (converts the provider's uncertainty into action rules), and a warned on-call manager (removes the 02:00 surprise). This is your ownership skill operating at its literal definition — "own an issue until a new owner has been found", where "found" means informed, equipped, and accepting, not merely next in the queue. Option A follows process while abandoning its purpose; the queue transfers the ticket but not the judgement. Option B substitutes heroics for handover — you cannot stay every night, an unfamiliar intermittent error may not yield by 19:00, and the desk must be able to transfer risk safely without you. Option D parks a critical-class error overnight on the user's initiative — the single worst outcome available. Handover quality is a senior craft: measured not by tickets transferred but by context that arrives with them.

### Preparation tips

- Before practising, reread your role summary and picture each duty as a dilemma: when would intervening conflict with developing? When would supporting an analyst conflict with serving a caller? SJT scenarios live at exactly those junctions, and rehearsing the tensions in advance sharpens your judgement under test conditions.
- Apply the two-beneficiary check: at your level, strong answers usually help the immediate person (caller, analyst) and strengthen the system (script, record, process). If your chosen option does only one, look for the option that does both.
- Watch your intervention reflexes: if you would always step in, or always hold back, the key will catch the inflexibility. The calibrated answer matches the intervention to the moment's stakes and the analyst's capability.
- For most/least questions, judge each option on its own merits first, then rank. The least effective is often the plausible-sounding one that shifts blame, hides information, or defers a live harm — not the obviously lazy one.
- Rehearse disagreeing upward: several senior scenarios test whether you can decline a problematic instruction while giving the instruction-giver something better. Practise the formulation "both figures, honestly labelled" until it generalises.
- Debrief your practice with a peer or your manager: comparing rationales, not just answers, is the fastest calibration — and it doubles as exactly the coaching conversation your role expects you to model.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Rescuing instead of developing.** Taking over every difficult call scores as poorly as ignoring them. The key rewards the smallest intervention that protects the caller, followed by learning.
- **Correcting people at maximum publicity.** Public credit, precise correction, systemic fix — in that order. Humiliation-based answers lose marks even when factually right.
- **Absorbing constraints personally.** Covering every gap yourself (rota holes, overnight tickets, others' promises) reads as ownership but scores as system failure — your duties are the desk's safety net, and consuming them is a cost, not a virtue.
- **Defending the "what" against a complaint about the "how".** Acknowledge the experience the evidence confirms, keep the sound decision, fix the technique.
- **Handing over tickets instead of context.** Queue-transfers of high-risk issues without enrichment, conversation, and triggers fail the ownership test at exactly the boundary where failures cluster.
- **Confusing documented disagreement with integrity.** A private note that you objected, while the misleading report ships, protects you and fails everyone else. The key rewards fixing the output, not the file.
