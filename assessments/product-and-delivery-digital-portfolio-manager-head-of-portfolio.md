## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures reasoning — pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, applied problem solving — through scenarios built from the material your role actually governs: investment portfolios, control frameworks, capacity models, escalation chains, organisational metrics and assurance regimes. At head level, the questions concentrate on integration and design: reasoning across several artefacts and constraints simultaneously, detecting the flaw in a system rather than a datum, and distinguishing what evidence establishes from what it merely permits — under time pressure calibrated to executive workloads.

Typical format: online, timed, 15 to 30 minutes, 20 to 30 questions, frequently adaptive, normed against senior professional comparison groups, reported as a speed-accuracy profile. Take the ungraded warm-ups; even experienced executives benefit from two minutes of interface calibration.

The relevance to your role is structural. A head of portfolio is an organisation's instrument for seeing its change portfolio whole: you aggregate what programmes report, correct for the biases aggregation introduces, and provide leaders with insights into the portfolio's future — which is to say, your reasoning is a governance control. When your deduction is loose, the organisation's picture is loose; when your error-checking misses the artefact-level contradiction, boards decide on contradictions; when your prioritisation logic is captured by urgency or seniority, the portfolio's resources follow. There is also a leadership multiplier: you build and lead the team whose collective reasoning produces the portfolio's evidence base, and the disciplines this section drills — verify before certifying, quantify before ranking, design controls that survive their edge cases — are the ones your portfolio managers learn largely by watching you exercise them.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **Communicating analysis and insight** and your duty to provide leaders with insights into the portfolio's future: foresight is pattern-reading — cross-programme recurrences, leading indicators, the metric whose shape signals a reporting problem — surfaced early enough for leaders to act.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Governance and assurance**: ensuring the right controls are used means reasoning exactly about what each control mandates, permits and omits, and how controls interact — including the edge cases where well-intended rules contradict each other.
- **Error checking** maps to **Governance and assurance** and your duty to lead the tracking of digital projects: at your level the errors that matter are systemic — the aggregation that conceals, the measure that broke when the process changed, the register that disagrees with the pack a board will see tomorrow.
- **Prioritisation** maps to **Planning** and **Strategic thinking**: prioritising the most important or high-value work across a portfolio — under capacity constraints, with complex dependencies, against strategic criteria you yourself defined — is the role's core resource decision, repeated at every scale from your own diary to the investment pipeline.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Planning** and your duty to address risks and deal with or escalate issues: the issues that reach you are the ones without clean answers; solving them means constraint-reasoning with organisational, commercial and human variables included — and knowing which residue genuinely requires escalation above you.
- **Team-system reasoning** maps to your duty to build and lead a diverse, skilled and motivated team: workload allocation, capability coverage, and development sequencing across a team of portfolio managers are themselves constraint problems, and several questions below treat them as such.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern in escalation flows**

Reviewing a year of escalations reaching your desk, you count by category: supplier/commercial 14; inter-programme dependency 11; funding/approval timing 9; data or reporting quality 3; team capability 2. Your portfolio managers handle escalations below you; anything they cannot resolve rises. What does the distribution most plausibly indicate?

- A) Supplier management is the portfolio's weakest discipline.
- B) The categories reaching you in volume — commercial, dependency, funding — are those whose resolution needs authority or cross-boundary reach your portfolio managers lack, which is the escalation system working; the near-absence of data-quality and capability escalations, however, deserves a check: genuinely rare, or being absorbed below you?
- C) Your portfolio managers are escalating too much.
- D) The distribution is random and carries no information.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Read the distribution against what escalation volume actually measures — not problem frequency, but the boundary between what your team can resolve and what needs you. Commercial disputes, cross-programme dependency deadlocks and funding-timing collisions are precisely the categories requiring negotiating authority, cross-boundary convening power, or access to leadership that sits at your level: their dominance is the system routing correctly, not a discipline failing (option A confuses "reaches the head" with "is broken"). The analytically interesting signal is the near-zero categories. Data-quality and capability issues certainly occur — every portfolio has them — so their absence from your desk means either your portfolio managers resolve them well (good) or they are being absorbed: normalised, worked around, or withheld as embarrassing (bad, and invisible by construction). Option B both reads the volumes correctly and targets the check where the information isn't. Option C judges rate with no baseline — 39 escalations across a year and a portfolio may be high or low; the stem gives no comparator. The head-level habit this trains: your inbox is a sensor with known biases; what reaches you measures the escalation system as much as the portfolio, and auditing the silences is as important as triaging the arrivals. Leading the tracking of projects includes tracking what isn't tracked upward.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction across control rules**

Your control framework states: (i) any programme exceeding £5m lifetime cost requires a gate review at each phase transition; (ii) gate reviews must be chaired by someone independent of the programme; (iii) heads of portfolio may chair gate reviews except for programmes within their own portfolio. Programme Tay, in your portfolio, costs £8m and is approaching a phase transition. Which one of the following must be true?

- A) You will need to find a chair from outside your organisation.
- B) Tay requires a gate review, and you cannot chair it.
- C) Tay's review can be waived since you cannot chair it.
- D) Rule (iii) conflicts with rule (ii) and one must be repealed.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Chain the rules against the facts. Tay exceeds £5m and approaches a transition: rule (i) makes the gate review mandatory. Chairing: rule (iii) bars heads of portfolio from chairing reviews of programmes in their own portfolio — Tay is in yours, so you are barred; note rule (ii) would independently bar you anyway if your leadership of the portfolio compromises independence, but (iii) settles it explicitly. Both conclusions — review required, you excluded — follow necessarily: option B. Option A overreaches: the rules require independence from the programme, not from the organisation — another head of portfolio, or any qualified independent chair internally, satisfies the text; "outside your organisation" adds a constraint the framework doesn't contain. Option C inverts the logic: your ineligibility to chair is a constraint on the review's staffing, not a ground for waiving a mandatory control — a non sequitur that, in real governance, is exactly how controls erode ("we couldn't convene it, so we skipped it"). Option D misreads harmony as conflict: (iii) is a specific application of (ii)'s independence principle, not a contradiction. The head-level relevance is direct: you ensure the right controls are used, which includes the controls that constrain you — and modelling the distinction between "this rule is inconvenient for me" and "this rule is defective" is among the most-watched behaviours in your role.

**Question 3 (moderate) — Error checking an aggregate before the executive committee**

Your quarterly executive pack drafts: "Portfolio delivery confidence: 78% (up from 74%). All ten programmes reported. Method: unweighted average of programme confidence scores." Checking the underlying data: nine programmes cluster between 70% and 85%; the tenth — your largest, holding 40% of portfolio spend — reports 45%, down from 60% last quarter. What is the most important defect in the draft?

- A) The arithmetic is wrong; the average of the ten scores cannot be 78%.
- B) The headline conceals a materially deteriorating largest programme: unweighted averaging lets nine small improvements mask a 15-point fall in the programme holding 40% of the money — the pack's "up from 74%" tells leaders the opposite of what the spend-weighted picture shows.
- C) Ten programmes is too small a sample for averaging.
- D) Confidence percentages should never be averaged by any method.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The arithmetic may well be internally correct — nine scores in the 70–85 band with one 45 can average to 78 (option A is unverifiable from the stem and beside the point). The defect is representational and it is severe: an unweighted mean gives your largest programme — 40% of spend, confidence collapsing from 60 to 45 — exactly one-tenth of the headline's attention, while nine smaller improvements pull the average up. A spend-weighted view would show portfolio confidence falling, and falling because of the programme that matters most; the draft's cheerful trajectory ("up from 74%") is therefore not imprecise but directionally wrong for the question leaders actually ask: "how safe is our money?" This is the aggregation failure at its most consequential altitude — your pack is the instrument through which the executive sees the portfolio, and a mean that democratises unequal programmes is a lens that inverts the image. The competent fix, worth specifying because head-level review is about prescribing not just detecting: report spend-weighted alongside unweighted, decompose the movement ("nine programmes improved; the largest deteriorated 15 points — pack leads with that"), and never let a single average carry a mixed story. Options C and D retreat into methodological nihilism — averages mislead when misapplied, not always. Providing leaders with insights they can act on begins with refusing to sign the aggregate that hides the insight.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Prioritising the head's week**

Monday, 8:30. Competing for your week: (1) Thursday's investment committee — you present the annual prioritisation recommendation allocating £30m across fourteen bids; your team's analysis is complete but you have not yet stress-tested the top three rankings; (2) a portfolio manager you line-manage has requested an urgent conversation — she is visibly struggling and you suspect she may resign; (3) the director general's office wants your view by Wednesday on a machinery-of-government change that would move two programmes out of your portfolio; (4) a supplier's account director has requested a meeting about "a commercial matter best discussed early", unspecified; (5) your monthly portfolio community session — you coach twelve portfolio managers across the organisation — is Friday, currently unprepared. Which allocation is most defensible?

- A) 2 Monday morning; 1 stress-testing Monday–Tuesday; 3 drafted Tuesday, submitted Wednesday; 4 scheduled Wednesday with an agenda requested first; 5 prepared Thursday afternoon after the committee.
- B) 1 all day Monday and Tuesday; 3 Wednesday; 2 Thursday after the committee; 4 and 5 next week.
- C) 3 first — the director general outranks all else; then 1; 2 Friday; delegate 5; decline 4.
- D) 4 first, since unspecified commercial matters are usually serious; then 1, 3; 2 and 5 as time allows.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The discriminating item is (2), and it is placed first deliberately. A trusted portfolio manager signalling crisis is both time-critical — resignation decisions crystallise fast, and a person in difficulty who waits four days for their leader has learned what they rank — and high-stakes: your role's explicit duties include building and leading a motivated team and line management; losing a strong portfolio manager costs more portfolio capability than any single meeting this week creates. An hour Monday morning is the cheapest highest-return slot on the board (option B's Thursday slot for her is the classic executive failure: people problems deferred behind content problems until they convert to exit interviews). Item (1) then rightly dominates Monday–Tuesday: a £30m allocation whose top rankings are untested is not ready for a committee that will probe exactly there — stress-testing your own recommendation before others do is the difference between presenting analysis and defending hope. Item (3) needs a considered view by Wednesday: drafting Tuesday allows overnight distance before submission — machinery-of-government positions, once given, are quoted. Item (4): an unspecified "commercial matter" gets scheduled with an agenda requested — walking unprepared into a supplier conversation that may be a claim, a renegotiation opener or a fishing expedition surrenders the information advantage (option D's urgency-by-vagueness is how account directors set agendas). Item (5) is prepared Thursday after the committee — late but genuinely prepared, because coaching twelve portfolio managers on stale content wastes twelve people's Friday; note it is neither delegated (option C — the community session's value is you) nor dropped. The head-level pattern: people first when people are at risk, then the decision with the largest irreversible consequence, then positions that become commitments — and never let vagueness or rank jump the queue.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Pattern in the investment pipeline**

Analysing three years of investment decisions, you find: bids sponsored by directorates with a seat on the investment committee were approved at 71%; bids from directorates without a seat, 34% — with business-case quality scores (independently assessed) statistically similar across both groups. Committee members, shown the figures, respond: "We fund the strongest cases; seated directorates simply understand our standards better." What is the most defensible reading?

- A) The members' explanation is confirmed: familiarity with standards explains the gap.
- B) With case quality controlled and a 37-point approval gap remaining, the evidence indicates a structural advantage for represented directorates — advocacy in the room, informal pre-socialisation — that quality differences cannot explain; the members' account, offered without evidence, is a hypothesis the data already undermines.
- C) Unseated directorates should be told to write better cases.
- D) The gap is acceptable if the funded work delivers.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The stem's crucial clause is the control: independently assessed case quality is similar across groups, which is precisely the variable the members' explanation requires to differ. "They understand our standards better" predicts higher-quality submissions from seated directorates; the assessment says otherwise; so the offered account is not merely unevidenced but contradicted by the one measurement designed to test it. What remains to explain 71% versus 34% is what differs between the groups: presence — a sponsor in the room to advocate, clarify, and pre-socialise, and the relational capital of repeated committee membership. That is a structural bias in an allocation mechanism, and option B names it with the right epistemics (the data "indicates"; the mechanism is inferred). Option C prescribes for the disproven diagnosis — coaching better cases from directorates whose cases are already equivalent treats the symptom the data just excluded. Option D changes the question: downstream delivery success cannot validate an upstream allocation that systematically under-funds unrepresented bids — the unfunded counterfactuals never get to demonstrate anything. The head-level consequences are concrete and yours to own: allocation credibility is portfolio infrastructure, and a 37-point representation gap, once visible, is a finding you take to the committee with redesign options — blind first-round scoring, standing advocacy for unseated bids, rotating membership. The deeper discipline is hearing "we fund the strongest cases" for what it is when the controls say otherwise: the system explaining itself to itself. Evaluating whether current mechanisms meet business requirements is your strategic-thinking duty — and mechanisms rarely self-report their biases.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Deduction through a delegation design**

You are drafting delegation limits. Proposed rules: (i) portfolio managers may approve programme-level changes with cost impact up to £250k; (ii) changes above £250k, or any change affecting a statutory deadline regardless of cost, require head of portfolio approval; (iii) any single programme may receive at most three delegated approvals per quarter — the fourth and subsequent changes in a quarter, regardless of size, come to you. A programme submits, in one quarter: a £180k change (its fourth change that quarter, no statutory impact). Under the rules as drafted, who approves — and what behavioural risk does rule (iii) create that the drafting should anticipate?

- A) The portfolio manager approves — £180k is within delegation; rule (iii) is advisory.
- B) You approve — rule (iii) routes any fourth-in-quarter change to you regardless of size; and the rule predictably incentivises change-bundling: teams packaging several small changes into one submission to preserve delegation headroom, so the design needs a companion rule about bundled changes or aggregate quarterly cost.
- C) You approve, and the behavioural risk is that portfolio managers will stop approving anything.
- D) Nobody can approve it this quarter; the change must wait.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The mechanical half first: rule (iii) is drafted as mandatory routing ("come to you"), not guidance — a fourth-in-quarter change of any size escalates, so the £180k change is yours to approve despite sitting within the £250k limit; options A (demoting the rule to advisory) and D (inventing a prohibition where the rule creates a routing) both misread the text, and this much any careful portfolio manager should get. The head-level half is anticipating how the rule will reshape behaviour, because every threshold teaches the governed to manage against it: a frequency cap on approvals makes approval slots scarce, and scarce slots get economised — teams will bundle four genuine changes into one submission, preserving headroom while degrading the change record's granularity (one approval now covers four decisions, and the audit trail blurs exactly where rule (iii) sought scrutiny). Well-known cousins of this effect: invoice-splitting under spend thresholds — which is why procurement rules ban disaggregation explicitly — and its mirror image, aggregation, under frequency caps. The competent drafting response is a companion provision: bundled submissions count per constituent change, or a quarterly aggregate-cost trigger operates alongside the frequency trigger. Option C predicts paralysis, which frequency caps don't cause — they cause packaging. The discipline this trains is the designer's second question, the one that separates writing rules from designing controls: after "what does this rule require?", always "what will this rule teach people to do?" Ensuring the right controls are used means ensuring the controls survive contact with the incentives they create.

**Question 7 (hard) — Error checking a benefits claim across artefacts**

Three artefacts describe Programme Lune's benefits. Business case (approved two years ago): £6m annual benefits from month 30, comprising £4.2m staff-time savings and £1.8m reduced error costs. Latest programme report: "On track for full £6m benefits; staff-time savings validated by time-and-motion study." Finance's benefits ledger: £1.9m annualised benefits banked to date, all from error-cost reduction; staff-time savings "recognised in principle; not yet cash-releasing — headcount unchanged." The programme is at month 27. Which reading should govern your report to leadership?

- A) The programme's "on track for £6m" stands: the study validated the savings.
- B) The artefacts agree £1.9m is real; the contested £4.2m is time saved but not released — staff are doing other work, headcount and budgets unchanged — so the honest position is: £1.9m cash-releasing benefits banked (error costs, exceeding their £1.8m target), £4.2m of efficiency claims validated as time but not yet converted to cash or capacity value, and the conversion mechanism (role redesign, headcount plan, or redeployment accounting) is the open question leadership should press.
- C) Finance's ledger is authoritative; report benefits at £1.9m and the £4.2m as failed.
- D) Split the difference and report £4m as a prudent midpoint.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The three artefacts are not contradicting each other about facts — they are measuring different things, and the head-level skill is naming the seam. The time-and-motion study can be perfectly sound: staff genuinely save the hours. Finance's ledger can be simultaneously right: no budget line has shrunk, because saved hours diffused into other work rather than converting to headcount reduction, redeployment against funded demand, or output increase — the classic fate of efficiency benefits, and the reason "validated" and "cash-releasing" are different claims. So option A over-reports (the study validates time, not money), option C over-corrects ("failed" is false — the time is real and the error-cost line over-delivered at £1.9m against £1.8m), and option D — the midpoint — is the seductive disgrace: £4m corresponds to no state of the world; prudence means representing uncertainty honestly, not averaging incompatible claims into a number nobody defends. Option B's structure is the reporting standard your office should enforce portfolio-wide: decompose by benefit type, state each type's evidential status (banked and cash-releasing; validated-in-principle; unevidenced), and surface the conversion mechanism as the decision point — because whether £4.2m of diffuse time-savings becomes real value is a management choice (role redesign, establishment reduction, formal redeployment) that leadership must own or explicitly decline. At month 27 of a month-30 promise, that question is urgent, not academic. Benefits realisation lives or dies at the seam between operational fact and financial recognition; heads of portfolio who report the seam give leaders decisions, and those who report a single number give them comfort.

**Question 8 (hard) — Multi-constraint reasoning: the team design problem**

You are assigning four portfolio managers (PMs) to lead relationships with four programme clusters for the coming year. Constraints: (i) the Justice cluster requires its PM to hold current security clearance — only PM Asha and PM Dan hold it; (ii) PM Bella led the Health cluster for the past two years, and your rotation policy bars a third consecutive year on the same cluster; (iii) the Borders cluster's programme director has a documented unresolved conflict with PM Dan — pairing them is excluded; (iv) PM Carl is six months into the role and, by your development design, must be paired with the cluster whose governance is most mature — which is Health. Which assignment satisfies all constraints?

- A) Asha–Justice, Bella–Borders, Carl–Health, Dan–Corporate
- B) Dan–Justice, Bella–Corporate, Carl–Health, Asha–Borders
- C) Asha–Justice, Bella–Health, Carl–Corporate, Dan–Borders
- D) Both A and B satisfy all constraints; the choice between them is a judgement call on development and succession grounds.

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Solve systematically. Constraint (iv) fixes Carl–Health immediately, which also satisfies (ii) by displacement — Bella cannot have Health anyway. Constraint (i) restricts Justice to Asha or Dan. Test Asha–Justice: remaining PMs Bella and Dan take Borders and Corporate; (iii) bars Dan–Borders, forcing Dan–Corporate and Bella–Borders — exactly option A, all constraints checked: clearance ✓, rotation ✓ (Bella to Borders), conflict avoided ✓, Carl–Health ✓. Test Dan–Justice: remaining Asha and Bella take Borders and Corporate; (iii) is moot (Dan is placed), so either works — Bella–Corporate with Asha–Borders is option B: clearance ✓ (Dan cleared), rotation ✓, conflict ✓, Carl–Health ✓. Both A and B are fully feasible; option C fails on inspection (Bella–Health breaches rotation; Dan–Borders breaches the conflict exclusion). So the constraint set does not determine the answer — option D, and its second clause is the actual head-level content: when feasibility leaves a choice, the decision moves to unmodelled criteria, and naming them is your job. Here they are real: Asha–Justice keeps your most experienced cleared PM on the security-sensitive cluster but leaves Dan's conflict unaddressed by structure (he simply avoids Borders another year); Dan–Justice invests his clearance, gives Asha the Borders relationship — perhaps rebuilding cross-cluster depth — and buys time to resolve the conflict properly rather than route around it permanently. A leader who says "the constraints force this" when they don't has delegated judgement to a spreadsheet; one who says "two designs are feasible; here is why I choose this one" is doing the job. Building a skilled, motivated team is a constraint problem exactly until it isn't — and knowing where the arithmetic stops and leadership begins is the question's point.

**Question 9 (hard) — The leading indicator**

You track a basket of portfolio health indicators. Over four months: milestone delivery holds steady at 82–85%; spend tracks profile within 3%; but three quieter series move together — voluntary attrition among delivery-team staff rises from 8% to 14% annualised; average time-to-fill vacancies rises from 6 to 11 weeks; and the proportion of programme risks flagged as "resource-related" doubles from 12% to 24%. Programme RAG ratings remain unchanged. What is the strongest interpretation for your leadership insight report?

- A) Delivery is fine — the primary indicators (milestones, spend) are stable, and the people metrics belong to HR.
- B) The portfolio is consuming its resilience: three independent people-capacity series deteriorating in concert are a leading indicator, and stable milestones alongside rising resource risk flags suggest teams are currently absorbing the strain — so delivery metrics will follow with a lag, and the insight for leaders is a forecast ("expect delivery deterioration in six to nine months absent intervention on retention and recruitment"), not a reassurance.
- C) The attrition rise is within normal market variation; revisit next quarter.
- D) RAG ratings are unchanged, so programmes themselves see no problem; defer to their assessment.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The question tests the difference between reporting the present and providing insight into the future — which your role description names explicitly. Milestones and spend are lagging indicators: they record what the portfolio's capacity already accomplished. The three moving series are leading: attrition raises load on remaining staff; lengthening time-to-fill converts each departure into months of gap; doubled resource-risk flags are the programmes themselves reporting the strain in the only channel that doesn't yet trigger scrutiny. Their concert matters — three independent measurement routes agreeing is how weak signals earn strong conclusions — and the combination of stable output with rising strain flags has a specific meaning: absorption. Teams are covering gaps with overtime, deferred non-critical work, and the goodwill that attrition itself is spending; absorption has a horizon, and when it ends, milestone metrics move late and fast. Option B converts this into what leaders can actually use: a dated forecast with named intervention levers — precisely "insights into the portfolio's future so they can make informed decisions". Option A commits two head-level sins in one sentence: primary/secondary indicator confusion (lagging metrics ranked above leading ones) and boundary abdication (people capacity is delivery capacity; "belongs to HR" outsources your portfolio's core dependency). Option C treats each series alone — where any one might indeed be noise — and thereby discards the correlation that is the entire signal. Option D defers to RAG ratings whose stability the strain data explains: programmes rate delivery, and delivery hasn't broken yet; that is what "leading indicator" means. Foresight is the head of portfolio's distinctive product — anyone can report that the milestones were met.

**Question 10 (hard) — Prioritisation under a funding cut**

Mid-year, your portfolio's budget is cut 15% (£4.5m of £30m), effective in one quarter. Analysis of the four discretionary candidates for reduction: Programme W — £2m remaining-year cost, delivers statutory compliance capability eight weeks before its legal deadline; Programme X — £2.5m remaining-year cost, 75% complete, benefits £3m/yr starting in five months; Programme Y — £2.5m remaining-year cost, 15% complete, benefits £5m/yr starting in two years, no dependencies; Programme Z — £2m remaining-year cost, delivers the shared data platform that X's final integration (worth £0.8m of X's benefits) and two next-year initiatives depend on. Which reduction package is most defensible?

- A) Cut W and Z (£4m) plus trim £0.5m of Y — the two cheapest programmes take the burden.
- B) Cut Y (£2.5m) and find £2m through a negotiated descope of Z's platform to the minimum X and the committed next-year initiatives require — protecting the statutory programme absolutely, the near-complete benefits engine, and the dependency spine, while the least-committed, furthest-horizon investment pauses.
- C) Cut X and W (£4.5m exactly) — a clean match to the target.
- D) Spread 15% cuts evenly across all four programmes.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Establish the hierarchy the numbers impose. W is near-untouchable: statutory compliance with eight weeks of margin — cutting it converts a funding decision into a legal breach decision, which is not within a portfolio's gift to make casually (option A cuts it; option C cuts it while also cancelling X, the portfolio's best near-term value, at 75% complete with £3m/yr five months away — "clean arithmetic" selecting the two worst candidates). D — the even spread — is the abdication option: it treats prioritisation as unnecessary, imposes 15% pain on a statutory deadline and a programme in its final integration phase (where cuts bite disproportionately), and signals to leadership that the portfolio cannot rank its own contents; salami cuts are what portfolios do when their heads won't decide. Option B does the ranking the role exists for: Y pauses — not because it lacks value (£5m/yr is the portfolio's largest prize) but because it is the least irreversible choice: 15% sunk, no dependents, benefits two years out; a pause costs delay, not destruction, and repauses cleanly if funding returns. The residual £2m comes from Z via descope rather than cancellation, because Z is the dependency spine: killing it strands £0.8m of X's benefits and two committed next-year initiatives — its effective value includes its dependents' — but "minimum viable platform for the committed dependents" is a negotiable scope, and negotiated descope is where the £2m lives without breaking the chain. Note what B preserves that the others don't: every pound of near-term benefit, the legal position, and the option value of Y's future. The head-level disciplines on display: irreversibility ranks above headline value in cut decisions; enablers carry their dependents' worth; statutory obligations are constraints, not candidates; and even spreading is a decision to make everything slightly fail. This is defining strategy under pressure — which is when it counts.

**Question 11 (hard) — The metric that improved**

A year after you introduced delivery-confidence targets into portfolio managers' performance objectives ("portfolio average confidence ≥75%"), the average stands at 81%, up from 69%. Independent evidence: milestone delivery is flat year-on-year; two programmes had late-stage surprises that their high confidence scores never foreshadowed; and confidence submissions now cluster tightly at 78–84%, where they used to range 45–90%. What is the soundest conclusion?

- A) The target worked: confidence is up 12 points.
- B) The evidence pattern — improved scores, unimproved delivery, vanished variance, and failed predictions — indicates the metric was captured by the incentive: once scored against a target, confidence stopped measuring delivery risk and started measuring compliance; the fix is to remove confidence from individual performance objectives, restore it as a forecasting tool scored on calibration (did 80% confidence events happen ~80% of the time?), and find delivery incentives that reward honest early warning.
- C) The two late surprises were unforeseeable; otherwise the system works.
- D) Raise the target to 85% to drive further improvement.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Assemble the four evidence strands, because each alone is survivable and together they are diagnostic. Scores rose 12 points while the thing scores claim to predict — delivery — didn't move: the measure has decoupled from its referent. The range collapsed from 45 points to 6: genuine risk varies enormously across programmes, so tight clustering just above the 75% target line is the signature of target-seeking, not of convergent delivery reality (variance is information; its disappearance under incentive is the tell this guide's senior sibling calls the ritual number, now industrialised). And the two late surprises are the system failing at its only job — confidence scores exist to foreshadow exactly those events, and theirs didn't. This is Goodhart's law running at portfolio scale: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure — and the head-level twist is that you built the incentive, so the question doubles as a self-implication test. Option B carries the full response: diagnosis, mechanism, and the redesign — de-couple the metric from individual reward (people report honestly when honesty isn't taxed), rescore it on calibration, which is the one property a forecast can be honestly held to, and move performance incentives to behaviours that improve delivery rather than its self-description, such as early flagging of risks that later materialised. Option A reads the corrupted instrument at face value. Option C excuses the failed predictions individually, ignoring the pattern that explains them. Option D is the escalation of error: a higher target applied to a captured metric buys more capture — scores will cluster at 86–88 within two quarters, and the next surprises will be foreshadowed by nothing. Your duty to ensure the right controls are used includes retiring your own control when the evidence shows it now manufactures the signal it was meant to detect.

**Question 12 (hard) — The cascading commitment**

At the executive board, the chief operating officer proposes committing the organisation publicly to "all twelve citizen-facing services meeting the top accessibility standard within eighteen months". Your portfolio data: five services already comply; four can comply within 12–15 months inside existing plans; three run on a legacy platform whose accessibility remediation is technically unbounded — the platform's own replacement completes in 24 months, after which compliance is straightforward. The COO turns to you in the meeting: "Portfolio can deliver that, yes?" What is the most effective response?

- A) "Yes" — the ambition is right, the majority is achievable, and the details can be managed later.
- B) "No" — three services cannot make eighteen months, so the commitment is impossible.
- C) "Nine of twelve, yes, within the window. The remaining three sit on the legacy platform that replatforms at month 24 — for those, remediation before replacement would be spent twice. I'd support committing publicly to nine by eighteen months and all twelve by twenty-six, or alternatively fund interim assisted-digital provision for the three — I can bring the costed options within a week."
- D) "I'll need to take that away and come back in due course."

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The moment is engineered to test whether precision survives the room: a public commitment is being priced in real time, your data is loaded, and the socially fluent answers are the wrong ones at both ends. Option A mortgages the organisation's public word against three services you know cannot comply in-window — and note the mechanics of how that failure arrives: eighteen months from now the miss is public, the "yes" is minuted, and the remediation options will be exactly the ones available today, minus eighteen months. Option B is accurate and useless — "impossible" hands the board a wall where your data actually contains a bridge, and heads of portfolio who only ever say no stop being asked. Option D defers what your data can answer now; taken-away answers to in-meeting questions spend credibility too, and the commitment may be drafted before you come back. Option C is the executive-grade answer, and its anatomy is worth internalising: verified fact first (nine achievable — five done, four in-plan), the constraint named with its reason (double-spend on a dying platform — a value-for-money argument the board can weigh, not a technical mystification), and then the move that distinguishes counsel from commentary: two committable alternatives, both honest — the phased public commitment, or the interim-provision fund that buys the three services' users something real while the platform completes — with a dated offer of costed detail. The COO gets ambition that survives its own announcement; the board gets a decision instead of a dilemma; the three services' users get either a truthful date or interim help rather than a broken promise. Influencing and negotiating with senior stakeholders, as your framework has it, is rarely about resisting them — it is about giving their ambition a load-bearing structure in the thirty seconds after "yes?" This is also communicating complex recommendations at strategic level in its purest form: fact, constraint, options, offer — four sentences, in the room.

### Preparation tips

- **Practise on your own instruments.** Your escalation flows, delegation rules, indicator baskets and incentive designs are this section's question bank made real. Run the checks: what do my silences hide, what do my thresholds teach, which of my metrics has stopped varying, which average conceals my largest risk?
- **Drill the designer's second question.** For every rule or target you review this month: first "what does it require?", then "what will it teach people to do?" Control design fails at the second question far more often than the first.
- **Rehearse decomposition before aggregation.** Any average, total or net figure crossing your desk: decompose by size, by direction, by evidential status — then decide what the aggregate may honestly say. The executive pack is a lens; keep it ground true.
- **Time-box strategic answers.** The in-meeting "portfolio can deliver that, yes?" moment rewards prepared structures: fact, constraint, options, dated offer. Practise compressing a loaded position into four sentences — it is a trainable skill, not a temperament.
- **Distinguish forced from chosen.** When constraints leave multiple feasible answers — team designs, cut packages, schedules — say so, and name the judgement criteria that decide. "The analysis forces this" is only ever true when it does.
- **Audit your leading indicators quarterly.** If everything you report is lagging, you are reporting history. Build and rehearse the strain-signal basket — attrition, time-to-fill, resource-risk flags, correction rates — until forecasting deterioration feels as natural as recording it.
- **Review errors as the norm-setter.** Your team calibrates against your reasoning. When practice reveals a gap — a promoted possibility, a misread modal, a comfortable aggregate — name it aloud in your next team session. Leaders who audit themselves publicly license rigour in everyone else.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Reading your inbox as the portfolio.** What escalates to you is filtered by authority, fear and habit. The categories that never arrive need auditing precisely because they never arrive.
- **Signing the average that inverts the story.** Unweighted means over unequal programmes, netted variances, headline trajectories that hide the largest mover — aggregation choices are editorial choices; make them consciously and disclose them.
- **Accepting systems' self-explanations.** "We fund the strongest cases" from the committee whose bias is being measured, "unforeseeable" from the process that failed to foresee — when the controls contradict the account, the account is the hypothesis, not the data.
- **Designing rules without modelling the response.** Thresholds invite splitting, frequency caps invite bundling, scored metrics invite clustering. Every control reshapes behaviour; anticipate the reshaping or inherit it.
- **Ranking lagging indicators above leading ones.** Milestones and spend record the past. Attrition, vacancy lag and strain flags forecast the future — and forecasting is your distinctive duty.
- **Letting arithmetic make leadership's choices.** Feasibility analysis narrows; it rarely decides. Where multiple designs survive the constraints, the criteria that choose are yours to name and own.
- **Answering the room instead of the record.** In-meeting pressure optimises for the fluent yes, the safe no, or the deferral. The answer that serves is the one still true when the commitment matures — build it from fact, constraint and options, and give it in the room.
