## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the reasoning your role demands, presented through practical materials rather than abstract puzzles. At senior level the materials are messier and the reasoning less mechanical. You will meet evidence supporting two incompatible readings, designs with a flaw that compromises some conclusions but not others, prioritisation problems with no dominant option, and claims made by other people that need diagnosing rather than accepting or rejecting.

The format is typically an online test of fifteen to thirty minutes covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective against a norm group of comparable seniority — so your comparison set is other experienced practitioners. Adaptive platforms raise difficulty after correct answers, and acuity reports distinguish speed from accuracy, both of which reward precision over pace.

Three things distinguish this section at your level.

The first is the shift from producing sound reasoning to auditing it. Your role names advising teams on how to design inclusively, helping teams use design hypotheses effectively, and building consensus by asking difficult questions and challenging assumptions. Each is a diagnostic act performed on somebody else's thinking, frequently under time pressure and often where saying "that does not follow" carries a social cost.

The second is systemic reasoning. When something goes wrong repeatedly, the informative feature is usually the distribution of the failures rather than any individual failure. Concentrated in one team, under one condition, among experienced people? That is a process telling you it cannot be complied with. Scattered? That is something else. Reading distributions diagnostically is what separates senior practice from very competent practice.

The third is comfort with irreducible uncertainty. Several questions have no clean answer, only a best one, and the discriminating factor is whether you can identify the strongest reading of ambiguous evidence and state its limits rather than collapsing into confidence. Holding "worth acting on" and "not yet established" together is what senior design judgement consists of.

You need to memorise nothing. What you need is the discipline not to let fluency substitute for examination.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The cognitive dimensions map onto the skills named in your role summary:

- **Auditing evidence and reasoning** maps to **Evidence-based design**, where you analyse, synthesise and clearly explain complex evidence such as large data sets, and help your team use design hypotheses effectively.
- **Challenging assumptions** maps to **Design communication**, where you build consensus around a design approach by asking difficult questions and challenging assumptions.
- **Systemic and risk reasoning** maps to **Designing strategically**, where you use risks, opportunities and constraints in technology, systems and policy to shape design.
- **Strategic prioritisation** maps to **Designing strategically** and to **Iterative design**, where you adapt designs quickly to changes in requirements, priorities or user needs.
- **Diagnosing exclusion** maps to **Designing for everyone**, where you advise teams on inclusive design and deliver ethical services that consider the personal and social context of users.
- **Reasoning about positions and incentives** maps to **Designing together**, where you work across team or profession boundaries, for example with policy teams.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Auditing a design against a standard**

Your organisation's standard requires every transactional page to: use words users would use, state what happens next, state how long it will take, avoid requesting information already held, offer a route to help, and work without JavaScript. You review a page that uses internal terminology, says the case will be assessed, gives no timescale, re-requests a held reference number, has no contact route, and degrades gracefully without JavaScript. How many requirements are unmet?

- A) 3
- B) 4
- C) 5
- D) 6

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Work through all six. Internal terminology fails the first. "The case will be assessed" satisfies the second, minimally. No timescale fails the third. Re-requesting held information fails the fourth. No contact route fails the fifth. Graceful degradation satisfies the sixth. Four unmet, giving B. Option C typically comes from marking "what happens next" as unmet because the statement is thin — a defensible reading, and the item is testing whether you apply the standard as written rather than as you would have written it, which is exactly the discipline assurance requires. Note also which two requirements are easiest to miss: the absent help route and the JavaScript behaviour, both because they concern something that should be present rather than something present and wrong. When auditing at scale, absences are the systematic blind spot.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction from a governance rule**

Your standard states: "A service may enter public beta only with sign-off from the service owner, unless it replaces an existing service on a like-for-like basis and has been assessed as low risk." A programme lead says: "We entered public beta last month without service owner sign-off." Which must be true?

- A) The standard was breached.
- B) It was a like-for-like replacement assessed as low risk.
- C) Either it was a low-risk like-for-like replacement, or the standard was breached.
- D) The programme should be paused.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A conditional with a stated exception, plus facts silent on the exception, licenses only the disjunction. Options A and B each assume a branch. Option D prescribes an action rather than stating what must be true, and the question asks the latter — a distinction worth noticing, because assessments at this level routinely offer a sensible action as a distractor in a logic item. Only C holds in every case. Note also that the exception is compound: like-for-like *and* low risk. A release satisfying only one does not qualify, and partial satisfaction of a compound exception is among the most common misreadings in real governance conversations. As the person likely to be advising on this, your opening move is to establish which route was taken, framed as an enquiry rather than a finding.

**Question 3 (moderate) — Diagnosing someone else's conclusion**

A designer you advise reports: "Users trust the new identity flow — all 18 participants said they were comfortable with it." Sessions were held in a government building, participants came from the departmental panel, and the comfort question was the facilitator's closing item. What is the most important observation?

- A) Eighteen participants is too small for a trust measure.
- B) The setting, the recruitment source and the direct closing question all bias in the same direction towards socially desirable responding, so unanimity is evidence about the conditions rather than about the flow.
- C) Comfort and trust are different constructs.
- D) A validated scale should have been used.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Unanimity is itself the alarm, and the strongest observation explains it. Each feature alone would warrant caution: a departmental panel selects for people already well disposed to the department, a government building primes deference, and a direct question from the facilitator at the close invites the answer the participant believes is wanted. Because all three push the same way they compound rather than partly cancel, and eighteen out of eighteen is close to what the conditions predict regardless of the flow. Option C is a legitimate secondary point that does not explain the inflation. Option A misidentifies validity as size — 180 participants under identical conditions would produce the same result. Option D is a technical fix leaving all three biases untouched. When you feed this back, name the compounding, because a designer who swaps in a validated scale while keeping the panel and the setting will produce the same finding with better packaging.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Strategic prioritisation across services**

You have responsibility across several services and can direct substantial design capacity to one this quarter:

1. Service A chooses a delivery model next month, committing four years.
2. Service B is live with a persistent 30% drop-off affecting 250,000 users a year.
3. Service C launches in six weeks with designs signed off.
4. Service D is preparing a business case due in nine months.

Which is strongest, and why?

- A) B, because it affects the most users.
- B) A, because the decision is imminent, commits four years, and design input is the only thing that could still change it — giving it much the highest value of information.
- C) D, because business cases shape long-term investment.
- D) C, because launching untested carries reputational risk.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Value of information is the right frame: how much does a decision change as a result of your input, and is there still time? Service A scores highest on both — a delivery model choice is exactly where design shifts the answer rather than decorating it, the window is open, and four years of commitment makes a wrong choice enormously expensive. Service B affects more users but has been stable for months and will remain so for three more, so deferral costs comparatively little and analytics may be the better first instrument. Service C is weakest despite sounding prudent: signed-off designs six weeks from launch means findings arrive with nowhere to go. Service D has nine months, and at your level the profile trap takes exactly this form — business cases attract attention, and attention is not decision leverage. Being able to say that to a programme director without sounding dismissive is the harder half of the skill.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Systemic risk in a channel decision**

A programme proposes closing a paper channel that carries 11% of a service's 300,000 annual applications, projecting a 25% processing saving. What is the most complete risk analysis?

- A) The risk is that 33,000 people cannot apply.
- B) The first-order risk is exclusion of about 33,000 people a year, but the second-order risks matter as much: those users will contact the service by other routes, raising contact centre volume and cost; some will apply late or not at all, shifting rather than removing cost; and if the paper cohort skews towards people with the greatest need, the policy outcome is affected as well as the operational metric — so the saving may be partly a transfer between budgets.
- C) There is no significant risk; 89% of users are unaffected.
- D) The projected saving is probably overstated.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The strongest analysis follows consequences beyond the first step, which is precisely what your role means by using risks and constraints in systems and policy to shape design. Removing a channel does not remove the people who used it: they telephone, they visit, they ask a relative, they apply late, or they do not apply. Each has a cost, and several land in budgets other than the one showing the saving — which is why option B's final clause matters, because a saving that is really a transfer will be defended by whoever books it and disputed by whoever absorbs it. The policy point is more important still: if paper applicants skew towards older users, users in crisis, or users with low digital confidence, the service is shedding cost by shedding the people it exists to serve. Option A stops at first order. Option C treats a majority as sufficient. Option D is narrower and follows from B.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Feedback on a colleague's synthesis**

A designer presents insight from eleven studies. One headline — "users want proactive notification across our services" — rests on material from three studies, all of services with statutory deadlines. What is the most useful feedback?

- A) The claim is invalid and should be removed.
- B) Note that the supporting evidence comes exclusively from deadline-driven services, so the claim over-reaches as written; suggest narrowing it to that service type, or testing whether it holds where there is no deadline, before it informs investment.
- C) Suggest adding more studies.
- D) Approve it; three studies is a reasonable basis.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The evidence is real and the sentence built on it reaches further than the evidence goes — the most common failure in synthesis, and one with financial consequences when the claim informs investment. Deadline-driven services supply an obvious and specific reason to want proactive notification: you are at risk of missing something. That motivation may not transfer to services used once, voluntarily, or where nothing can be missed. Option B preserves the finding, corrects the scope, and offers two proportionate routes. Option A discards something real. Option D lets an over-generalised claim justify spending across services it was never evidenced for. Option C reaches for the expensive remedy before the free one — narrowing the claim costs nothing. Your role names critiquing to assure best practice, and this is its texture: specific, fixable, framed so the designer can act without feeling dismantled.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Prototype fidelity for a complex idea**

You need to test whether a new cross-service concept — a single "tell us once" change of circumstances — makes sense to users, before any build commitment. You have three weeks. What is strongest?

- A) A high-fidelity interactive prototype of the full flow.
- B) A low-to-mid fidelity prototype focused on the concept and the moments where it touches other services, plus a simple service walkthrough that includes the offline and cross-organisational steps — because the question is whether the concept coheres across the journey, not whether an interface works.
- C) A written concept description discussed in interviews.
- D) A technical spike to establish feasibility first.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Match the artefact to the question. What you need to know is whether a cross-service concept makes sense to people — whether they believe it, whether they understand what happens to their information, whether the moments where it touches other organisations feel coherent. That is a journey question rather than an interface question, and a high-fidelity prototype of screens would answer a narrower question expensively while making participants comment on buttons. Option B keeps the fidelity low enough to change between sessions and, crucially, includes the offline and cross-organisational steps, which is where a "tell us once" concept actually succeeds or fails. Option C removes the concrete anchor that makes an abstract concept discussable. Option D answers a different and later question — feasibility matters, and establishing it before knowing whether the concept works for people is the wrong order.

**Question 8 (harder) — Scoping a flaw**

A study you are assuring used a prototype whose save-and-return function was broken throughout. Four findings: (i) participants could not locate the eligibility criteria; (ii) participants abandoned when interrupted; (iii) sessions took longer than expected; (iv) participants misread the declaration. Which are compromised?

- A) All four.
- B) None; the fault was incidental.
- C) Findings (ii) and (iii) are directly compromised, since inability to save and return plausibly produced both the abandonment on interruption and the extended durations, while (i) and (iv) concern comprehension of content and are largely unaffected.
- D) Only (ii).

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The skill is scoping precisely rather than treating a flaw as total or trivial. A broken save function makes interruption catastrophic rather than inconvenient, so (ii) is manufactured by the artefact; it also removes a natural pause, plausibly extending sessions, so (iii) is compromised. Findings (i) and (iv) concern locating and understanding content, which the fault does not touch. Option A discards two sound findings and, when you are the person embedding good practice, does real damage: a senior designer who condemns wholesale teaches teams that any admitted fault invalidates everything, which is a direct incentive to conceal faults. Option B leaves two artefactual findings circulating, where they will justify fixes to problems that do not exist. Option D catches the obvious compromise and misses the duration one.

**Question 9 (harder) — Reading a good number for the problem inside it**

Analytics show 40% of users who abandon at the evidence step return within seven days and complete. The team treats this as a success and focuses on the 60% who do not return. What is the strongest observation?

- A) Agree; the 60% is the real problem.
- B) The 40% are also evidence of a problem — they are making two or more attempts at something that should take one, at a cost in time and repeat contact — and the returners are a natural experiment: whatever enabled them to come back and succeed may be the mechanism that would help the 60%.
- C) The 40% should be excluded from the drop-off figure.
- D) Send reminders to everyone who abandons.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This is opportunity reasoning, which your role names explicitly. The team has read a number as a success because it ends well, and missed that a returning user is a user who could not finish first time — almost always because a document was not to hand. That is a design problem with a design answer: tell people what they will need before they start, or make saving and resuming genuinely easy. The more valuable half of option B is the natural experiment: something let the returners come back and succeed, and identifying it is likely faster and cheaper than studying the failures from scratch. Option A accepts the framing. Option C is a reporting choice that would conceal the phenomenon. Option D leaps to one intervention without the cause, and a reminder does not help someone who cannot obtain the document.

**Question 10 (harder) — Advising a team that is measuring the wrong thing**

A team reports success because average handling time on a form fell from 14 to 9 minutes after a redesign. Contact centre calls about the same service rose 30% over the same period. What is the strongest analysis?

- A) The redesign succeeded; handling time is the agreed metric.
- B) The two movements are plausibly connected: a shorter form may be shorter because it explains less, pushing users to telephone for the explanation — so the apparent efficiency may be a cost transfer, and the right measure is end-to-end effort including contact, not time on the form.
- C) The contact centre increase must have another cause.
- D) Both metrics should be reported without interpretation.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Two metrics moving in opposite directions at the same time is a prompt to ask whether they are connected, and here there is an obvious mechanism: content removed to shorten a form is content a user then has to obtain some other way. Option B names the mechanism and, more importantly, names the measurement error underneath it — optimising time on a single step while ignoring what happens around it is precisely the failure service design exists to prevent, and it will keep recurring as long as the metric is scoped to the form. Option A accepts a metric because it is the agreed one, which is how a service gets locally optimised into a worse whole. Option C asserts an alternative cause without evidence, and may well be right, which is why the correct move is to investigate rather than assume either way. Option D abdicates the interpretation your role exists to supply.

**Question 11 (harder) — Reading the distribution of failures**

Reviewing a year of work across teams, you find that accessibility problems reached live in four services. All four were in one directorate, all under ministerial deadline pressure, all designed by experienced practitioners. What is the strongest response?

- A) Report the four cases and require remediation.
- B) Treat the concentration as diagnostic: four failures in one directorate under identical pressure by experienced people points at a process that cannot be complied with when time is short — investigate what accessibility assurance actually demands and how long it takes, propose a fast route, and remediate the four cases alongside.
- C) Remind all designers of the accessibility requirements.
- D) Introduce a mandatory accessibility gate for every service.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The distribution is the most informative feature. Scattered failures by different people would indicate individual conduct or capability; four concentrated under identical pressure by experienced practitioners indicate a process that does not survive a deadline. Option B investigates the mechanism and fixes it, while the closing clause preserves the point that a systemic diagnosis is not a reason to leave the individual failures unremediated — real users are affected now. Option A handles the instances and guarantees recurrence. Option C is the weakest available response to any compliance failure: people who knew the requirement and missed it under pressure will not change on being told again. Option D imposes friction on every compliant service to catch a localised failure, which is how organisational burden accumulates and how the next workaround gets invented.

**Question 12 (harder) — Working across a profession boundary**

A policy team has designed an eligibility rule requiring evidence that around a fifth of eligible users will struggle to obtain. They are defensive about it, having spent months on the policy. What is the strongest approach?

- A) Present the research showing the difficulty and ask them to change the rule.
- B) Establish first what the rule is protecting against — most likely a specific fraud or error risk — then bring evidence on both sides: how many eligible users the current evidence requirement excludes, and whether an alternative form of evidence would address the same risk; and frame the conversation as jointly solving their problem rather than removing their rule.
- C) Escalate to the service owner.
- D) Design the best possible interface for the existing requirement.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The defensiveness is information: they have invested months and expect to be told their work is wrong. The productive move is to change what the conversation is about. A rule requiring particular evidence exists to manage a risk, and once that risk is named the question becomes which instrument manages it best — which is a design question you can contribute to rather than a policy question you can only object to. Option B also brings the exclusion number, because a fifth of eligible users is an argument and "some people find it hard" is not. Option A asks them to abandon their work on the strength of your evidence alone, which is both unlikely to succeed and unnecessary. Option C escalates a solvable disagreement and costs you a working relationship across a boundary you will need repeatedly. Option D gives up on the substance and polishes the exclusion.

### Preparation tips

- **Read outputs for the study behind them.** Practise inferring recruitment, setting and question order from what a report says and omits.
- **Scope flaws precisely.** Name which claims a fault compromises and which survive; wholesale condemnation encourages concealment.
- **Follow consequences beyond first order.** Removing a channel does not remove the people who used it.
- **Read the distribution of failures.** Concentrated non-compliance diagnoses a process; scattered non-compliance diagnoses conduct.
- **Ask what a rule is protecting against.** It converts a policy objection into a design conversation.
- **Look for metrics moving in opposite directions.** A local optimisation is usually a cost transfer.
- **Frame prioritisation as value of information.** Decision leverage and remaining time, not importance or profile.
- **Read a good number for the problem inside it.** A 40% return rate is a 40% failure-first-time rate.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Treating effect size as causal certainty.** A large gap can be entirely confounded.
- **Missing absent requirements when auditing.** What is not there is systematically harder to see.
- **Applying a standard as you would have written it.** Assurance means applying it as written.
- **Reaching for reminders or training as the answer to a systemic problem.** Diagnose the mechanism first.
- **Adding friction for the compliant majority.** How burden accumulates and workarounds are born.
- **Accepting a metric because it is the agreed one.** Agreed metrics get locally optimised into worse wholes.
- **Reading a partially satisfied compound exception as satisfied.** "Like-for-like and low risk" requires both.
- **Asking a policy team to abandon a rule.** Ask what it protects against instead.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A numeric reasoning assessment measures how accurately you handle quantitative material. At senior level the emphasis shifts decisively from calculating to interrogating. You will still need to compute correctly, but the harder items ask whether a figure means what somebody claims — which is, not coincidentally, the question you are frequently the only person in the room positioned to ask.

The format is typically a timed online test of fifteen to thirty minutes, each item presenting a table, chart or paragraph of figures with a multiple-choice question. Calculators are usually provided and the arithmetic is deliberately tractable. Time per item is tight, so the operative skill is locating what matters and disregarding what does not.

The professional stake is substantial. Quantitative evidence carries unearned authority: a number in a board paper is treated as fact while a design argument is treated as a view, regardless of the rigour behind either. Your role names analysing and explaining complex evidence such as large data sets, and working with service managers and programme directors — people whose world is expressed in numbers. Senior designers who cannot interrogate a figure confidently lose arguments they should win, and occasionally win arguments they should lose. Being able to ask "what is the denominator, and who could not have appeared in this data?" is a large part of the value you add at that table.

Four habits carry most of the load. Establish what the question asks before looking at the data. Identify the denominator and the population it represents before calculating. Estimate, calculate, compare. And sanity-check against the world — a figure implying that 40% of users complete a form in under three seconds is measuring something other than what it claims.

Distractors are constructed from a predictable catalogue: wrong denominator, percentage confused with percentage point, new value used as the base in a percentage change, rates averaged without weighting, vendor or benchmark metrics compared to internal ones without checking definitions. Recognising your answer among the options proves nothing; knowing the catalogue lets you see the trap being laid.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The numerical skills tested map onto your named skills:

- **Analysing complex evidence** maps to **Evidence-based design**, where you analyse, synthesise and clearly explain complex evidence such as large data sets.
- **Comparing rates between groups** maps to **Designing for everyone**, where gaps between groups are the first numerical trace of exclusion.
- **Judging what a measurement can establish** maps to **Evidence-based design**, where you help teams use design hypotheses effectively.
- **Quantifying risk and opportunity** maps to **Designing strategically**, where risks and constraints shape design and where scale determines what matters.
- **Presenting figures at defensible strength** maps to **Design communication**, where you document and communicate decisions, risks and unresolved issues.
- **Interrogating others' claims** maps to **Design communication** again, where you build consensus by asking difficult questions and challenging assumptions.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Rate from counts**

Across a programme, 36 of 48 participants completed the core task unaided. What percentage is that?

- A) 66%
- B) 72%
- C) 75%
- D) 80%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 36 ÷ 48 = 0.75, so 75%, and the answer is C. Estimate first: 36 of 48 is three quarters, since 24 would be half. Options A and B come from dividing by 54 and 50. Even here, note the prior question you would ask in real work: aggregating 48 participants across a programme into a single rate is meaningful only if the studies tested comparable tasks with comparable populations. If they did not, the figure is arithmetically correct and referentially empty — a distinction you will spend a good deal of time enforcing in other people's work.

**Question 2 (easy) — Drop-off against the right base**

A step is reached by 7,200 users and passed by 5,400. What is the drop-off rate?

- A) 25%
- B) 33%
- C) 75%
- D) 30%

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The drop-off is 7,200 − 5,400 = 1,800, measured against those who reached the step: 1,800 ÷ 7,200 = 0.25, or 25%, giving A. Option B is 1,800 ÷ 5,400, dividing by those who continued — the most common error in journey analytics, and one that overstates every drop-off it touches. The rule is that a drop-off rate is measured against arrivals. This matters most when steps are compared: if two steps are computed with different denominators, the ranking of where design effort should go can be simply wrong, and at your level you are frequently the person adjudicating that ranking.

**Question 3 (easy) — Disaggregated rates**

| Group | Users | Completed |
|---|---|---|
| Digitally confident | 6,000 | 5,400 |
| Low digital confidence | 3,000 | 1,500 |
| Assistive technology users | 1,000 | 250 |

What is the overall completion rate, and the gap between highest and lowest group rates?

- A) 60% overall; 50 point gap
- B) 71.5% overall; 65 point gap
- C) 71.5% overall; 50 point gap
- D) 65% overall; 65 point gap

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Completions total 5,400 + 1,500 + 250 = 7,150, across 6,000 + 3,000 + 1,000 = 10,000 users, so 71.5% overall. Group rates are 90%, 50% and 25%, giving a gap of 65 points. Hence B. The substantive point sits behind the arithmetic: the overall figure of 71.5% describes no group in the data, and reporting it alone conceals that assistive technology users fail three times in four. Whenever an aggregate can be disaggregated it should be, and at your level advising teams on inclusive design largely means insisting that reporting is disaggregated by the groups most likely to be failing.

**Question 4 (easy) — Centre and spread**

Eleven sessions produced task times in minutes: 6, 7, 8, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 14, 16, 71. Which summary is most defensible?

- A) Mean 15.6 minutes
- B) Median 10 minutes, range 6 to 71, with the 71-minute case described separately
- C) Median 10 minutes
- D) Mean 15.6 minutes, range 6 to 71

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The mean of about 15.6 exceeds nine of the eleven observations, so it represents almost nobody. The median is the sixth value, which is 10, and describes the group well. But the median alone conceals a session lasting more than four times the typical duration, and that session is very likely the most informative observation in the set. Option B supplies centre, spread and explicit treatment of the outlier. Option D pairs the range with a misleading centre. Option C buries the extreme case. The rule to enforce in the work you assure: a central figure never travels alone, and an outlier is a lead rather than noise.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Weighted aggregation**

Four studies: 10 participants at 50% completion; 20 at 65%; 10 at 70%; 20 at 80%. What is the overall rate across all 60 participants?

- A) 66%
- B) 68%
- C) 69%
- D) 71%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Convert each rate to counts. Study 1: 50% of 10 = 5. Study 2: 65% of 20 = 13. Study 3: 70% of 10 = 7. Study 4: 80% of 20 = 16. Total completions 5 + 13 + 7 + 16 = 41, across 60 participants, so 41 ÷ 60 = 0.683, which is approximately 68% and the answer is B. Option A is the unweighted mean of 50, 65, 70 and 80, which is 66.25 and over-weights the two smallest studies. Always return to counts. And ask the prior question: aggregating four studies is meaningful only if the task and population were comparable, and if they were not, the combined figure is a number without a referent.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Funnel denominators**

Funnel: 100,000 start, 75,000 pass identity, 45,000 upload evidence, 40,500 submit. What proportion of those who passed identity went on to submit?

- A) 40.5%
- B) 54%
- C) 60%
- D) 90%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The stem names users who passed identity, so the denominator is 75,000 and the part is 40,500: 40,500 ÷ 75,000 = 0.54, giving B. Option A uses 100,000, option C is the identity-to-upload rate, option D is the upload-to-submit rate. All are true of this funnel; one answers the question. Strategically the funnel also tells you where design effort belongs: 25,000 lost at identity and 30,000 between identity and upload, against 4,500 at submission. A team proposing to redesign submission would need a very strong reason, and being able to see that in fifteen seconds is what lets you redirect a programme's plan in the meeting rather than afterwards.

**Question 7 (moderate) — A benefits claim**

A benefits paper states that a redesign raised completion from 55% to 66%, describing this as "a 20% improvement". Is the description accurate?

- A) No; it is an 11% improvement.
- B) Yes as arithmetic — 11 ÷ 55 = 20% relative increase — but the expression is ambiguous, and the paper should say "20% relative increase" or "11 percentage points".
- C) No; it is 11 percentage points, and calling it 20% is wrong.
- D) No; it should be 16.7%.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The relative increase is 11 ÷ 55 = 0.2, so 20% is defensible arithmetic. The problem is labelling: "a 20% improvement" will be read by many readers as a rise from 55% to 75%. Option B captures both halves. Option C over-corrects into an error of its own, since relative change is a legitimate expression. Option A confuses the units and option D divides by the new value. At your level this is governance rather than arithmetic: benefits papers routinely select the flattering framing, usually without intent to mislead, and asking for both numbers as a standing expectation rather than a challenge to any individual is the cheapest control available.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Quantifying an exclusion**

A service receives 480,000 applications a year, 7% on paper. A programme proposes closing the paper channel. How many people a year would need another route?

- A) 3,360
- B) 33,600
- C) 34,300
- D) 336,000

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 7% of 480,000 is 0.07 × 480,000 = 33,600, giving B. Option A is 7% of 48,000, option D is 70%. The reason this belongs in a design guide rather than a maths one is what it does to a conversation: "7% of applications" sounds like a rounding error, and "33,600 people a year" does not. Converting a percentage into people takes five seconds and is one of the most effective moves available when arguing about a channel closure with a programme director. It also enables the next question — what happens to those 33,600, how many will telephone, how many will apply late, and how many will not apply at all — which is where the second-order cost analysis begins.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Reconstructing an implied value**

A business case claims a 4 percentage point completion improvement on 900,000 annual starts is worth £4.32 million a year. What is the implied value per additional completed application?

- A) £4.80
- B) £48
- C) £120
- D) £96

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A 4 percentage point improvement on 900,000 starts gives 0.04 × 900,000 = 36,000 additional completions. Dividing: £4,320,000 ÷ 36,000 = £120, so C. Option A divides by 900,000, valuing every start. The value of this calculation is that it takes thirty seconds and immediately tells you whether a business case is plausible — £120 per additional completed application may be reasonable for a high-value transaction and implausible for a simple information service. It also enables a far more effective challenge than disputing the total: "so we are valuing each additional completion at £120 — is that the right order?" is specific, answerable, and requires you to accuse nobody of anything.

**Question 10 (harder) — A metric that conflates populations**

A dashboard reports "average time to complete: 12 minutes" for a service your research shows most users taking over forty minutes across several sittings. What is the likely explanation and the right intervention?

- A) The dashboard is wrong and should be corrected.
- B) The metric almost certainly measures single sessions rather than end-to-end journeys, so multi-sitting completions are excluded or counted only for their final short visit, and abandoned sessions may be included — ask for the definition and the distribution, and propose an end-to-end measure alongside it.
- C) The research sample was atypical.
- D) The two figures cannot be reconciled.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Session-based analytics and journey-based reality diverge systematically for any service completed over more than one sitting, and the divergence always flatters the service. A user spending forty minutes across three visits typically appears as one short final session, and abandoned sessions may drag the mean lower still. The figure is probably calculated correctly and defined in a way that cannot answer the question people think it answers. Option B names the mechanism and proposes a constructive intervention — asking for a definition is not an accusation, and offering an end-to-end measure gives the metric owner something to build rather than something to defend. Option A picks a fight over a number that is not wrong, which is how senior designers acquire a reputation for obstruction. Option C blames the research. Option D forecloses a reconciliation that is available and strategically useful.

**Question 11 (harder) — Sampling adequacy**

A team proposes measuring whether a redesign improved completion by running 40 moderated sessions before and 40 after. Baseline is 58%; a 6 percentage point improvement is considered meaningful. What is the strongest response?

- A) Agree; 80 sessions is substantial.
- B) Explain that a 6 point difference on 40 per group is about two participants and well inside ordinary variation, so the comparison cannot detect it — recommend measuring the rate through live analytics across the full user base, and spending the 80 sessions on mechanisms, which moderated research does uniquely well.
- C) Increase to 60 per group.
- D) Run it and report the difference with caveats.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Forty per group cannot distinguish a six point difference from chance; reliable detection at that size needs several hundred per group. Option B says so and redirects rather than merely objecting, which is the substantive contribution: analytics measures rates across the whole population at negligible marginal cost, while moderated sessions are the only instrument that explains why a rate is what it is. Dividing the work along that line is the recommendation. Option A endorses a design that cannot answer its own question. Option C moves towards adequacy without reaching it while still misallocating scarce sessions. Option D is the most damaging at your level, because caveated findings from underpowered comparisons get cited without their caveats, and once your organisation establishes that forty-session comparisons can measure rates, you will be arguing against that precedent for years.

**Question 12 (harder) — Two metrics moving apart**

Average handling time on a form fell from 15 to 10 minutes after a redesign. Over the same period, contact centre calls about the service rose 28% and completion was unchanged. What is the strongest analysis?

- A) The redesign succeeded on its metric; the call increase needs separate investigation.
- B) The pattern is consistent with content having been removed to shorten the form, pushing users to telephone for the explanation — an apparent efficiency that is probably a cost transfer, with unchanged completion suggesting no benefit to users at all; the right measure is end-to-end effort including contact.
- C) The call increase must have another cause.
- D) Report both metrics without interpretation.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Three facts fit together: the form got shorter, calls rose sharply, and completion did not move. The most economical explanation is that content removed from the form is content users now obtain by telephone, which means the handling-time saving has been transferred rather than realised — and the unchanged completion rate is the decisive detail, because a genuine improvement to the form should have moved it. Option B names the mechanism and the measurement error underneath it: optimising one step while ignoring what happens around it is exactly the failure your discipline exists to prevent, and it will recur as long as the metric is scoped to the form. Option A accepts the metric because it was agreed, which is how services get locally optimised into worse wholes. Option C asserts an alternative cause without evidence. Option D abdicates the interpretation your role exists to supply.

### Preparation tips

- **Find the denominator and the population before calculating.** Most consequential errors are denominator errors.
- **Convert percentages into people.** "7% of applications" and "33,600 people a year" are the same fact and only one changes a decision.
- **Return to counts before combining rates.** Unweighted averages over-weight the smallest study.
- **Reconstruct implied unit values.** Thirty seconds of division establishes whether a business case is plausible.
- **Ask for the definition and the distribution.** Most misleading metrics are correctly calculated and wrongly defined.
- **Watch for metrics moving in opposite directions.** A local optimisation is usually a cost transfer.
- **Know what sample sizes can detect.** No power calculation is needed to see that 40 per group cannot see six points.
- **Insist on disaggregation.** An aggregate that describes no group conceals the group that is failing.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Averaging rates without weighting.** The commonest real-world error in aggregated reporting.
- **Measuring drop-off against those who continued.** It overstates every step it touches.
- **Confusing percentages with percentage points.** Both are correct labels for different quantities.
- **Accepting a metric because it is the agreed one.** Agreed metrics get optimised into worse services.
- **Accepting a mean without a spread or a definition.** Session metrics systematically flatter multi-sitting services.
- **Endorsing an underpowered comparison "with caveats".** Caveats do not travel; the number does.
- **Leaving a percentage as a percentage.** People do not feel percentages.
- **Aggregating studies that are not comparable.** Arithmetically correct and referentially empty.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately you read. In a senior role that runs on documents — service standards, accessibility regulations, policy submissions, evaluation reports, business cases, contracts and the words users say — reading accuracy is the substrate everything else rests on. A misread exception clause becomes a compliance failure across a programme; a misread finding in a colleague's report becomes advice given confidently to a programme director.

The classic format presents a short passage of dense workplace prose, typically three to six sentences, followed by statements to classify as True, False or Cannot Say. True means the statement follows necessarily. False means the passage contradicts it. Cannot Say means the passage neither establishes nor excludes it. Alongside these come comprehension items, main-point items, and questions asking which of four summaries is most accurate.

The governing rule is that you answer from the passage alone, and here is the part specific to your level: expertise actively costs you marks. The more standards and policy documents you have read, the more readily you supply the clause you expect to be there, complete the sentence the way such sentences usually end, and feel confident doing it. Senior candidates underperform on verbal reasoning at rates that surprise them, and this is almost always the mechanism. The counter-habit must be mechanical: before marking anything True, put your finger on the words.

Cannot Say is the answer experienced practitioners avoid most. Forming a view under incomplete information is what your job consists of, and the reflex is deeply worn in. Here it is a liability. If you are filling a gap with knowledge or inference, however reasonable, the answer is Cannot Say.

Small words carry the load. "All" is not "most". "May" is not "must". "Should" is not "will". "Consider whether" is not "recommend". "Solely", "entirely", "unless", "provided that" and "where practicable" govern everything around them. Wrong answers are built almost entirely from swaps of these terms.

There is a direct professional payoff. Your role names building consensus by asking difficult questions and challenging assumptions, and a great many assumptions in government are about what a document requires. Reading the requirement precisely is how you find the room that everyone believed was not there.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The verbal skills tested map onto your named skills:

- **Reading precisely** maps to **Designing for everyone**, where you ensure a design meets appropriate standards such as accessibility regulations.
- **Separating statement from inference** maps to **Evidence-based design**, where you analyse and clearly explain complex evidence.
- **Identifying the main point** maps to **Design communication**, where you clearly explain complex problems and ideas to teams or stakeholders outside design.
- **Handling qualified language** maps to **Designing strategically**, where you use constraints in technology, systems and policy to shape design — and where knowing which constraints are hard tells you where design can move.
- **Reading for absence** maps to **Designing for everyone**, where noticing that a framework never mentions access needs has an ethical consequence.
- **Documenting risks and unresolved issues accurately** maps to **Design communication**, where you document and communicate decisions, related risks and unresolved issues.

### Practice questions

*Passage A — for Questions 1 to 4*

"All new public-facing services must meet the accessibility standard before entering public beta, unless an exemption has been agreed in writing by the accessibility lead. Where a service cannot meet a specific requirement, the team must publish an accessibility statement setting out the gap and the plan to close it. Services already in live operation must meet the standard within 18 months of this policy taking effect. Internal-facing tools are outside scope, although teams are encouraged to apply the same standard."

**Question 1 (easy)** — Statement: "Every new public-facing service must meet the accessibility standard before public beta." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The passage attaches an exception: a written exemption agreed by the accessibility lead. The statement's "every" contradicts that, so it is False. The pattern — absolute term in the statement, exception in the passage — is the most reliable in the section. Note that this passage carries two distinct provisions that are easy to conflate: an exemption route, which removes the requirement, and an accessibility statement route, which handles a specific unmet requirement while the obligation remains. Those are different mechanisms, and Question 4 turns on the distinction.

**Question 2 (easy)** — Statement: "Internal tools must meet the accessibility standard." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The passage places internal tools outside scope and says teams are *encouraged* to apply the same standard. Encouragement is not requirement, so the statement's "must" contradicts the passage. False. The two-part structure — exclusion followed by soft aspiration — recurs constantly in policy prose and is misreported in both directions: as an obligation by people who want the standard applied, and as a prohibition by people who do not. Reading it accurately is what lets you tell a team truthfully that they are not required to do this and that doing it is a good idea.

**Question 3 (moderate)** — Statement: "A service that entered public beta two years ago without meeting the standard breached the policy." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The pre-beta requirement applies to *new* services, and services already live have 18 months from the policy taking effect — but the passage never says when the policy took effect. A service entering beta two years ago may not have been subject to the policy at the time. Because the fact you would need is absent, the answer is Cannot Say. Whenever a passage sets an obligation relative to a policy's commencement, check whether the commencement date is actually given; its absence is deliberate and it appears in this section repeatedly.

**Question 4 (harder)** — Statement: "A team that publishes an accessibility statement no longer needs to meet the requirement it describes." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The statement route requires setting out the gap *and the plan to close it*, which presupposes that the requirement still stands and will be met. The exemption route, agreed in writing by the accessibility lead, is the mechanism that removes an obligation. The statement conflates the two, asserting that publishing discharges the requirement, which the passage's own wording contradicts. So it is False rather than Cannot Say. This is a genuinely important distinction beyond the test, because accessibility statements are frequently treated in practice as a permanent exemption mechanism rather than a disclosure of work outstanding — and reading the clause accurately is what lets you say so.

*Passage B — for Questions 5 to 8*

"The evaluation of the assisted digital route found that users completed more slowly than online users and required an average of 2.4 contacts with the service. The evaluation notes that the two groups differed in age, first language and prior use of the service. It did not assess whether completed applications were of comparable quality, nor whether the additional contacts affected outcomes. The evaluation recommends that the programme consider whether additional guidance would reduce contact volume."

**Question 5 (easy)** — Statement: "Assisted digital users required more than two contacts on average." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The passage states an average of 2.4 contacts, and 2.4 exceeds two, so the statement follows and is True. Answer quickly and bank the time. The only care needed is that the claim concerns assisted digital users specifically — a statement about "users" generally would broaden beyond what the passage supports.

**Question 6 (moderate)** — Statement: "The assisted digital route causes slower completion." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The passage reports an association and then names three ways the groups differed: age, first language and prior use. Any could be responsible. The passage neither asserts nor excludes a causal role for the route itself, so the answer is Cannot Say. The design consequence is direct and worth stating: if the route's design causes the delay you redesign the route; if assisted digital users are largely people for whom English is an additional language, the answer is translation or plainer content; if they are largely first-time users, it is guidance. Treating an association as a cause is among the most reliable ways for a programme to spend a quarter improving the wrong thing, and the evaluators were careful precisely to prevent it.

**Question 7 (moderate)** — Statement: "Additional contacts with the service worsen outcomes for users." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The passage says explicitly that the evaluation did not assess whether the additional contacts affected outcomes. Declared silence establishes nothing in either direction, so Cannot Say. Note that the intuitive reading runs the wrong way here: more contact sounds like friction, and it might equally mean users received help that produced a better application. That possibility is exactly why the evaluators flagged the gap rather than implying an answer, and a reader who supplies the intuitive direction has substituted a plausible assumption for the text. An evaluation that did not look cannot be evidence about what it did not look at.

**Question 8 (harder)** — Statement: "The evaluation recommends providing additional guidance." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The evaluation recommends that the programme *consider whether* additional guidance would reduce contact volume — a recommendation to evaluate an option, including the possibility of concluding it would not. The statement asserts a recommendation to provide, which was not made, so it is False rather than Cannot Say, because the passage tells you what was recommended and it was something different. This distinction pervades real evaluation documents and produces a familiar sequence: a recommendation to consider becomes a commitment to deliver, is entered on a tracker, and is reported against for a year without anyone having decided it was the right thing to do.

*Passage C — for Questions 9 to 12*

"Teams should offer users a choice of channel wherever practicable. A digital channel must not be the only option for services used predominantly by people at risk of digital exclusion. Where a team withdraws a channel, it must assess the impact on affected users and record the assessment; records are reviewed annually by the accessibility lead. Users who need assistance must be able to reach a person by telephone during published service hours."

**Question 9 (easy)** — Statement: "Users must be able to reach a person at any time." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The passage says users *who need assistance* must be able to reach a person by telephone *during published service hours*. The statement drops both qualifications and replaces the second with "at any time", which the passage does not support. False. Two qualifiers removed is a compact demonstration of why small words matter: a statement can be assembled entirely from the passage's own vocabulary and still assert something the passage does not.

**Question 10 (moderate)** — Statement: "A team that withdraws a channel without recording an impact assessment has breached the requirements." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The passage requires that where a team withdraws a channel it must assess the impact and record the assessment. Withdrawing without recording fails that directly, so the statement follows and is True. Contrast with Passage A, where a route was supplied for handling an unmet requirement and the breach lay in not taking the route. Here the requirement is unconditional. Distinguishing a rule with an escape route from one without is among the most useful habits this section builds, and it transfers directly to reading real standards, where teams routinely assume an escape route exists because one usually does.

**Question 11 (harder)** — Statement: "A digital-only design for a general-population service complies with this passage." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The hard prohibition is specific to services used predominantly by people at risk of digital exclusion, and a general-population service is not described by that condition — so that prohibition does not bite. But the passage also says teams *should* offer a choice of channel *wherever practicable*, and whether it was practicable here is not stated. Compliance therefore depends on a fact you do not have, making this Cannot Say. The item rewards holding two provisions in mind and noticing that the narrow scope of the hard rule does not discharge the soft one — which is also how these clauses work in practice, and why "we're not in scope of the prohibition" is not the same as "we're compliant".

**Question 12 (harder) — Main point**

Which best captures the main point of Passage C?

- A) Digital services are unsuitable for many users.
- B) Users should be able to reach services through a channel that works for them, with digital-only ruled out where exclusion is likely, withdrawals assessed, recorded and reviewed, and human help available during service hours.
- C) Teams must record an impact assessment when withdrawing a channel.
- D) Users must be able to speak to a person.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The main point must span the passage while adding nothing. Option A overstates a targeted restriction into a general judgement. Options C and D are each accurate about one clause and silent on the rest — and D drops both qualifications. Option B captures all four elements and the logic joining them: channel choice is the principle, digital exclusion is the boundary case, assessment and annual review protect people when things change, and human contact is the backstop. Main-point items reward the option broad enough to cover everything and disciplined enough to invent nothing, and three of these four are true details promoted beyond their scope.

### Preparation tips

- **Point at the words.** Before marking True, identify the phrase that makes it true. Experience makes this harder, not easier.
- **Treat Cannot Say as decisive.** The reflex to form a view is precisely what costs marks here.
- **Circle every qualifier.** All, every, entirely, solely, may, must, should, encouraged, unless, wherever practicable, predominantly, consider whether.
- **Distinguish an exemption route from a disclosure route.** They look similar and do different things.
- **Look for the missing fact.** Some items are Cannot Say because a commencement date or threshold was never given.
- **Separate rules with escape routes from rules without them.** The first gives False when you assume a breach; the second gives True.
- **Treat declared silence as silence.** An evaluation that did not examine something has told you nothing about it.
- **Notice when the intuitive direction is the trap.** More contact might mean more help, not more friction.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Answering from professional knowledge.** The dominant senior error by a wide margin.
- **Completing the clause you expected.** Policy prose has conventions; passages exploit them.
- **Reading "encouraged" as "required".** Soft aspirations sit next to hard rules constantly.
- **Treating a disclosure mechanism as an exemption.** Accessibility statements are the standard example.
- **Dropping a qualifier.** "Who need assistance" and "during published hours" are load-bearing.
- **Treating association as causation.** A passage naming confounds is warning you off.
- **Reading "consider whether X" as "recommend X".** Evaluation prose runs on this.
- **Assuming out of scope of a prohibition means compliant.** A softer expectation may still apply.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment presents realistic workplace scenarios and asks what you would do. There is no passage to parse and no arithmetic. You get a dilemma of the kind your role produces, four or five candidate responses, and a question asking which is most effective and often which is least.

This section measures whether your judgement matches what the profession considers good practice at your level. Your role description places you as an experienced designer working with minimal support, who influences and mentors others, works with service managers and programme directors, may carry responsibility across complex services, helps set direction and embed good practice, and makes important decisions based on research while understanding how that research affects others. The strongest answers therefore involve independent professional judgement, responsibility for other people's practice as well as your own, holding positions under genuine organisational pressure, and developing capability rather than solving everything personally.

That last point separates your level from a competent practitioner's more sharply than anything else. A service designer who fixes a colleague's flawed approach has solved a problem. A senior service designer who fixes it without the colleague learning anything has done half the job. Several items below turn on that distinction.

Four principles carry most scenarios.

Users come first, and specifically the users struggling most. When an option protects someone who cannot complete a journey, it almost always outranks one protecting a schedule, a budget or somebody's comfort — including your own.

Protect the integrity of the evidence and of the people whose job it is to protect it. You are increasingly the person a junior designer looks to when they are pressed to say something they should not, and options that leave them alone with that pressure are weak even when the finding survives.

Develop capability while solving the problem. The best answers at your level frequently teach, delegate with support, or change a process, because a senior designer who only produces outputs does not scale.

Escalate what genuinely requires authority you do not hold, and handle everything else. Accessibility blockers at launch, decisions that commit the organisation, and serious ethical concerns go up. Methodological disagreements, stakeholder pressure and peer feedback you handle.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The judgement dimensions map onto your named skills:

- **Building consensus and challenging assumptions** maps to **Design communication**, where you build consensus by asking difficult questions and challenging assumptions.
- **Advising on inclusive and ethical design** maps to **Designing for everyone**, where you advise teams and deliver ethical services considering users' personal and social context.
- **Running and adapting sessions, and coaching others to** maps to **Designing together**, where you advise others how to plan and run design sessions and adapt them to achieve a useful outcome.
- **Working across profession boundaries** maps to **Designing together**, where you work with policy and other teams.
- **Embedding practice and mentoring** maps to **Leading design**, where you support other designers and communicate the value of user-centred design, and to **Iterative design**, where you help other designers apply iterative principles.
- **Documenting risk honestly** maps to **Design communication**, where you document decisions, related risks and unresolved issues.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — A designer under pressure from a director**

A designer you mentor tells you a director has asked them to remove an accessibility finding from a board summary because it "muddies the message". They are unsure what to do. What is the most effective response?

- A) Tell them to leave it in and say you said so.
- B) Talk it through so they understand what is at stake, agree together how the finding should be presented — including a constructive framing with proposed mitigation — offer to attend the meeting, and take the request itself up with the director yourself.
- C) Contact the director and instruct them not to make such requests.
- D) Escalate to the head of profession.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Two things must happen: the finding survives, and the designer emerges more capable and less exposed. Option B does both. Talking it through builds their judgement rather than substituting yours; agreeing the presentation gives them something constructive to bring rather than a refusal; offering to attend supplies backing without takeover. Most importantly, taking the request up with the director is your job — a power differential of that size is what a senior colleague exists to absorb, and leaving them to manage it alone is a failure of mentoring even if the finding survives. Option A is least effective: it leaves them defending a position by proxy, teaches nothing, and reframes integrity as hierarchy. Option C treats a conversation as an instruction you are not positioned to give. Option D escalates something within your remit, though informing your head of profession if it recurs is sensible.

**Question 2 (easy) — Embedding practice in a resistant team**

You are asked to embed user-centred design practice in a delivery team that has never worked with a designer and whose delivery manager says design is welcome "as long as it doesn't slow us down". What is the strongest first move?

- A) Present the service standard's design requirements.
- B) Find the team's nearest consequential decision, offer to answer that specific question inside their sprint, and get the whole team observing sessions — so the value is demonstrated in their terms before you ask for more.
- C) Ask the programme director to mandate design involvement.
- D) Run a full discovery to establish user needs properly first.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** A team that has never worked with a designer has no reason to believe design repays its cost, and no explanation substitutes for watching a user struggle with something you built. Option B is calibrated to that: it answers a question the team actually has, fits their cadence rather than demanding a change to it, and gets them into sessions as observers, which converts design from a report they receive into an experience they had. Option C is least effective because mandated involvement is performed rather than used, and it makes you the director's enforcement mechanism, which poisons the relationships you need. Option A wins compliance and loses goodwill. Option D is right in ambition and wrong in sequence — a full discovery in a team worried about pace confirms their fear before you have any credit.

**Question 3 (easy) — A design session that is producing one voice**

You are running a session with senior stakeholders and frontline staff. Twenty minutes in, two stakeholders dominate and no frontline staff have spoken. What is the most effective response?

- A) Let it run; the stakeholders know the service.
- B) Change the structure — write first then round the table, or split into pairs — so quieter participants contribute without anyone being told to stop.
- C) Ask the stakeholders directly to let others speak.
- D) Follow up with the frontline staff separately.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** The session is producing one perspective when you convened it for several, and structural solutions outperform confrontational ones. Writing before speaking gives quieter participants time to form a thought and gives you a legitimate reason to change who is talking. Your role names adapting a design session to ensure you achieve a useful outcome, and this is precisely that — adapting the format when it is not working is the skill, not a workaround. Option A is least effective: it wastes the frontline staff's time and produces a design based on how senior people imagine the service works, which is the specific failure mode that makes services look fine on paper. Option C may succeed and creates avoidable friction. Option D is a useful backup and does not rescue this session.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Advising on work that is not good enough**

A designer in another team, who does not report to you, has produced a service map whose central claim about user behaviour is not supported by the research cited. It has been shared with stakeholders. What is most effective?

- A) Correct the record with the stakeholders.
- B) Speak to the designer first, privately and specifically, and agree how the claim should be corrected and communicated to those already relying on it — with a clear next step if they decline.
- C) Say nothing; it is not your team.
- D) Raise it as a general point about evidence standards in the design community.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** The claim needs correcting and the designer needs to remain a functioning colleague, and option B is the only option achieving both. Going to them first is not merely courteous: they may hold context you lack, and if they do not, the correction lands far better from them. The conditional escalation matters — B is not naive, it has a next step. The element most often omitted is communicating to those already relying on the claim, without which a corrected map sits alongside stakeholders' uncorrected understanding. Option A bypasses the designer, damages a relationship, and risks acting on your own misreading. Option C is least effective: stakeholders are currently acting on an unsupported claim, and team boundaries are not a reason to let that stand. Option D is passive-aggressive in effect — a general point made publicly after a specific failure is understood by everyone as being about that failure.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Pressure to predetermine a conclusion**

A programme director tells you, before work starts, that the design work "needs to show the new digital-only channel will work, because that decision is effectively made". What is the most effective response?

- A) Focus the work on the channel's strengths.
- B) State plainly that the work will report what users experience, and reframe around what they actually need — if the decision is made, the useful questions become who will struggle, what support they will need, and what would signal the change is failing.
- C) Agree, then report honestly and handle the fallout.
- D) Refuse and report the conversation.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** The request as phrased asks you to predetermine a conclusion, which would make the work worthless to everyone including the director. Option B declines the premise without a confrontation and redirects to something genuinely useful: if the channel decision really is made, work aimed at relitigating it wastes everyone's time, while work identifying who will be excluded, what assisted provision they need, and what early indicators would show failure is both honest and directly valuable. That reframe converts an integrity problem into a scoping conversation. Option A is least effective — designing to demonstrate a conclusion is a rigged exercise and rigged exercises surface, usually at the worst moment. Option C leaves the expectation uncorrected and guarantees a worse conflict later. Option D may become necessary if pressure persists, and the first move is a professional conversation you are equipped to have.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Developing someone whose practice is weak**

A designer you mentor consistently overstates findings — small samples described as "users", preferences reported as needs. You have raised it twice generally and it continues. What is the most effective next step?

- A) Rewrite their outputs before they circulate.
- B) Move from general feedback to something structured: work through one of their outputs together, line by line, agreeing what each claim can support; then agree they will apply that test themselves before the next one, and review it together.
- C) Raise it in their performance conversation.
- D) Reduce their responsibility for reporting.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Twice-repeated general feedback has not worked, which usually means the person agrees in principle and cannot yet see the problem in their own writing. Option B addresses that directly: line by line makes the abstract concrete, an agreed test gives them something portable, and reviewing the next one closes the loop. This is deliberate capability building rather than repeated correction, which is the distinction your level turns on. Option A is least effective because it removes the problem from view while guaranteeing it persists, and it signals that you do not think they can learn. Option C is a legitimate component eventually and premature before a structured intervention. Option D protects the outputs at the cost of the person's development and standing.

**Question 7 (moderate) — A constraint from a policy team**

A policy team requires evidence that about a fifth of eligible users will struggle to obtain. They are defensive, having spent months on the rule. What is the strongest approach?

- A) Present the research and ask them to change the rule.
- B) Establish first what the rule protects against, then bring evidence on both sides — how many eligible users the requirement excludes, and whether an alternative form of evidence addresses the same risk — framing it as jointly solving their problem rather than removing their rule.
- C) Escalate to the service owner.
- D) Design the best possible interface for the existing requirement.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** The defensiveness is information: they have invested months and expect to be told their work is wrong. The productive move changes what the conversation is about. A rule requiring particular evidence exists to manage a risk, and once that risk is named the question becomes which instrument manages it best — a design question you can contribute to rather than a policy question you can only object to. Option B also brings the exclusion number, because a fifth of eligible users is an argument and "some people find it hard" is not. Option A asks them to abandon their work on your evidence alone, which is unlikely to succeed and unnecessary. Option C is least effective as a first move: escalating a solvable disagreement across a profession boundary costs you a relationship you will need repeatedly, and your role explicitly names working across those boundaries. Option D polishes the exclusion.

**Question 8 (harder) — An accessibility blocker close to launch**

Two weeks before launch you find a key step cannot be completed with a screen reader. Fixing it delays launch. What is most effective?

- A) Launch and add it to the backlog.
- B) Raise it immediately with the delivery manager and service owner, describe precisely what cannot be done and by whom, ask for an explicit recorded decision, and offer any interim route that would let affected users complete the journey another way.
- C) Attempt the fix yourself in the two weeks.
- D) Launch and publish an accessibility statement noting the gap.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A step that cannot be completed with a screen reader means some people cannot use the service, and weighing a delay against an exclusion is a decision for people with authority to make it. Raising it precisely and now is squarely yours, and the phrase "explicit recorded decision" is what makes option B senior rather than merely diligent: an unrecorded decision drifts, and a recorded one makes the trade-off owned. Option B also looks for an interim route so the choice is not simply launch-or-delay. Option A is least effective — "add it to the backlog" is where accessibility problems go to remain unfixed while real people cannot use a public service. Option D looks responsible and frequently is not: an accessibility statement discloses a gap you are closing, not a mechanism for launching with a known blocker. Option C risks a fortnight and leaves nobody with authority aware.

**Question 9 (harder) — Evidence against a public commitment**

Your work shows that a publicly announced channel closure will substantially disadvantage users without reliable digital access. Colleagues propose presenting this as "opportunities for future enhancement". What is most effective?

- A) Accept the framing; the commitment is public.
- B) Report the findings accurately and do the constructive work alongside — quantify who is affected, specify what assisted provision would mitigate it, and present finding and mitigation together so the organisation has a route that is neither reversal nor denial.
- C) Report starkly and let the organisation respond.
- D) Escalate before acting.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** The proposed framing protects the organisation from discomfort and produces the worst available outcome: the disadvantage occurs anyway, nobody owns it, and evidence that could have driven mitigation has been defused. Option B keeps the finding intact and makes it actionable. Quantifying who is affected converts an abstract concern into something a delivery plan can address, and arriving with mitigation means the finding is not a problem dropped on people who cannot reverse a ministerial announcement. Option A is least effective — softening findings to accommodate a commitment is the precise failure your professional standards exist to prevent, and it falls hardest on the users least able to absorb it. Option C is accurate and unhelpful, and at your level a finding delivered without a route forward gets managed around. Option D is worth doing in parallel and is not a substitute.

**Question 10 (harder) — Consensus that would harm users**

A team you advise has converged on a solution everyone likes, which your evidence indicates will fail the users who struggle most. Reopening it reverses a decision people are relieved to have made. What is most effective?

- A) Let it go; the team has decided.
- B) Raise it once, clearly and specifically, showing what the evidence indicates will happen to those users and what mitigation might look like — then if the team proceeds, ensure the risk is documented and propose measuring the outcome after release.
- C) Escalate to the service owner over the team's head.
- D) Keep raising it until the team changes course.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** You have a professional obligation to say the thing and you also have to keep advising these people. Option B threads that: raise it once and properly, with evidence and a constructive mitigation rather than a veto; then if the decision stands, do the two things that protect users and your position — document the risk so the decision is knowing rather than accidental, and set up a measurement so reality gets a vote later. Documenting risks and unresolved issues is a named part of your role, and this is what it is for. Option A is least effective because consensus harming the users who struggle most is not a value, and silence makes you complicit in a decision you had the evidence to inform. Option C escalates before using the direct route and costs the team's trust. Option D substitutes persistence for persuasion, and an adviser who relitigates every decision stops being heard on any of them.

**Question 11 (harder) — A flaw in work you endorsed**

Three months ago you endorsed a design decision based on research whose screener excluded anyone who had contacted the support desk. The design is now live. What should you do?

- A) Note it in your guidance for future assurance.
- B) Tell the team and decision-makers now, scope precisely which conclusions are compromised and which stand, propose targeted follow-up with the excluded group, and review why your own check did not catch it.
- C) Commission follow-up quietly and correct the record if findings differ.
- D) Raise it with your head of profession and ask how to proceed.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** Four things need doing and option B does all of them. Telling people now matters because a live design is running on the findings. Precise scoping matters because conclusions about users who never needed help may remain sound, and overstating the damage is as unhelpful as understating it. Targeted follow-up addresses the gap. And reviewing your own check is the element specific to your level: you endorsed this, so the interesting question is not only what the study missed but why your review did not surface it and what to add. Option C is least effective — concealing a known flaw while a live design proceeds is indefensible, and the concealment rather than the original error is what would be remembered. Option A converts a live problem into a training note. Option D is worth doing alongside; routing your own error through someone else reads worse than owning it.

**Question 12 (harder) — A recurring problem across teams**

Across three teams you advise, design work consistently arrives after build decisions are taken. Each team is appreciative and nothing changes. What is most effective?

- A) Work with each team separately to speed up design delivery.
- B) Gather specific examples across all three showing what each late input would have changed, take them to the programme's governance forum with a proposed change to how decisions are sequenced, and secure a director's support for the change.
- C) Raise it in each team's retrospective.
- D) Reduce the depth of design work so it can be delivered sooner.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Three teams with the same problem is a programme-level sequencing issue, not three local ones, and it needs a programme-level forum, evidence and a proposal. Option B assembles all three and adds the element that distinguishes senior from competent practice: securing sponsorship from someone with authority to change how decisions are sequenced. Specific examples across three teams are what turn "design arrives too late" from a complaint into a case. Option C is a reasonable component and insufficient alone, since three retrospectives will produce three local accommodations rather than a structural change. Option A treats a sequencing problem as a speed problem and will exhaust you without fixing it. Option D is least effective, trading the value of the work for its timeliness and delivering, on time, design that is not worth having.

### Preparation tips

- **Answer as a senior practitioner.** Independent judgement, responsibility for others' practice, and capability building alongside problem solving.
- **Check whether the option develops anyone.** At your level, solving it yourself is frequently the second-best answer.
- **Absorb pressure downwards.** If a power differential is above the level of the person facing it, it is yours.
- **Redirect rather than refuse.** "Not that, but here is the question I can answer" is the highest-scoring move in stakeholder scenarios.
- **Ask what a rule protects against.** It converts a policy objection into a design conversation.
- **Bring mitigation with bad news.** A finding plus a route forward is actionable; a finding alone invites softening.
- **Document the risk when you lose the argument.** It makes the decision knowing rather than accidental.
- **Look for the programme-level version of a repeated local problem.** Three teams with one issue is one issue.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Solving without developing.** The characteristic senior failure — outputs improve, capability does not.
- **Softening findings to accommodate a commitment.** It disadvantages exactly the users least able to absorb it.
- **Mandating practice before demonstrating value.** Compliance theatre is the reliable result.
- **Correcting a colleague's work by going straight to stakeholders.** Speak to the person first, with a next step if they decline.
- **Escalating across a profession boundary as a first move.** It costs a relationship you will need repeatedly.
- **Treating an accessibility statement as a way to launch with a blocker.** It is a disclosure, not an exemption.
- **Treating consensus as a terminal value.** A comfortable decision that harms excluded users is not a good outcome.
- **Trading depth for timeliness.** On-time work that is not worth having helps nobody.

## Conclusion

You have reached the end, and it is worth registering what that involved: forty-eight questions across four assessment types, with the reasoning worked through on each. That is a serious piece of professional preparation and rather more than most senior candidates undertake.

Look back at the ground. In the cognitive section you audited a page against a standard as written rather than as you would have written it, spotted compounding bias where three factors pushed the same way, followed a channel closure through to its second-order cost transfers, read a 40% return rate as both a failure and a natural experiment, connected two metrics moving in opposite directions, and read a concentration of accessibility failures as a process diagnosis rather than a conduct one. In the numeric section you weighted rather than averaged, converted 7% into 33,600 people, reconstructed an implied unit value from a business case, recognised a session metric masquerading as a journey measure, and declined to endorse a comparison that could not detect what it was designed to find. In the verbal section you separated an exemption route from a disclosure route, checked for a commencement date that was never given, noticed that the intuitive reading of "more contacts" was the trap, and caught "consider whether" pretending to be a recommendation. And in the situational judgement section you absorbed a director's pressure on a designer's behalf, moved from general feedback to structured coaching, asked a policy team what their rule protected against, documented a risk after losing an argument, and took a repeated local problem to a programme forum.

A thread runs through all of it. Almost every item was asking two things at once: do you know the limits of what you have, and can you act on the mechanism rather than the instance? Those two capabilities are most of what your role consists of. Teams can generate opinions without you and read dashboards without you. What you supply is a design rationale that holds when someone pulls at it, and a practice around you that keeps producing them.

A word about the particular difficulty of your level. The errors that cost you most are no longer the ones you would notice. They are made fluently, in areas where your experience runs deep enough that checking feels unnecessary — and the verbal reasoning section is the clearest demonstration, because expertise measurably costs marks there. The senior designers whose judgement holds up across a career are, with striking consistency, the ones who kept a habit of deliberate scepticism about their own reasoning long after it stopped feeling required.

If you want to build on this, a few things repay effort more than rereading. Audit a design decision you endorsed and ask who could not have appeared in the evidence behind it. Trace three figures in a live business case back to source. Take a policy constraint you have accepted for months and ask what it protects against. Sit with an analyst and ask how the metrics you rely on are defined rather than calculated. And the next time you give a designer feedback that has not landed twice already, work through one output line by line instead of saying it a third time.

Finally, for the day itself. An assessment samples your reasoning on one morning under artificial conditions. It is not a verdict on your judgement or your career. Read the stem twice, set expertise aside in the verbal section, find the denominator, trust Cannot Say, and remember that at your level the strongest answer is usually the one honest about uncertainty and aimed at the mechanism rather than the symptom.

Good luck. Go carefully, ask what things are for, and keep zooming out.
