First, a friendly reassurance: psychometric assessments are not about catching you out, and they are not IQ tests dressed up in work clothes. They are structured, standardised exercises that measure the thinking skills and workplace judgement that predict success in a role. For a junior graphic designer, that means the assessments in this guide use the real materials of your job — layout grids, spacing scales, colour palettes, type choices, icon sets, design patterns and components, analytics reports, and feedback from design crits. Why do these assessments matter for your role in particular? As a junior graphic designer, you work independently after being given direction, and you are trusted to identify user issues and important needs. That takes sharp eyes and clear thinking. You check that content is legible and readable. You spot when spacing breaks a grid, when a colour fails a contrast check, or when an icon does not match the pattern library. Cognitive, numeric, verbal, and situational judgement assessments measure exactly these abilities — so practising them is genuinely practising your job.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the thinking skills your role actually demands, using practical workplace scenarios rather than abstract puzzles. Instead of asking you to rotate strange shapes, it shows you the everyday materials of your job — a spacing scale, a draft page layout, an icon set, a component library, a set of design principles — and asks you to reason quickly and accurately about them.

The typical format is an online, timed test lasting between 15 and 30 minutes. You will usually see somewhere between 15 and 30 questions covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective: your answers are compared against a norm group of people at a similar level. Many modern platforms are adaptive, which means the questions get slightly harder when you answer correctly and slightly easier when you do not — so do not panic if the test feels like it is getting more difficult. That usually means you are doing well! Employers often receive a breakdown of your speed and your accuracy, so working carefully matters just as much as working quickly. Most platforms offer a few ungraded practice questions before the real test starts, so you can get comfortable with the buttons and layout without any pressure.

Why do employers use this kind of assessment for graphic designers? Because your core professional value is visual and structural thinking. You create graphic elements that underpin interaction and service design, and that means holding rules in your head — grids, spacing systems, type scales, colour palettes, naming conventions — and noticing instantly when something breaks them. You also need to reason about what follows from a design decision: if the body text size increases, what happens to the line length? If a pattern changes, which pages are affected? A well-designed cognitive test simulates those demands in miniature, and at junior level it focuses on the day-to-day checking, spotting, and reasoning tasks you do with support from senior designers.

One more reassurance before the questions begin: nobody expects perfection. These tests are designed so that very few people finish with everything correct. A calm, steady approach — read carefully, answer what you can, move on when stuck — beats a frantic one every time.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The dimensions of a cognitive assessment line up closely with the named skills in your role summary:

- **Pattern recognition** maps to your **Iterative design** skill, especially the duty to use and iterate design patterns and components. Spotting when a button, card, or icon breaks the established pattern is pure pattern recognition — and it is one of the most valuable things a junior designer does when checking work across a service.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Design communication** and **Designing strategically**. When you clearly explain design decisions to others, you are really presenting a chain of reasoning: the evidence, the principle, the decision. Deduction questions test whether that chain holds. Explaining the value of using patterns and components is also a logical argument: consistency, speed, and accessibility follow from reuse.
- **Error checking** maps to **Designing for everyone**. Ensuring a design meets appropriate standards — for example accessibility regulations, with support — means methodically comparing artefacts against rules: contrast values against thresholds, text sizes against minimums, focus states against requirements.
- **Prioritisation** maps to your role-level responsibility to identify user issues and important needs, and to work independently after being given direction. When several issues compete for your attention, you must rank them by impact on users, not by which one is easiest or loudest.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Evidence-based design** — analysing, synthesising and clearly explaining evidence relevant to users, such as web analytics data — and to working with researchers or analysts to use evidence to inform, develop and test a design idea. Problem-solving questions give you tangled information and ask you to find the clear next step, which is exactly what you do when research findings arrive.

Notice what this mapping means for you: practising cognitive questions is not a detour from your professional development. It is a workout for the same mental muscles you use every day at your desk.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern recognition in a spacing scale**

Your team's design system uses a spacing scale based on multiples of 8 pixels: 8, 16, 24, 32, 40, and so on. While reviewing a draft page layout, you note the following spacing values used between components:

1. 16px
2. 24px
3. 30px
4. 40px
5. 8px

Which value breaks the spacing scale?

- A) Value 2
- B) Value 3
- C) Value 4
- D) Value 5

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The rule is simple: every spacing value must be a multiple of 8. Check each one: 16 = 8 × 2, 24 = 8 × 3, 40 = 8 × 5, and 8 = 8 × 1. But 30 is not a multiple of 8 (8 × 3 = 24 and 8 × 4 = 32, so 30 falls between them). Value 3 breaks the scale, so the answer is B. This is exactly the kind of consistency check you perform when using and iterating design patterns and components. A stray 30px gap looks tiny in isolation, but inconsistent spacing makes pages feel subtly untidy and makes components harder to reuse. Train your eye to scan for the rule first, then test each item against it — that method is faster and more reliable than staring at the whole list hoping something jumps out.

**Question 2 (easy) — Pattern recognition in icon naming**

Your icon library names files using the pattern "icon-{category}-{name}" in lower case, for example "icon-navigation-arrow". A teammate has added five new icons:

1. icon-navigation-chevron
2. icon-action-download
3. Icon-action-upload
4. icon-status-warning
5. icon-status-error

Which file name breaks the naming pattern?

- A) File 2
- B) File 3
- C) File 4
- D) File 5

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The pattern requires lower case throughout. File 3 begins with a capital "I" — "Icon-action-upload" instead of "icon-action-upload". Every other file follows the pattern exactly. Naming conventions may feel like small details, but they matter enormously in a shared component library: on some systems, "Icon-action-upload" and "icon-action-upload" are treated as different files, which can break links in prototypes and production code. Spotting this kind of slip is part of your Iterative design skill — patterns and components only deliver their value when everyone applies them consistently. In the test itself, questions like this reward careful, character-by-character checking rather than a quick skim.

**Question 3 (easy) — Logical deduction from a design rule**

Your team's design guidance states: "All body text must use the standard type size unless the content designer approves an exception." You are reviewing a page where the body text does not use the standard type size. Which one of the following conclusions must be true?

- A) The page breaks the design guidance.
- B) The content designer approved an exception.
- C) Either the content designer approved an exception, or the page breaks the guidance.
- D) The page is hard to read.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The rule allows exactly one exception: content designer approval. From what you know, you cannot tell whether that approval exists. So option A might be true, and option B might be true, but neither must be true. Option D adds information the question never gave you — non-standard text is not automatically hard to read. The only statement guaranteed to be true in every possible case is C: one of the two possibilities must hold. This careful habit — concluding only what the facts guarantee — is valuable in your real work too. Before telling a teammate their page breaks the rules, you would check whether an exception was agreed. That protects your working relationships as well as your logic, and it models the constructive, evidence-first behaviour that good design communication depends on.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Error checking against accessibility rules**

You are checking a draft screen against three of your team's accessibility rules: (i) body text must be at least 16px; (ii) text colour must meet the minimum contrast standard against its background; (iii) links must be visually distinct from ordinary text in more than colour alone. Your notes on the draft say:

- Body text: 16px, passes contrast check
- Caption text: 14px, passes contrast check, marked as "supporting text, not body text"
- Links: same size and colour as body text, underlined
- Button label: 18px, fails contrast check

How many of the three rules does the draft break?

- A) 0
- B) 1
- C) 2
- D) 3

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Test each rule in turn. Rule (i): body text is 16px, which meets "at least 16px"; the caption is 14px but is explicitly not body text, so rule (i) is not broken. Rule (ii): the button label fails the contrast check, so rule (ii) is broken. Rule (iii): links are underlined, which distinguishes them by more than colour alone, so rule (iii) is satisfied. Exactly one rule is broken, so the answer is B. Two traps are worth noticing. First, the 14px caption tempts you to count a second failure — but the rule as written applies only to body text, and error checking means testing against the rule as stated, not the rule you expect. Second, "same colour as body text, underlined" might feel wrong, but the underline satisfies the requirement. This mirrors ensuring a design meets appropriate standards with support: you check methodically, note exactly what fails, and avoid over-reporting, so the issues you raise are trusted.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Prioritisation of design issues**

It is Tuesday morning. During a review of a live service page, you have identified four issues. Your senior designer has asked you to fix them in priority order:

1. The error message text fails the colour contrast standard, so some users with low vision may not be able to read it.
2. An icon in the footer is the older version from before the last pattern update.
3. The page heading uses a spacing value that is 4px off the spacing scale.
4. A decorative image is missing its rounded corners.

Which order is most defensible?

- A) 3, 2, 4, 1 — fix the quickest jobs first
- B) 1, 2, 3, 4 — user-blocking accessibility issue first, then pattern consistency, then visual polish
- C) 2, 1, 3, 4 — pattern consistency is the design system's top rule
- D) 4, 3, 2, 1 — visible cosmetic issues damage trust most

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Prioritisation questions reward ranking by user impact first. Issue 1 can prevent some users from reading an error message — and error messages appear exactly when users most need help — so it clearly comes first. Issue 2 is a genuine consistency problem: an outdated icon can confuse users and undermines the pattern library, so it reasonably comes next. Issues 3 and 4 are visual polish; they matter for quality, but no user is blocked by a 4px spacing slip or a missing rounded corner. Option A ranks by convenience, not impact. Option C puts consistency above access, which inverts the "designing for everyone" principle: an inaccessible service fails users in a way an inconsistent one does not. Option D confuses visibility with importance. At your level, identifying user issues and important needs means exactly this: asking "who is harmed, and how much?" before asking "what is easiest to fix?"

**Question 6 (moderate) — Logical deduction about component changes**

Your design system team announces: (i) the card component will be updated next sprint; (ii) every page that uses the card component must be re-checked after the update; (iii) the service homepage uses the card component; (iv) the contact page does not use the card component. Which one of the following conclusions must be true?

- A) The contact page will not need any re-checking next sprint.
- B) The service homepage must be re-checked after the update.
- C) The contact page will not change next sprint.
- D) The homepage re-check will find problems.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Chain the statements together: the card component is being updated (i); every page using it must be re-checked afterwards (ii); the homepage uses it (iii). Therefore the homepage must be re-checked — option B follows with certainty. Option A is tempting but goes too far: the rule tells you card-using pages must be re-checked; it does not say other pages will not be re-checked for other reasons. Option C similarly overreaches — the contact page avoids this particular change, but nothing rules out other changes. Option D predicts an outcome no statement supports. Notice the pattern in the wrong answers: they all convert "the rule doesn't require X" into "X won't happen". In real work, this discipline helps you scope your checking honestly when a pattern is iterated — you can say exactly which pages the change commits you to reviewing, without promising the rest are untouched.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Error checking a design specification**

You are preparing a simple specification table for a developer. Your team's type scale defines: heading level 1 = 32px, heading level 2 = 24px, heading level 3 = 19px, body = 16px. Your draft table reads:

| Element | Size |
|---|---|
| Page title (heading level 1) | 32px |
| Section heading (heading level 2) | 24px |
| Sub-heading (heading level 3) | 16px |
| Body paragraph | 16px |
| Caption (body) | 19px |

How many rows contain an error against the type scale?

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Compare each row against the scale. Page title at 32px: correct for heading level 1. Section heading at 24px: correct for heading level 2. Sub-heading at 16px: wrong — heading level 3 should be 19px. Body paragraph at 16px: correct. Caption marked as "body" at 19px: wrong — body is defined as 16px. Two rows are in error, so the answer is B. Notice how the two mistakes mirror each other — the 19px and 16px values have swapped between the sub-heading and caption rows. Transposition errors like this are extremely common in real specifications, and they are easy to miss because both values individually look "legal" (each appears somewhere in the scale). The reliable method is to check each row against the scale definition, not against your memory of what looks right. Careful specs are part of clear design communication: a developer should be able to build from your table without guessing.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Applied problem solving with analytics evidence**

You are working with an analyst on a form page. The analytics show: 1,000 users start the form each week; 900 complete page one; 450 complete page two; 430 complete page three and finish. Your senior designer asks: "Where should we focus design effort first?" What is the most reasonable answer?

- A) Page one, because it loses 100 users — the first drop matters most.
- B) Page two, because it loses 450 of the 900 users who reach it — half of them.
- C) Page three, because it is closest to the end, where drop-outs are most costly.
- D) Nowhere yet — 430 completions is a healthy number.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Work through the funnel. Page one loses 100 of 1,000 starters (one in ten). Page two loses 450 of the 900 who reach it — exactly half. Page three loses only 20 of 450 (a small fraction). The steepest drop by far is page two, so that is where design attention will help most users. Option A confuses "first" with "biggest". Option C uses position in the journey rather than evidence. Option D judges a raw number without comparing it to the number who started — 430 finishers from 1,000 starters means fewer than half succeed, which is not obviously healthy. This is your Evidence-based design skill in action: analysing and synthesising web analytics data, then clearly explaining what it means. In the real workplace you would go on to work with a researcher to understand why page two loses people — the analytics tell you where to look, and research tells you what is wrong.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Prioritisation with direction from a senior designer**

Your senior designer gave you direction on Monday: "This week, your priority is the icon refresh for the renewals service. Fit other requests around it." On Wednesday you have: (1) the icon refresh, about two days of work remaining; (2) a request from a content designer for a quick banner graphic "by Friday if possible"; (3) an invitation to Thursday's design crit, where your icon work could get useful feedback; (4) an email from another team asking you to redesign their team poster, no deadline. What is the most sensible plan for the rest of the week?

- A) Do the poster first as a warm-up, then the icons.
- B) Continue the icon refresh, attend the crit to test the icons, fit the banner in around them, and politely defer the poster.
- C) Pause the icons to finish the banner and poster, then return to icons next week.
- D) Skip the crit to maximise heads-down icon time, and decline the banner.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** You were given clear direction: the icon refresh is the priority. Option B honours that direction while using good judgement about the rest. Attending the crit is not a distraction from the icon work — it directly improves it, because you can bring the icons for feedback, which reflects the importance of constructive feedback in the design process. The banner is small and has a soft Friday deadline, so it can fit around the main work. The poster has no deadline and comes from outside your immediate priorities, so deferring it politely (and telling the requester when you could look at it) is the professional move. Option A ignores your direction entirely. Option C pauses the stated priority for two unprioritised requests. Option D protects the icon time but wastes the best feedback opportunity of the week and abruptly declines a reasonable small request. Working independently after being given direction means exactly this balance: follow the priority, use judgement on the margins, and communicate about what you defer.

**Question 10 (hard) — Multi-step deduction about page layout**

A page template has these rules: (i) if the page has a sidebar, the main content column must be narrow; (ii) if the main content column is narrow, tables wider than four columns must be replaced with stacked lists; (iii) the guidance page you are designing must include a six-column data table displayed as a table (a stakeholder requirement that cannot change). Which conclusion must be true?

- A) The guidance page must not have a sidebar.
- B) The stakeholder requirement must be dropped.
- C) The six-column table must become a stacked list.
- D) The main content column must be narrow.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Work backwards from the fixed requirement. The page must show a six-column table as a table (iii). Rule (ii) says a narrow main column forces tables wider than four columns to become stacked lists — which would violate (iii). So the main column must not be narrow. Rule (i) says a sidebar forces a narrow main column. Since the column cannot be narrow, the page cannot have a sidebar. That is option A. Option B is wrong because the question states the requirement cannot change — and there is no contradiction anyway, since dropping the sidebar resolves everything. Options C and D each directly contradict the chain you just built. This backwards-reasoning technique — start from the constraint that cannot move, then eliminate whatever conflicts with it — is exactly how layout decisions get made in real services, and it gives you a clear, logical story to tell when you explain the design decision: "We removed the sidebar because the data table you need cannot fit a narrow column."

**Question 11 (hard) — Pattern recognition across a component audit**

You are auditing button styles across five pages of a service. Your notes record each page's primary button as (colour, corner style, label case):

- Page 1: (green, rounded, sentence case)
- Page 2: (green, rounded, sentence case)
- Page 3: (green, square, sentence case)
- Page 4: (green, rounded, Title Case)
- Page 5: (blue, rounded, sentence case)

The design system standard is the style used by the majority of pages for each attribute. How many pages fully match the standard?

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** First derive the standard attribute by attribute. Colour: four pages use green, one uses blue — standard is green. Corner style: four rounded, one square — standard is rounded. Label case: four sentence case, one Title Case — standard is sentence case. So the standard button is (green, rounded, sentence case). Now check each page against all three attributes: Page 1 matches fully; Page 2 matches fully; Page 3 fails on corners; Page 4 fails on case; Page 5 fails on colour. Exactly two pages fully match, so the answer is B. The interesting structure here is that each deviant page fails on a different attribute — a very common real-world audit result, because different people made different pages at different times. Component audits like this are how design systems stay healthy, and they are a task frequently given to junior designers precisely because they build the pattern-spotting eye. Method matters: derive the rule first, then test each item against the whole rule, not just one attribute.

**Question 12 (hard) — Applied problem solving under conflicting evidence**

You are iterating a service start page. Three pieces of evidence arrive the same week: (i) analytics show 40% of users click the small help link instead of the large start button; (ii) a usability test with five participants found that three of them said the start button "looked like an advert" and hesitated to click it; (iii) a stakeholder emails saying the start button "looks great, don't change it". You have been given direction to improve the start rate. What is the strongest next step?

- A) Change nothing — the stakeholder is happy, and five test participants is too few to matter.
- B) Redesign the start button so it looks less like an advert, then measure whether the help-link clicks fall and the start rate rises.
- C) Remove the help link so users have no choice but the start button.
- D) Ask for a larger usability test before touching the design, since evidence is conflicting.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Look at how the evidence fits together. The analytics (i) show what is happening: many users avoid the start button. The usability test (ii) suggests why: it resembles an advert, a well-known problem sometimes called "banner blindness". These two sources are not conflicting — they are complementary, one quantitative and one qualitative, which is exactly how working with researchers and analysts is meant to work. The stakeholder's opinion (iii) is a preference, not user evidence. Option B applies the iterative design method: form a hypothesis from the evidence, change the design, and test whether the numbers improve. Option A wrongly weighs one opinion above two evidence sources, and small usability samples routinely reveal real problems even though they cannot give precise percentages. Option C punishes users instead of fixing the design — the help link may serve a genuine need. Option D delays action when the existing evidence already points one way; iteration means acting on good-enough evidence and testing the result. When you present this decision, you can explain it clearly: evidence, hypothesis, change, measure — a complete design communication story.

### Preparation tips

- **Practise with your real materials.** Spend ten minutes checking a page of your own service against your spacing scale, type scale, and pattern library. This is genuinely the same skill the cognitive test measures, and it improves your daily work at the same time.
- **Learn the rule before checking the items.** In every checking question — and every real audit — read the rule or convention first, then test each item against it. Working the other way round (skimming items and hoping errors jump out) is slower and less accurate.
- **Write down deductions in hard questions.** For multi-step logic, jot the chain: "no wide table in narrow column → column not narrow → no sidebar". A three-line note beats holding it all in your head, and most online tests allow rough paper.
- **Time yourself gently, then strictly.** Start by practising without a timer to build accuracy. Add a timer only once your method feels steady. Speed grows naturally from a reliable method; it rarely grows from rushing.
- **Do a short warm-up before the real test.** Two or three practice questions get your brain into checking mode. Also prepare the practical things: quiet space, charged laptop, water, and the ungraded practice questions the platform offers.
- **Reflect after each practice session.** Ask: which question type slowed me down? That is your next practice target. This is the same iterate-and-improve habit you apply to designs.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Answering from design opinion instead of the given rule.** You may personally prefer 20px body text, but if the question's rule says 16px minimum and the text is 16px, it passes. Tests reward reading the rule as written — and so do real standards checks.
- **Concluding more than the facts allow.** "The rule doesn't require it" is not the same as "it won't happen". Watch for answer options that quietly overreach, like option A in Question 6.
- **Ranking by ease instead of impact.** Under time pressure it is tempting to prioritise quick fixes. Both in tests and at work, ask "who is harmed, and how much?" first.
- **Rushing character-level checks.** Naming and specification errors hide in single characters — a capital letter, a swapped number. Slow down for exactly those questions; they are designed to punish skimming.
- **Panicking when questions get harder.** On adaptive tests, harder questions usually mean you are performing well. Treat difficulty as a good sign and keep your method steady.
- **Skipping the explanation when you got the answer right.** A correct answer reached by luck teaches nothing. The worked reasoning is where the learning lives — read it every time.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures how confidently and accurately you work with the numbers that appear in your job. This is not a maths exam. There is no algebra, no trigonometry, and nothing you did not meet at school. Instead, the test presents tables, charts, and short data descriptions drawn from realistic work situations, and asks questions that need percentages, ratios, averages, simple rates, and careful chart reading.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 20 to 30 minutes with 15 to 20 questions. Each question usually shows a small data set — a table of analytics, a bar chart of test results, a budget line — and four or five answer options. A basic on-screen calculator is often provided, and rough paper is normally allowed. As with cognitive tests, scoring compares you with a norm group, some platforms adapt difficulty as you go, and employers see accuracy as well as speed. Expect the early questions to be single-step calculations and the later ones to need two or three steps chained together.

Why does a graphic designer need numeric reasoning? Because modern design work is quietly full of numbers. Colour contrast is expressed as a ratio, and accessibility standards set numeric thresholds for it. Type and spacing live on numeric scales. Layouts are built on grids with column widths and gutters measured in pixels. Analytics arrive as percentages and counts: completion rates, click rates, drop-offs. Usability findings arrive as "3 of 5 participants". Image files have dimensions and sizes that affect page performance. When you analyse, synthesise and explain evidence such as web analytics data — a named part of your Evidence-based design skill — you are doing numeric reasoning. When you check a design against accessibility regulations with support, you are comparing measured values against numeric standards.

At junior level, employers are not looking for a statistician. They want to see that you can read a table without misreading a row, take a percentage without slipping a decimal point, compare two ratios fairly, and notice when a number cannot be right. Those four abilities cover almost everything in this section — and almost everything the job will throw at you.

A gentle note if numbers make you nervous: numeric anxiety is common, including among talented designers, and it responds very well to practice. Every question below shows its arithmetic step by step. Follow the steps, copy the method, and you will find the same question types feel much friendlier the second time around.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Percentages and rates** map to **Evidence-based design**: analysing and clearly explaining web analytics data means handling completion rates, drop-off percentages, and week-on-week changes without error, and explaining them in plain words to your team.
- **Ratios** map to **Designing for everyone**: colour contrast is a ratio (such as 4.5:1), and ensuring a design meets appropriate standards, with support, means comparing measured ratios against required thresholds correctly.
- **Reading tables and charts** maps to **Design communication** and **Evidence-based design**: before you can clearly explain evidence to others, you must extract the right numbers from the right rows — and when you present data visually yourself, understanding how charts can mislead helps you design honest ones.
- **Averages and simple summaries** map to working with researchers or analysts to inform, develop and test a design idea: usability timings, satisfaction scores, and test results usually arrive as averages, and you need to know what an average does and does not tell you.
- **Grid and dimension arithmetic** maps to **Iterative design**: prototyping at different fidelities and building layouts on grids involves quick, reliable arithmetic with column widths, gutters, margins, and image dimensions.
- **Proportional scaling** maps to the craft core of your role — layout, spacing, colour, type and iconography: resizing images and graphic elements without distortion is ratio arithmetic, done many times a day.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Reading an analytics table**

Your analyst shares this table of weekly visits to three pages you design for:

| Page | Visits this week | Visits last week |
|---|---|---|
| Start page | 5,200 | 5,000 |
| Help page | 1,300 | 1,300 |
| Contact page | 800 | 1,000 |

Which page had fewer visits this week than last week?

- A) Start page
- B) Help page
- C) Contact page
- D) None of them

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Compare each row, this week against last week. Start page: 5,200 versus 5,000 — an increase of 200. Help page: 1,300 versus 1,300 — no change. Contact page: 800 versus 1,000 — a decrease of 200. Only the contact page fell, so the answer is C. Easy table questions like this are really tests of careful row-reading: the most common error is answering from the wrong row or the wrong column, especially under time pressure. A reliable habit is to put your finger (or cursor) on the row you are reading and say the comparison to yourself: "800 this week, 1,000 last week — down." That tiny bit of deliberateness prevents most table mistakes, in tests and in real analytics reviews alike.

**Question 2 (easy) — A simple percentage from analytics**

The start page received 5,200 visits this week. Of those, 1,300 visitors clicked the green "Start now" button. What percentage of visitors clicked the button?

- A) 20%
- B) 25%
- C) 30%
- D) 40%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The percentage is the part divided by the whole, times 100. Step 1: part ÷ whole = 1,300 ÷ 5,200 = 0.25. Step 2: 0.25 × 100 = 25%. So one visitor in four clicked the button — answer B. A quick sense-check makes this even easier: 1,300 is exactly a quarter of 5,200 (because 1,300 × 4 = 5,200), and a quarter is 25%. Building the habit of sense-checking ("is the answer roughly what I'd expect?") catches decimal slips, like accidentally getting 2.5% or 250%. Click-through percentages like this are bread-and-butter Evidence-based design: when you and your senior designer discuss whether the start button works, this is the number you will be discussing.

**Question 3 (easy) — Percentage change after an iteration**

Last month, 400 users per week completed a form. After your team's design iteration, 500 users per week complete it. What is the percentage increase?

- A) 20%
- B) 25%
- C) 80%
- D) 100%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Percentage change is the change divided by the original value, times 100. Step 1: change = 500 − 400 = 100. Step 2: change ÷ original = 100 ÷ 400 = 0.25. Step 3: 0.25 × 100 = 25%. The answer is B. The classic trap is dividing by the new value instead of the original: 100 ÷ 500 = 20%, which is exactly option A — test writers include it deliberately. Remember the rule: percentage change always compares against where you started. This calculation is how you will report the impact of design iterations — "completions rose 25% after we simplified the layout" — which is precisely the iterate-and-measure loop in your Iterative design skill, and a very satisfying sentence to be able to say with confidence.

**Question 4 (easy) — Scaling an image proportionally**

A photo is 1,200 pixels wide and 800 pixels tall. You need to scale it down so it is 600 pixels wide, keeping the same proportions. How tall will it be?

- A) 200 pixels
- B) 300 pixels
- C) 400 pixels
- D) 500 pixels

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Keeping proportions means multiplying both dimensions by the same factor. Step 1: find the scale factor from the widths: 600 ÷ 1,200 = 0.5 (the image is being halved). Step 2: apply the same factor to the height: 800 × 0.5 = 400 pixels. The answer is C. You can also think in ratios: the width-to-height ratio is 1,200:800, which simplifies to 3:2, and 600:400 is also 3:2 — the proportions are preserved. Designers do this arithmetic constantly when preparing images and graphic elements at different sizes, and getting it wrong produces stretched or squashed images that instantly look unprofessional. In a test, watch for the trap answer that halves only one dimension or subtracts instead of scaling (800 − 600 = 200 is option A's bait).

**Question 5 (moderate) — Colour contrast ratios**

Accessibility guidance requires normal body text to have a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 against its background, and large text to have at least 3:1. You measure four text elements:

| Element | Text type | Measured contrast |
|---|---|---|
| Body paragraph | Normal | 4.6:1 |
| Caption | Normal | 4.2:1 |
| Page heading | Large | 3.2:1 |
| Hero banner text | Large | 2.9:1 |

How many elements fail the guidance?

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Check each element against the threshold for its own text type. Body paragraph: normal text needs at least 4.5, measured 4.6 — passes (4.6 is greater than 4.5). Caption: normal text needs 4.5, measured 4.2 — fails. Page heading: large text needs 3.0, measured 3.2 — passes. Hero banner: large text needs 3.0, measured 2.9 — fails. Two failures, so the answer is B. The essential skill is matching each measurement to the correct threshold — a common slip is testing large text against 4.5:1, which would wrongly fail the heading too. This is the numeric heart of Designing for everyone: contrast ratios are how "legible and readable for all users" becomes checkable, and at your level you run these checks with support and bring the failures to your team with the numbers to back them up.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Grid arithmetic**

Your page layout is 960 pixels wide and uses a 12-column grid. Each of the 11 gutters between columns is 20 pixels wide, and there are no outer margins inside the 960 pixels. How wide is each column?

- A) 55 pixels
- B) 60 pixels
- C) 61.7 pixels
- D) 80 pixels

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Work step by step. Step 1: total gutter width = 11 gutters × 20 pixels = 220 pixels. Step 2: width remaining for columns = 960 − 220 = 740 pixels. Step 3: width per column = 740 ÷ 12 = 61.666..., which rounds to 61.7 pixels. The answer is C. The tempting wrong answer is D (960 ÷ 12 = 80), which forgets the gutters entirely — the single most common grid arithmetic mistake. Option B comes from wrongly assuming 12 gutters instead of 11: with columns side by side, the number of gaps between them is always one fewer than the number of columns, just as four fence posts have three gaps. Grid calculations like this underpin every layout you build, and being able to do them quickly means you can adapt a grid confidently when a template changes rather than nudging boxes until they look right.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Averages from a usability test**

In a usability test, five participants completed a task you designed, taking 40, 50, 45, 55, and 210 seconds respectively. Your researcher asks you to summarise the timings. What is the mean (average) time, and why might it be misleading here?

- A) 80 seconds; misleading because one very slow time pulls the mean far above the typical time
- B) 80 seconds; not misleading, because the mean always represents the typical user
- C) 50 seconds; misleading because five participants is too few to calculate a mean
- D) 210 seconds; the slowest user defines the experience

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Step 1: add the times: 40 + 50 + 45 + 55 + 210 = 400 seconds. Step 2: divide by the number of participants: 400 ÷ 5 = 80 seconds. So the mean is 80 seconds. But look at the individual times: four participants took between 40 and 55 seconds, and one took 210 — almost four times longer than anyone else. That single outlier drags the mean up to 80 seconds, a time slower than four of the five people actually experienced. The middle value (the median, 50 seconds) describes the typical experience better here, and the outlier deserves its own investigation — perhaps that participant hit a genuine usability problem worth understanding. Option A captures both the arithmetic and the caveat. This is exactly the judgement you need when working with researchers and analysts: report the number and what it hides, so your team is informed rather than misled.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Ratios in a colour palette**

Your team's guidance says page designs should use the primary colour, secondary colour, and accent colour in the ratio 6:3:1 by area. A draft page of 900 total colour-area units uses 500 units of primary, 300 units of secondary, and 100 units of accent. Which colour is furthest from its target?

- A) Primary
- B) Secondary
- C) Accent
- D) All three match the guidance

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** First convert the ratio to targets. The ratio 6:3:1 has 6 + 3 + 1 = 10 parts, so each part is 900 ÷ 10 = 90 units. Targets: primary 6 × 90 = 540 units; secondary 3 × 90 = 270 units; accent 1 × 90 = 90 units. Now compare with actuals: primary 500 versus 540 — short by 40; secondary 300 versus 270 — over by 30; accent 100 versus 90 — over by 10. The biggest gap is primary at 40 units, so the answer is A. The method — total the ratio parts, find the unit value, scale up, compare — works for every ratio question, and it appears in design work more often than you might expect: balancing colour areas, splitting layout space, and even planning how many of each icon style a set needs. Note that the question asks which is furthest from target, not which is over target — read the question stem precisely.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Reading a bar chart honestly**

A stakeholder shows a bar chart of task completion rates: this month's bar (82%) towers over last month's bar (78%) because the chart's vertical axis starts at 75% rather than 0%. The stakeholder says completion "more than doubled". What is the accurate description of the change?

- A) Completion rose by 4 percentage points, from 78% to 82%.
- B) Completion doubled, as the bars show.
- C) Completion rose by 50%, matching the bar heights.
- D) The data cannot be interpreted from a truncated chart.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Read the values, not the bar heights. Completion went from 78% to 82%: a rise of 82 − 78 = 4 percentage points (as a relative change, 4 ÷ 78 ≈ 0.051, or about 5%). The bars only look dramatically different because the axis starts at 75%, so the visible bar heights represent 3 points and 7 points — the truncation exaggerates the change visually, which is what fooled the stakeholder. Option A states the change accurately. Option D goes too far: a truncated chart can mislead the eye, but the labelled values remain perfectly readable. This question matters doubly for you. As a reader of evidence, you must not be fooled by chart tricks. As a maker of graphics — someone who may design charts and data displays — you carry responsibility for honest visual communication: axis choices are design decisions, and explaining them clearly is part of Design communication.

**Question 10 (hard) — Two-step funnel percentages**

A service journey has two steps. This week, 2,000 users started step one, and 70% of them completed it. Of those who completed step one, 60% went on to complete step two. How many users completed the whole journey, and what overall percentage of starters is that?

- A) 840 users; 42%
- B) 1,200 users; 60%
- C) 1,400 users; 70%
- D) 600 users; 30%

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Chain the percentages carefully. Step 1: completions of step one = 2,000 × 0.70 = 1,400 users. Step 2: completions of step two = 1,400 × 0.60 = 840 users. Step 3: overall completion percentage = 840 ÷ 2,000 = 0.42 = 42%. The answer is A. Notice the shortcut: multiplying the two rates directly gives the overall rate, 0.70 × 0.60 = 0.42. The classic errors are stopping after one step (1,400 users, option C), applying the second percentage to the original 2,000 (2,000 × 0.60 = 1,200, option B), or adding rather than chaining. Funnel arithmetic like this is the backbone of analysing a multi-page journey — exactly the evidence you and your analyst will examine when deciding which step of a service needs design attention, as you practised in the cognitive section.

**Question 11 (hard) — Comparing proportions fairly**

Two versions of a page were shown to different numbers of users. Version A: 320 of 400 users completed the task. Version B: 405 of 540 users completed the task. Your senior designer asks which version performed better on completion rate. What should you report?

- A) Version B, because 405 completions is more than 320.
- B) Version A, because its completion rate is 80% versus 75% for B.
- C) They performed equally.
- D) Version B, because it was tested on more users, making it more reliable.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Raw counts cannot be compared when the group sizes differ — you must convert to rates. Version A: 320 ÷ 400 = 0.80 = 80%. Version B: 405 ÷ 540 = 0.75 = 75%. Version A's completion rate is 5 percentage points higher, so the answer is B. Option A makes the classic mistake of comparing counts across unequal groups. Option D confuses two different ideas: a larger sample does make an estimate more stable, but it does not change which observed rate is higher, and 400 versus 540 users are both reasonably sized groups. When you report this to your team, say both numbers plainly: "A completed at 80%, B at 75%." Converting to comparable rates before comparing is one of the most useful habits in all of evidence-based design — it appears in test results, analytics, accessibility audit summaries, and almost every table an analyst will ever hand you.

**Question 12 (hard) — Percentage points versus percentages**

An accessibility audit found that 20% of pages in your service had at least one contrast failure. After a quarter of fixes, 15% of pages have a failure. A teammate drafts two summary sentences: (i) "Failures fell by 5%"; (ii) "Failures fell by a quarter". Which assessment of the sentences is correct?

- A) Only (i) is accurate.
- B) Only (ii) is accurate.
- C) Both are accurate if "%" in (i) means percentage points; (ii) is the relative change.
- D) Neither is accurate.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Work out both kinds of change. The absolute change is 20% − 15% = 5 percentage points. The relative change is the drop divided by the starting level: 5 ÷ 20 = 0.25, so failures fell by 25% — a quarter. Sentence (ii) is therefore accurate as a relative change. Sentence (i) is accurate only if "5%" is understood as "5 percentage points"; written as "5%", it is ambiguous and could be misread as a relative fall of just 5%. Option C captures this precisely. The distinction sounds pedantic but has real consequences: "failures fell 5%" and "failures fell 25%" tell very different stories about the same fixes. When you clearly explain evidence — a duty in your Evidence-based design skill — choose words that cannot be misread: "failures fell from 20% of pages to 15%, a drop of one quarter". Precision in numbers is the numeric twin of legibility in type: both exist so the reader cannot misunderstand.

### Preparation tips

- **Rebuild each calculation yourself.** For every worked example above, cover the explanation and redo the arithmetic on paper. The methods are few — part ÷ whole, change ÷ original, scale factors, ratio parts — and they repeat endlessly.
- **Practise on your own service's numbers.** Ask your analyst for last month's page analytics and compute three things: the biggest percentage change, the completion rate of a journey, and one comparison of two rates. Real numbers make the methods stick.
- **Say the sense-check out loud.** Before accepting an answer, ask "is this roughly right?" If 1,300 of 5,200 clicked, the answer must be about a quarter — so 2.5% or 250% must be a slip.
- **Learn the three headline contrast numbers.** 4.5:1 for normal text and 3:1 for large text cover most day-to-day checks. Knowing the thresholds by heart frees your attention for the comparison itself.
- **Use rough paper for multi-step questions.** Write each intermediate result down. Chained percentages go wrong when you juggle them mentally.
- **Keep calm about the calculator.** If the platform provides one, practise with a similarly basic calculator beforehand — a phone in simple mode is ideal. Fumbling an unfamiliar tool costs more time than the maths.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Dividing by the wrong base.** Percentage change divides by the original value, not the new one. This single slip accounts for a large share of all wrong answers.
- **Comparing counts when group sizes differ.** Always convert to rates first, as in Question 11. Bigger group, bigger counts — it proves nothing by itself.
- **Forgetting the gutters.** In any layout arithmetic, account for gutters and margins before dividing space among columns. And remember: gaps between items number one fewer than the items.
- **Muddling percentage points with percentages.** A fall from 20% to 15% is 5 points but 25% relative. Write whichever you mean unambiguously.
- **Trusting bar heights over labelled values.** Truncated axes exaggerate change. Read the numbers; the bars are decoration until you have checked the scale.
- **Letting one outlier speak for the group.** An average dragged by an outlier misdescribes the typical user. Notice outliers, report them separately, and investigate them — they are often where the interesting design problem hides.
