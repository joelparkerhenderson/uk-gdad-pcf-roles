## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation and applied problem solving through operational scenarios rather than abstract items. At head level, the scenarios shift altitude: less "diagnose this incident" and more "diagnose this portfolio" — patterns across services, quarters and teams; deductions through policy, contract and architecture simultaneously; error checking of the analyses and assurances others have already signed; prioritisation of organisational attention rather than personal task lists; and problem solving where the constraints are budget, capability and risk appetite as much as dependency and time.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 15 to 30 minutes, adaptive difficulty, with accuracy and speed reported separately against senior norm groups. Expect multi-source questions: a table, a policy extract and a claim that must be reconciled; a pattern whose significance depends on what it implies for strategy rather than for tonight's shift.

Employers use cognitive assessment at this level because the role concentrates *judgement about judgement*. You rarely execute the analysis; you decide whether analyses are sound, whether patterns warrant investment, whether assurances deserve belief, and where the organisation's attention should go. Errors at that layer are expensive precisely because they are wholesale: a head who misreads one capacity trend misallocates a budget line; a head who accepts one flawed assurance model inherits every incident it fails to prevent. The practice below trains the specific reasoning acts your signature commits the organisation to.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **Service focus** and your responsibility for the **strategy roadmap**: seeing the bigger picture across groups of services now includes seeing it across time — which recurring signatures, tooling gaps and demand shifts should shape next year's roadmap rather than tonight's response.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Change management** and **Asset and configuration management**: managing high-impact, complex change and managing service compliance and risk both require deducing consequences through layered rules — policy, contract, architecture — without letting any layer slip.
- **Error checking** maps to **Problem management** and **Continuity management**: ensuring the right actions are taken to investigate, resolve and anticipate problems means auditing the causal reasoning in what you sign; managing and testing runbooks at head level means auditing the assurance, not performing the test.
- **Prioritisation** maps to **Ownership and initiative** and your duty to **address issues across IT operations**: accountability at head level is the allocation of organisational attention — yours, your managers', your budget's — to the risks only your level can retire.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Availability and capacity management** and your duty to ensure **tools are fit for purpose**: identifying capacity issues, implementing the required changes and initiating remedial action are multi-constraint problems where the constraints include procurement, capability and integration, not just arithmetic.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern across a year of majors**

Your annual review tabulates 16 major incidents: 6 traced to a single legacy integration layer; 4 to capacity exhaustion in shared components, each preceded by an unactioned trend warning; 3 to failed high-impact changes; 3 with unique causes. Which reading best serves the strategy roadmap?

- A) Sixteen majors is the headline; target a 25% reduction across the board.
- B) Two structural concentrations dominate: the legacy integration layer (6) is an architectural liability for the roadmap, and the four capacity majors reveal a process gap — warnings are generated but not actioned — which is cheaper to fix than the architecture and should move first.
- C) The three failed changes prove change governance is the priority.
- D) The unique causes show incidents are random; no pattern exists.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Head-level pattern reading means resolving a distribution into differently-shaped problems with differently-shaped fixes. The integration layer's 6 is an *asset* problem: it belongs on the roadmap as remediation or replacement, with investment and time. The capacity cluster's 4 is subtler and more valuable: warnings existed and were unactioned, so the defect is the trend-to-action pathway — a process and ownership fix, achievable in weeks, that converts existing monitoring into prevented incidents. Sequencing the cheap process fix ahead of the expensive architectural one is portfolio logic, and B states both the diagnosis and the sequence. Option A treats the total as the object — across-the-board targets spread attention evenly over concentrated causes, the opposite of what the data supports. Option C elevates the smallest cluster; 3 of 16 merits review, not priority. Option D misreads the residual: three unique causes alongside two concentrations is a normal distribution of operational failure, and the concentrations are the signal. This is your service focus and roadmap duty in one exercise: annual incident data is strategy input, and the skill is reading it as a portfolio of causes, not a count.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction through policy and contract together**

Your release policy: "High-impact changes to citizen-facing services require a tested rollback and CAB approval." Your supplier contract for the managed hosting platform: "Supplier-initiated platform maintenance is not subject to customer change approval, but the supplier must give 10 working days' notice of maintenance affecting service availability." The supplier emails: "Platform upgrade in 12 days; brief availability impact expected on hosted citizen services; as per contract, proceeding under our maintenance provisions." Which statement must be true?

- A) The upgrade requires your CAB's approval because citizen-facing services are affected.
- B) The supplier has breached the contract's notice requirement.
- C) Your change policy does not bind the supplier's maintenance, and the contractual notice condition is met (12 days ≥ 10 working days is not yet established — 12 calendar days may be fewer than 10 working days, so notice compliance actually cannot be confirmed without a calendar).
- D) The supplier's maintenance is exempt from your CAB, and whether the 10-working-day notice is satisfied depends on whether the 12 days contain at least 10 working days — which the email alone does not establish.

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Two instruments govern, and each must be applied to its own subject. Your release policy binds *your* changes; the contract explicitly carves supplier maintenance out of customer approval — so option A fails: the CAB has no purchase, however citizen-facing the impact. The notice question turns on a unit trap: the contract counts *working* days, the email counts days. Twelve calendar days spanning two weekends contains only 8 working days; twelve working days would comfortably satisfy. The email does not say which, so breach can neither be affirmed (option B) nor denied — option D holds exactly this: exemption established, notice undetermined pending a calendar check. Option C reasons correctly and then contradicts its own parenthesis — an option whose conclusion and reasoning diverge is wrong even when the reasoning is right, and spotting that is itself an error-checking exercise. The head-level habit this trains: contracts and policies use defined units (working days, continuous minutes, quarters), and compliance verdicts issued before the units are checked are guesses. Your service compliance and risk duty lives in precisely these definitional seams — as does the practical action: reply seeking the maintenance date, then count.

**Question 3 (easy) — Error checking a compliance dashboard**

Your quarterly compliance dashboard reports: "Runbook currency: 94% (47 of 50 runbooks reviewed within 12 months). Continuity tests: 100% of scheduled tests completed. Configuration accuracy: 98.2% (sample audit)." A footnote reads: "Continuity test schedule revised in Q2; tests for 8 services deferred to next year as their runbooks were under review." What is the most important observation?

- A) The dashboard shows strong compliance across all three measures.
- B) The 100% continuity figure is true of the *revised* schedule but conceals that 8 services went untested this year — the denominator was edited to fit the numerator, and those 8 services' continuity assurance is a year staler than the dashboard's headline implies.
- C) The 94% runbook currency is the weakest figure and should be the priority.
- D) The sample-based configuration figure is meaningless without the sample size.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The dashboard's most dangerous number is its best one. "100% of scheduled tests completed" is literally true and materially misleading: the schedule itself was revised mid-year to remove 8 services, so the metric measures conformance to a shrunk plan, not assurance of the estate. Denominator management — editing the base until the percentage pleases — is the signature pathology of compliance reporting, and the footnote is where it always lives. Option B also names the real-world consequence: 8 services carrying stale continuity assurance, invisible at headline level. Option C picks the number that *looks* weakest, which is the trap's mirror image: 94% with an honest denominator is better information than 100% with a managed one. Option D raises a fair methodological point that is second-order here — a sample audit with undisclosed size is a quality issue; a full metric with an edited base is an integrity issue. Option A is the signature the pathology needs. Your asset and configuration skill at head level — manage service compliance and risk — is largely the discipline of reading dashboards as arguments rather than facts: every percentage has a denominator, every denominator has a history, and the footnotes are load-bearing.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Prioritisation of the head's attention**

Monday, 08:30. Four items in your queue: (i) final sign-off on the monitoring tool procurement — the business case is solid, your operational control managers are unanimous, the deadline is Friday; (ii) a request from your director to "have a look at" a peer division's incident data as a favour, no deadline; (iii) the third consecutive monthly rise in a junior team's attrition, now visible in the workforce report, cause unknown; (iv) a vendor briefing on an AI operations product your roadmap tentatively references for next year. Where does your personal attention go first, and why?

- A) Item (i) — the deadline is nearest and the sum is largest.
- B) Item (iii) — it is the only item where something is genuinely unknown, worsening, and unowned; the procurement is decision-ready and can be signed after, the favour and the briefing can be scheduled.
- C) Item (ii) — director requests take precedence.
- D) Item (iv) — strategy must lead operations.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Distinguish decision-ready from investigation-needed. Item (i) is large but *finished thinking*: unanimous managers, solid case, Friday deadline — it needs your signature and perhaps an hour's final scrutiny, schedulable with certainty. Items (ii) and (iv) are elastic: schedulable favours and briefings. Item (iii) is the discriminating case: a worsening trend, cause unknown, and — the head-level tell — *unowned*: attrition in a junior team shows up in no one else's objectives until it becomes a capability crisis, and building the capability of the team is your named responsibility. Three consecutive rises is a pattern, not noise; the cause (a manager problem? workload? pay banding? a competitor hiring?) determines the remedy, and every month of delay compounds the loss — experienced analysts leave, then the experience gap drives more leaving. First attention goes to commissioning the inquiry: a conversation with the team's manager, exit interview data, a skip-level or two — an hour that starts the diagnosis. Option A confuses size with need; decision-ready items don't improve with earlier attention. Option C converts courtesy into priority. Option D mistakes a vendor's calendar for strategy. The principle, identical in shape to escalation-level triage but at organisational altitude: your attention is the scarcest resource in the function, and it belongs to what is worsening, unknown and unowned — not to what is large, loud or senior.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Integration logic in tooling strategy**

Your roadmap must consolidate monitoring: today, four tools cover infrastructure, application performance, user-experience and security monitoring, each with its own alerting; correlation across them is manual, and post-incident reviews repeatedly cite "correlation delay" as a resolution-time driver. Three consolidation options are costed: (1) replace all four with a single-vendor platform — highest cost, longest migration, one alerting model; (2) keep all four, build an event-correlation layer that ingests from each — lowest cost, fastest, adds a fifth component to operate; (3) consolidate infrastructure and application tools into one platform now (the two whose correlation gap drives most cited delay), defer user-experience and security consolidation, revisit in 18 months. Post-incident data shows 80% of correlation-delay citations involve exactly the infrastructure-application boundary. Which option does the evidence best support?

- A) Option 1 — a single pane of glass is the recognised end-state.
- B) Option 2 — lowest cost and fastest is the fiduciary choice.
- C) Option 3 — it applies 20%-effort-to-80%-cited-problem, tests consolidation on the highest-value boundary before committing to the full end-state, and keeps the security tool's specialised requirements out of a general-purpose migration.
- D) None — more analysis is needed before any choice.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Anchor on the evidence: 80% of the cited correlation delay lives at one boundary. Option 3 spends effort exactly there, and its deferred remainder is not indecision but sequenced learning — the 18-month revisit happens with real data about whether consolidation delivered the resolution-time gains the reviews predict. It also respects a domain fact option 1 flattens: security monitoring has requirements (retention, access separation, audit) that general-purpose platforms satisfy poorly, and forcing it into a single-vendor migration imports risk for the 20% of the problem it barely touches. Option 1's "recognised end-state" is architecture by brochure — end-states are hypotheses until your estate's data supports them. Option 2's correlation layer answers the cost question and dodges the operational one: a fifth component, operated by your team, whose failure modes sit precisely at the correlation point the incidents already cite — complexity added at the exact spot complexity is the problem. Option D fails the evidence already in hand; "more analysis" is the head-level procrastination that dresses as rigour. This is your tools-fit-for-purpose duty exercised as reasoning: fitness is demonstrated against *your* incident data and *your* requirements, sequenced to learn before it commits — and the 80/20 structure of the answer is worth keeping as a template for every integration decision the roadmap contains.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Error checking an availability improvement claim**

A paper for your sign-off claims: "Since the resilience programme began in January, major incidents have fallen from 11 (previous year) to 6 (this year to October) — a 45% reduction demonstrating programme effectiveness. Recommend extending the programme with £250k next year." Independently, you know: this year's figure covers 10 months; two services were migrated out of your estate in March; and the previous year included a single cluster of 4 majors from one firmware defect since fixed by the vendor. What is the soundest assessment?

- A) The reduction is real and the extension justified as argued.
- B) Annualise first: 6 in 10 months projects to ~7.2 in 12 — against 11, a ~35% fall, not 45%. Then adjust the baseline: 4 of the prior year's 11 came from one vendor-fixed defect (leaving ~7 comparable), and the estate shrank by two services in March. The like-for-like comparison is roughly 7 against 7 — the data does not yet demonstrate programme effect, which neither proves nor disproves the programme; the paper must be sent back for an attribution analysis before £250k rides on it.
- C) The programme has failed and should be cancelled.
- D) Incident counts are too noisy to analyse; decide on judgement alone.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Run the three corrections in order. Periods: 6 incidents in 10 months is a 7.2-incident annual pace; comparing 10 months against 12 inflates any reduction — the claimed 45% becomes ~35% before anything else is examined. Baseline composition: a 4-incident cluster from a single, externally-fixed cause is not part of the recurring baseline the programme could have affected; the comparable prior-year figure is nearer 7. Denominator: two services left the estate in March, so even a genuine fall partly reflects a smaller estate. After corrections, the like-for-like picture is approximately flat — which, as B states carefully, does not show the programme failed (option C overshoots symmetrically: perhaps underlying risk rose and the programme held the line; perhaps its benefits lag) but does show the paper's *evidence does not carry its claim*, and £250k should wait for an attribution analysis: which incident classes was the programme designed to prevent, and what happened to those specifically? Option D abandons analysis because the first analysis was flawed — the corrections themselves prove the data is analysable. The head-level point is about your signature: the paper's arithmetic errors each flatter the programme, which is the direction motivated arithmetic always leans; the discipline of annualise-adjust-attribute before funding is how ensuring "the right actions are taken" — your problem management duty's phrasing — applies to programmes and not just incidents.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Deduction about capability coverage**

Your on-call rota requires two qualified incident leads available at all times. Qualification requires completing your incident leadership programme (6 months) plus shadowing (3 months). Today you have 5 qualified leads. Known futures: one retires in 4 months; one starts maternity leave in 6 months (12 months away); promotion of one into a day-role that removes rota eligibility is agreed for month 8. Two candidates entered the programme last month. When does coverage first become impossible to guarantee, and what does that imply now?

- A) Month 8 — when the third lead leaves the rota, 2 remain, exactly meeting the requirement with zero resilience; but "two available at all times" across leave and sickness cannot be guaranteed with only 2 qualified people, so the genuine exposure begins at month 6 when the pool drops to 3.
- B) Month 12 — when the maternity leave begins.
- C) Never — the two candidates qualify in time.
- D) Month 4 — the retirement immediately breaks the requirement.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Track the pool arithmetic against the requirement's real meaning. Pool: 5 now; 4 from month 4 (retirement); 3 from month 6 (maternity — note it starts at month 6 per the stated timeline: "in 6 months"); 2 from month 8 (promotion). The candidates entered the programme last month: 6 months' programme + 3 months' shadowing completes around month 8–9 — arriving at or after the crunch, not before (option C's comfort requires ignoring the shadowing). Now interpret the requirement: "two available at all times" is not "two employed" — with a pool of exactly 2, any leave, sickness or training day breaks guarantee; sustained compliance needs a pool of 4–5 given normal absence rates. So the hard breach risk is month 8, but the *planning* exposure begins at month 6 with a pool of 3 — and option A holds both layers. Option D misreads 4 remaining as breach; option B misorders the timeline. The implications now, month 0: accelerate the two candidates if quality permits (shadowing overlapping the programme's tail?), recruit or borrow a qualified lead, negotiate the promotion's rota-exit date, and — the durable fix — treat qualified-lead coverage as a monitored capacity metric with the same projection discipline you apply to storage. Building the capability of the team, your role summary's phrase, is exactly this: capability is capacity with a nine-month lead time, and heads who model it arithmetically stop discovering rota crises four weeks out.

**Question 8 (moderate) — The anomaly in a familiar report**

Your monthly service report has shown user-experience availability within 0.2 points of infrastructure availability for eighteen consecutive months. This month: infrastructure 99.7%, user-experience 97.1% — a 2.6-point gap — while incident counts, change volumes and desk contacts are all normal. The report's commentary does not mention the gap. What is the best first move?

- A) Accept the report; all the operational indicators are normal.
- B) Treat the divergence of two normally-coupled metrics as the month's most important finding despite — indeed because of — the normal indicators everywhere else: commission a specific investigation (measurement change? new user path outside infrastructure monitoring? synthetic-check blind spot? user-experience tool defect?) and ask why the commentary process failed to surface a 13-fold departure from the historical gap.
- C) Ask for the user-experience figure to be re-measured; it is probably wrong.
- D) Report the infrastructure figure upward this month since the two usually agree.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Eighteen months of tight coupling makes the *relationship* between these metrics an instrument in its own right — and this month the instrument moved while every conventional indicator stayed still. That combination is the interesting case: if incidents, changes and contacts are normal but users experienced 2.9% unavailability (versus infrastructure's 0.3%), then either something real is harming users invisibly to your operational telemetry (a new failure mode below alerting thresholds; a path — CDN, third-party dependency, client-side — outside infrastructure monitoring) or one measurement system has silently changed or broken. Every branch matters: two are estate problems, one is an instrumentation problem, and all three are invisible to a process that only investigates when operational indicators move. Option C picks one branch by assumption — "probably wrong" is a hypothesis, not a finding, and re-measurement without investigation would bury the estate branches. Option D is the disqualifying move: knowingly reporting the flattering metric because the honest one is inconvenient converts an anomaly into a concealment. Option A outsources attention to indicators the anomaly has just demonstrated are insufficient. B's second limb is equally head-level: the commentary's silence on a 13-fold gap departure is a *reporting process* defect — someone compiled that page without the relationship check — and fixing the process (automated divergence flags, commentary standards) is what prevents next month's silent anomaly. Your user focus skill at this level — insight into how user needs and experience move over time — depends on exactly these instruments staying trustworthy; the head's job is noticing when they stop.

**Question 9 (hard) — Multi-constraint remediation sequencing**

Audit has issued three findings with deadlines: (F1) legacy integration layer lacks failover — remediate within 12 months (est. 8 months' work, £400k); (F2) monitoring gaps on two citizen services — remediate within 6 months (est. 3 months, £80k); (F3) continuity tests outstanding for 8 services (from Question 3) — complete within 9 months (est. 4 months elapsed, mostly coordination). Constraints: the same two senior engineers are critical-path for F1 and F2; continuity tests (F3) require change-freeze windows that conflict with F1's migration phases; budget releases quarterly at £150k. Which plan satisfies all deadlines?

- A) Sequence F1 first (months 1–8), F2 next (9–11), F3 last (12–15) — biggest risk first.
- B) F2 first (months 1–3, engineers dedicated, £80k inside Q1's release), F3 in parallel from month 1 (coordination-led, using pre-migration freeze windows, completing ~month 5), then F1 from month 4 with both engineers freed (months 4–11, £400k spread across remaining quarterly releases) — F2 done month 3 (deadline 6), F3 month 5 (deadline 9), F1 month 11 (deadline 12).
- C) F1 and F2 in parallel from month 1, sharing the engineers; F3 from month 6.
- D) Request deadline extensions for all three before planning.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Test each plan against deadlines, resources and windows. Option A sequences by risk size and breaches immediately: F2's 6-month deadline dies at month 9, and F3's tests collide with F1's migration freezes throughout. Option C's parallelism is fictional — two critical-path engineers shared across F1 and F2 halves both, pushing F2 toward month 6 (knife-edge) and F1 toward month 16 (breach); resource-constrained parallelism is sequential work with coordination overhead added. Option D concedes before analysing — and B proves the concession unnecessary. Walk B: F2's short, cheap, deadline-tight work takes the engineers first and lands month 3, comfortably inside its 6-month limit and Q1's £150k release covers its £80k. F3 is coordination-led — it doesn't need the engineers — and running it *before* F1's migration begins converts the freeze-window conflict into a scheduling convenience: pre-migration months are freeze-rich. F1 then gets both engineers from month 4, an 8-month run landing month 11 against a 12-month deadline, with £400k drawn across Q2–Q4 releases (£150k × 3 = £450k available). Every constraint audits clean, with a month of margin on the binding deadline. The construction heuristics are the transferable content: schedule the deadline-tightest short work first when it shares scarce resources with long work; route non-competing workstreams around the resource constraint entirely; and treat sequencing conflicts (freezes) as potentially *dissolvable by ordering* rather than fixed. This is availability-and-capacity remedial action as your summary means it — identify, implement, initiate — expressed as a feasibility proof you can defend to audit committee line by line.

**Question 10 (hard) — Judging a problem investigation portfolio**

Reviewing your problem management function's quarterly output: 22 problem records closed. You sample the closures: 9 closed with root cause identified and preventative action completed; 6 closed as "no longer reproducible" after low investigation effort; 4 closed with workarounds documented but root cause "not economically justifiable to pursue"; 3 closed with root cause found and preventative actions raised but transferred to delivery teams' backlogs, status unknown. Which assessment best serves the function?

- A) A 100% closure achievement — the function is performing.
- B) Four distinct closure qualities are being counted as one outcome: the 9 are genuine; the 6 "not reproducible" closures at low effort are a recurrence pool (intermittent problems close themselves and return); the 4 economic judgements may each be sound but need a visible risk-acceptance record with an owner, not a closure; and the 3 with actions lost into unowned backlogs are prevention promised but not delivered. The function needs closure categories with different exit criteria, recurrence tracking against "not reproducible" closures, and a followed-through path for transferred actions.
- C) The 6 "not reproducible" closures should be prohibited; problems must stay open until reproduced.
- D) The sample is too small to conclude anything about the function.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** "Closed" is doing four different jobs in this portfolio, and head-level review means separating them because each has a different failure mode. The 9 full closures are the function working. The 6 "not reproducible" closures are individually reasonable and collectively a signature: intermittent problems — the hardest and often most important class — present exactly this way, and a function under closure-count pressure learns to let them close; the control is not prohibition (option C would freeze the register with unfalsifiable open records) but *recurrence tracking*: tag such closures, and when the same signature returns, it reopens with its history attached rather than starting as a new record. The 4 economic closures contain a category error your compliance-and-risk duty should catch: "root cause not worth pursuing" is a *risk acceptance*, and risk acceptances need owners, review dates and visibility in the risk register — filed as mere closures, they become permanent invisible acceptances no one ever re-examines as the cost-benefit shifts. The 3 transferred-action closures are the quiet defect with the largest prevention leak: the problem function's output was handed to backlogs nobody audits, so "preventative measures determined" became "preventative measures deferred indefinitely" — the fix is a tracked handover with status visibility, which is precisely your summary's "co-ordinate the team to investigate problems, implement solutions and take preventive measures" applied across team boundaries. Option A reads the count; option D hides behind sample size when the finding is structural (categories and their exit criteria), not statistical. The instrument to leave with: closure quality categories, each with its own exit criterion and follow-up — because what gets counted as done defines what the function actually does.

**Question 11 (hard) — Strategic deduction from user-channel data**

Five-year channel data for a citizen service your centre supports: phone contact share fallen 60%→35%; digital share risen 30%→58%; assisted-digital stable at ~7%. Overnight digital usage (22:00–06:00) has tripled and now carries 18% of digital transactions. Your operational posture is unchanged from five years ago: full support 08:00–18:00, skeleton overnight cover, maintenance windows concentrated 01:00–04:00. Deduce the strategic misalignments and the appropriate roadmap response.

- A) None — the service meets its availability targets on current measures.
- B) Three misalignments: support weighting still mirrors the old channel mix (phone-heavy day support for a now digital-majority service); maintenance windows sit inside a window now carrying material and growing usage — the "quiet hours" assumption has expired; and the availability measures themselves, set in the phone era, likely under-weight overnight digital harm. Roadmap response: rebalance support capability toward digital-incident response, re-derive maintenance windows from current usage-trough data with a periodic review trigger, and re-specify availability measures to weight when users actually transact — while protecting the stable assisted-digital minority in any change.
- C) Close the phone channel to fund digital support, since the trend is clear.
- D) Move maintenance to daytime, when staff are plentiful.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The deduction runs from data to expired assumptions to roadmap items. Assumption one: support posture was designed when contact was phone-heavy and daytime; at 58% digital, incident response capability (who is skilled, when they are rostered) should track where failures now harm users — a capability rebalance, not just a rota change. Assumption two: the 01:00–04:00 maintenance concentration was rational when overnight was dead; overnight digital has *tripled* to 18% of transactions, so the windows now sit on live usage — and the correct fix is not a one-off move but *re-derivation from current trough data plus a review trigger*, because the pattern that expired once will expire again (this is the same "temporal separation is capacity you must not spend by accident" logic as shared-peak analysis, applied to time-of-day). Assumption three is the subtle one: measures themselves age — availability targets weighted for phone-era usage can be met while overnight digital users absorb unmeasured harm, which is why option A's "meets its targets" is not reassurance but the symptom (compare the dashboard question: the instrument, not just the estate, needs auditing). The assisted-digital caveat is user focus at head level, verbatim from your summary — "meeting the needs of users across a variety of channels" and "how user needs have changed over time": a stable 7% minority through a period of channel upheaval indicates a population that *cannot* shift, and strategies that fund the trend by cutting the tail (option C) convert channel evolution into exclusion of precisely the users public services exist to reach last. Option D solves staffing by scheduling outages into peak usage — posture serving the organisation instead of the user. B is the full strategic read: user data ages operational assumptions silently; the roadmap's job is to re-derive them on evidence, on a cycle, with the vulnerable minority protected by design.

**Question 12 (hard) — The assurance chain under load**

A ministerial priority service launches in 6 weeks and will route through your command and control centre. Assurances you currently hold: the delivery programme reports "monitoring integration complete" (their own test); your tooling team reports the integration "receiving events but not yet validated against failure scenarios"; the continuity runbook exists but is untested ("no test window before launch," says the programme); and your on-call rota for the launch period shows the qualified-lead thinning from Question 7 already beginning. The programme director asks you to confirm to the senior responsible owner that "command and control is ready". What does sound reasoning require your confirmation to contain?

- A) Confirm readiness — the components exist, and the programme's own test covers the integration.
- B) Decline to confirm anything until every element is fully tested, whatever the launch consequence.
- C) A differentiated assurance: state what is verified (events flowing), what is claimed but unverified by you (integration under failure scenarios — with a proposal to run scenario validation in weeks 1–2), what is untested with a mitigation (runbook — desk walkthrough plus an on-call specialist retainer for the launch month, and a test scheduled at the first post-launch window), and what you are resourcing to guarantee (named incident leads for the launch period, borrowed if needed) — so the SRO receives a readiness statement whose every clause is either evidenced or accompanied by its compensating control and residual risk.
- D) Confirm readiness verbally but keep an internal note of the gaps in case of later challenge.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The question is what "ready" may honestly mean over your signature, and C answers with the instrument senior assurance actually requires: a differentiated statement in which every clause carries its evidence status. Walk its anatomy, because the anatomy is the lesson. *Verified*: events flowing — your team's own observation; assert it plainly. *Claimed but unverified*: the programme's "integration complete" rests on their test, and your tooling team — your evidence — says failure scenarios are unvalidated; the difference between "they tested it" and "we verified it" is the entire content of an assurance function (a head who countersigns others' self-assessments adds no assurance, only a signature), and C converts the gap into a two-week validation plan rather than a stand-off. *Untested with compensating control*: the runbook cannot be environment-tested before launch — a fact, not a failing — so the honest move is naming the mitigation (walkthrough, specialist retainer, scheduled post-launch test) and the residual risk it leaves; this is your continuity duty operating under constraint, which is where it mostly operates. *Resourced commitment*: the rota thinning is yours to fix and yours to guarantee — named leads, borrowed if necessary — because launch-period incident leadership is the one element no compensating control substitutes for. Option A signs the programme's homework. Option B is the assurance function's other failure mode: binary readiness standards that no real launch meets convert your confirmation into either an obstacle or, under pressure, a rubber stamp — differentiated assurance exists precisely so that neither happens. Option D is the integrity trap in senior clothing: confirming publicly while filing private reservations is concealment with an audit trail — worse than concealment, because the note proves you knew. The transferable template for every "confirm readiness" moment your role contains: verified / claimed-unverified with a plan / untested with mitigation and residual / committed-and-resourced. An SRO served that statement can make a launch decision; a minister's office served option A's version discovers the difference during the launch.

### Preparation tips

- **Practise on your own governance artefacts.** Last quarter's compliance dashboard, one audit response, one funding paper: re-read each hunting denominators, baselines and evidence-status per clause. Your estate's documents are the highest-fidelity question bank available.
- **Build the annualise-adjust-attribute reflex.** Every improvement claim crossing your desk gets the three corrections — period, baseline composition, denominator — before its conclusion gets your belief. Two minutes, most errors caught.
- **Model your capability pipeline numerically.** Qualified-role coverage against known departures and training lead times, projected eight quarters out. It converts workforce surprises into scheduled decisions, and the arithmetic is exactly what assessments test.
- **Rehearse differentiated assurance.** For the next readiness or compliance statement you sign, draft it in the four-clause form: verified, claimed-unverified-with-plan, untested-with-mitigation, committed. The format survives timed conditions once it is habitual.
- **Sequence one multi-constraint plan on paper monthly.** Deadlines, shared resources, windows, budget phasing — prove feasibility line by line. Audit responses and roadmap phasing both improve, and so does your speed on the hardest question class.
- **Time-box, then review your errors by type.** Under the clock, note which questions you missed and whether the cause was reading, arithmetic, or premature closure. At senior level the distribution is diagnostic: most heads miss by closing early, not by calculating wrongly.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Signing the best-looking number.** The 100% with an edited denominator is worse information than the 94% with an honest one. Read footnotes as the main text.
- **Confusing size with need for attention.** Decision-ready items, however large, need signatures; worsening-unknown-unowned items need you. Allocate accordingly, on tests and Mondays alike.
- **Architecture by end-state.** "Single pane of glass" and its cousins are hypotheses. Let your incident data, not the brochure, sequence the roadmap.
- **Accepting self-assessment as assurance.** "The programme tested it" and "we verified it" are different clauses. Differentiate or your signature launders the difference.
- **Treating expired assumptions as facts.** Quiet hours, channel mixes, phone-era measures: usage data ages every operational assumption silently. Re-derive on a cycle.
- **Closure counts as outcomes.** What gets counted as done defines what your functions actually do. Audit the categories behind every count before the count reassures you.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures fluency and rigour with the quantitative material a head of function is accountable for: budgets and their phasing, availability and risk economics, capacity investment cases, workforce and capability arithmetic, supplier commercials, and the KPI structures by which your function is judged and judges others. The format is standard — 20 to 30 minutes online, 15 to 20 questions, calculator permitted, norm-referenced against senior groups — but the content at this level leans hard toward *interrogation*: not "compute the figure" so much as "decide whether the computed figure means what the paper claims it means".

For your role the relevance is structural. You hold budget authority: every business case that reaches you argues in numbers, and approving arithmetic you haven't stress-tested is delegating your authority to whoever built the spreadsheet. You manage service compliance and risk, which is increasingly quantified — risk scores, compliance percentages, error budgets — and each quantification embeds choices (denominators, weightings, exclusions) that your review either audits or inherits. You identify capacity issues and initiate remedial action, which means adjudicating between competing projections. And you report upward: the figures your function sends to directors and boards carry your implicit warranty, so the habits tested here — decomposition, sensitivity, attribution, honest aggregation — are the quality control on that warranty.

Every explanation shows its arithmetic step by step, and — deliberately, at this level — several questions turn on the judgement *around* the arithmetic: which baseline, which denominator, which comparison is honest. That is the examinable skill your signature exercises daily.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Budget and investment arithmetic** maps to your responsibility for the **strategy roadmap** and **tools fit for purpose**: phased spend, cost-per-outcome comparisons, and total-cost-of-ownership reasoning are how roadmap choices are actually made and defended.
- **Risk and availability economics** map to **Asset and configuration management** (service compliance and risk) and **Availability and capacity management**: expected-value reasoning over incidents, error budgets and remediation options is the numerate core of risk acceptance and remedial action.
- **Workforce and capability arithmetic** maps to **building the capability of the team**: pipeline projections, coverage modelling, attrition costs and training lead times — capacity planning where the capacity is people.
- **Supplier and contract figures** map to **Change management** and **Continuity management** at the governance level: SLA verification, penalty and exclusion arithmetic, and the commercial numbers behind release and continuity commitments.
- **KPI structure and aggregation** map to **Service focus**, **User focus** and **Problem management**: choosing measures that reflect user-experienced reality across services and channels, and auditing the counts by which functions demonstrate their own performance.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Budget phasing check**

Your roadmap's tooling consolidation is costed at £520k: £190k in Q1, £150k in Q2, £110k in Q3, £70k in Q4. Finance releases £140k per quarter, with unspent allocation carrying forward. In which quarter, if any, does the plan first exceed available funds?

- A) Q1
- B) Q2
- C) Q3
- D) It never exceeds available funds

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Track cumulative availability against cumulative need. Q1: available £140k, needed £190k — short by £50k immediately. The carry-forward mechanism cannot help in Q1: there is nothing yet to carry. Check the later picture for completeness: by Q2, cumulative release is £280k against cumulative need £340k (still short £60k); by Q3, £420k against £450k (short £30k); by Q4, £560k against £520k — the year funds the plan in total, but the *profile* fails from the first quarter. This is the most common defect in roadmap financials: annual affordability presented as if it were quarterly feasibility. The remedies are standard — rephase the work, negotiate an advanced release, or re-sequence deliverables into the funding envelope — but they only get designed if the head reading the paper runs cumulative-versus-cumulative rather than comparing totals. Thirty seconds of arithmetic; a quarter of delay avoided.

**Question 2 (easy) — Cost of attrition versus cost of retention**

Your workforce data: replacing a senior analyst costs roughly £45k (recruitment, backfill gap, and a productivity ramp of 6 months at 50% effectiveness on a £60k salary — the ramp alone contributing £15k of that total). The junior-team attrition from your cognitive section is running at 6 leavers/year above historical norm. A proposed retention package (progression framework, allowances, training budget) costs £120k/year and is projected to halve the excess attrition. What is the first-year net position, and what should you check before believing it?

- A) Net cost £120k — retention packages are an expense.
- B) Excess attrition costs 6 × £45k = £270k/year; halving saves £135k against a £120k spend — net +£15k in year one, roughly break-even. Check the two load-bearing assumptions: the £45k replacement cost (audit its components against your actual recruitment data) and especially the "halve attrition" projection (what evidence — exit interview alignment with what the package addresses?). Near-break-even in year one with retained capability as the real return.
- C) Savings of £270k make it a clear win.
- D) The package should be rejected because attrition is a market condition beyond influence.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The arithmetic first: excess attrition cost = 6 × £45k = £270k annually. The package's claimed effect halves that: saving £135k. Net: £135k − £120k = +£15k — approximately break-even in cash terms, not the windfall option C claims (C takes the full £270k as saving, an effect-size error: the package addresses half the problem by its own projection). Now the head-level layer, which B makes explicit: a near-break-even case rests entirely on its two soft numbers. The £45k is auditable — your own recent hires give real recruitment and ramp figures. The "halve" is the fragile one: projections of behavioural response deserve interrogation against the diagnosis (if exit interviews cite workload and the package offers allowances, the projection is hope; if they cite progression and the package builds a framework, it has a mechanism). And the honest case for the package is not the £15k — it is that the cash arithmetic merely needs to *not lose* for the capability argument to win: six senior-analyst departures a year is an operational resilience drain (on-call depth, incident experience, mentoring capacity) that the £45k unit cost only partially prices. Option D surrenders a managed variable to fatalism. This is building-the-team arithmetic as your role practises it: retention cases are capacity investment cases, audited the same way.

**Question 3 (easy) — Weighted risk scoring**

Your risk register scores risks as likelihood (1–5) × impact (1–5). Three open risks: R1 likelihood 4, impact 2 (score 8); R2 likelihood 1, impact 5 (score 5); R3 likelihood 3, impact 3 (score 9). The register ranks remediation priority by score: R3, R1, R2. What is the strongest caveat to this ranking?

- A) The ranking is arithmetically wrong.
- B) Multiplicative scoring treats a likely-minor risk (R1) as more urgent than a rare-catastrophic one (R2), but a 1×5 risk — low-probability, maximum-impact — is exactly the class (continuity failures, security breaches) where score-driven deprioritisation is most dangerous: the impact axis is usually capped and non-linear in reality (a "5" can be an order of magnitude worse than a "4"), so tail risks need a floor rule — any impact-5 risk gets senior review regardless of score — alongside the arithmetic ranking.
- C) Likelihood should be weighted double, as it is more measurable.
- D) The register should rank alphabetically to avoid bias.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The arithmetic is right (4×2=8, 1×5=5, 3×3=9; descending: R3, R1, R2 — eliminating A), which is precisely why the question matters: the defect is in the *instrument*, not the computation. Multiplicative scores compress two different quantities into one number and silently assert linearity — that impact 5 is merely 25% worse than impact 4. For operational estates the top of the impact scale is where non-linearity lives: a maximum-impact event (sustained multi-service outage, data breach on a citizen service) is not incrementally worse; it is categorically different, and its low likelihood is often the least reliable estimate on the register (rare events resist estimation). Hence B's floor rule — impact-5 risks receive senior scrutiny regardless of score — a standard maturity feature in risk frameworks, and exactly the kind of instrument-level correction your compliance-and-risk duty exists to impose. Options C and D replace one arbitrary scheme with another. The habit for every scoring system your function operates (risk, incident priority, change impact): run the extremes through the formula and ask whether the ranking it produces is one you would defend to a select committee after the tail event occurred. If not, the formula needs a rule the arithmetic can't supply.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Comparing tooling options on total cost**

Two monitoring consolidation options over 4 years. Option P (platform): £300k implementation + £120k/year licence + one 0.5 FTE administrator (£35k/year all-in). Option Q (build on open source): £150k implementation + £20k/year hosting + 2.0 FTE engineering support (£70k/year each all-in). What are the 4-year totals, and which non-arithmetic factor most deserves weight in the decision?

- A) P: £920k; Q: £790k — Q is cheaper; choose Q.
- B) P: £300k + 4×(£120k + £35k) = £300k + £620k = £920k. Q: £150k + 4×(£20k + £140k) = £150k + £640k = £790k. Q is £130k cheaper over 4 years — but Q's cost is 71% specialist staffing: the decision should weight whether you can *sustain* 2.0 FTE of niche engineering capability (recruitment, retention, key-person risk, and the opportunity cost of those engineers not working the roadmap) against P's contractual certainty; a £130k saving that depends on retaining two specialists in a market that pays more for them is a projection, not a price.
- C) P: £820k; Q: £710k — Q is cheaper.
- D) The options cannot be compared because one involves staff.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Compute both: P = £300k + 4 × £155k = £300k + £620k = £920k. Q = £150k + 4 × (£20k + 2 × £70k) = £150k + 4 × £160k = £150k + £640k = £790k. (Option C's figures drop a year of running costs — the arithmetic near-miss.) Q wins by £130k, about 14% — real but not decisive, and B's second half is the actual decision content. Decompose Q's cost structure: £560k of its £790k is specialist staffing — the cost that behaves least like a price. It carries key-person risk (your continuity of *monitoring* now depends on retaining specific engineers — compare the qualified-lead pipeline arithmetic), market risk (niche skills inflate faster than licence fees), and opportunity cost (2.0 FTE of engineering talent operating a tool is 2.0 FTE not delivering the roadmap those tools serve). P converts those risks into a contract — more expensive, more certain, and transferable to a supplier who carries the specialist bench. Neither answer is universally right; the examinable skill is B's move of *decomposing totals by cost behaviour* (contractual vs. staffing vs. usage-variable) before comparing, because two equal totals with different compositions are different risks. Option D abandons a comparison the decomposition makes perfectly tractable. This is tools-fit-for-purpose governance in its commercial dimension: fitness includes the sustainability of the operating model, and the 4-year spreadsheet is where that question either gets asked or gets discovered.

**Question 5 (moderate) — SLA penalty arithmetic**

Your hosting contract: service credits of 2% of the £80k monthly charge per 0.1 percentage points below 99.9% availability, capped at 20% of the monthly charge. This month the supplier delivered 99.4% availability. What credit applies, and what does the cap structure tell you?

- A) £8,000 — 5 increments of 0.1pp below target × 2% × £80k.
- B) The shortfall is 99.9% − 99.4% = 0.5pp = 5 increments; 5 × 2% = 10% of £80k = £8,000. The cap (20% = £16,000) engages at 1.0pp below target, i.e. at 98.9%: below that, every further point of failure is free to the supplier — so for severe outages the credit regime stops incentivising recovery, and your protection must come from other clauses (termination triggers, service improvement obligations), which is what a head should verify exist before a bad month, not during one.
- C) £16,000 — the cap always applies to any breach.
- D) No credit — 99.4% rounds to 99.9% within tolerance.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The computation: shortfall 0.5 percentage points → 0.5 ÷ 0.1 = 5 increments → 5 × 2% = 10% → £80k × 0.10 = £8,000. Options C and D fail the arithmetic (the cap hasn't engaged; no rounding tolerance exists in the stated terms). But option A, arithmetically identical to B, is the lesser answer, and the difference is the head-level content: B reads the *structure*. Find where the cap binds: 20% ÷ 2% = 10 increments = 1.0pp, so at 98.9% the credits max out at £16,000 — and every additional hour of failure beyond that costs the supplier nothing. For context, 98.9% monthly availability is roughly 8 hours of downtime; a catastrophic 24-hour outage yields the same £16,000 as a bad-but-bounded month. Service credits are therefore an incentive instrument for *moderate* underperformance only; your exposure to severe failure is governed by whatever else the contract contains — termination rights, mandatory improvement plans, liability provisions — and the time to map those is at signature or renewal, in calm. The transferable discipline: for every capped or stepped commercial structure your estate relies on, compute the point where the counterparty's marginal incentive goes to zero, and ensure a different mechanism takes over there. That is service compliance and risk management performed on the contract's arithmetic rather than its adjectives.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Capacity investment timing**

A shared platform runs at 68% peak utilisation, growing 2 percentage points per month. Performance degrades measurably above 85%, and procurement-plus-implementation of the capacity expansion takes 5 months. The expansion costs £200k, and finance asks: "Why buy now? We're only at 68%." What is the correct quantitative answer?

- A) Buy later — 17 points of headroom remain, which is 8.5 months at current growth.
- B) At +2pp/month, 85% is reached in (85 − 68) ÷ 2 = 8.5 months. The expansion takes 5 months, so the decision deadline is 8.5 − 5 = 3.5 months away — "now" is not required, but "within one quarter" is, and any acceleration in growth (check the trend's own trend) shortens it; the honest position for finance is a commitment this quarter with the growth rate monitored monthly against the trigger.
- C) Buy immediately — capacity should always lead demand by a wide margin.
- D) Wait until 85% is reached, then expedite procurement.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The structure is threshold minus lead time. Months to threshold: (85 − 68) ÷ 2 = 8.5. Subtract the 5-month procurement-and-implementation lead: the *order* must be placed by month 3.5, or the platform crosses into degradation before capacity lands. So finance's "why now?" has a precise answer: not now, but within 3.5 months — and B's monitoring caveat is what makes the answer robust rather than merely correct: growth rates drift (the earlier storage question's lesson), so the 3.5-month cushion is itself a projection needing monthly re-verification, and a new workload or seasonal effect can consume it. Option A performs half the calculation — 8.5 months of headroom is true and irrelevant, because headroom must be measured in *decision time* (headroom minus lead time), not in threshold time; presenting 8.5 months to a finance committee when 3.5 is the real number is how capacity crises get scheduled. Option D is option A's terminal form: initiating a 5-month process at the threshold guarantees 5 months of degradation, and "expedite" is not a plan — it is a premium paid for a calendar error. Option C overshoots into unfalsifiable prudence that wastes capital. This is your availability-and-capacity duty verbatim — identify capacity issues, implement required changes, initiate remedial action — with the head-level addition that the *initiation date* is a computed quantity, and defending it to finance in lead-time arithmetic is precisely how remedial action gets funded on time.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Interpreting a fall in reported incidents**

Two quarters after you introduced a new incident categorisation policy (stricter criteria for what counts as an "incident" versus a "service request"), the board pack shows incidents down 30% and celebrates improved stability. Desk contact volumes are flat; monitoring alert volumes are flat; user-experience availability is unchanged. What is the sound interpretation?

- A) Stability has improved 30% as reported.
- B) Every independent indicator (contacts, alerts, user experience) is flat: the 30% fall coincides with the reclassification and most plausibly measures the *policy*, not the estate — some events previously counted as incidents are now counted as requests. The board pack is committing (innocently) a measurement-change attribution error, and the honest correction is to restate the trend on a like-for-like basis (recount prior quarters under the new criteria, or report the combined incident-plus-request operational volume alongside), with the definitional break annotated in the pack permanently.
- C) The desk should be investigated for suppressing contact volumes.
- D) Both things are true: the policy changed and stability improved 30%.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Triangulate. If stability had genuinely improved 30%, correlated indicators should move: fewer incidents ought to mean fewer alerts, fewer contacts, better experienced availability. All three are flat — so the change lives in the one thing that did change: the counting rule. This is a measurement-regime break, the reporting sibling of the "projecting a retired baseline" error: comparing counts across a definitional boundary attributes the definition's effect to the estate. The correction B specifies is the professional standard — restate history under current criteria (or dual-report across the break), annotate the discontinuity in every pack that spans it, and let any *residual* trend claim credit. Option D is worth dwelling on because it is the seductive one: both things could in principle be true, but the flat independent indicators leave no room for a 30% stability effect — D is what a head *wants* to believe with their own policy in the numerator, which is exactly when the discipline matters most. Option C invents suppression where flatness is the evidence *against* anything odd at the desk. The governance sting: you introduced the categorisation policy, and the board is now crediting you for its measurement artefact — a comfortable error, compounding quietly until an incident-heavy quarter "reverses the improvement" and demands explanation. Correcting the record now, from your own side, at the cost of a less flattering pack, is service compliance applied to your own function's reporting — and it is the cheap moment; the expensive one arrives with the reversal.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Expected value across a remediation portfolio**

£300k of discretionary remediation budget; four candidates. Using your estate's incident cost model (staff, recovery, citizen-impact valuation): (W) legacy integration failover, £200k, removes an estimated 2 major incidents/year at £60k each; (X) trend-to-action process automation, £40k, removes an estimated 3 majors/year at £60k each; (Y) monitoring gap closure on two services, £80k, removes 1 major/year at £60k plus an estimated £30k/year of prolonged-minor costs; (Z) continuity test acceleration, £60k, no direct incident reduction but cuts estimated invocation-failure risk, valued at £45k/year expected. Which portfolio maximises first-year expected return within budget?

- A) W + X (£240k): benefit £120k + £180k = £300k.
- B) X + Y + Z (£180k): benefit £180k + £90k + £45k = £315k, leaving £120k unspent.
- C) W + X + Z (£300k): benefit £120k + £180k + £45k = £345k.
- D) X + Y + Z, then reconsider W next year against the remaining case — the ranked returns are X (4.5:1), Z (0.75:1)... recompute: X £180k/£40k = 4.5; Y £90k/£80k ≈ 1.1; Z £45k/£60k = 0.75; W £120k/£200k = 0.6 — so best value-per-pound is X, Y, Z, W; but within the £300k cap, total benefit is maximised by C's £345k, so approve C while noting X carries the portfolio.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Two optimisation questions hide here, and the option set forces you to separate them. Benefit arithmetic first: W = 2 × £60k = £120k/yr; X = 3 × £60k = £180k/yr; Y = £60k + £30k = £90k/yr; Z = £45k/yr. Ratios (benefit per pound): X 4.5, Y 1.125, Z 0.75, W 0.6. If the objective were *return on each pound*, you would buy in ratio order until the budget ran out: X (£40k) + Y (£80k) + Z (£60k) = £180k spent, £315k benefit — option B — leaving £120k, not enough for W (£200k). But the stated objective is *maximum first-year expected benefit within the £300k cap*, and unspent discretionary budget typically returns to the centre: option C spends £300k exactly (W £200k + X £40k + Z £60k) for £345k — £30k more benefit than B — by substituting W+Z (£165k benefit, £260k cost) for Y+Z-plus-nothing. Verify no better combination: X is in every sensible portfolio (highest ratio, lowest cost); X+W+Y = £320k breaks the cap; X+Y+Z = £315k benefit; X+W+Z = £345k. C wins. Option D reasons well, ranks correctly, and then hedges into approving C anyway — its "reconsider W next year" preamble contradicts its conclusion; as in the earlier contract question, an option at war with itself fails even when its components are individually sound. The head-level lessons: state the objective before optimising (ratio-maximising and budget-constrained-benefit-maximising choose different portfolios — B versus C is exactly that difference); and note the fragility worth recording in the approval — every figure is an estimate from your cost model, and X's spectacular ratio (the process fix from your cognitive section, cheap and high-yield) is the robust core of any portfolio, so sequence it first regardless of what else survives scrutiny.

**Question 9 (hard) — The benchmarking claim**

A consultancy's benchmarking report, commissioned by your directorate, states: "Comparable government operations centres run at 2.1 incidents per analyst per day; yours runs at 1.4 — a 33% productivity gap indicating overstaffing of approximately 5 FTE." You establish: the benchmark population's "incident" definition includes service requests (yours does not, since your reclassification); your centre carries the 24/7 on-call obligation in-house while three of the five comparators outsource overnight; and your analysts spend a recorded 25% of time on problem investigation and preventative work the benchmark's activity model doesn't capture. What is the sound response?

- A) Accept the finding — external benchmarks are independent and the directorate commissioned it.
- B) Reject benchmarking entirely as impossible across organisations.
- C) Show the comparison is currently uninterpretable and rebuild it: the definitional mismatch (incidents-plus-requests vs incidents-only) inflates the comparator numerator by an unknown but estimable factor — using your own desk data, requests run at roughly the same volume as incidents, suggesting your like-for-like figure is nearer 2.8; the 24/7 in-house obligation loads roster hours the comparators don't carry; and 25% of recorded time is preventative output the metric counts as idle. Offer the consultancy your data to re-run like-for-like, and present the directorate the corrected picture *including* whatever residual gap survives — with the preventative work's value argued from your incident-reduction data, not asserted.
- D) Quietly increase your incidents-per-analyst figure by reversing the reclassification policy so future benchmarks compare favourably.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Take the three distortions in turn, because each is a standard benchmarking pathology. Definitional: your reclassification (Question 7) means the comparators count a superset of what you count — if requests roughly equal incidents in volume at your desk, your like-for-like rate is ~1.4 × 2 = 2.8 against 2.1, *reversing* the headline's direction (a gap in your favour). Structural: in-house 24/7 coverage requires roster depth that shows up as "analysts" in the denominator while comparators' outsourced overnight staffing sits in a supplier's headcount — an apples-to-staffing-model comparison. Activity-model: 25% of time on problem and preventative work is production the metric reads as slack; its value is demonstrable from your own data (the trend-to-action fix's incident reduction) rather than merely asserted, and B... rather, C insists on exactly that evidentiary form. The response's *manner* matters as much as its content: option C engages the benchmark rather than dismissing it (option B), because the directorate commissioned it and "uninterpretable, here is the corrected version" preserves your credibility where "benchmarks are impossible" spends it — and because the corrected comparison might still contain a residual finding worth having; a head who only rebuts benchmarks that flatter is running defence, not analysis. Option A signs a 5-FTE cut on arithmetic you can refute in three paragraphs. Option D is the corrosive one: reshaping your *measurement policy* to game future comparisons — reversing a reclassification you made for operational truth — subordinates your function's information quality to its appearance, the exact inversion of everything your compliance-and-risk duty protects. The durable technique: for any external comparison, audit definitions, structures and activity models *before* the delta, and respond with a rebuilt comparison plus your data — the counterparty who offers their data to the benchmarker is the one the final report believes.

**Question 10 (hard) — Weighted aggregation for the board**

Your board KPI aggregates availability across five services, currently as a simple average. This month: four back-office services at 99.9% each, and the flagship citizen service — carrying 70% of all user transactions — at 97.0%. Simple average: (99.9 × 4 + 97.0) ÷ 5 = 99.32%. The board target is 99.3%. The transaction-weighted figure: back-office services share the remaining 30% of transactions equally (7.5% each). What does weighting show, and what should you report?

- A) 99.32% — target met; report as usual.
- B) Weighted: (97.0 × 0.70) + (99.9 × 0.30) = 67.9 + 29.97 = 97.87% — dramatically below both the simple average and the target, because the failing service is where the users are. Report the weighted figure alongside the simple one, flag that the KPI's construction has been masking user-experienced performance (a 97.0% month on the flagship met a 99.3% target), and propose the board adopt transaction weighting permanently — accepting that this makes your own headline worse this month and honest thereafter.
- C) Weighted: 98.45% — marginally below target; note it verbally.
- D) Report 99.32% this month but adopt weighting from next month so the change doesn't coincide with a bad result.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Compute the weighted figure: flagship contributes 97.0 × 0.70 = 67.9; the four back-office services contribute 99.9 × 0.30 = 29.97; total 97.87% (option C's 98.45% miscarries the weighting). The 1.45-point gap between simple and weighted averages is not a technicality — it is the difference between "target met" and "the service most citizens used delivered 97% availability", roughly 22 hours of user-experienced monthly downtime on the flagship. The simple average conceals it because averaging *services* pretends each service matters equally when one carries 70% of the users — the aggregation-across-different-things error at board level, where its consequences are governance-grade: a board reading 99.32% green will not fund the flagship's remediation. Now the integrity sequencing, which is where B beats D and the question earns "hard": D adopts the honest measure *starting next month*, so that this month's breach reports as a pass and the methodology change never coincides with bad news — measurement timed to flatter, the same pathology as Question 9's option D and the compliance dashboard's edited denominator, executed with better manners. The moment you have computed 97.87%, reporting 99.32% without it is a decision to withhold; option B reports both figures, names the construction defect, and takes the reputational cost of proposing the honest measure in the month it hurts — which is precisely what makes the proposal credible. (A board that later learns the weighting was known and deferred learns something about every number you have ever sent them.) The user-focus core, in your summary's own terms — strategic thinking to provide the best service for the *end user* — is that KPI construction is user representation: whoever chooses the weights decides whose experience the organisation sees. Choose the users' weights, especially the month it costs you.

**Question 11 (hard) — Continuity investment under uncertainty**

A full failover capability for the flagship service costs £450k to build and £50k/year to maintain, and would reduce estimated annual downtime from major events by 20 hours. Your citizen-impact cost model values flagship downtime at £8k/hour, but the model is acknowledged rough: defensible range £4k–£15k/hour. Over a 5-year horizon (ignore discounting), what does the analysis support?

- A) Central case: benefit 5 × 20 × £8k = £800k against cost £450k + 5 × £50k = £700k — net +£100k; proceed.
- B) Run the range, not just the centre. Costs: £700k over 5 years. Benefits: at £4k/hour, 100 hours × £4k = £400k (net −£300k); at £8k, £800k (net +£100k); at £15k, £1.5m (net +£800k). The decision *flips* within the model's own uncertainty range, so the arithmetic alone cannot decide: the case rests on where the true hourly cost sits and on unmodelled factors (reputational and ministerial tolerance for flagship outages, the 20-hour estimate's own reliability). The analysis supports either commissioning a better impact estimate before deciding, or making the decision explicitly on risk appetite with the range disclosed — not presenting +£100k as if it settled the matter.
- C) Reject: at the low end the case loses £300k.
- D) Proceed: at the high end it nets £800k.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The computations: cost = £450k + £250k = £700k; benefit spans 100 hours (20 × 5) priced at the range ends — £400k, £800k, £1.5m; nets of −£300k, +£100k, +£800k. Options A, C and D each pick one point on the range and pronounce — A the centre, C the pessimistic end, D the optimistic — and all three commit the same meta-error: treating a decision that *changes sign inside the stated uncertainty* as if a point estimate could close it. That sign-flip is the analytical finding; B is the only option that reports it. What follows from a sign-flip is procedural, and B names both legitimate paths: reduce the uncertainty (a focused impact study — what do flagship outages actually cost in staff response, user harm, recovery? — is a £20k answer to a £700k question), or decide on explicitly-stated risk appetite with the range in front of the decision-maker, which is what risk appetite is *for* when analysis runs out. Note the contrast with Question 8's £3k-precision portfolio and the earlier resilience-programme case: sensitivity analysis is not decoration — it is the test that sorts decisions arithmetic can make from decisions it can only inform. A head who presents +£100k to an investment committee without the range is not simplifying; they are transferring their uncertainty to people who were not told they were receiving it. Continuity investment lives permanently in this territory — low-frequency, high-impact, soft-costed — and the differentiated honesty of B is the numeric twin of the differentiated assurance in your cognitive section's final question: state what the analysis establishes, what it cannot, and which authority the residue belongs to.

**Question 12 (hard) — The workforce plan's compound arithmetic**

Your 3-year capability plan: the centre needs 24 qualified analysts steady-state. Current: 21 qualified, 5 in training (9-month pipeline, historically 80% completion). Annual attrition among qualified staff runs at 15%, and — from your retention analysis — leavers are disproportionately experienced: assume attrition applies to the qualified pool including newly-qualified. Recruitment into training can run at most 8/year. Does a steady intake of 8/year reach and hold 24, and what does the arithmetic reveal about the plan's real constraint?

- A) Yes, comfortably: 8 recruits against ~3 leavers per year grows the pool rapidly.
- B) Model year by year. Year 1: start 21; attrition ≈ 21 × 0.15 ≈ 3.2 → ~17.8; current trainees deliver 5 × 0.8 = 4 → ~21.8; new intake of 8 is still in the pipeline. Year 2: attrition ≈ 3.3 → ~18.5; year-1 intake delivers 8 × 0.8 = 6.4 → ~24.9. Year 3: attrition ≈ 3.7 → ~21.2; intake delivers 6.4 → ~27.6, but attrition then takes ~4.1 of a 27.6 pool... steady state: pool stabilises where intake-completions equal attrition: 6.4 = 0.15 × P → P ≈ 42.7 — far above 24, so 8/year overshoots eventually; holding 24 needs completions of 0.15 × 24 = 3.6/year, i.e. intake of 3.6 ÷ 0.8 = 4.5/year. The plan reaches 24 during year 2–3 and the real constraints are the transition dip (year 1 runs at ~22 against a 24 requirement) and the 15% attrition itself: at 10% attrition, holding 24 needs only 3 completions (intake ~3.75) — retention is worth more than recruitment capacity.
- C) No: attrition outpaces an 8/year intake permanently.
- D) The question cannot be answered without monthly cohort data.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The model is intake × completion versus pool × attrition, run through time. Year 1 exposes the *transition* problem: the current 5 trainees yield only 4 qualified while attrition removes ~3, so the pool treads water around 21–22 — below the 24 requirement — for the whole pipeline lag; no intake decision now fixes year 1 (the 9-month pipeline is a physical constraint), so year 1 needs bridging tactics: retention focus on the experienced leavers, borrowed qualified staff, or overtime-tolerable rota design — the qualified-lead lesson from your cognitive section at whole-team scale. From year 2, the 8/year intake delivers 6.4 completions against ~3.5 attrition and the pool *grows past* the requirement toward an eventual ~43 steady state — so option C is wrong in the opposite direction to option A: the plan doesn't fail; it eventually over-provisions (a real cost: training capacity, salary drift) — sustained 8/year is the wrong steady-state setting, and ~4.5/year holds 24 once reached. B's closing insight is the strategic one: differentiate the levers. Recruitment capacity (the constraint the plan obsesses over) is not binding after year 2; the binding quantities are the pipeline lag (fixable only by anticipation) and the attrition rate — where a 5-point improvement (15%→10%) roughly halves the required intake, which is why the £120k retention package from Question 2 is not an HR nicety but the cheapest capacity instrument in the plan. Option D demands precision the decision doesn't need — annual cohort arithmetic answers the shape of the plan; monthly data refines it. Building the capability of the team, your summary's flagship phrase, is at head level exactly this: a stock-and-flow system with lags, leaks and levers, and the head who models it chooses levers by arithmetic while the head who doesn't chooses them by anecdote.

### Preparation tips

- **Rebuild one board figure from raw data monthly.** Take a number your function reported upward and reconstruct it — denominator, weights, exclusions, definitional breaks. Finding nothing is the good outcome; the habit is the point.
- **Run sensitivity on every case you sign.** Both ends of the softest estimate: does the decision survive? Sort your decisions into "arithmetic decides" and "appetite decides, arithmetic informs" — and label them honestly in the paper.
- **Keep a lead-time ledger.** For each major capacity, capability and procurement dependency: threshold, current position, growth rate, lead time — and therefore the computed decision date. Review quarterly. It converts most "urgent" investment decisions back into scheduled ones.
- **Practise decomposing totals by cost behaviour.** Contractual, staffing, usage-variable: equal totals, different risks. Apply it to the next two business cases that cross your desk.
- **Audit one scoring or aggregation instrument per quarter.** Risk scores, KPI weights, closure categories: run the extremes through the formula and check the ranking survives a select-committee test.
- **Do timed mixed practice, then classify your misses.** Senior numerical errors cluster in transitions (units, periods, denominators) and in premature closure on point estimates. Knowing your cluster is half the cure.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Annual affordability read as quarterly feasibility.** Cumulative-versus-cumulative or the plan fails in Q1 while the year "works".
- **Point estimates presented where the range flips the sign.** Disclose the range or you are transferring hidden uncertainty to your decision-makers.
- **Measurement changes credited as performance.** Definitional breaks, reclassifications and edited denominators must be annotated and restated — especially when the artefact flatters you.
- **Benchmarks accepted or rejected whole.** Audit definitions, structures and activity models, then rebuild like-for-like. Offer your data; keep the residual finding if one survives.
- **Optimising without stating the objective.** Ratio-maximising and budget-constrained-benefit-maximising choose different portfolios. Name the objective before the arithmetic, or the spreadsheet names it for you.
- **Stock-and-flow systems managed by anecdote.** Workforce, capability, capacity: model intake, leakage and lag, and pull the lever the arithmetic ranks first — it is usually retention, and it is usually underpriced.
