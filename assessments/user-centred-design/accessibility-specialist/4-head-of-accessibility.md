# Head of Accessibility - Psychometric Assessment Resources

## Introduction

Welcome. This guide is written for you as a head of accessibility in the UK Government Digital and Data profession — an expert practitioner who creates an accessibility vision or strategy for an organisation. Your role description adds that you define and assure best practice while influencing, leading and mentoring others; influence and develop organisational strategy working with counterparts across government; ensure the right conditions for accessibility specialists to work effectively; develop departmental capability by shaping career paths and recruiting talent; and own the overall approach to accessibility risk, including how that risk is communicated and how corporate legal compliance risk is managed.

That last item distinguishes your role from every level below it. You are the person who owns how an organisation understands its own exposure — not merely whether individual services conform, but whether the organisation knows what it does not know, whether its statements are accurate, and whether the risk sits somewhere it can be seen and managed.

You may be approaching a formal assessment as part of a selection process, or you may be here because reasoning that has become second nature is worth periodically re-examining. The risk at your level is not gaps in technical knowledge. It is that judgement has become automatic, automatic judgement feels certain, and nobody in your organisation is positioned to check you.

Four features shape everything that follows. First, you are accountable for conformance you cannot personally verify, across an estate you have not seen, judged from reports produced by others. Second, you occupy the boundary between a design discipline and a legal exposure, which means your findings carry weight and your errors carry it too — a head of accessibility who overstates a requirement damages the organisation's willingness to act on the genuine ones. Third, you influence senior stakeholders in a domain most of them find abstract, which puts unusual weight on your ability to express barriers in terms of people and consequence. Fourth, you build capability and conditions: the practice you leave behind matters more than any audit you sign.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the reasoning your role demands, presented through practical materials rather than abstract puzzles. At head-of-profession level the materials are organisational: audit summaries produced by others, conformance claims from suppliers, risk registers, remediation programmes, and standards applied unevenly across an estate.

The format is typically an online test of fifteen to thirty minutes covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective against a norm group of comparable seniority.

Three things characterise this section at your level.

The first is reasoning at a distance. You judge from reports rather than from services, which makes reading an output for the shape of the work behind it a core competence — and makes the absence of information as informative as its content.

The second is that the interesting questions concern mechanisms. Recurring defects are produced by something: a component library without a compliant variant, a framework whose defaults omit accessible names, a procurement process that never asks, a review that happens after the code is merged, or a market from which you cannot recruit. Fixing services one at a time leaves every mechanism intact.

The third is that you own risk rather than compliance. Those differ: an organisation with known, disclosed, planned gaps is in a materially better position than one with the same gaps unexamined, and a substantial part of your judgement concerns where the risk sits and whether anyone can see it.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The cognitive dimensions map onto the skills named in your role summary:

- **Auditing at a distance** maps to your role level's requirement to define and assure best practice.
- **Systemic diagnosis** maps to **Leadership and guidance**, where you change organisational structures to fixable and sustainable designs.
- **Risk reasoning** maps to your role level's ownership of the overall approach to accessibility risk and corporate legal compliance risk.
- **Strategic prioritisation** maps to **Leadership and guidance**, where you make and justify decisions characterised by high risk, impact and complexity.
- **Governance analysis** maps to **Governance and assurance (accessibility)**, where you analyse governance systems and add measures to build accessibility into all project and change activity.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Reading an audit summary**

A summary states: "Service X: 41 findings, no blockers." What is the most important thing to establish?

- A) How the 41 break down by severity.
- B) What the audit covered — because "no blockers" across an estate is a claim about what was examined as much as about what was found, and an audit of five representative pages that missed the authenticated journey has told you nothing about where blockers usually live.
- C) When the audit was conducted.
- D) Who conducted it.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** All four questions are worth asking and one of them determines whether the others matter. Scope is decisive because blockers cluster in exactly the places lightweight audits skip: authenticated journeys, error states, upload flows, payment steps and anything behind a form. An audit of public pages reporting no blockers is entirely consistent with a service that traps keyboard users at checkout. Option B names this. Option A is the natural first question and presupposes the findings represent the service. Options C and D are legitimate due diligence that does not touch the gap. At your level the useful discipline is to treat "no blockers" as a prompt for a scoping question rather than as reassurance, because the reassuring reports are the ones you are least likely to interrogate and most likely to repeat.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction from a regulatory scope**

A regulation exempts "pre-recorded time-based media published before 23 September 2020" and "content on an intranet published before 23 September 2019 and not substantially revised since". A directorate says: "Our training videos are exempt; they date from 2019." Which must be true?

- A) They are exempt.
- B) They are not exempt.
- C) They are exempt if pre-recorded and published before the 2020 date; the intranet exemption is irrelevant unless they are intranet content, in which case the earlier date and the revision condition apply instead.
- D) The regulation does not cover video.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Two exemptions with different dates and different conditions are in play, and the directorate has cited a date without saying which exemption it is claiming. If the videos are on the public website, the relevant date is September 2020 and being pre-recorded is the condition. If they are on an intranet, the relevant date is a year earlier and the videos must also not have been substantially revised. Option C sets both out. This matters practically because directorates cite the date they remember, and a head of accessibility who accepts or rejects on that basis is doing the analysis badly in either direction — and an exemption wrongly granted becomes an organisational position you will later have to reverse publicly.

**Question 3 (moderate) — What a conformance percentage conceals**

A dashboard reports the estate as "93% conformant". What is the strongest observation?

- A) 93% is a reasonable position.
- B) Conformance is binary per criterion and per page, so a percentage is a count of passed checks — it can be high while a service is unusable, and it obscures whether the 7% contains cosmetic issues or total barriers; the meaningful question is which criteria are unmet and on which journeys.
- C) The remaining 7% should be prioritised.
- D) The figure should be broken down by service.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This is a category error that appears in dashboards across government, and recognising it is central to your role's ownership of how risk is communicated. A percentage of passed checks says nothing about severity, and a service failing one criterion — a keyboard trap — can score 99% while being unusable for a group of people. Option B names that and states the meaningful alternative. Option D is a genuine improvement and still reports the wrong quantity more granularly. Option C accepts the framing and treats the remainder as proportionate. At your level the substantive action is to change what the dashboard reports, because a metric that cannot distinguish a missing alt attribute from a keyboard trap will produce complacency indefinitely, and you own that metric.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Strategic prioritisation**

You can direct substantial capacity to one of four things this year:

1. Embedding an automated accessibility check in the shared build pipeline used by most services.
2. Auditing the twelve services not audited in three years.
3. Delivering training to every delivery team.
4. Rewriting the organisation's accessibility statements.

Which is strongest?

- A) Item 2, because unaudited services are the unknown risk.
- B) Item 1, because it prevents a defined class of defect across most services continuously, whereas the others detect, inform or document — and prevention is the only one of the four that reduces the rate at which defects are created.
- C) Item 3, because capability is foundational.
- D) Item 4, because statements are the legal exposure.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The four options represent prevention, detection, education and documentation, and only one changes the rate at which defects appear. An automated check in a shared pipeline catches missing accessible names, invalid ARIA and missing language attributes on every commit, in every service using it, without anyone remembering — which is a permanent reduction rather than a one-off improvement. Option A addresses real unknown risk and produces a list rather than a change, and the twelve services will regenerate defects afterwards. Option C is the intervention most often chosen and least often effective when unattached to a routine. Option D is genuine legal exposure and, at your level, worth noting that inaccurate statements are usually a symptom: they are wrong because nobody knows the real position, which item 1 and item 2 address and rewriting does not.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Cross-estate synthesis**

Three signals reach you in a month. An audit flags missing form labels in one service. A second service's user research reports screen reader users abandoning at forms. A third service's contact centre reports form completion queries. What is the strongest synthesis?

- A) Three services have form problems.
- B) Three independent signals converging on forms across three services suggests a shared cause — most likely a form component or framework that permits or produces unlabelled fields — so the diagnostic step is to check whether the three use the same component, and if so the fix is one change rather than three.
- C) Forms are difficult; teams need training.
- D) All three should be audited.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Three signals of different kinds — an audit, user research and contact data — converging on the same interaction across three services is unusually strong evidence, and the strongest synthesis asks what could produce it. A shared form component is the obvious candidate and is checkable in an afternoon. Option B names it and identifies the multiplier: if the three services share a component, one fix resolves all three and prevents the fourth. Option A describes without synthesising. Option C reaches for training before diagnosis, and if the component is at fault no amount of training will help teams who are using it correctly. Option D commissions three audits to establish what one question could.

**Question 6 (moderate) — An inaccurate accessibility statement**

You discover that a service's published accessibility statement claims full conformance, while an internal audit six months ago found four unresolved serious issues. What is the strongest response?

- A) Correct the statement.
- B) Correct the statement immediately, since a published claim of full conformance that the organisation knows to be false is a materially worse position than a disclosed gap — then establish how it came to be published, because a statement contradicting an internal audit indicates that whoever writes statements does not see audit output, which will be true across the estate.
- C) Escalate to legal.
- D) Prioritise fixing the four issues.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Two things are wrong and they are different in kind. The four issues are a conformance problem. The statement is a disclosure problem, and it is the more serious of the two: an organisation with known gaps and an honest statement is in a defensible position, while one publishing a false claim of full conformance has converted a remediation issue into a misrepresentation. Option B corrects that first and then asks the structural question, which is the part specific to your level — a statement contradicting an audit means the process that produces statements is disconnected from the process that produces audits, and that disconnection will not be confined to one service. Option A fixes the instance. Option D addresses the lesser problem first and leaves a false claim published. Option C may follow and is not the first action.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Diagnosing a capability problem**

You cannot recruit experienced accessibility specialists; posts sit vacant for months. What is the strongest response?

- A) Keep advertising and escalate the difficulty.
- B) Treat it as capability design rather than recruitment: build entry routes from adjacent roles, since testers, front-end developers and content designers convert well and are easier to find; invest in structured development for the people you have; and reduce the demand by devolving routine checks to teams so that specialist time is spent where specialist judgement is needed.
- C) Lower the stated experience requirements.
- D) Use contractors.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Months of unfilled posts in a small, competitive market is not a problem more advertising solves. Option B works on three fronts, and the first is specific to this discipline and unusually effective: accessibility specialists are rarely recruited ready-made because the field is small, and people from testing, front-end development and content design arrive with most of the technical foundation and need the standards knowledge, which is teachable. The third front matters as much — devolving routine checks reduces the demand you are trying to hire against, which is the only lever that works quickly. Option C is least effective, lowering the requirement without changing the support structure and setting people up to fail in a role where errors carry legal weight. Option D leaves when the contract ends and builds nothing.

**Question 8 (harder) — Scoping a flaw in an audit**

An audit you are relying on was conducted using automated tooling only. Four findings: (i) 47 missing alt attributes; (ii) no keyboard traps detected; (iii) 12 contrast failures; (iv) all form fields correctly labelled. Which findings are most compromised?

- A) All four.
- B) None; automated tooling is reliable for what it tests.
- C) Findings (ii) and (iv) are most compromised, because they are negative findings — automated tools detect keyboard traps poorly or not at all, and can confirm a label exists but not that it is meaningful or matches the visible text — while (i) and (iii) are positive detections in areas tooling handles reliably.
- D) Only (iii).

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The distinction that matters is between what a tool detects and what its silence means. Automated tooling is reliable at finding missing alt attributes and measuring contrast, so findings (i) and (iii) can be trusted as far as they go. But "no keyboard traps detected" is a statement about the tool's capability rather than about the service, and "all form fields correctly labelled" confirms only that labels exist — a field labelled "Field 3" or one whose accessible name differs from its visible text passes automated checking and fails users. Option C says this. Option A discards two sound findings. Option B is the error that produces organisations believing they are compliant on the strength of a tool that examines perhaps a third of the criteria. At your level the useful output is a rule about what an automated-only audit may be reported as, because those reports circulate as assurance.

**Question 9 (harder) — Reading the distribution of failures**

Across the year, five services released with unresolved blockers. All five were in one directorate, all under ministerial deadline pressure, and all had accessibility sign-off recorded. What is the strongest response?

- A) Report the five and require remediation.
- B) The recorded sign-offs are the most informative feature: five services signed off and released with blockers means the sign-off is not detecting them, so investigate what sign-off actually involves in that directorate — most likely a form completed rather than a service tested — and fix the mechanism, while remediating the five.
- C) Remind the directorate of the requirement.
- D) Introduce a second sign-off stage.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The detail that changes the analysis is that sign-off was recorded. Without it, this would be a compliance failure; with it, it is an assurance failure — the control exists, was operated, and did not work, which is considerably more serious because the organisation believed it was protected. Option B identifies that and names the likely mechanism: sign-off that has become a form somebody completes rather than a test somebody runs. Option A treats the instances and leaves a control that produces false assurance across every service using it. Option C reminds people of a requirement they formally complied with. Option D adds a second stage to a first stage that does not work, which is how governance accumulates without improving — and you would be defending both in two years.

**Question 10 (harder) — Owning the risk position**

You are asked to state the organisation's accessibility risk. Eleven services have known unresolved serious issues; nineteen have been audited within a year; fourteen have not been audited at all. What is the strongest formulation?

- A) Eleven services have known issues.
- B) The known risk is eleven services with documented serious issues and dated remediation plans; the unknown risk is fourteen unaudited services, which is the larger exposure precisely because it is unquantified — and the organisation's position is materially better on the eleven than on the fourteen, even though the eleven are the ones with findings against them.
- C) Thirty of forty-four services have accessibility risk.
- D) The risk is low; most services have been audited.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This is the judgement your role's ownership of accessibility risk turns on, and it is counter-intuitive to most audiences. A service with documented issues and a plan is a managed risk; a service nobody has looked at is an unmanaged one, and the absence of findings is not the absence of defects. Option B says this and makes the comparison explicit, which is the part that changes how a board allocates attention — left to itself, a board will focus on the eleven services with findings, because findings are visible, and will treat the fourteen as fine. Option A reports only the known half. Option D infers safety from audit coverage, inverting the logic. Option C aggregates known and unknown risk into a number that conceals the distinction on which the whole position rests.

**Question 11 (harder) — Governance analysis**

You are asked to build accessibility into the organisation's project governance. The existing gateway reviews cover business case, delivery confidence and benefits. What is the strongest approach?

- A) Add an accessibility question to each gateway.
- B) Identify the decision points where accessibility is actually determined — procurement, technology selection, and the definition of done — and add requirements there, since a gateway question asked after those decisions produces a status report rather than an intervention.
- C) Introduce a separate accessibility gateway.
- D) Require an accessibility statement at each gateway.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Governance is only useful where it can change something, and the decisions that determine whether a service will be accessible are made long before a gateway review: which component library is bought, which framework is chosen, and what a team considers a story complete. A question at a gateway after those decisions produces an answer, not a change. Option B places the requirements where the decisions are, which is what your role means by analysing governance systems and adding appropriate measures. Option A is the default response and produces reporting. Option C adds a stage teams must pass and does not reach the decisions. Option D requires a document rather than an outcome, and documents at gateways are produced to satisfy gateways.

**Question 12 (harder) — A pattern across your specialists**

Two of your specialists produce audits teams act on; three produce audits teams dispute. Reading a sample, you find the first two consistently state user impact alongside the criterion, and the others cite criteria alone. What is the most useful inference and action?

- A) The three need training on the standards.
- B) The difference is in how findings are written rather than in technical knowledge — stating user impact makes a finding actionable and hard to dispute, while a criterion reference invites a debate about interpretation — so the action is a reporting standard requiring location, fault, impact and remedy, with examples from the two whose reports work.
- C) Reassign auditing to the two.
- D) Review all audits before issue.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The pattern points at a writing practice rather than a knowledge deficit, and the mechanism is worth understanding: a finding stating that a screen reader user hears seven unlabelled inputs is a fact a developer can verify and act on, while "fails 1.3.1" is an interpretation somebody can argue about. Disputes cluster around the second kind because there is nothing in them to check. Option B names that and produces a transferable standard with worked examples, which is how a practice propagates. Option A misdiagnoses a communication difference as a technical one, and training three specialists on standards they already know will not change how they write. Option C concentrates the work on two people and develops nobody. Option D makes you a permanent bottleneck and leaves the three writing the same way.

### Preparation tips

- **Treat "no blockers" as a scoping question.** Blockers live where lightweight audits do not go.
- **Distinguish what a tool detects from what its silence means.** Negative findings from automated tooling are claims about the tool.
- **Separate known risk from unknown risk.** The unaudited estate is usually the larger exposure.
- **Look for prevention rather than detection.** Only one of them changes the rate.
- **Read a recorded sign-off that failed as an assurance failure, not a compliance one.**
- **Place governance requirements where decisions are made, not where they are reported.**
- **Ask what could produce three independent signals.** Usually a shared component.
- **Reject conformance percentages.** Ask which criteria on which journeys.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Accepting a reassuring report without asking its scope.** Those are the ones you are least likely to interrogate.
- **Treating an automated-only audit as assurance.**
- **Inferring safety from audit coverage.**
- **Reporting known and unknown risk as one number.**
- **Adding a gateway question after the determining decision.**
- **Reaching for training where the fault is in a component or a way of writing.**
- **Adding a second control to a first that does not work.**
- **Fixing a false statement without asking how it came to be published.**

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A numeric reasoning assessment measures how accurately you handle quantitative material. At your level the emphasis is almost wholly on interrogation, and the material is unusually consequential: conformance figures reported to boards, prevalence estimates used in business cases, audit coverage statistics, remediation costings, and the numbers in which your organisation's legal exposure is expressed.

The professional stake is direct. Your role owns how accessibility risk is communicated, which means the numbers you put in front of a board become the organisation's understanding of its own position. A figure that flatters — a conformance percentage, an audit coverage rate, a defect count from a tool that examines a third of the criteria — produces complacency that you will spend years correcting. Conversely, a barrier expressed as a criterion reference competes with everything else in a portfolio, while the same barrier expressed as forty thousand people a year and a statutory duty does not.

The format is typically a timed online test of fifteen to thirty minutes, each item presenting a table, chart or paragraph of figures with a multiple-choice question. Calculators are usually provided.

Four habits carry the load. Establish what the question asks before looking at the data. Identify the denominator and the population it represents. Estimate, calculate, compare. And sanity-check against the world.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The numerical skills tested map onto your named skills:

- **Interrogating reported figures** maps to your ownership of how accessibility risk is communicated.
- **Prevalence and population arithmetic** maps to **Communicating information**, where you represent the community to large audiences and manage expectations.
- **Coverage and exposure reasoning** maps to **Governance and assurance (accessibility)**.
- **Weighted aggregation across an estate** maps to **Leadership and guidance**, where decisions of high risk and complexity rest on figures that describe reality.
- **Estimating remediation** maps to **Leadership and guidance** and to resourcing decisions.
- **Detecting misleading metrics** maps to **Consultancy**, where advice built on a flattering number is hard to withdraw.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Thresholds at the boundary**

A criterion requires 4.5:1 for text under 24 pixels and 3:1 for text 24 pixels or larger. Four items: 14px at 4.4:1; 18px at 4.5:1; 24px at 2.9:1; 28px at 3.0:1. How many conform?

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 14px needs 4.5:1 — 4.4 fails. 18px needs 4.5:1 — 4.5 meets it exactly, since "at least" is inclusive. 24px falls into the larger category because the wording is "24 or larger", so needs 3:1 — 2.9 fails. 28px needs 3:1 — 3.0 passes. Two conform, giving B. Each reading produces real errors: reporting 4.5 as a fail is over-application, applying 4.5:1 at 24px uses the wrong threshold, and rounding 4.4 up is a false pass. At your level the useful output is that these three readings belong in your organisation's guidance explicitly, because they are exactly what specialists and teams get wrong and exactly what one paragraph can prevent across an estate.

**Question 2 (easy) — Prevalence arithmetic**

An organisation's services have 4.2 million users a year. If around 16% of working-age adults report a disability, roughly how many affected users a year, and how should you present it?

- A) 672,000, presented as a precise figure
- B) Roughly 670,000, presented as an estimate with its source and the caveat that reported disability understates the population affected by digital barriers
- C) 42,000
- D) 1.6 million

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 16% of 4,200,000 is 672,000, so the arithmetic gives roughly 670,000 — and how you present it matters as much as the figure. Option B rounds honestly, cites the basis, and adds the caveat that matters: self-reported disability understates the population encountering digital barriers, because it excludes people with temporary impairments, situational limitations, age-related changes they do not describe as disability, and those who simply do not identify with the term. That caveat makes your estimate conservative rather than contestable, which is the position you want when a stakeholder pushes back. Option A presents an estimate as a count, inviting a challenge about the source that distracts from the point. Options C and D miscalculate.

**Question 3 (easy) — Audit coverage**

An organisation has 44 services. Nineteen have been audited within a year, eleven more than a year ago, and fourteen never. What proportion has current audit coverage?

- A) 32%
- B) 43%
- C) 68%
- D) 75%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Nineteen of 44 is 19 ÷ 44 = 0.432, about 43%, giving B. Option C is the proportion audited at any point, which is the figure most likely to be quoted because it sounds better and is the wrong one — an audit from three years ago tells you about a service that has since been rebuilt. Option A is the never-audited proportion. The distinction between "audited" and "audited recently" is exactly the kind of thing that makes a coverage statistic reassuring and meaningless, and at your level you set the definition, so it is worth defining currency explicitly rather than letting each report choose.

**Question 4 (easy) — Reflow arithmetic**

A criterion requires content to reflow without horizontal scrolling at an effective viewport width of 320 CSS pixels. At what zoom level does a 1,280-pixel viewport reach that width?

- A) 200%
- B) 300%
- C) 400%
- D) 500%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Zoom reduces effective width proportionally: 1,280 ÷ 4 = 320, so 400% zoom. Option A gives 640 and option D gives 256. The relationship is worth holding because it is how the requirement is tested in practice, and because it comes up when teams ask why 400% rather than some other figure — the answer is that 400% of a common desktop width is the small-screen equivalent, which makes the requirement about supporting magnification and small screens with one test rather than two.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Weighted aggregation across an estate**

Four directorates report defects per hundred components: 3 on 500 components, 9 on 100, 5 on 200, and 4 on 200. What is the estate-wide rate per hundred?

- A) 4.2
- B) 4.6
- C) 5.25
- D) 6.0

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Convert to counts. 3 per hundred on 500 = 15 defects. 9 per hundred on 100 = 9. 5 per hundred on 200 = 10. 4 per hundred on 200 = 8. Total 42 defects across 1,000 components, so 4.2 per hundred, giving A. Option C is the unweighted mean of 3, 9, 5 and 4, which is 5.25 and over-weights the 100-component directorate against the 500-component one. The organisational point matters more than the arithmetic: the unweighted figure describes the average *directorate*, while the weighted figure describes the average *component* — and only the second describes what users encounter, which is the figure that belongs in a board report on your signature.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Comparing groups**

Of 2,000 general users, 1,800 completed a task. Of 200 screen reader users, 60 completed. What is the gap, and how should you express it?

- A) 60 percentage points; screen reader users complete at 30%
- B) 60 percentage points, and the more useful expression is that screen reader users complete at one third the rate of other users
- C) 30 percentage points
- D) 90 percentage points

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** General users: 1,800 ÷ 2,000 = 90%. Screen reader users: 60 ÷ 200 = 30%. The gap is 60 percentage points, so options C and D are wrong on the arithmetic. Between A and B the expression decides it, and the ratio form is stronger for a senior audience: "one third the rate" is a single comparison somebody can hold and repeat, while two percentages require the listener to do the subtraction and then decide whether 60 points is a lot. At your level, choosing the form in which a finding travels is part of the finding, because the version that gets repeated in rooms you are not in is the one that determines what happens.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Small-base caution**

A report states "only 5% of screen reader users completed the journey", based on 1 of 19 participants. What is the strongest observation?

- A) The finding is clear.
- B) The arithmetic is right and the base is too small for a percentage at that precision — one more success would make it 11% — so report "1 of 19 participants completed", which is more forceful and cannot be challenged on precision.
- C) More participants are needed.
- D) The figure should be rounded.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The arithmetic is correct and the presentation is not. A percentage from a base of 19 with a single success implies a precision the data does not have, and a stakeholder who spots it will use it to discount the whole report — which is the specific risk for a head of profession, because your reports carry weight and are read adversarially by people who dislike their conclusions. Option B also makes the point that "1 of 19" is the more forceful form: eighteen failures out of nineteen is vivid in a way that "5%" is not. Option C would delay a conclusive finding. Option A invites the challenge. Option D adjusts presentation without addressing the issue.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Scale of a shared defect**

A component with a keyboard trap is used across 12 services with 6 million combined annual sessions. Around 3% involve keyboard-only navigation. How many sessions a year are affected, and what should you add?

- A) 18,000
- B) 180,000, noting the estimate is conservative because a keyboard trap also affects switch, voice control and temporary keyboard users
- C) 1,800,000
- D) 60,000

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 3% of 6,000,000 is 180,000, giving B — and the addition matters. The 3% figure covers keyboard-only navigation specifically, while a keyboard trap also affects switch access users, voice control users who navigate by keyboard interface, and anyone using a keyboard temporarily because a trackpad has failed or an arm is in a sling. Presenting the estimate as conservative rather than precise removes the obvious counter-argument and is honest. This calculation is what converts a component defect into a board-level decision: "a keyboard trap in the modal component" is a ticket, and "at least 180,000 sessions a year in which a user cannot leave a dialogue" is not.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Reconstructing an implied cost**

A remediation business case claims that fixing accessibility defects across 12 services will cost £480,000 and take 8 months. What is the implied cost per service, and what should you check?

- A) £40,000 per service, and check whether the twelve services share components — because if a substantial share of defects sit in shared code, the cost is not twelve times a per-service figure
- B) £60,000 per service
- C) £4,000 per service
- D) £480,000 is reasonable for twelve services

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** £480,000 ÷ 12 = £40,000 per service, giving A — and the check it names is the one that matters. A costing built by estimating each service independently will systematically overstate the work where services share a component library, because a single fix resolves the defect everywhere it appears. Conversely it will understate work where each service has bespoke code. Establishing which applies takes one question and can move a business case by a factor of two in either direction. At your level, being able to interrogate a costing rather than only receive it is what lets you defend the number when finance asks, and what stops you asking for money you do not need.

**Question 10 (harder) — A coverage statistic that misleads**

A report states: "98% of pages pass automated accessibility testing." What is the strongest observation?

- A) 98% is a strong position.
- B) Automated testing detects perhaps a third of success criteria, so the figure says 98% of pages pass the subset a tool can check — it is entirely consistent with widespread failures in keyboard operation, focus management, meaningful alternative text and logical reading order, none of which the tool examines.
- C) The remaining 2% should be prioritised.
- D) Manual testing should supplement it.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This is the most dangerous figure in accessibility reporting, because it is high, precise and produced automatically, and it measures something quite different from what its readers assume. Automated tools reliably detect a minority of criteria — missing attributes, contrast, some structural issues — and cannot assess whether alternative text is meaningful, whether focus order is logical, whether a keyboard user can escape a dialogue, or whether an error message is announced usefully. A service can pass automated testing entirely and be unusable. Option B says this. Option D is correct as a recommendation and does not correct the misapprehension already created. Option C accepts the framing. At your level the substantive action is to stop the figure being reported in that form, because you own how risk is communicated and this is the report that most reliably produces false confidence.

**Question 11 (harder) — Known against unknown risk**

Nineteen services are audited with 41 documented issues between them. Fourteen have never been audited. A board asks where the risk is. What is the strongest formulation?

- A) The risk is 41 documented issues.
- B) The documented issues are the managed risk, with owners and plans; the fourteen unaudited services are the unmanaged risk and probably the larger exposure, since the audited nineteen average about two issues each and there is no reason to expect the unaudited fourteen to differ — the difference is that nobody can see theirs.
- C) The risk is low; most services have been audited.
- D) The risk is 55 services' worth.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The formulation matters because a board left to itself will attend to the visible half. Option B makes the comparison explicit and adds the arithmetic that makes it concrete: 41 issues across nineteen services is roughly two each, and applying that to fourteen unaudited services implies something like twenty-eight undocumented issues nobody has seen. That estimate is rough and is enough to establish that the unknown half is comparable in size and worse in kind, because unmanaged risk cannot be planned against. Option A reports the visible half. Option C infers safety from coverage, inverting the logic. Option D aggregates known and unknown into a figure that conceals the distinction on which the whole answer rests.

**Question 12 (harder) — Percentage change with attribution**

Reported defects across the estate fell from 720 to 540 over a year, after an automated check was added to the shared pipeline. What is the change, and what should you check before attributing it?

- A) 25% decrease, demonstrating the check worked
- B) 25% decrease, and the check is whether the fall is concentrated in the defect types the tool detects — if it is, attribution holds; if the fall is spread evenly, something else is happening, possibly reduced reporting
- C) 33% decrease, demonstrating the check worked
- D) 33% decrease, with the same caution

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The difference is 180, and 180 ÷ 720 = 0.25, a 25% decrease, so options C and D divide by the new figure. Between A and B the analysis decides it, and B names the specific test: an automated check detects a defined class of defect, so a fall concentrated in that class supports attribution while an even distribution does not. There is a second possibility worth holding, which B mentions — reported defects fall when fewer are found as well as when fewer exist, and an organisation that has just automated part of its checking may be looking less hard manually. At your level this matters because you will be asked whether the investment worked, and claiming credit for a fall you have not attributed is a position you cannot defend when the next year's figures move.

### Preparation tips

- **Read thresholds at the boundary, and put those readings in your organisation's guidance.**
- **Present prevalence as an estimate with a conservative caveat.** It removes the counter-argument.
- **Define audit currency explicitly.** "Audited" and "audited recently" are different statistics.
- **Return to counts before combining rates.** The weighted figure describes what users encounter.
- **Express gaps as ratios for senior audiences.** "One third the rate" travels; two percentages do not.
- **Report small bases as counts.** "1 of 19" is stronger and unchallengeable.
- **Interrogate costings for shared components.** It can move a business case by a factor of two.
- **Separate known from unknown risk, and estimate the unknown half.**

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Letting "98% pass automated testing" be reported as an accessibility position.**
- **Accepting a conformance percentage.**
- **Quoting audit coverage without currency.**
- **Reporting a percentage from a base of 19.**
- **Aggregating known and unknown risk into one number.**
- **Claiming attribution for a fall you have not tested.**
- **Averaging directorate-level rates without weighting.**
- **Presenting an estimate as a precise count.** It invites a challenge that distracts from the point.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately you read. For your role it is close to a core competence rather than a proxy: accessibility practice rests on documents written with unusual precision — success criteria, regulations, exemptions, procurement standards, conformance claims — and your organisation's legal position depends on those documents being read exactly as written.

The classic format presents a short passage of dense workplace prose, typically three to six sentences, followed by statements to classify as True, False or Cannot Say. Alongside these come comprehension items, main-point items, and questions asking which of four summaries is most accurate.

The governing rule is that you answer from the passage alone. Your professional knowledge must be set aside, and this is harder for you than for almost any other candidate: you know these documents well enough to complete their sentences, which is precisely the reflex that produces errors. Heads of profession underperform on verbal reasoning at rates that surprise them, and this is nearly always the mechanism.

Cannot Say deserves particular attention, and the trap is sharpest where the claim would support something you want — more resource for your function, a case for a common approach, a reason to reject a supplier.

Small words carry the load. "All" is not "most". "Or" offers alternative routes. "Substantially", "pre-recorded", "essential" and "disproportionate burden" are the load-bearing qualifiers in accessibility regulation, and each has decided real disputes.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The verbal skills tested map onto your named skills:

- **Reading precisely** maps to **Technical understanding (accessibility)**, where you communicate the requirements of standards and legislation.
- **Separating statement from inference** maps to your ownership of accessibility risk, where a position built on an inference is one you cannot defend.
- **Identifying the main point** maps to **Communicating information**, where you represent the community to large audiences.
- **Handling qualified language** maps to **Governance and assurance (accessibility)**, where what a requirement bites on determines the risk position.
- **Reading exemptions accurately** maps to your ownership of corporate legal compliance risk.
- **Reading cross-government prose** maps to your role level's collaboration with counterparts.

### Practice questions

*Passage A — for Questions 1 to 4*

"Public sector bodies must ensure their websites meet the accessibility standard. This does not apply to pre-recorded time-based media published before 23 September 2020, to live time-based media, to online maps provided essential information is available in an accessible alternative format, or to content on an intranet published before 23 September 2019 and not substantially revised since. A body may claim that meeting a requirement would impose a disproportionate burden, provided it carries out and documents an assessment of that burden."

**Question 1 (easy)** — Statement: "All content on a public sector website must meet the standard." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The passage lists four categories outside scope and a fifth route via disproportionate burden. The statement's "all" contradicts them, so False. Worth noting the scale of what is excluded: between media, maps, older intranet content and the burden provision, a substantial proportion of what a large organisation publishes may be outside the requirement as written. A head of accessibility carrying "everything must conform" as a working assumption will direct effort at content outside scope and be corrected by somebody less expert, which is an expensive way to lose standing.

**Question 2 (easy)** — Statement: "A live-streamed event published in 2018 is exempt." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The passage exempts live time-based media with no date condition attached — the 2020 date applies to the *pre-recorded* exemption. A live stream is therefore exempt regardless of publication date, and the statement follows. True. The item tests whether you attach the date qualifier to the right clause, and a reader carrying "there is a 2020 date on media" as a general rule reaches the right answer here for the wrong reason, then fails the moment they meet a live stream from 2024.

**Question 3 (moderate)** — Statement: "A body may claim disproportionate burden for any requirement it finds costly." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The provision is conditional: a body may claim disproportionate burden *provided it carries out and documents an assessment*. The statement drops the condition and substitutes a subjective test — finding something costly — for a documented assessment, so it is contradicted. False. This is the most misused provision in the regulations and the one most likely to reach you as an organisational position: teams describe work as a disproportionate burden without any assessment, which is not a claim at all, and a body relying on an undocumented burden claim is in a worse position than one that simply disclosed the gap. Knowing that the condition is procedural rather than substantive is what lets you say so.

**Question 4 (harder)** — Statement: "Intranet content published in 2018 and rewritten last month is exempt." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The intranet exemption has two conditions: published before 23 September 2019 *and* not substantially revised since. Content rewritten last month fails the second, so the exemption does not apply. False. The compound structure is the point, and its practical implication is counter-intuitive and worth communicating across an organisation: substantially revising old intranet content brings it into scope, so a directorate improving its guidance also acquires an accessibility obligation. That is not a reason to leave content unimproved, and it is a reason to plan the accessibility work alongside the rewrite — which teams will only do if somebody tells them, and that somebody is you.

*Passage B — for Questions 5 to 8*

"The cross-government review found considerable variation in how departments assure accessibility. Six departments run automated checks in their build pipelines; the remainder rely on periodic audit. Departments with automated checks reported fewer defects at release, although the review did not examine whether their services were more accessible in use. The review recommends that the profession consider whether a common assurance approach would be proportionate."

**Question 5 (easy)** — Statement: "Some departments do not run automated checks in their pipelines." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The passage states that six departments run automated checks and the remainder rely on periodic audit, establishing directly that some do not. True. Answer quickly and bank the time. The only conceivable ambiguity — whether "the remainder" might be empty — is closed by the passage describing what the remainder does.

**Question 6 (moderate)** — Statement: "Automated checks produce more accessible services." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The passage reports fewer defects at release and states explicitly that accessibility in use was not examined. Those differ, and the gap is instructive: automated checks catch a defined subset of criteria, so departments using them may genuinely produce fewer of that subset while performing identically on keyboard operation, focus management and meaningful alternatives — which is where the difficult barriers are. Cannot Say. This is the specific trap for someone in your position, because "automation improves accessibility" is a claim you would like to be true and would cite when proposing a common approach. It is worth arguing for automation on the grounds the evidence supports — earlier detection of a known class of defect at negligible marginal cost — which is a strong enough case without overstating it.

**Question 7 (moderate)** — Statement: "The review recommends introducing a common assurance approach." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The review recommends that the profession *consider whether* a common approach would be proportionate, which includes concluding it would not be. The statement asserts a recommendation to introduce, which was not made, so False rather than Cannot Say. At your level you are likely to be both a recipient of this recommendation and a contributor to whatever the profession decides, so misreading it commits you to advocating something nobody asked for — and, more awkwardly, to reporting progress against it.

**Question 8 (harder)** — Statement: "Periodic audit is a weaker assurance approach than automated checking." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The passage describes two approaches and reports one difference in outcome while declining to assess accessibility in use. It makes no judgement about relative strength, and there is a substantive reason to be careful here: the two approaches detect different things, so "weaker" is not obviously the right axis at all — automated checking catches a narrow class continuously while periodic audit catches a broad class occasionally, and an organisation doing only one of them has a gap either way. Cannot Say. What makes this harder than Question 6 is that the passage's structure leans towards automation, and structural lean is not content.

*Passage C — for Questions 9 to 12*

"An accessibility statement must state the standard and level the service aims to meet, describe any non-accessible content and why, give the date of preparation and last review, and explain how to report problems. Where non-accessible content is claimed to impose a disproportionate burden, the statement must record the assessment. A statement must be reviewed at least annually and whenever the service changes substantially. A statement claiming full conformance may not be published where known non-accessible content exists."

**Question 9 (easy)** — Statement: "A statement must explain how to report problems." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The first sentence requires the statement to explain how to report problems. The statement restates that, so True. Answer quickly. It is worth noting why the requirement exists and why it is the element most often reduced to a generic contact form: a reporting route is how an organisation learns about barriers it has not found, and one that leads nowhere converts the statement from a disclosure into a formality.

**Question 10 (moderate)** — Statement: "A statement reviewed eleven months ago is compliant." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The review requirement has two limbs: at least annually, *and* whenever the service changes substantially. Eleven months satisfies the first, but the statement does not tell you whether the service has changed substantially in that period. If it has, a review was due at that point regardless of the calendar. Cannot Say. This compound structure catches organisations that treat the annual review as the whole obligation and let statements go stale through a major release — which is precisely how a statement comes to describe a service that no longer exists, and precisely the kind of thing an enforcement enquiry finds.

**Question 11 (harder)** — Statement: "A service with two known minor non-accessible items may publish a statement claiming full conformance if it plans to fix them." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The final sentence prohibits a full conformance claim where known non-accessible content exists, without qualification as to severity or remediation plans. Two known items, however minor and however soon they will be fixed, are known non-accessible content, so the claim may not be made. False. The absence of a severity qualifier is the point, and it is a provision worth being firm about across an organisation: a statement is a public representation, and a false claim of full conformance is a materially worse position than an honest disclosure of two minor issues with a fix date. Teams reach for the full claim because it looks better, and it is the one thing in this area that can convert a small remediation problem into a misrepresentation.

**Question 12 (harder) — Main point**

Which best captures the main point of Passage C?

- A) Statements must be reviewed annually.
- B) An accessibility statement is a public disclosure that must be accurate, current and complete — naming the standard aimed at, the gaps and their reasons, any documented burden assessment, and a route to report problems — and may never overstate conformance.
- C) Full conformance may not be claimed where non-accessible content exists.
- D) Statements must record disproportionate burden assessments.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The main point must cover the whole passage while adding nothing. Options A, C and D are each true of one provision and silent on the rest. Option B captures the four content requirements, the currency obligation and the prohibition, and it names the logic joining them: a statement exists so that users and regulators can rely on it, which is why it must be accurate, current, complete and never overstated. Note that B is the only option conveying that the statement is a *disclosure* rather than a compliance artefact — the distinction that determines whether an organisation treats it as something to get right or something to get done, and which you are the person responsible for establishing.

### Preparation tips

- **Point at the words.** Your familiarity with these documents is the specific hazard.
- **Attach qualifiers to the right clause.** The 2020 date applies to pre-recorded media, not to live.
- **Treat conditional provisions as conditional.** Disproportionate burden requires a documented assessment.
- **Watch compound conditions.** Two limbs mean either can trigger the obligation.
- **Trust Cannot Say, especially where the claim would support more resource for your function.**
- **Notice the absence of a severity qualifier.** Where a prohibition has none, minor issues are still caught.
- **Separate "consider whether X" from "recommend X".**
- **Ask what a statement is for.** Disclosure, not compliance decoration.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Answering from professional knowledge.** The dominant error at your level.
- **Carrying "everything must conform" as a working assumption.**
- **Accepting an undocumented disproportionate burden claim.** Without an assessment it is not a claim.
- **Assuming an exemption survives a substantial revision.**
- **Treating an annual review as the whole review obligation.**
- **Marking True on a claim that would support your case.**
- **Reading a proxy outcome as the thing itself.** Fewer defects at release is not greater accessibility.
- **Letting a full conformance claim be published with known gaps.**

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment presents realistic workplace scenarios and asks what you would do. There is no passage to parse and no arithmetic. You get a dilemma of the kind your role produces, four or five candidate responses, and a question asking which is most effective and often which is least.

This section measures whether your judgement matches what the profession considers good practice at your level. Your role places you as the person who creates an accessibility vision for an organisation, defines and assures best practice, influences organisational strategy, ensures the conditions for specialists to work effectively, develops departmental capability, and owns the overall approach to accessibility risk including how legal compliance risk is managed.

Four features distinguish your level.

Almost nothing you do is executed by you. An option solving a problem through your personal intervention is usually second-best, because it does not survive your absence and does not scale past your calendar.

You own the risk position rather than individual conformance. That means how a gap is known, disclosed and planned for matters as much as whether it exists — and an organisation that has stopped telling you about problems is in a worse position than one with more problems and honest reporting.

You absorb pressure downwards. When a director general dismisses a specialist's finding, the specialist should not be holding that line.

And you are accurate before you are forceful. Your findings carry statutory weight, which makes over-application expensive: an organisation that learns your function overstates requirements will discount the genuine ones.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The judgement dimensions map onto your named skills:

- **Owning risk and disclosure** maps to your role level's ownership of accessibility and corporate legal compliance risk.
- **Governance intervention** maps to **Governance and assurance (accessibility)**.
- **Mediation and difficult discussions** map to **Communicating information**, where you mediate, mend relationships and moderate discussions about high risk and complexity.
- **Building capability and conditions** maps to your role level's requirements on career paths, recruitment and conditions for specialists.
- **High-risk decisions** map to **Leadership and guidance**, where you make and justify decisions of high risk, impact and complexity.
- **Cross-government work** maps to your role level's engagement with counterparts and the accessibility community.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — A specialist under pressure**

A senior specialist tells you a director general dismissed their audit finding as gold-plating and instructed the team to launch. What is the most effective response?

- A) Tell the specialist to hold the line.
- B) Establish with them which findings are genuinely blockers and which are proportionality questions where the director general may have a point, then take the conversation yourself — with the blockers described by their consequence and a request that the launch decision be recorded.
- C) Escalate to the permanent secretary's office.
- D) Accept the decision; it is the director general's to make.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Two things must happen: the genuine blockers get a proper decision, and the specialist is not left arguing with a director general. Option B does both, and the first clause is what makes it credible — "gold-plating" sometimes describes a real over-application, and going into the conversation having separated the blockers from the minor findings means you are defending a short, strong list rather than a long, contestable one. Taking the conversation yourself is your job. Requesting a recorded decision matters because it moves the risk to somebody accountable and makes it visible. Option A is least effective, leaving a specialist to refuse a director general by proxy and teaching them that your support is rhetorical. Option D accepts a decision made without the information.

**Question 2 (easy) — A false accessibility statement**

You discover a published statement claiming full conformance for a service with four known serious issues. What is the most effective response?

- A) Correct the statement.
- B) Correct it immediately, then establish how it came to be published — because a statement contradicting an internal audit means whoever writes statements is not seeing audit output, which will be true across the estate — and fix that connection.
- C) Escalate to legal.
- D) Prioritise fixing the four issues.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Two problems exist and they differ in kind. The four issues are a conformance problem with a plan. The false claim is a disclosure problem, and it is worse: an organisation with known gaps and an honest statement is defensible, while one publishing a claim it knows to be untrue has converted remediation into misrepresentation. Option B corrects that first and asks the structural question, which is the part specific to your level — the disconnection between audit and statement will not be confined to one service, and finding it is worth more than the correction. Option D is least effective, addressing the lesser problem while a false public claim stands. Option A fixes the instance. Option C may follow and is not the first action.

**Question 3 (easy) — Conditions for specialists**

Your specialists are spread across directorates, spend most of their time on audits, have no time for coaching or standards work, and two of five have left in a year. What is the strongest response?

- A) Discuss workload with each directorate.
- B) Treat it as structural: reduce audit demand by devolving routine checks to teams and automating what can be automated, protect time for coaching and standards work, and change how specialists are deployed so they are not isolated.
- C) Recruit replacements.
- D) Introduce a wellbeing initiative.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** Specialists spending all their time auditing is the signature of a function operating as a service desk rather than a capability, and it produces exactly this attrition — the work is repetitive, the findings are disputed, and nothing changes structurally. Option B attacks the demand rather than the supply, which is the only lever that works: devolving routine checks and automating a defined class of defect reduces the audit load, and the freed time goes to the work that reduces it further. Option C is least effective, recruiting into conditions that produced two departures, in a small market where your reputation as an employer travels fast through the community. Option A addresses symptoms individually. Option D treats a design-of-work problem as morale.

**Question 4 (moderate) — A governance intervention**

You are asked to build accessibility into project governance. Existing gateways cover business case, delivery confidence and benefits. What is most effective?

- A) Add an accessibility question at each gateway.
- B) Identify where accessibility is actually determined — procurement, technology selection and the definition of done — and place requirements there, since a gateway question asked after those decisions produces a status report rather than an intervention.
- C) Introduce a separate accessibility gateway.
- D) Require an accessibility statement at each gateway.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Governance is useful only where it can change something, and whether a service will be accessible is largely determined before any gateway: which component library is bought, which framework is chosen, what a team considers done. Option B places requirements at those points, which is what your role means by analysing governance and adding measures that build accessibility into all project and change activity. Option D is least effective, requiring a document at a checkpoint — statements produced to satisfy gateways are produced to satisfy gateways, and it also risks normalising statements as compliance artefacts rather than disclosures. Option A produces reporting. Option C adds a stage teams must pass without reaching the determining decisions.

**Question 5 (moderate) — A supplier's claim in procurement**

Procurement asks whether a supplier's "fully WCAG 2.2 AA conformant" claim is sufficient assurance for a major system. What is most effective?

- A) Accept it; the claim is explicit.
- B) Ask what the claim covers — which components, in isolation or composed, with which assistive technologies, at what date — and advise procurement to require the scope in the contract along with a remediation obligation and the right to test.
- C) Reject it; supplier claims cannot be trusted.
- D) Commission an independent audit before contract.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** An unscoped conformance claim is close to meaningless, and the questions in option B make it assessable. The second half is what makes this a head of profession's answer rather than a specialist's: converting the claim into contractual obligations — stated scope, a duty to remediate, and a right to test — is worth far more than any assessment you could make of the claim as it stands, because it gives the organisation leverage for the life of the system. Option A is least effective, treating an unscoped marketing statement as assurance, which is how organisations acquire inaccessible systems with paperwork saying otherwise. Option C is unhelpfully absolute and gets you excluded from procurement. Option D may be justified for a major system and is slow, expensive, and often unnecessary once the scoping questions are asked.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Reporting to a board**

You must report the organisation's accessibility position to a board. Nineteen services are audited with 41 documented issues; fourteen have never been audited. What is most effective?

- A) Report the 41 issues and the remediation plan.
- B) Report both halves and make the comparison explicit: the documented issues are managed risk with owners and dates, while the fourteen unaudited services are unmanaged risk and probably the larger exposure — because a board left to itself will attend to the visible half.
- C) Report that 43% of services have current audit coverage.
- D) Report that most services have been audited and the position is improving.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** This is your role's central judgement about risk communication. A board attends to what is visible, and findings are visible while absences are not, so a report listing 41 issues will produce a conversation about those 41 and none about the fourteen services nobody has examined. Option B makes the comparison explicit and states the counter-intuitive conclusion plainly: the services with findings against them are in a better position than the ones without. Option D is least effective, inferring safety from coverage and inverting the logic, and it is the version a board most wants to hear — which is precisely why it is dangerous. Option A reports the visible half. Option C reports a coverage statistic without the interpretation that makes it meaningful.

**Question 7 (moderate) — A specialist over-applying**

You notice that one of your specialists routinely reports usability problems as conformance failures, and teams have started disputing accessibility findings generally. What is most effective?

- A) Correct the specific reports.
- B) Speak to them directly and privately with two or three examples, explain the consequence you are both experiencing — that conflating the two gives teams grounds to dispute everything including genuine failures — and agree a reporting convention that keeps usability findings visible without miscategorising them.
- C) Raise it with the whole team.
- D) Review their reports before issue.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** The problem is real, the consequence is shared, and the direct private conversation is the right route. Option B uses specific examples, which makes the pattern discussable rather than a characterisation, and it names the consequence rather than the error — teams disputing findings generally is a far better basis for agreement than being told you are wrong. The final clause matters: usability findings are valuable and should be reported, just not as conformance failures, so the answer is a convention rather than suppression. Option C is least effective, raising an individual's practice in a group setting, which will be experienced as a rebuke and will harden the position. Option D makes you a permanent bottleneck and teaches nothing.

**Question 8 (harder) — A finding that arrives at the worst moment**

Two days before a high-profile launch, a specialist finds a barrier on the payment step affecting screen reader users. The programme director says the risk is small. What is most effective?

- A) Insist on a delay.
- B) Supply the arithmetic — even a conservative proportion of the user base runs to thousands a year, and a barrier at payment means those users cannot transact at all — state the statutory position, establish whether an assisted route exists as a temporary measure, and put the decision to the accountable owner with a recorded answer.
- C) Agree; the numbers are small.
- D) Escalate immediately to the director general.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** The programme director's argument rests on an intuition about proportion, which is why option B leads with arithmetic rather than principle. It then does three further things: states the statutory position, because a barrier at payment carries a legal dimension a content page may not; looks for a temporary assisted route, since an alternative channel may make launching defensible and is the option nobody considers under pressure; and puts the decision to the accountable owner with a recorded answer, because an unrecorded decision drifts into launch and leaves the risk unowned. Option C is least effective, accepting an argument that would justify excluding any minority and establishing it as a precedent. Option A offers no route. Option D bypasses the person who should decide.

**Question 9 (harder) — Capability rather than dependency**

Your function's audit queue is now eight weeks. Teams cannot get reviews before release. What is most effective?

- A) Recruit more specialists.
- B) Treat the queue as evidence that the function is a dependency rather than a capability: devolve the routine checks teams can reliably perform, automate the class of defect that can be automated, and reserve specialist review for complex or high-risk cases — then measure whether the queue shortens.
- C) Prioritise the queue by service criticality.
- D) Tell teams to release without review.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** An eight-week queue means teams are waiting on a small function to do work much of which they could do themselves, and it will grow with your reputation. Option B reframes it correctly — a capability problem wearing the costume of a capacity problem — and attacks it on the two fronts that work: transferring routine checks and automating a defined class. Your role names developing departmental capability precisely so this is possible. Option D is least effective, and it is the outcome an unmanaged queue produces by default anyway, which is the real danger: an eight-week queue does not stop releases, it stops reviews, and the function becomes irrelevant while appearing overloaded. Option C is a sensible interim measure that does not change the underlying position. Option A may be justified and is a far easier ask once routine work has been devolved.

**Question 10 (harder) — Mediating between teams**

A delivery directorate and your function are in open conflict: they say your findings are unrealistic and late; your specialists say the directorate ignores them. Both are partly right. What is most effective?

- A) Defend your specialists.
- B) Convene both, establish the specific instances behind each grievance, and address the parts that are true — if findings arrive late, fix when review happens; if findings include over-applications, fix how they are written — since a conflict where both sides are partly right is only resolvable by conceding your half first.
- C) Escalate to the directorate's director.
- D) Require the directorate to accept findings.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Your role names mediating between people and mending relationships, and this is the case. The structure of the situation is what determines the response: when both sides are partly right, nothing moves until one of them concedes their half, and it must be you — partly because you have more to gain from the relationship working, and partly because a function that concedes nothing is one whose findings will always be resisted. Option B establishes specifics rather than positions, which is what makes the concession possible rather than merely gracious. Option D is least effective, requiring acceptance of findings that include over-applications, which entrenches the conflict and guarantees the next round. Option A defends a position you know to be partly wrong. Option C escalates before attempting resolution.

**Question 11 (harder) — Strategy that will not survive**

Your draft accessibility strategy commits to full AA conformance across all services within two years. A realistic assessment says the estate cannot reach that. What is most effective?

- A) Publish it; ambition drives progress.
- B) Revise it: replace an unachievable universal with a sequenced commitment — highest-risk and highest-volume services first, all services assessed and disclosed within a defined period, and a prevention mechanism in the pipeline — because a commitment everyone knows to be undeliverable devalues every other commitment beside it.
- C) Extend the timescale to five years.
- D) Remove the conformance commitment.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A commitment nobody believes is not ambition; it is a statement that will be quietly ignored, and its presence teaches readers that the document's other commitments need not be taken seriously either. Option B replaces it with something true and better structured: sequencing by risk and volume puts effort where the most people are affected, committing to assess and disclose everything addresses the unknown risk which is the larger exposure, and the prevention mechanism is the only element that changes the rate at which the problem is created. Option A is least effective and is the option most likely to be argued for in the room. Option C makes an unachievable commitment less obviously so without making it deliverable. Option D removes the standard rather than making the commitment realistic.

**Question 12 (harder) — Cross-government collaboration**

Three departments each maintain their own accessibility guidance for the same technology stack, each slightly different. You have no authority over the other two. What is strongest?

- A) Focus on your own department's guidance.
- B) Convene the three heads of accessibility, establish whether the guidance is genuinely addressing the same problems, and if so propose one maintained version with the evidence and testing behind it — accepting that you are building consensus rather than exercising authority, and that one well-tested guide is more achievable than three adequately maintained ones.
- C) Ask the cross-government community to mandate a common version.
- D) Publish your version for others to adopt.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Three departments maintaining three versions of the same guidance is precisely the waste cross-government collaboration exists to prevent, and the accessibility argument is unusually strong: keeping technical guidance current against changing assistive technology and browser behaviour is genuinely hard, and three under-maintained guides are worse than one maintained one — divergent advice on the same technical question is actively confusing to developers who move between departments. Option B is calibrated to the authority you have: convening is available, mandating is not, and establishing whether the problems are genuinely common avoids agreeing something that fits nobody. Option A is least effective, accepting solvable waste while three sets of developers receive inconsistent advice. Option C reaches for a mandate before building the case. Option D publishes without the evidence or the maintenance commitment.

### Preparation tips

- **Separate blockers from proportionality questions before defending a list.** A short strong list wins where a long contestable one does not.
- **Treat a false statement as a disclosure problem, not a conformance one.**
- **Attack audit demand, not supply.** Recruiting into a service-desk function reproduces the attrition.
- **Place governance requirements where decisions are made.**
- **Convert supplier claims into contractual obligations.** Scope, remediation duty, right to test.
- **Make the known-versus-unknown comparison explicit to boards.** They attend to what is visible.
- **Concede your half first when both sides are partly right.**
- **Revise commitments that cannot be met.** They devalue the ones beside them.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Leaving a specialist to refuse a senior stakeholder by proxy.**
- **Fixing a false statement without asking how it was published.**
- **Accepting an unscoped conformance claim as procurement assurance.**
- **Reporting only the audited half of an estate.**
- **Letting an audit queue grow.** It does not stop releases, it stops reviews.
- **Defending a position you know to be partly wrong.**
- **Publishing an unachievable commitment.**
- **Requiring acceptance of findings that include over-applications.**

## Conclusion

You have reached the end, and it is worth registering the scale: forty-eight questions across four assessment types, with the reasoning worked through on each. That is a substantial piece of professional preparation and more than most people at your level undertake.

Look back at the ground. In the cognitive section you treated "no blockers" as a scoping question, distinguished what an automated tool detects from what its silence means, read a recorded sign-off that failed as an assurance failure rather than a compliance one, separated managed from unmanaged risk and named the unaudited estate as the larger exposure, and placed governance requirements at the decisions rather than the reviews. In the numeric section you read thresholds at their boundaries, presented a prevalence estimate with a conservative caveat, defined audit currency, expressed a gap as a ratio rather than two percentages, and refused to let "98% pass automated testing" stand as an accessibility position. In the verbal section you attached a date qualifier to the right clause, treated disproportionate burden as the procedural condition it is, noticed a review obligation with two limbs, and declined to mark True on a claim that would have supported your own case. And in the situational judgement section you separated blockers from proportionality questions before defending them, converted a supplier claim into contractual obligations, conceded your half of a conflict first, and revised a strategy commitment nobody believed.

Two threads run through all of it, and they pull against each other productively. The first is precision: your function's findings carry statutory weight, which makes being exactly right at the boundary the basis on which anyone acts on them, and over-application the specific way accessibility functions lose organisational standing. The second is that precision is not the point. People using services is the point, and a function that is technically impeccable, consulted late and quietly resented has less effect than one equally precise that arrives with routes rather than verdicts.

A word about the particular difficulty of your level. You own how an organisation understands its own accessibility risk, which means your errors are not caught — a flattering number you accept becomes the organisation's position, a scoping question you fail to ask becomes assurance, and an exemption you grant becomes a precedent. The verbal reasoning section is the clearest demonstration of why familiarity is a hazard rather than a protection, and the pattern generalises to every judgement you make alone.

If you want to build on this, a few things repay effort more than rereading. Sit with somebody using a screen reader on a service your function has signed off, and notice how much of their difficulty the report did not capture. Take three published accessibility statements from your organisation and check them against the audits behind them. Ask what proportion of your specialists' time goes on audits, and what would have to be true for it to be half that. Read one exemption properly, including its conditions, and write the plain version your organisation can use. And ask a head of accessibility in another department to critique your strategy as harshly as they would a stranger's.

Finally, for the day itself. An assessment samples your reasoning on one morning under artificial conditions. It is not a verdict on your judgement or your career. Read the stem twice, read thresholds at the boundary, trust Cannot Say, describe consequences rather than criteria, and remember that at your level the strongest answer is almost always the one that is precisely right, honest about what is unknown, and aimed at the mechanism rather than the instance.

Good luck. Go carefully, be exact, and keep the unknown half of the estate in view.
