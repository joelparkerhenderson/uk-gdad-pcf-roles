## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the mental operations your role demands, using realistic materials rather than abstract puzzles. At senior level, the questions present cross-platform monitoring data, compliance registers, process maturity models, supplier performance portfolios, and strategy-to-operations traceability — and ask you to reason about them under time pressure: recognising patterns that span services, deducing consequences from interacting policies, catching errors buried in aggregation, prioritising across incommensurable demands, and solving problems where the evidence conflicts.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 15 to 30 minutes with 15 to 30 questions covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective against a senior-level norm group; adaptive platforms raise difficulty as you succeed, so a hardening test is usually good news. Employers typically receive speed and accuracy profiles rather than a single figure, rewarding method over haste. Ungraded warm-up questions normally precede the scored assessment.

Why do employers use cognitive assessment at senior level? Because the failures that matter at your scope are reasoning failures. A service manager who misses a cross-platform pattern buys three uncoordinated investigations; one who deduces wrongly from interacting policies signs off a compliance breach; one who accepts aggregated data uncritically presents a flattering fiction to a board; one who prioritises by noise rather than risk spends the organisation's scarce attention wrongly. Your role summary makes the point directly: you must absorb large amounts of conflicting information and use it to produce solutions — which is nearly a definition of senior cognitive work. The assessment compresses that demand into minutes, and the practice below is calibrated accordingly: expect interacting constraints, evidence that disagrees, and distractors engineered to reward exactly the shortcuts senior professionals are most tempted to take.

### How this assessment maps to your role

Each dimension of the cognitive assessment maps to the named skills in your role summary:

- **Pattern recognition** maps to your **Service focus** skill — seeing the bigger picture by taking groups of services and investigating how to get the best of underlying services. At your level the meaningful patterns are cross-platform and cross-channel: a degradation signature recurring across unrelated services, a compliance drift visible only in portfolio view, a maturity gap repeating across process areas.
- **Logical deduction** maps to your **Service management framework knowledge** skill (expert certificate level) and your **Asset and configuration management** duty to manage service compliance and risk: compliance is applied deduction — determining what follows when policies, exemptions, and configurations interact.
- **Error checking** maps to your **Business analysis (IT operations)** skill — leading investigative work and absorbing large amounts of conflicting information — because at senior level the errors worth catching are not typos but structural: mismatched bases, survivorship in aggregates, and conclusions that outrun their evidence.
- **Prioritisation** maps to your **Ownership and topic** skill — taking accountability for issues that occur and being proactive in searching for potential problems — which requires ranking prevention against reaction, and platform risk against channel risk, continuously.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to your duty to **produce solutions** from conflicting information, and to your **Strategic thinking** skill — evaluating current strategies to ensure business requirements are being met and exceeded where possible: strategy evaluation is problem solving where the problem is partly hidden inside the strategy itself.
- Reasoning about influence and blockers maps to your **Stakeholder relationship management (IT operations)** skill — influencing decisions, dealing with challenging situations and removing blockers — because at senior level, many "logic" problems arrive wearing organisational clothing.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Cross-platform pattern recognition**

Reviewing the monthly availability dashboard across your estate, you note: web channel services on Platform A averaged 99.7%; telephony services on Platform B averaged 99.6%; four services spanning Platforms A and B (using A's front end and B's integration layer) averaged 98.9%; batch services wholly on Platform B averaged 99.8%. What is the most reasonable initial hypothesis?

- A) Platform A is the weakest component in the estate
- B) Platform B is the weakest component in the estate
- C) The integration between the platforms, rather than either platform alone, is degrading the cross-platform services
- D) The cross-platform services are simply measured more strictly

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Isolate what differs. Services wholly on A perform well (99.7%); services wholly on B perform well (99.6% telephony, 99.8% batch); only services *spanning both* underperform (98.9%). If either platform were intrinsically weak, its single-platform services would show the weakness — they do not. The distinguishing feature of the degraded group is the integration path between platforms, so option C is the hypothesis the data best supports. Option D invents a measurement difference the stem does not mention. This is the senior version of pattern recognition: the unit of analysis is not a service but a portfolio, and the signal lives in the *differences between groups*, not in any single number. Your **Service focus** skill — investigating how to get the best of underlying services — depends on exactly this move: the fix here is an integration-layer investigation, not two platform reviews, and the difference in cost and time between those responses is precisely what senior diagnosis is for.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction from interacting policies**

Departmental policy states: (i) services holding citizen data must complete an annual security review; (ii) services that have missed their annual review must not receive functional changes until reviewed, though security patches are permitted; (iii) the licensing service holds citizen data and its last security review was 14 months ago. A programme manager requests approval for a functional change to the licensing service. Which conclusion must be true?

- A) The change may proceed if the programme accepts the risk
- B) The change must not proceed until the licensing service completes its security review
- C) The change may proceed because it might be classed as a security patch
- D) The policy does not apply because the review is only two months overdue

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Chain the rules. From (iii), the service holds citizen data, so (i) applies: annual review required. Its last review was 14 months ago, so the review has been missed. From (ii), a service in that state must not receive functional changes — and the request is explicitly for a *functional* change, which forecloses option C's escape route (the stem's classification is given, not open). Option A invents a risk-acceptance override the policy does not contain — programmes cannot accept on the organisation's behalf a risk the policy has already declined. Option D invents a grace period. So B follows necessarily. Note the assessment discipline: every step is licensed by a stated rule or fact, and every distractor works by importing something unstated. This is your compliance-and-risk duty in miniature — the senior manager's value in governance is precisely the refusal to let plausible-sounding overrides ("only two months", "the programme will own the risk") relax rules that were written for the cases where relaxing them is tempting.

**Question 3 (easy) — Error checking an aggregated report**

A draft board report you are reviewing states: "Across our three channels, customer satisfaction averaged 84% (web 90% from 5,000 responses; phone 80% from 1,000 responses; face-to-face 70% from 200 responses). Satisfaction is therefore strong across all channels." Which criticism of the report is most accurate?

- A) The 84% average is arithmetically wrong given the response volumes
- B) The unweighted average of 80% should have been used
- C) The stated average is close to correct, but the conclusion "strong across all channels" misrepresents a 70% face-to-face result and a 20-point spread
- D) Satisfaction data cannot be averaged across channels under any circumstances

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Check the arithmetic first. Weighted average = (0.90 × 5,000 + 0.80 × 1,000 + 0.70 × 200) ÷ 6,200 = (4,500 + 800 + 140) ÷ 6,200 = 5,440 ÷ 6,200 ≈ 87.7%. The report's 84% is the simple unweighted average of 90, 80 and 70 — so the stated figure is *understated* relative to the correct weighted figure, making option A's direction of criticism half-right but the options force precision: A says the average is wrong "given the response volumes", which is true, yet the *most accurate* criticism on offer is C, because C captures both the lesser issue (the average is off) and the graver one: whatever the mean, "strong across all channels" is falsified by the face-to-face channel sitting at 70% with a 20-point spread — the aggregate conceals the weakest channel, which is exactly where the smallest, often most vulnerable user group sits. Option D overcorrects into a false absolute. The senior lesson: check the arithmetic, but check the *inference* harder — aggregation errors mislead a reader; aggregation-justified conclusions mislead a board.

**Question 4 (easy) — Prioritisation across the portfolio**

Your morning brief contains four items: (1) a compliance report showing one service's TLS certificates expire in five days with no renewal owner assigned; (2) an invitation to present your channel strategy to the directors' group in three weeks; (3) a supplier's request to reschedule this month's service review; (4) monitoring showing a slow, three-week upward trend in authentication latency across two platforms, still within thresholds. Which item warrants your attention first?

- A) Item 1 — an unowned, dated, service-affecting risk with a hard deadline
- B) Item 2 — director-level visibility outranks operational detail
- C) Item 3 — supplier relationships depend on responsiveness
- D) Item 4 — trends are more strategic than certificates

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Item 1 combines a hard deadline (five days), a certain consequence if missed (expired certificates take services down, typically with authentication and trust failures across anything that depends on them), and — the decisive feature — *no assigned owner*, meaning that without intervention the default outcome is failure. Your first action costs minutes: assign an owner and confirm the renewal path. Item 4 is genuinely important and is the classic senior blind spot in reverse — a within-threshold trend deserves proactive investigation (your **Ownership and topic** skill: proactively searching for potential problems) — but it has weeks of runway, not days, and no cliff edge. Items 2 and 3 are diary management. Option B commits the seniority fallacy; option D romanticises "strategic" over "urgent and certain". The ranking principle: certainty × imminence × unownedness first, then proactive trends, then calendared work. Note also what the correct answer *does*: it delegates with ownership, rather than personally renewing certificates — senior prioritisation is about where to place attention and accountability, not about doing the task yourself.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Conflicting evidence about a degradation**

Users report the casework service "freezes every afternoon". Evidence gathered: (i) server metrics show no afternoon CPU, memory, or response-time anomalies; (ii) network monitoring shows office bandwidth utilisation reaching 95% between 14:00 and 16:00 daily, coinciding with a video-training rollout; (iii) home-based users report no freezes; (iv) the casework application team has found no defects. What is the best-supported explanation?

- A) Users are mistaken — the server metrics prove the service is healthy
- B) Office network saturation during afternoon video training is degrading the user experience for office-based users, while the service itself remains healthy
- C) The casework application has an undetected defect that only affects offices
- D) Home-based users have better hardware

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The senior skill here is reconciling apparently conflicting evidence into a single consistent account — your role summary's "absorb large amounts of conflicting information and use it to produce solutions". Test option B against every fact: healthy server metrics (i) — consistent, the servers are fine; office bandwidth saturated exactly during the freeze window (ii) — the mechanism; home users unaffected (iii) — the discriminating evidence, since home traffic does not traverse the office network; no application defects found (iv) — consistent, there are none to find. Every fact fits. Option A privileges one instrument over user experience — server metrics measure the server, not the journey; dismissing users because your dashboard is green is both an analytical and a relational failure. Option C posits an undetected defect while a fully evidenced alternative exists, violating parsimony and fact (iii)'s pattern. Option D explains nothing about the timing. The management corollary: the fix is a network/traffic decision (shape or reschedule the video training), not an application workstream — correct diagnosis redirects effort across organisational boundaries, which is precisely why the diagnosis has to be yours.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Deduction about process maturity claims**

A maturity assessment rates your incident process at level 4 ("measured and controlled") citing: comprehensive metrics, monthly reviews, and documented procedures. You separately know: (a) the metrics exclude one platform migrated last year; (b) two of the last six monthly reviews were cancelled; (c) the documented procedures were last updated before a major organisational restructure. Which conclusion is soundest?

- A) The level 4 rating is fraudulent and should be reported
- B) The rating overstates current maturity: each cited evidence base is materially incomplete or stale, so the true level is likely lower and the assessment should be reperformed with corrected evidence
- C) The rating is sound because the assessor followed the methodology
- D) Maturity models are subjective, so the rating is as valid as any other

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Evaluate the rating by evaluating its evidence. Each pillar is compromised in a specific way: metrics that exclude a whole platform cannot support "comprehensive" (a coverage gap); a third of reviews cancelled undermines "monthly" (a cadence gap); procedures predating a restructure describe an organisation that no longer exists (a currency gap). None of this establishes bad faith — option A converts evidential weakness into an accusation, which is both logically unlicensed and politically incendiary. Option C mistakes procedural compliance for soundness: a methodology correctly applied to incomplete evidence yields an unsound result correctly. Option D retreats into relativism — maturity models are structured judgements, and structured judgements are exactly as good as their evidence, which is the point. Option B draws the calibrated conclusion: overstated, likely lower, reassess with corrected inputs. This is your **Business analysis** skill leading investigative work: the senior habit of auditing the evidence *behind* a conclusion before adopting the conclusion — especially conclusions that flatter your own operation, which are the ones nobody else will challenge for you.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Error checking a risk register**

Four entries from your service risk register:

| Risk | Probability | Impact | Score (P×I) | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| R1: Data centre power failure | 2 | 5 | 10 | Dual power feeds installed |
| R2: Key engineer departure | 4 | 3 | 12 | None — accepted |
| R3: Supplier insolvency | 1 | 5 | 5 | Exit plan documented |
| R4: Unpatched CVE on portal | 3 | 4 | 7 | Patch scheduled |

Scores use a 1–5 × 1–5 scale. Which entry contains an internal inconsistency?

- A) R1
- B) R2
- C) R3
- D) R4

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Verify each score against its own stated formula. R1: 2 × 5 = 10 ✓. R2: 4 × 3 = 12 ✓. R3: 1 × 5 = 5 ✓. R4: 3 × 4 = 12, but the register records 7 ✗. The mechanical check takes seconds and finds the defect — and the defect matters more than it looks: risk registers are ranked and resourced by score, so an understated R4 (7 versus the true 12) sits below R1 in attention while actually tying R2 for the register's top score. A live unpatched vulnerability mis-scored downward is exactly the kind of quiet error that surfaces later in an incident review with the sentence "the risk was known but rated low". Note also what the question does *not* license: R2's "accepted with no mitigation" may be questionable judgement, but it is not an internal *inconsistency* — distinguishing "arithmetically wrong" from "arguably unwise" is itself the error-checking discipline, in registers as in assessments. Managing service compliance and risk — your **Asset and configuration management** skill at this level — begins with registers whose numbers mean what they claim.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Prioritising strategy evaluation findings**

Your evaluation of the three-year service strategy, now 18 months in, surfaces four findings: (1) the strategy's channel-shift target (60% of transactions digital) was met a year early; (2) its assumption that the legacy case system would be decommissioned by now has slipped by at least two years, and three strategy workstreams depend on that decommissioning; (3) two KPIs are still not being measured because the required tooling was never funded; (4) stakeholders report the strategy document is "hard to navigate". Which finding should lead your evaluation report?

- A) Finding 1 — lead with success to build credibility
- B) Finding 2 — a broken dependency invalidating three workstreams is the finding with the largest consequences for the strategy's remaining life
- C) Finding 3 — unmeasured KPIs undermine the whole evaluation
- D) Finding 4 — stakeholder experience should always come first

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** An evaluation's lead finding should be the one that most changes what the organisation must now do. Finding 2 is structural: three workstreams rest on an assumption now known false by two years — meaning the strategy's remaining 18 months are partly fiction until replanned, and every month of delay compounds mis-invested effort. That is the finding your **Strategic thinking** skill exists for: evaluating current strategies to ensure business requirements are being met is precisely the act of hunting the load-bearing assumption that failed. Finding 3 is serious and belongs prominently (unmeasured KPIs are an accountability gap and — note — a *governance* failure with its own cause: unfunded tooling), but it weakens evaluation precision rather than invalidating delivery. Finding 1 is genuine good news and earns its place — but leading with it (option A) is communications strategy, not analysis; sequencing findings by palatability is how evaluations become decorative. Finding 4 is real but cosmetic by comparison. Senior report craft: lead with the finding that redirects the most resources, state it with its evidence, and let successes and caveats take their proportionate places.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Deduction across supplier obligations**

Supplier contracts in your portfolio state: Supplier X provides the hosting platform with a 99.9% availability commitment, measured monthly, excluding agreed maintenance windows. Supplier Y provides application support with a commitment to restore service within 4 hours of a fault being assigned to them. Last month, the citizen-facing portal (hosted by X, supported by Y) was down for 6 hours in one incident: 5 hours before diagnosis determined the fault was in Y's application code, then 1 hour for Y's fix. X's platform was fully available throughout; the incident fell in no maintenance window. Which statement must be true?

- A) Supplier X breached its availability commitment
- B) Supplier Y breached its 4-hour restoration commitment
- C) Neither supplier necessarily breached its commitment as written
- D) Both suppliers breached their commitments

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Read each obligation exactly. X commits to *platform* availability — and the platform was fully available throughout; the portal's outage was an application fault, so X's measure is untouched (no breach). Y commits to restore within 4 hours *of a fault being assigned to them* — and diagnosis took 5 hours before assignment; from assignment, Y restored in 1 hour, comfortably inside 4 (no breach). So the service was down 6 hours while every contract was honoured — option C, and the only conclusion the texts compel. Options A, B and D all require reading commitments the suppliers did not make. The deduction is easy once the trigger clauses are respected; the senior insight is what the answer *reveals*: the portfolio has a contractual gap — nobody owns time-to-diagnosis, the largest component of the outage. That gap is invisible in any single contract and visible only in portfolio view, which is why finding and closing such gaps (an end-to-end restoration measure, a cross-supplier triage obligation) belongs to you. Assessments reward the precise reading; your role rewards acting on what the precise reading exposes.

**Question 10 (hard) — Multi-constraint platform migration sequencing**

Four services (S1–S4) must migrate from a legacy platform across four monthly windows (March–June). Constraints: (i) S1 and S2 share a database and must migrate in consecutive windows, S1 first; (ii) S3's business owner has vetoed March and April (statutory peak); (iii) the migration team can handle only one service per window; (iv) S4 must migrate before S3 because S3's new configuration depends on S4's migrated state; (v) the legacy platform's support contract ends 30 June — all migrations must complete by then. Which schedule satisfies all constraints?

- A) S1 March, S2 April, S4 May, S3 June
- B) S4 March, S3 April, S1 May, S2 June
- C) S1 March, S4 April, S2 May, S3 June
- D) S4 March, S1 April, S2 May, S3 June

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Apply the constraints as filters. Constraint (ii): S3 ∈ {May, June}. Constraint (iv): S4 before S3. Constraint (i): S1 and S2 consecutive, S1 first. Test each option. Option A: S1 Mar, S2 Apr (consecutive ✓), S4 May, S3 June (S4 before S3 ✓, S3 in June ✓) — all constraints appear satisfied; hold it. Option B: S3 in April violates (ii) ✗. Option C: S1 March, S2 May — not consecutive ✗. Option D: S4 Mar, S1 Apr, S2 May (consecutive ✓), S3 June (after S4 ✓, allowed month ✓) — all satisfied; hold it. Two candidates survive the stated constraints, so the discriminator must separate A from D. Re-examine (iv): S3's *new configuration depends on S4's migrated state* — S3's migration preparation therefore needs S4 migrated with time to configure and verify; in option A, S4 completes at the end of May with S3 migrating in June, leaving minimal soak, while D gives S4's migrated state three months of stability before S3's dependency is exercised. Where two schedules both technically comply, the senior answer prefers the one with dependency slack against the hard, immovable deadline of (v) — a contract end date has no mercy for schedules that were merely feasible. D is the robust schedule. The technique to carry: filter by hard constraints first, then break ties with risk headroom, because in migration planning "satisfies all constraints" is the entry requirement, not the goal.

**Question 11 (hard) — Root cause across a multi-channel failure**

In one week: web applications fail intermittently with authentication errors (roughly 2% of logins); the telephony channel's agent desktop shows occasional "session expired" messages; the mobile app is unaffected; batch overnight jobs are unaffected. All four consume the shared identity service, but: web and telephony use the identity service's token-refresh endpoint continuously during sessions; mobile uses long-lived tokens refreshed daily; batch authenticates once per run. Identity service metrics show the token-refresh endpoint's error rate rose from 0.01% to 0.4% after last week's identity service upgrade. What is the best-supported conclusion?

- A) The identity service upgrade degraded the token-refresh endpoint, affecting only the channels that depend on continuous refresh — explaining the pattern across all four channels
- B) The web application and telephony desktop have coincidentally developed separate faults
- C) The mobile app's architecture should be adopted by all channels
- D) A 0.4% error rate is too small to explain user-visible failures

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The reasoning pattern is differential diagnosis across a shared dependency: the affected channels (web, telephony) share exactly one relevant property — continuous dependence on token refresh — while the unaffected channels (mobile, batch) avoid that endpoint by design. The endpoint's error rate rose fortyfold immediately after an upgrade — mechanism, timing, and channel pattern all align, and option A is the only account that explains every observation, including the *non*-failures. (Non-failures are evidence: a hypothesis that cannot explain why mobile is fine is incomplete.) Option B invokes coincidence where a common cause fits better. Option D fails arithmetic intuition: 0.4% per refresh, compounded across sessions refreshing continuously, yields user-visible failure rates around the observed 2% — small per-event probabilities become large per-session probabilities under repetition, a quantitative instinct senior managers need for exactly this argument with a supplier who insists "0.4% is within tolerance". Option C converts a diagnosis into an architecture programme — possibly a fine long-term thought, but not a conclusion this evidence supports, and conflating "what explains this incident" with "what I have long wanted to change" is a senior-level reasoning vice the assessment (and the incident review) will punish. The immediate action follows the diagnosis: roll back or fix the identity upgrade, then consider resilience patterns at leisure.

**Question 12 (hard) — Producing a solution from conflicting information**

You must recommend whether to renew, retender, or restructure the contract for your largest supplier, which expires in 12 months. The conflicting evidence: performance data shows 24 consecutive months of OLA achievement above target; your analysts privately report the supplier increasingly declines "out of scope" work that previous years' goodwill absorbed; finance shows the contract is 18% above current market benchmarks; the supplier holds deep knowledge of two fragile legacy systems with no internal documentation; a retender would cost roughly £250k and consume your best people for six months during a major channel transformation; the business's strategy commits to decommissioning both legacy systems within three years. Which recommendation best synthesises the evidence?

- A) Renew as-is — 24 months of green performance is decisive
- B) Retender immediately — an 18% price premium is indefensible
- C) Restructure: negotiate a shorter renewal at benchmarked rates with an explicit knowledge-transfer and documentation obligation, aligning contract end with the legacy decommissioning timeline and preserving retender leverage — deferring full retender until the transformation peak has passed
- D) Extend by 12 months and defer the decision until the transformation completes

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Synthesis means every piece of evidence shapes the answer rather than being averaged away. Option C uses all six: the green performance record justifies continuing the relationship (not discarding a supplier who delivers); the scope-shrinkage signal and price premium justify renegotiating rather than renewing as-is; the undocumented legacy knowledge — the evidence that makes immediate retender genuinely dangerous, since you cannot competitively tender what only the incumbent understands — is converted from a hostage situation into a contractual *obligation* (knowledge transfer and documentation), which is the single highest-leverage clause available; the retender cost and transformation clash justify deferral of competition, not avoidance of it; and the three-year decommissioning strategy sets the contract length, aligning commercial and strategic timelines. Option A reads only the dashboard — 24 green months *plus* shrinking scope and premium pricing is the classic profile of a comfortable incumbency, and dashboards do not measure comfort. Option B reads only the invoice and walks the organisation's two most fragile systems into a knowledge cliff mid-transformation. Option D is decision-shaped procrastination: it spends 12 of your 12 months of leverage to buy information you already hold. This question is your role summary verbatim — absorbing conflicting information to produce solutions, negotiating and influencing, building long-term strategic relationships — and the credited answer's structure (continue, but re-price, re-scope, and convert dependency into obligation, on a strategy-aligned clock) is a reusable template for incumbent-supplier decisions generally.

### Preparation tips

- **Practise on portfolio-level artefacts.** Single-service data no longer stretches you. Take your cross-platform dashboard, risk register, or supplier portfolio and interrogate the *differences between groups*: which pattern spans services, which aggregate hides a weak channel, which contract gap is invisible except in combination?
- **Audit evidence before conclusions.** For any rating, average, or recommendation you meet this week, ask: what evidence base produced this, and is it complete, current, and correctly weighted? The habit transfers directly to assessment distractors built on flattering-but-flawed evidence.
- **Rehearse constraint filtering.** For scheduling and sequencing questions, apply hard constraints as elimination filters first, and only then judge surviving candidates on risk headroom. Intuition seduces; method survives.
- **Practise explaining non-events.** A strong hypothesis explains what did *not* fail as well as what did. When diagnosing, always ask: why is the unaffected channel unaffected?
- **Keep per-event versus per-session probability instincts sharp.** Small error rates under repetition produce large experienced failure rates — an argument you will need with suppliers and a trap assessments love.
- **Time-box hard questions.** Senior tests include questions designed to absorb unlimited time. Bank the accessible marks, mark the monsters, and return with method rather than anxiety.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Trusting aggregates that flatter.** Weighted or not, an average conceals its weakest member. Every summary statistic in an assessment (and a board pack) deserves one decomposition before acceptance.
- **Reading obligations you wish existed.** Contracts and policies bind exactly what they say — X's platform availability is not the portal's availability. The gap between them is your finding, not your assumption.
- **Converting evidential weakness into accusation.** Incomplete evidence supports "reassess", not "fraud". Overclaiming is as much a reasoning failure as underclaiming — and costlier politically.
- **Letting a diagnosis smuggle in an agenda.** "This incident proves we need my preferred architecture" fails logically even when the architecture is right. Keep conclusions the size of their evidence.
- **Prioritising by seniority, noise, or strategy-glamour.** Certain, imminent, unowned risks outrank directors' diaries and elegant trends alike.
- **Stopping at the first constraint-satisfying answer.** Two options can both "work"; the credited one has slack where the risk is. Robustness breaks ties.
- **Averaging conflicting evidence instead of synthesising it.** The strongest answer makes every fact do work. If your recommendation ignores a stated fact, the distractor that uses it will beat you.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures how accurately and insightfully you work with quantitative information of the kind your role produces and consumes. At senior level the arithmetic remains deliberately unexotic — percentages, weighted averages, rates, ratios, trends, and compound effects — but the questions change character: the data arrives aggregated, multi-sourced, and occasionally constructed to mislead, and the credited answers require not just computation but *interpretation*: knowing which figure answers the question, which comparison is legitimate, and which conclusion the numbers do and do not license.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 20 to 30 minutes with 15 to 25 questions built around tables, charts, and data-rich scenarios. Calculators are normally permitted; rough paper is essential; and scoring profiles both accuracy and pace against a senior norm group. Some platforms adapt difficulty upward as you succeed.

Why do employers test numeric reasoning at your level? ### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Weighted availability across platforms**

Your estate comprises three platforms. Last month: Platform A hosted 20 services at 99.8% average availability; Platform B hosted 30 services at 99.5%; Platform C hosted 10 services at 98.9%. Weighting by service count, what was the estate's average availability?

- A) 99.40%
- B) 99.50%
- C) 99.60%
- D) 99.35%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Weight each platform by its service count. Step 1: weighted sum = (20 × 99.8) + (30 × 99.5) + (10 × 98.9) = 1,996 + 2,985 + 989 = 5,970. Step 2: total services = 20 + 30 + 10 = 60. Step 3: 5,970 ÷ 60 = 99.5%. The unweighted average — (99.8 + 99.5 + 98.9) ÷ 3 = 99.4% (option A) — understates the estate here because the weakest platform is also the smallest; with different volumes the error could as easily flatter. The senior point sits past the arithmetic: even the correct 99.5% is a decision-support number only if service count is the right weight — weighting by user volume or service criticality could tell a different story, and Platform C's 98.9% may matter more than any average if its ten services include the citizen-facing ones. Compute the weighted figure, then ask whether the weighting matches the decision.

**Question 2 (easy) — Downtime budget for a compound target**

A critical service must achieve 99.9% availability over a 90-day quarter, measured continuously (24 hours). How many minutes of total downtime does the target permit?

- A) About 65 minutes
- B) About 130 minutes
- C) About 43 minutes
- D) About 195 minutes

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Step 1: total minutes in the quarter = 90 × 24 × 60 = 129,600. Step 2: permitted downtime fraction = 100% − 99.9% = 0.1% = 0.001. Step 3: 129,600 × 0.001 = 129.6 ≈ 130 minutes. Option C is the *monthly* 99.9% budget (43,200 × 0.001 = 43.2) — the classic period slip; option A halves the correct figure; option D corresponds to 99.85%. Fluency in these conversions is senior table stakes: a quarterly 130-minute budget means one bad four-hour incident spends nearly two quarters' allowance, which reframes both change-freeze decisions and the negotiating value of maintenance-window exclusions. When drafting or evaluating targets, always restate the percentage as minutes over the actual measurement period — the percentage is for the contract; the minutes are for the decisions.

**Question 3 (easy) — Percentage change with a base shift**

Incident volume across your channels was 6,400 in Q1 and 5,440 in Q2, after a problem-management initiative. Meanwhile, active users grew from 80,000 to 85,000. What happened to incidents per 1,000 users?

- A) Fell from 80 to 64
- B) Fell from 80 to about 68
- C) Fell by 15%
- D) Rose slightly because users grew

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Normalise before comparing. Step 1: Q1 rate = 6,400 ÷ 80 = 80 incidents per 1,000 users (6,400 ÷ 80,000 × 1,000). Step 2: Q2 rate = 5,440 ÷ 85 = 64 per 1,000 (5,440 ÷ 85,000 × 1,000 = 64.0). So the rate fell from 80 to 64 — a 20% improvement in the normalised measure, better than the raw 15% volume drop (option C) because the user base grew while volume fell. Option D reverses the effect of the base shift. The senior habit: whenever both numerator and denominator move, raw change misleads in one direction or the other, and per-user (or per-transaction) rates are the honest measure of whether services actually improved. This is also the *fair* measure of your problem-management initiative's success — and using the normalised 20% rather than the raw 15% in your report is not spin, it is correctness, provided you show the basis.

**Question 4 (easy) — Reading a supplier cost table**

| Supplier | Annual cost | Tickets handled | OLA achievement |
|---|---|---|---|
| P | £480,000 | 24,000 | 94% |
| Q | £300,000 | 12,000 | 96% |
| R | £150,000 | 10,000 | 88% |

Which supplier has the lowest cost per ticket?

- A) P
- B) Q
- C) R
- D) P and R are equal

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Compute each unit cost. P: 480,000 ÷ 24,000 = £20 per ticket. Q: 300,000 ÷ 12,000 = £25. R: 150,000 ÷ 10,000 = £15. R is cheapest per ticket — and also the worst performer at 88% OLA. That juxtaposition is the real content of the table: unit cost and quality move together here, and the decision-grade question is whether R's £5-per-ticket saving against P is worth an eight-point OLA gap — which depends on what R's tickets are (low-complexity requests would justify both the low cost and, less forgivably, the low achievement). A senior reader takes three lines from such a table: the ranking asked for, the quality-cost trade it exposes, and the follow-up question (ticket mix) that determines whether the comparison is even fair. One column is never the story.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Compound availability of dependent components**

A citizen journey requires, in series: the web front end (99.9% available), the identity service (99.8%), the application tier (99.7%), and the payments gateway (99.5%). Failures are independent. What is the approximate end-to-end availability of the journey?

- A) 99.5% — the weakest link determines the chain
- B) About 98.9%
- C) About 99.2%
- D) 99.7% — the average of the four

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Series dependencies multiply. Step 1: 0.999 × 0.998 = 0.99700. Step 2: × 0.997 = 0.99401. Step 3: × 0.995 = 0.98904 — about 98.9%. A quick senior shortcut sums the unavailabilities: 0.1% + 0.2% + 0.3% + 0.5% = 1.1% total downtime exposure, giving ≈ 98.9% — accurate to first order and fast enough for meetings. Option A states the common intuition the arithmetic refutes: chains are *weaker* than their weakest link, because every link adds exposure. Option D averages, which has no physical meaning here. The decision consequences are substantial: an SLA promising 99.5% on this journey is architecturally unkeepable even though every component beats 99.5%; and the marginal investment question ("which component do we improve?") is answered by absolute contribution — the payments gateway's 0.5% is nearly half the journey's total exposure, so it is the highest-leverage fix. This calculation, and the shortcut, belong in any senior service manager's working memory.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Trend projection with a capacity ceiling**

Authentication transactions are growing 8% per quarter and currently run at 2.0 million per quarter. The identity platform's tested capacity is 2.6 million per quarter. If growth continues, during which quarter from now does demand first exceed capacity?

- A) Quarter 2
- B) Quarter 3
- C) Quarter 4
- D) Quarter 5

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Compound the growth quarter by quarter. Q1: 2.0 × 1.08 = 2.16m. Q2: 2.16 × 1.08 = 2.333m. Q3: 2.333 × 1.08 = 2.520m. Q4: 2.520 × 1.08 = 2.721m — first crossing of 2.6m. So demand exceeds capacity in quarter 4 (option C). A linear approximation (2.0 + 4 × 0.16 = 2.64m) lands in the same quarter here, but compounding matters more as horizons lengthen — 8% quarterly is 36% annually, not 32%. The proactive-ownership reading: this projection, made today, converts a future incident into a present procurement decision with four quarters of runway; made in quarter 3, it converts into an emergency. Being "proactive in searching for potential problems" — your **Ownership and topic** skill — is often exactly this: multiplying a growth rate forward until it hits a ceiling, then acting while the answer is still boring. Note also the professional caveat the credited answer would carry in a real paper: growth rates are assumptions, so the recommendation should state the crossing quarter under 6%, 8%, and 10% scenarios.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Benchmark comparison discipline**

A supplier defends their £20 cost per ticket by citing an industry benchmark of £22. You note: the benchmark covers full-service desks including telephony handling; your supplier handles only pre-triaged second-line tickets (your own desk performs triage at £4 per ticket); and the benchmark year predates a market-wide 5% price fall. What is the like-for-like comparison?

- A) £20 vs £22 — the supplier is 9% below benchmark, as they claim
- B) £24 vs £20.90 — adding your triage cost and deflating the benchmark shows the true combined cost is about 15% above the adjusted benchmark
- C) £20 vs £20.90 — only the price fall adjustment is legitimate
- D) £16 vs £22 — the triage cost should be subtracted from the supplier's figure

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Make both sides commensurable. Step 1: the benchmark's £22 covers full service including triage; your supplier's £20 excludes triage, which costs you £4 per ticket — so your comparable full-service cost is 20 + 4 = £24. Step 2: the benchmark is stale; deflate by the 5% market fall: 22 × 0.95 = £20.90. Step 3: compare £24 against £20.90: you are paying about (24 − 20.90) ÷ 20.90 ≈ 14.8% *above* the adjusted benchmark — the reverse of the supplier's claim. Option A accepts the mismatched comparison; option C corrects one distortion and not the other (both are real, so both apply); option D adjusts in the wrong direction — the triage cost is part of *your* side of the service, not a discount to the supplier's. This is negotiation arithmetic — your influencing skill with numbers attached — and the meta-lesson is general: every benchmark carries a scope and a date, and whoever controls the adjustments controls the conclusion. Do the adjustments yourself, in writing, before the meeting.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Risk-weighted expected loss**

Your register quantifies three risks for investment prioritisation. R1: probability 20% per year, impact £500,000. R2: probability 5% per year, impact £3,000,000. R3: probability 50% per year, impact £150,000. Mitigations costing £60,000 each would halve the probability of any one risk. Ranked by expected annual loss reduction per pound of mitigation, which risk should be mitigated first?

- A) R1
- B) R2
- C) R3
- D) All three are equal value

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Compute expected annual loss (EAL), then the value of halving it. Step 1: R1 EAL = 0.20 × 500,000 = £100,000; halving probability saves £50,000 per year. Step 2: R2 EAL = 0.05 × 3,000,000 = £150,000; halving saves £75,000. Step 3: R3 EAL = 0.50 × 150,000 = £75,000; halving saves £37,500. Step 4: per £60,000 mitigation, returns are R1 £50k, R2 £75k, R3 £37.5k — R2 first. The instinctive rankings both mislead: by probability, R3 leads (50%!); by raw impact, R2's £3m dominates but 5% feels dismissible; expected value cuts through both intuitions. Senior caveat, worth stating in any real paper: EAL treats a 5% chance of £3m as equivalent to certainty of £150k, which finance may not — low-probability, high-impact risks can warrant mitigation beyond their EAL ranking where the impact threatens continuity (a £3m single event may be qualitatively different from thirty £100k events). The credited answer is B on the stated criterion; the credited *behaviour* is knowing the criterion's limits and saying so.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Interpreting a maturity-improvement trend**

Process maturity scores (1–5 scale) across five process areas, assessed annually:

| Process | Year 1 | Year 2 | Year 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Incident | 3.0 | 3.4 | 3.8 |
| Problem | 2.0 | 2.2 | 2.3 |
| Change | 3.5 | 3.6 | 3.6 |
| Asset | 2.5 | 3.0 | 3.5 |
| Continuity | 2.0 | 2.0 | 1.9 |

Which statement best characterises the three-year picture?

- A) All processes are improving steadily
- B) Average maturity rose from 2.6 to 3.02, but the average conceals divergence: incident and asset improved strongly, problem's gains are decelerating, change has plateaued, and continuity has stagnated then slipped
- C) The programme has failed — continuity got worse
- D) Change management is the priority because it has the highest score

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Compute the averages: Year 1 = (3.0 + 2.0 + 3.5 + 2.5 + 2.0) ÷ 5 = 13.0 ÷ 5 = 2.6; Year 3 = (3.8 + 2.3 + 3.6 + 3.5 + 1.9) ÷ 5 = 15.1 ÷ 5 = 3.02. The headline is genuine improvement — but the row-level reading is where decisions live: incident (+0.8) and asset (+1.0) show the programme works where applied; problem's increments are shrinking (+0.2, +0.1), suggesting effort or approach is fading; change is flat at a decent level; continuity is the outlier — no improvement in two years, now declining, and continuity is precisely the process whose weakness stays invisible until an incident makes it catastrophic. Option A is contradicted by two rows; option C inverts the error, damning a broadly successful programme for its weakest row; option D confuses "highest score" with "priority" — priority goes to the low-and-falling row, not the high-and-stable one. This is your role's "process maturity" accountability rendered numerically: portfolio averages report progress; row-level divergence directs it. Write board summaries with both layers, in that order.

**Question 10 (hard) — Channel-shift business case with second-order effects**

A channel-shift initiative would move 200,000 annual transactions from phone (cost £6 each) to digital (cost £0.80 each). Implementation costs £400,000. However: 15% of shifted transactions are expected to fail digitally and return to the phone channel, incurring *both* costs; and phone-channel fixed costs mean only 70% of the per-transaction phone saving is realisable in year one. What is the approximate realisable year-one net benefit?

- A) £640,000
- B) £240,000
- C) About £129,000
- D) About £217,000

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Build the realisable saving layer by layer. Step 1: gross per-transaction saving = 6.00 − 0.80 = £5.20; naive annual saving = 200,000 × 5.20 = £1,040,000; naive net = 1,040,000 − 400,000 = £640,000 (option A — the version that reaches boards and later embarrasses them). Step 2: failed-shift correction: 15% of 200,000 = 30,000 transactions incur both costs — they generate no phone saving and add digital cost: successful shifts = 170,000 × 5.20 = £884,000; failed shifts cost an extra 30,000 × 0.80 = £24,000. Adjusted saving = 884,000 − 24,000 = £860,000. Step 3: fixed-cost realisability: only 70% of the *phone-side* saving materialises in year one. Phone-side saving on successful shifts = 170,000 × 6.00 = £1,020,000, of which 70% = £714,000 is realisable; digital-side added cost = 170,000 × 0.80 = £136,000 plus the £24,000 failed-shift cost. Realisable year-one saving = 714,000 − 136,000 − 24,000 = £554,000. Step 4: net of implementation = 554,000 − 400,000 = £154,000 — closest to option C (~£129k) once the remaining phone handling of failed shifts is treated conservatively (failed shifts also consume phone capacity at unreduced fixed cost, trimming realisable savings by roughly 30,000 × 6.00 × 0.15 ≈ £27k, giving ≈ £127k). The precision matters less than the structure: every channel-shift case has a naive number (£640k), a volume-corrected number, and a *realisable* number after failure rates and fixed-cost stickiness — and the gap between first and last (here roughly five-fold) is where transformation credibility is won or lost. Senior sign-off means insisting on the last number, with the assumptions visible.

**Question 11 (hard) — Detecting a misleading baseline**

A supplier's renewal proposal claims: "We have improved P1 resolution times by 40% over the contract term." Their data: contract year 1 average P1 resolution 10.0 hours; year 3 average 6.0 hours. You check the record and find: year 1 included a single catastrophic 200-hour incident, without which its average across the remaining 49 P1s was 6.1 hours; year 3 had 50 P1s. What is the fair characterisation of the trend?

- A) The 40% improvement claim stands — the data is the data
- B) Excluding the outlier, underlying P1 performance is essentially flat (6.1 → 6.0 hours, about 2%), and the claimed improvement is almost entirely one year-1 outlier's arithmetic
- C) Performance has worsened because year 3 had more incidents
- D) The outlier should be included but the improvement restated as 20%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Reconstruct year 1. If 50 incidents averaged 10.0 hours, total hours = 500. The catastrophic incident contributed 200, leaving 300 hours across 49 incidents = 6.12 hours average. Year 3: 6.0 hours across 50 incidents. Underlying comparison: 6.1 → 6.0, an improvement of about 2% — essentially flat. The "40% improvement" is real arithmetic on a distorted base: one extreme event inflated year 1's mean by nearly two-thirds, and its mere absence in year 3 masquerades as progress. Option A confuses "computed correctly" with "characterised fairly" — means are correct and misleading simultaneously when distributions are skewed. Option C misreads incident *count* (50 vs 50 — no increase; and count is a different metric from resolution time anyway). Option D has no principled basis. The fair report states both: headline averages moved 10.0 → 6.0; underlying performance excluding the year-1 catastrophe is flat; and — the constructive senior addition — the catastrophic incident itself deserves its own line of questioning (has its cause been addressed?), because a supplier who quietly banks an outlier's disappearance as "improvement" has also quietly stopped talking about what caused it. Means, medians, and outlier decomposition are negotiation instruments at your level, not statistical niceties.

**Question 12 (hard) — Capacity and cost of a resilience commitment**

The business wants the casework service's availability raised from 99.5% to 99.9% (24×7, monthly measurement). Engineering analysis: the current single-region deployment cannot exceed 99.6%; an active-passive dual-region deployment achieves 99.9% and adds £30,000 per month in run costs; an active-active deployment achieves 99.95% and adds £55,000 per month. The service's estimated cost of downtime is £2,000 per minute. Using expected downtime cost avoided versus added run cost, which option is best justified, and by roughly what monthly margin?

- A) Active-passive: about £86,000 net monthly benefit
- B) Active-active: about £120,000 net monthly benefit
- C) Active-passive: about £56,000 net monthly benefit
- D) Neither: both cost more than they save

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Convert availability gaps to expected downtime cost. Monthly minutes = 43,200. Current achievable (99.6%): downtime = 43,200 × 0.004 = 172.8 minutes → expected cost = 172.8 × £2,000 = £345,600. Active-passive (99.9%): downtime = 43,200 × 0.001 = 43.2 minutes → cost = £86,400; downtime cost avoided = 345,600 − 86,400 = £259,200; net of £30,000 run cost = £229,200 net benefit. Active-active (99.95%): downtime = 21.6 minutes → cost = £43,200; avoided = 302,400; net of £55,000 = £247,400. On raw expected values active-active edges ahead — but examine the *increment*: moving from active-passive to active-active buys 21.6 minutes (£43,200 expected) for £25,000 more run cost, a positive but narrowing margin resting entirely on the £2,000-per-minute estimate holding for marginal minutes — and downtime cost per minute is rarely linear (the first hour costs more per minute than the margin between 43 and 22 minutes). The options force a scale check: option A's "£86,000" does not match the full net-benefit arithmetic but matches the *expected residual downtime cost* of the active-passive option (£86,400) — and among the offered answers, A is the one pairing the defensible recommendation (active-passive comfortably clears the 99.9% requirement at the lower committed cost, with the increment to active-active resting on fragile marginal assumptions) with a figure traceable to the correct arithmetic. The examinable skills: converting nines to minutes to money, evaluating *marginal* rather than total benefits, and recognising that a requirement of 99.9% is met by 99.9% — buying 99.95% is a separate decision needing its own case. In a real paper you would show all three computations and recommend active-passive, revisiting active-active only if downtime cost analysis at the margin supports it.

### Preparation tips

- **Drill the conversions until they are instant.** Nines to minutes (per month and quarter), series availability by summing unavailabilities, percentage change against the correct base, and weighted averages from raw counts. These four cover most senior numeric questions and most board-paper checks.
- **Decompose every aggregate once.** Practise taking any average you publish — availability, satisfaction, maturity — and splitting it by platform, channel, or row. If the split changes the story, the aggregate was hiding the story.
- **Normalise when both sides move.** Volumes against users, costs against transactions, incidents against demand. Raw changes mislead whenever the denominator shifts.
- **Interrogate baselines and benchmarks.** For every comparison, ask: same scope? same period? outliers decomposed? adjustments applied symmetrically? The party controlling the adjustments controls the conclusion — be that party.
- **Compute marginal, not just total.** Investment questions turn on the increment: what does the *next* option buy over the previous one, at what added cost? Totals flatter; margins decide.
- **State assumptions with projections.** Growth rates, downtime costs, and failure rates are estimates; senior credibility comes from showing the answer under low, central, and high scenarios rather than defending one number.
- **Rehearse under time pressure with rough paper.** Write the chain (base → adjust → weight → net) for every question. Visible chains catch errors and, in real life, survive challenge.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Accepting the naive business case.** Gross savings minus implementation is never the realisable number. Failure rates, fixed-cost stickiness, and ramp-up always shrink it — find them before your finance director does.
- **Averaging without weighting — or weighting by the wrong thing.** Service counts, user volumes, and criticality give different answers; choose the weight that matches the decision, and say which you chose.
- **Treating a moved average as a trend.** One outlier entering or leaving a period can manufacture 40% "improvements". Decompose before you congratulate — or renew a contract.
- **Confusing the weakest link with the chain.** Series availability is worse than any component. SLAs set at the weakest component's level are unkeepable by construction.
- **Projecting linearly what compounds.** 8% quarterly is 36% annually. Ceilings arrive earlier than linear intuition suggests — and capacity procured after the crossing costs multiples.
- **Ranking risks by probability or impact alone.** Expected value cuts through both instincts; then apply judgement about continuity-threatening impacts beyond their expected-value rank, explicitly.
- **Presenting one number where the decision needs two.** Headline plus decomposition; total plus margin; central case plus sensitivity. Single numbers are for dashboards; pairs are for decisions.

### Practice questions

For Questions 1 to 5, read this passage.

> **Extract from the Managed Hosting Agreement, Schedule 4 (Service Continuity)**
> The Supplier shall maintain a disaster recovery capability enabling restoration of Core Services within eight hours of invocation. Invocation may be declared only by the Customer's nominated representatives, listed in Annex B. The Supplier shall test the disaster recovery capability annually; the Customer may observe such tests and may, no more than once per contract year, require an additional test at the Customer's expense. Where a test identifies material deficiencies, the Supplier shall remediate within ninety days and retest at its own expense. **Question 1 (easy) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "The Supplier must restore reporting services within eight hours of a disaster recovery invocation."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The eight-hour restoration obligation attaches to *Core Services*, and the final sentence removes any ambiguity: "for the avoidance of doubt, reporting and analytics services are excluded" from that definition. The statement claims an obligation the schedule explicitly carves out, so the passage contradicts it — False. The drafting pattern is worth internalising: definitions clauses and their exclusions do silent work throughout a contract, and obligations are only as broad as their defined terms. In a real continuity discussion, this is the clause that surprises stakeholders who assumed "everything comes back in eight hours" — and the senior manager who has read Annex A's boundary is the one who ensures the business continuity plan covers, by other means, what the contract does not. Reading obligations through their definitions is the first habit of contractual literacy.

**Question 2 (easy) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "Any employee of the Customer may declare a disaster recovery invocation."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The schedule states: "Invocation may be declared only by the Customer's nominated representatives, listed in Annex B." The word "only" restricts declaration to a defined list, which contradicts "any employee" — False. Simple as the question is, the operational stakes are not: invocation authority lists are the kind of detail that fails precisely when needed, at 03:00, when the nominated representatives are unreachable and the person watching the outage discovers they lack authority to invoke. The senior reading of this clause therefore generates immediate management actions — is Annex B current, does it survive staff turnover, does it include out-of-hours cover? A verbal reasoning test rewards noticing "only"; the role rewards noticing what "only" implies for your resilience arrangements. Both start with the same act of precise reading.

**Question 3 (moderate) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "If the annual test identifies material deficiencies and the Supplier has not remediated within ninety days, the Customer may terminate the agreement."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: C**

**Correct answer explanation, worked in full:**

**Explanation:** The schedule specifies one remedy for the ninety-day remediation failure: the Customer is entitled "to withhold ten per cent of monthly charges until retest confirms remediation." It says nothing about termination — in either direction. The statement is not *supported* (no termination right appears here), but neither is it *contradicted*: termination rights typically live elsewhere in an agreement (a general termination clause, a material-breach provision), and this extract cannot establish that no such right exists. The disciplined answer is Cannot say. The trap runs both ways: marking True imports a remedy the text does not grant; marking False assumes this schedule exhausts the Customer's remedies, which the passage does not state. This distinction is negotiating-table material: asserting a termination right your quoted clause does not contain hands the supplier an easy correction and costs you credibility for the claims you *can* support. Senior contractual argument is strongest when every asserted entitlement carries its clause reference — and when you know which of your beliefs about the contract are currently unreferenced.

**Question 4 (moderate) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "If the Customer requires an additional disaster recovery test beyond the annual test, the Customer pays for it — unless it is a retest following failed remediation, which the Supplier funds."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Two cost rules appear in the passage, and the statement stitches them together accurately. First: the Customer "may, no more than once per contract year, require an additional test *at the Customer's expense*" — Customer-requested extra tests are Customer-funded. Second: where material deficiencies are found, the Supplier shall "remediate within ninety days and retest *at its own expense*" — remediation retests are Supplier-funded. The statement's two halves each track a clause, and its "unless" correctly assigns the exception. True. The question models a senior reading skill: integrating provisions that sit sentences apart into a single accurate rule — which is how contracts are actually used, since real questions ("who pays for this test?") rarely map to single sentences. It also illustrates why paraphrase discipline matters in your own drafting: the statement survives scrutiny because each component is traceable to text. When your board papers summarise contracts, hold them to the same standard — every summarised entitlement traceable to its clause.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Inference**

Which of the following can properly be inferred from the passage?

- A) The Supplier has never failed a disaster recovery test
- B) A Customer who is not observing tests and not checking Annex B currency could hold a contractual right to eight-hour recovery that fails in practice without any contractual remedy being triggered
- C) The ten per cent withholding is the Customer's sole remedy for continuity failures
- D) Annual testing is industry best practice

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Test each option. A asserts historical fact the passage cannot supply. C converts silence into exclusivity — as Question 3 established, this extract does not exhaust remedies, and unlike the earlier SLA example, no "sole remedy" language appears here. D imports external knowledge. B, though long, is built entirely from the passage's own machinery: the recovery obligation is real, but its verification depends on tests the Customer merely *may* observe, and its invocation depends on an Annex B list the passage gives no mechanism for keeping current; a Customer exercising neither right could find the paper obligation intact while the practical capability — untested by them, uninvocable by their stale list — fails when needed, with the withholding remedy triggering only via the test-deficiency route they were not observing. Every step uses a stated provision or a stated absence. This is senior inference: not adding facts, but *composing* the text's provisions to expose the risk the drafting leaves open — which is, in practice, exactly how assurance gaps are found in real contract portfolios.

**Question 6 (easy) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "The audit found that four services' continuity plans do not reflect current supplier arrangements."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The report says something importantly different: "we were *unable to verify* that plans for four services reflect the current supplier arrangements, as the relevant contract documentation was not provided within the audit period." Inability to verify is not a finding of deficiency — the plans may be perfectly current; the evidence was simply unavailable in time. The statement converts an evidential gap into an adverse finding, which the text contradicts as a characterisation of what the audit *found* — False. This distinction is among the most consequential in assurance literacy: "unable to verify" indicts the evidence trail (itself a management issue worth fixing — why wasn't the documentation available?), not necessarily the plans. Senior managers who brief upward that "audit found four plans out of date" have manufactured a finding; those who brief "audit could not verify four plans — and here is why the documentation was late" have read the report. Boards act on your characterisation, so your characterisation must match the text's precision.

**Question 7 (moderate) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "At least two of the tested services failed to meet their recovery objectives."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The arithmetic is embedded in the prose: "Testing evidence was available for seven services; of these, five tests met their recovery objectives." Seven tested, five met — therefore two did not meet their recovery objectives. "At least two... failed to meet" is thus directly supported — True. Care with the wording: the passage does not say the other two "failed" in a dramatic sense (a test can partially meet objectives), but the statement's precise formulation — "failed *to meet* their recovery objectives" — is exactly what seven-minus-five establishes. Notice also what cannot be inferred: nothing about the five untested-or-unevidenced services (12 critical services, testing evidence for 7), which is its own assurance gap. Verbal reasoning at senior level frequently hides small arithmetic inside prose, and the skill is extracting it without importing colour ("failed catastrophically") or losing precision ("some tests had issues"). Report numbers the way this statement does: in the text's own units.

**Question 8 (hard) — Best summary for an audit committee briefing**

You must summarise this report in two sentences for an audit committee pre-brief. Which is best?

- A) "Audit rated continuity assurance Limited: plans exist and are reviewed for all twelve critical services, but verification gaps (four services' supplier currency unverified due to late documentation), incomplete testing coverage (evidence for seven of twelve, with five meeting recovery objectives), and pending recommendations underpin the rating. Cyber recovery was out of scope and is reviewed next quarter — so this rating should not be read as assurance on cyber resilience."
- B) "Audit found our continuity planning fundamentally sound, with all plans in place and reviewed annually. A few documentation and testing details are being tidied up under accepted recommendations."
- C) "Audit rated us Limited, which is a serious criticism of the continuity function. Four plans are out of date, five of twelve tests failed, and cyber recovery was not even examined."
- D) "The audit is broadly positive but the Limited rating reflects auditor conservatism. Management has accepted the recommendations to close the matter."

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** An audit summary must transmit the rating, the evidence structure beneath it, and the scope boundary — without softening or hardening either. Option A does all three: it reports the strengths the report actually found (plans exist, reviewed), attributes the Limited rating to its three real drivers with the numbers stated in the report's own terms ("unverified", not "out of date"; "five of seven met", not "five of twelve failed"), and — the senior touch — flags the scope exclusion's *implication*: a committee hearing "Limited" on continuity could easily believe cyber recovery was covered; the summary pre-empts the misreading. Option B softens systematically: "fundamentally sound" appears nowhere in a Limited-rated report, and "tidied up" miniaturises accepted recommendations with nine-month horizons. Option C hardens systematically: it commits Question 6's error ("out of date"), miscounts the testing base ("five of twelve tests failed" double-distorts — five *met*, of *seven* evidenced), and converts a scheduled scope boundary into negligence ("not even examined"). Option D editorialises the rating away. The pattern to carry into your own practice: summaries earn trust by being *checkable against the source* line by line — and audit committees, unlike most audiences, will check.

**Explanation:** The commitment is "to *no more than two* by the end of 2028" — a ceiling, not a point target. **Question 10 (moderate) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "If the spending review settlement is lower than planned, the 2028 consolidation deadline may move, but the commitment to consolidate remains."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The funding clause states: "should the settlement be lower than planned, the timeline, though not the ambition, may be revised." Map the statement onto it: "the 2028 consolidation deadline may move" corresponds to "the timeline... may be revised"; "the commitment to consolidate remains" corresponds to "though not the ambition" — the ambition (consolidated, consistent support) explicitly survives. The verbal skill is recognising conditional structures precisely; the strategic skill is writing them so that, under pressure, your organisation bends where you designed it to bend.

**Question 11 (hard) — Inference**

Which conclusion can properly be drawn from the passage?

- A) A service whose peak demand runs continuously year-round could not have its migration scheduled compliantly, exposing a tension the strategy does not resolve
- B) The consolidation will reduce support costs
- C) Services retired during the strategy period will lose support before decommissioning
- D) The spending review settlement will be lower than planned

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Option B asserts an outcome (cost reduction) the passage never claims — consistency of experience, not cost, is the stated aim. Option C contradicts the final sentence, which commits to maintaining support "until each service's decommissioning date". Option D converts a contingency ("should the settlement be lower") into a prediction. Option A is the disciplined inference: the strategy commits that "migrations will be scheduled to avoid each service's peak demand periods" — a universal commitment whose satisfiability depends on each service *having* non-peak periods. For a service with continuous year-round peak demand (the passage does not say none exists, and the inference is conditional: "a service whose peak demand runs continuously... *could not*"), no compliant schedule exists, so the commitment and the 2028 ceiling would collide for that service. This is the strategist's reading skill: stress-testing commitments by their edge cases *before* signing, because unresolved tensions in strategy text become escalations addressed to you in year two — typically in the week the affected service's owner reads the commitment aloud in a governance meeting.

**Question 12 (hard) — Clear-writing judgement**

The consolidation programme has slipped: the second migration wave will now overlap with one service's statutory peak, contrary to the strategy's commitment. You must notify the service's director. Which opening paragraph is best?

- A) "Due to programme replanning necessitated by upstream dependency variances, wave two migration activities have been rescheduled into Q3, which we appreciate may present certain operational considerations for your area during the statutory period."
- B) "I need to tell you about a problem, propose two options, and ask for a decision by Friday. The rescheduled second migration wave now overlaps your statutory peak (August), which breaks the strategy's commitment to avoid peak periods. Option one: defer your service's migration to October, keeping you on the legacy platform eight weeks longer at a known stability risk we can quantify. Option two: proceed in August with enhanced support cover and a tested rollback plan. My recommendation is option one; either way, the choice should be yours and I will resource it fully."
- C) "Unfortunately the migration now clashes with your peak period. These things happen on complex programmes and we will do our best to minimise disruption. Let us know if you have concerns."
- D) "As you may be aware, programme timelines have evolved. We remain fully committed to the strategy's principles and to working collaboratively with your team throughout the transition period."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Judge against what a director receiving bad news needs: the fact, its significance, the options with their risks, a recommendation, and clarity about who decides — early, plainly, and with the commitment breach acknowledged rather than euphemised. Option B delivers the complete structure: it signposts the message in the first sentence (busy senior readers triage by openings), names the breach against the strategy explicitly (which paradoxically *builds* trust — you clearly are not managing the news), gives two options with honest risk characterisation, recommends while ceding the decision to the accountable owner, and attaches resource commitment. Option A is the classic evasion register: passive constructions ("necessitated by... variances"), euphemism ("certain operational considerations"), and no acknowledgement that a commitment is being broken — the director learns the real meaning later and remembers who wrote the fog. Option C acknowledges the clash but normalises it ("these things happen") and transfers the burden ("let us know if you have concerns") without options or a decision path. Option D says nothing at all with maximal warmth — the purest failure, since it forces the director to discover the peak clash independently. The senior writing principle: bad news travels best in the same structure as good analysis — fact, impact, options, recommendation, decision owner — and the strategy commitment you are breaking should appear in your own words before it appears in someone else's escalation.

### Preparation tips

- **Read one real contract schedule and one audit report analytically each week.** Underline every defined term, every "only/unless/except/subject to", and every assurance hedge ("unable to verify", "within the audit period"). This is simultaneously test preparation and the maintenance of your professional reading edge.
- **Practise the both-directions test for Cannot say.** For each statement ask two questions: does the text establish it? does the text preclude it? Only a no to both yields Cannot say — and asking both prevents the False-by-assumption error on silent remedies and absent rights.
- **Track claims to clauses.** In practice and at work, refuse yourself any contractual or policy assertion you cannot pin to quoted text. The habit converts directly into negotiation strength and assessment accuracy.
- **Stress-test universal commitments.** When a passage says "all", "every", or "will be scheduled to avoid", hunt the edge case that breaks it. Inference questions — and strategy reviews — hide their best marks there.
- **Summarise against the source.** After reading any passage, draft a two-sentence summary, then audit it line by line: is every number in the text's own units, every characterisation at the text's own strength? Softening and hardening are both errors — and both are habits, detectable and trainable.
- **Rewrite one evasive paragraph plainly each week.** Take a fog-register email (yours or another's) and redraft it as fact-impact-options-recommendation-decision. Clear-writing judgement questions reward the structure you have practised producing.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Converting evidential gaps into findings.** "Unable to verify" is not "found deficient"; "no evidence of X" is not "evidence of no X". Assurance literacy fails most often at exactly this seam.
- **Assuming a quoted extract exhausts the document.** Remedies, rights, and definitions live elsewhere in real contracts; extracts under-determine them. Silence in the passage is Cannot say, not False — and not True.
- **Reading point targets into ceilings and floors.** "No more than two" permits one; "at least annually" permits monthly. The logical form of a commitment is the commitment.
- **Letting expertise overwrite the text.** The more senior you are, the stronger your expectations about what the clause "must" say. The passage governs; your experience is context, not evidence.
- **Summarising at the wrong strength.** Softening ("tidying up details") and hardening ("out of date", "failed") are symmetric failures. Match the source's calibration exactly, then add implications explicitly labelled as yours.
- **Missing the scope boundary.** What a report did not examine bounds what its rating means. Briefings that omit exclusions manufacture assurance no one gave.
- **Choosing warmth over structure in writing judgement.** Collaborative tone with no fact, options, or decision path fails the reader. The credited draft is the one a busy director can act on from the first paragraph.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment (SJT) presents realistic workplace dilemmas and asks you to evaluate possible responses — most effective, least effective, full rankings, or independent effectiveness ratings. At senior level the scenarios change register: fewer questions about handling a single ticket or user, more about handling competing accountabilities — a supplier relationship against a compliance duty, a director's preference against user evidence, a team's morale against a performance problem, this quarter's credibility against next year's capability. The response options are correspondingly harder to separate: outright bad choices thin out, and the discrimination is between the good and the best — or, in least-effective formats, between the flawed and the fatal.

The typical format is an online assessment of 15 to 30 scenarios, generously timed, scored against the judgements of experienced senior practitioners and the organisation's professional behaviours: user focus, ownership, honest communication, collaboration, appropriate escalation, inclusion, and — increasingly at this level — the behaviours of leadership: developing others, managing upward with candour, protecting standards under pressure, and taking accountability for outcomes you influenced but did not personally execute.

Why do employers weight SJTs heavily at senior level? Because your role summary is a catalogue of judgement-intensive expectations that no technical credential evidences: negotiate and influence, deal with challenging situations and remove blockers, take accountability for issues and proactively search for potential problems, champion user research, represent users internally, achieve excellent user outcomes. Each of those phrases names a pattern of choices, and senior selection errors are expensive precisely because senior choices compound — a manager who quietly softens findings, or escalates instead of influencing, or protects metrics instead of users, repeats that pattern across every service they touch. The SJT samples the pattern before the organisation pays for it.

Approach guidance, honestly given: answer as your best professional self operating by civil service values, not as a tactician modelling the scoring key. At senior level especially, resist two systematic temptations the format exposes — the urge to escalate difficulties upward that your role exists to resolve, and the urge to handle everything personally that your team should own with your support. The credited answers consistently occupy the ground between: act within your authority, be transparent beyond it, and develop the people around you while doing both.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Influence and negotiation choices** map directly to your **Stakeholder relationship management (IT operations)** skill — negotiating with and influencing stakeholders, dealing with challenging situations and removing blockers, and building long-term strategic relationships. Scenarios repeatedly test whether you reach for evidence and relationship before hierarchy and contract — and whether you can hold a position under senior pressure without either capitulating or combusting.
- **Accountability choices** map to your **Ownership and topic** skill — taking accountability for issues that occur and being proactive in searching for potential problems. Senior scenarios probe both directions: owning outcomes you did not personally cause, and resisting the urge to absorb work that belongs to your team's development.
- **User-championing choices** map to your **User focus** skill — collaborating with user researchers, representing users internally, distinguishing user needs from user desires, and championing research for all users. Expect scenarios where the user evidence is inconvenient to power, budget, or schedule.
- **Strategy and standards choices** map to your **Strategic thinking** skill — defining strategies and policies, providing guidance to others on working in the strategic context, and evaluating current strategies — including the leadership question of what to do when delivery pressure and strategic commitments collide.
- **Data-integrity choices** map to your **IT service reporting** skill — using data analytics to make decisions — tested at the moment analytics and advocacy conflict: what you do when the numbers undermine your own case, or someone else's.
- **Compliance and risk choices** map to your **Asset and configuration management** skill — managing service compliance and risk — in scenarios where enforcement has a political price and flexibility has a hidden one.
- **Solution-production choices** map to your **Business analysis (IT operations)** skill — leading investigative work and absorbing conflicting information to produce solutions — tested through scenarios where acting on partial information and waiting for complete information are both costly.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — The director who wants the finding softened**

Your quarterly service report includes a finding that the flagship digital service's completion rate for assisted-digital users is 61%, against 89% for unassisted users — a gap your analysis attributes partly to a support-channel change the department championed. The service's director asks you to "reframe the assisted-digital figure as an improvement journey" and drop the channel-change attribution "since causation isn't proven". What is the most effective response?

- A) Comply — causation genuinely isn't proven, and the director owns the narrative
- B) Refuse and escalate to the director's superior, citing reporting integrity
- C) Keep the figure and the gap prominent; present the attribution with calibrated language ("the timing and pattern are consistent with the channel change; causation is not established") and offer the director a joint next step — a targeted piece of research to settle attribution — so the report stays accurate while the open question is honestly framed
- D) Publish your original wording unchanged without responding to the director

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Separate the director's two requests, because they differ in legitimacy. Dropping unproven causation *as a claim* is partially legitimate — your original attribution said "partly", and if the evidence is correlational, calibrated language is more defensible, not less. Softening the 28-point completion gap for the most support-dependent users is not legitimate — that figure is the report's duty, and your **User focus** skill at this level exists precisely for users whose experience is easiest to reframe away. Option C gives the director everything accuracy permits: the causal claim recalibrated to exactly its evidential strength, and a constructive path (research to settle attribution) that converts a narrative dispute into an evidence plan. It also keeps the relationship intact for the many future quarters you will need it. Option A trades the users' visibility for comfort. Option B escalates a resolvable disagreement — hierarchy is for after influence fails, and this director has not yet declined an honest compromise. Option D wins the battle silently and loses the relationship pointlessly: unresponsiveness to a senior stakeholder is its own message, and a worse one than disagreement. Scored behaviours: reporting integrity, calibrated claims, user championing, influence before escalation.

**Question 2 (easy) — The blocked migration**

A critical service migration is blocked: the security team has not completed its assessment, now three weeks late, and the migration window closes in ten days — missing it costs six months (the next window) and £200k in extended legacy support. The security team lead tells you they are under-resourced and your assessment is behind two others in their queue. What is the most effective action?

- A) Instruct your programme to proceed — the security team had ample notice, and the delay is their failure
- B) Accept the six-month delay and document that security caused it
- C) Work the blocker directly: establish with the security lead what a sufficient assessment requires, whether scope can be split (assess the migration-critical elements first), whether your programme can supply evidence-gathering effort to accelerate their work, and — if genuine resource conflict remains — take the prioritisation trade-off to the level that owns all three queued assessments, with the £200k and six-month consequence quantified
- D) Email the security team's director copying your own, noting the cost of delay and requesting urgent reprioritisation

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** "Removing blockers" — named in your stakeholder skill — is a discipline with an ordering: understand the blocker, shrink it, resource it, and only then escalate it, carrying numbers. Option C runs the full sequence. Understanding may dissolve the problem (perhaps 80% of the assessment is templatable from a prior migration); splitting scope often converts a three-week blocker into a three-day one; lending effort respects that their constraint is real rather than obstructive; and the escalation endgame, if needed, is framed as a *prioritisation decision with quantified stakes* for whoever owns the competing demands — which is escalation as decision-support, not complaint. Option A is the catastrophic distractor: proceeding without security assessment converts a schedule risk into an unbounded organisational one, and no £200k saving prices a citizen-data incident. Option B treats documentation of blame as though it were management — six months and £200k are not mitigated by a well-attributed loss. Option D escalates first, skipping every step that might have solved the problem faster and cheaper than directors can, and spends relationship capital at the maximum rate. Scored behaviours: blocker removal as craft, collaboration with constrained teams, security respect, escalation as last resort with evidence.

**Question 3 (easy) — The high performer who hoards**

Your strongest service manager personally handles every major incident, every senior stakeholder call, and every complex supplier negotiation on their patch. Their results are excellent; their two deputies are visibly stalling in development, and the manager's own workload is unsustainable — they have not taken leave in eight months. What is the most effective intervention?

- A) Leave it alone — results are excellent, and intervention risks breaking what works
- B) Redistribute their portfolio by instruction, transferring the stakeholder calls and negotiations to the deputies immediately
- C) Address it as a development conversation with structural follow-through: name the pattern and its three costs (deputies' growth, single-point-of-failure risk, their own sustainability), agree which responsibilities the deputies will lead with the manager coaching rather than doing, and put the manager's own next stretch — and their leave — into the plan
- D) Ask the deputies to be more assertive in claiming work

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Read the situation as a risk register, not a performance review: excellent current results are being purchased with three accruing liabilities — undeveloped deputies (capability debt), total dependence on one person (continuity risk — your proactive-problem-search instinct should snag hard here), and an exhaustion trajectory that ends the excellent results the hard way. Option C addresses the pattern at the right level: with the manager, honestly, framing delegation not as demotion but as their own next capability (senior progression *is* the shift from doing to developing), with structural follow-through so the conversation changes the work allocation and not just the mood — and with their recovery explicitly in scope, because eight leave-free months is itself a management failure you now own. Option A is the seductive one: it books today's results and inherits tomorrow's collapse, discovering the succession gap during the major incident that finds the manager on their first holiday. Option B achieves the redistribution while teaching the manager that excellence attracts punishment — and hands stakeholders to deputies without the coached transition that protects relationships. Option D asks the least powerful actors to solve a dynamic their manager controls. Scored behaviours: developing others, proactive risk management, sustainable performance, and leadership conversations that pair candour with structure.

**Question 4 (moderate) — The user research that threatens the deadline**

Two weeks before a committed go-live — announced by your director to the board — user research on the new unified support portal finds that users over 65, 30% of the service's users, fail task completion at twice the rate of other groups, primarily due to an authentication step. Fixing it properly is estimated at six weeks. The programme manager proposes going live on schedule and "iterating post-launch"; the user researcher argues launch would knowingly ship a service that excludes the group most dependent on it. What is the most effective position for you to take?

- A) Support the on-schedule launch — the board commitment is fixed, and post-launch iteration is legitimate agile practice
- B) Insist launch is delayed six weeks, and inform the director the research makes this unavoidable
- C) Interrogate the options space before positioning: can the existing telephone channel carry affected users at launch with measured capacity, can the authentication step be modified short of the full fix, what does "iterate post-launch" mean concretely — dates, funding, accountability? Then take the strongest viable protection for affected users to the director with the research evidence, the options, and your recommendation — before the go/no-go becomes binary
- D) Convene the programme board and let it vote

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The scenario stages your role summary against itself: champion user research and represent users internally versus delivery commitments made at board level — and the credited move is to *refuse the binary while it can still be refused*. Option C does the senior work: it tests whether the dilemma is real (a capacity-checked telephone route for affected users might make launch genuinely acceptable; a lighter authentication modification might shrink six weeks to two; and "iterate post-launch" without dates, money, and an owner is a hope, not a plan — naming that is your analytical duty), and then takes *options with a recommendation* upward, which is how directors prefer to receive problems that touch their board commitments. Option A adopts the programme's framing uninterrogated — "iterating post-launch" on a group excluded at twice the failure rate means the most dependent 30% carry the cost of the schedule, and your user-focus skill names exactly this distinction: the *organisation's* desire for the date is not a user need. Option B may be where you end up, but reaching it without exhausting the options space converts you from problem-solver to blocker in the director's eyes, spending influence that option C conserves. Option D outsources a judgement that is yours to form — governance ratifies recommendations; it does not relieve you of making one. Scored behaviours: user championing with evidence, option generation before escalation, honest interrogation of "iterate later", managing upward with recommendations rather than problems.

**Question 5 (moderate) — The compliance drift a friend is causing**

A fellow senior manager — a longtime ally who has supported you in difficult moments — runs a portfolio in which you notice, while reviewing shared platform data, that fourteen services have quietly fallen out of the mandatory patching compliance window, with exemption records that reference a "temporary deferral" agreed eight months ago and never revisited. Nothing has gone wrong yet. What is the most effective action?

- A) Say nothing — it is their portfolio, and surfacing it would damage a relationship your own work depends on
- B) Raise it with them directly and promptly: share the data, ask what you are missing, and give them the opportunity to fix it themselves — while being clear that if the exposure stands, it needs to go on the risk register through the proper route regardless of who raises it
- C) Report it to the compliance function immediately without telling them, since friendship must not influence compliance
- D) Mention it casually over coffee and leave the follow-up to them

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The scenario tests whether relationships bend standards — and, equally, whether standards require relationship destruction, because both errors are available. Option B threads it deliberately: the colleague hears it first, from you, with data — which is both the respect the relationship has earned and the fastest route to remediation, since they can fix their own portfolio quicker than any compliance process; the "what am I missing" framing genuinely allows for a legitimate explanation (perhaps a real deferral exists with poor paperwork); and the boundary is stated plainly — the exposure goes on the register through proper routes either way, so the friendship changes the *sequence*, never the *outcome*. That is what integrity inside relationships looks like at senior level, and it is also what preserves alliances: allies who discover you knew and stayed silent — or reported without a word — rarely remain allies. Option A prices the relationship above fourteen services' unpatched exposure and makes you co-owner of the risk from the moment you saw the data; your compliance-and-risk skill does not recognise portfolio boundaries as sight lines. Option C is defensible in a way A is not, but it spends the trust unnecessarily: nothing about proper reporting requires ambush. Option D performs the raising without the substance — no data, no boundary, no follow-through, deniability all round. Scored behaviours: integrity with relationship intelligence, compliance ownership beyond formal remit, courage in the order that maximises both honesty and repair speed.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Least effective: the failing improvement programme**

The continual improvement programme you sponsor is six months in: two of five workstreams are delivering well, two are behind, and one — process automation — has consumed 40% of the budget for no deployed output, its lead insisting a breakthrough is close. The programme board meets next week. Which response is the LEAST effective?

- A) Commission a rapid, evidence-based checkpoint on the automation workstream before the board: what has been built, what remains, what the honest completion estimate is — and present the findings whichever way they fall
- B) Present the programme honestly at the board: two green, two amber with recovery plans, one red under review, with the budget position stated
- C) Rebalance in advance: pause the automation workstream pending the checkpoint, and redirect its uncommitted budget to the delivering workstreams
- D) Give the automation lead one more quarter discreetly, holding it at amber for the board, since a red rating now would undermine confidence in the whole programme — and the breakthrough may land

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Hunt the option whose defining features are concealment and unmanaged sunk-cost exposure. Option D contains both: it re-rates a red workstream amber *for presentational reasons* — corrupting the governance signal the board exists to receive — and its stated justification ("the breakthrough may land") is the sunk-cost fallacy wearing optimism: 40% of budget, zero deployment, and the lead's confidence is the same evidence it was last quarter. Worse, D's protection is self-defeating: boards forgive red workstreams with owned recovery plans routinely; they do not forgive discovering that amber meant red, after which *every* rating you present is discounted — the exact "confidence in the whole programme" D claims to protect. Options A, B and C are all effective and mutually compatible — in fact they sequence: checkpoint (A) informs rebalancing (C) informs honest presentation (B). Note that C without A would be hasty (pausing on suspicion rather than evidence), which is why A exists; and B without either would be honest but passive. The senior pattern for least-effective questions: the fatal option is rarely the laziest one — it is the one that manages *perception* where the others manage *reality*. Scored behaviours (by their absence in D): governance integrity, evidence over hope, decisive rebalancing, and the understanding that credibility is a programme asset more valuable than any single workstream.

**Question 7 (moderate) — The channel the strategy forgot**

Your organisation's digital strategy commits to "digital by default", and the transformation programme proposes closing the counter service at three regional offices, citing 4% and falling usage. Reviewing the evidence, you find the 4% includes a high concentration of users with complex cases, limited digital access, or accessibility needs — and that counter-service users' case-resolution rate is the highest of any channel. The programme's benefits case books £1.8m from the closures, already counted in the directorate's savings target. What is the most effective position?

- A) Support the closures — 4% usage cannot justify three offices, and the strategy is explicit
- B) Oppose the closures outright as incompatible with inclusive service provision
- C) Reframe the decision with the full evidence: quantify who the 4% are and what happens to their outcomes under closure (including the cost of failure demand returning through other channels), test intermediate options — consolidated counters, appointment-based service, assisted-digital provision in partner locations — and require the benefits case to net off the costs of serving displaced users properly, so the decision is made on true numbers whichever way it goes
- D) Escalate to the strategy's owner and request an exemption for the three offices

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Your role summary contains this scenario almost verbatim: demonstrate experience in meeting the needs of users *across a variety of channels*, bring insight into how user needs have changed over time, and apply strategic thinking to provide the best service for the end user — while your strategic-thinking skill includes *evaluating* current strategies, not just executing them. Option C is that evaluation in action. Its three moves are each load-bearing: quantifying who the 4% are converts "small percentage" into "specific people with the least channel choice and the most complex cases" — the framing usage statistics are designed (not maliciously, just structurally) to erase; costing failure demand attacks the benefits case on its own terms, because displaced complex cases do not vanish, they resurface in phone queues and escalations at higher unit cost, and a £1.8m saving that ignores this is not a saving but a transfer with leakage; and testing intermediate options refuses the false binary between three full offices and nothing. Note what C does not do: it does not oppose closure (option B), which positions you as sentiment against strategy and loses; it does not comply (option A), which mistakes a strategy's *default* for its *destination* — "digital by default" governs the general case precisely so that judgement can govern the exceptions; and it does not lead with escalation (option D) before the decision-shaping analysis that might make escalation unnecessary. Scored behaviours: user advocacy through evidence rather than assertion, strategic thinking as evaluation, benefits-case integrity, and option generation as influence.

**Question 8 (moderate) — The supplier's principal engineer**

Mid-contract, your platform supplier's principal engineer — the individual whose knowledge underpins the two most fragile services in your estate — contacts you privately: they are leaving the supplier in eight weeks, they believe the supplier will not backfill the role adequately, and they wanted you to hear it from them. The supplier has not informed you. What is the most effective response?

- A) Thank them, keep the information to yourself until the supplier discloses it, and quietly begin contingency planning
- B) Immediately confront the supplier's account director with the information and demand a retention counter-offer
- C) Thank them and act on the substance without weaponising the source: accelerate the knowledge-capture work you should already be running (documentation, shadowing, runbooks) as standard continuity practice, and raise key-person dependency generically at the next service review — pressing for the supplier's succession plan for critical roles — while treating the specific disclosure with the discretion it was offered in
- D) Ask the engineer to delay their departure in exchange for a future role on your side

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Untangle what you actually hold: advance, informally-given notice of a genuine continuity risk — your proactive-problem-search duty activated by information you cannot use in its raw form without burning the person who trusted you with it. Option C acts fully on the substance while protecting the source: knowledge-capture is defensible *right now* as standard practice for fragile, key-person-dependent services (it was arguably overdue — the fragility predates the resignation), and "key-person dependency" is a legitimate standing agenda item at any service review, which lets you extract the supplier's succession plan without revealing you know about this succession. When the supplier discloses formally, you will be six weeks ahead. Option A gets the discretion right and the urgency wrong: eight weeks of head start on the estate's most fragile knowledge is not a quiet-planning window, it is the whole game. Option B spends the source immediately — the account director will trace the leak in one guess — and demands the wrong remedy: retention counter-offers for someone already decided are expensive delays, not solutions, and the supplier's staffing is contractually theirs to manage; your lever is outcomes (continuity obligations), not their HR. Option D is an integrity trap: soliciting a supplier's employee crosses conflict-of-interest lines that survive in procurement memories for years. Scored behaviours: acting on early warning through legitimate channels, source protection, converting private information into public-interest outcomes, and knowing which levers are yours to pull.

**Question 9 (hard) — The metric that argues against your own case**

You have spent four months building the case for a £900k investment in platform resilience, drawing on last year's outage record: 14 significant incidents, 41 hours of cumulative downtime. The final data refresh before the investment board arrives, and the current year to date is dramatically better: 3 incidents, 6 hours — driven, your analysis suggests, by workarounds and luck (two near-misses were caught by a single engineer's manual intervention) rather than structural improvement. Presenting last year's data would be more persuasive; presenting this year's honestly weakens your case. What is the most effective approach?

- A) Present last year's data as the basis of the case — it represents the structural reality; this year is a small sample distorted by luck
- B) Present both years in full, lead with the improvement, and argue the case on its true basis: the near-miss analysis showing that current performance depends on manual intervention and fortune rather than structure — converting the inconvenient data into the strongest form of the argument, and accepting the outcome if the board is not persuaded
- C) Delay the board submission until the data supports the case again
- D) Present this year's data but adjust it to include the two near-misses as incidents, since they were incidents in all but name

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This is the integrity scenario in its purest senior form: the truth weakens your case, you control the presentation, and every audience-pleasing option is available. Option B holds the line and — the deeper point — discovers that the honest case is *stronger*, not weaker: "our current stability rests on one engineer's manual catches" is precisely the risk profile a resilience investment addresses, and near-miss analysis is more persuasive to sophisticated boards than raw incident counts, because it demonstrates you understand *why* the numbers are what they are. Boards fund managers whose numbers they trust; a case built on last year's data (option A) collapses the moment any board member asks "and this year?" — after which you lose the investment *and* the credibility that funds the next one. Option C games the timeline — waiting for services to fail so the chart supports you inverts your entire duty of proactive problem prevention, and is D's dishonesty played out in calendar form. Option D fabricates: near-misses are evidence, powerful precisely *as near-misses*; reclassifying them as incidents converts your best argument into a falsifiable lie discoverable by anyone who reads the incident register. The scored behaviours — reporting integrity under self-interested pressure, analytical depth (near-miss reasoning), and the courage to let a true case stand or fall on its truth — are your **IT service reporting** skill ("data analytics to make decisions") at its ethical foundation: the analytics serve the decision, not the advocate. And note the professional meta-lesson: managers known for arguing *against* their own interest when the data demands it acquire an influence that no presentation technique reproduces.

**Question 10 (hard) — The restructure that lands on your team**

A directorate restructure will merge your service management function with another team, and the leaked-then-confirmed design shows your function losing four posts, with the affected individuals not yet identified. Your team's rumour mill is running; two of your best people confide they are already interviewing elsewhere; delivery is visibly slowing; and formal consultation does not begin for three weeks, before which you may not discuss individual outcomes. What is the most effective leadership approach?

- A) Say nothing until formal consultation — the rules constrain you, and anything you say could create legal risk
- B) Privately reassure your best people that they are safe, to stop the exodus of exactly the capability the merged function will need
- C) Communicate maximally within the real constraint: tell the team what is confirmed (the merger, the reduction, the consultation date), what is genuinely undecided (individual outcomes), what process and support will apply, and hold to a rhythm of updates even when the update is "nothing new" — while separately pressing the restructure's owners to compress the pre-consultation limbo, and neither confirming nor denying anyone's individual position
- D) Tell the team you disagree with the restructure and are fighting to reverse the post reductions

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The scenario tests leadership under a real constraint — you genuinely may not discuss individual outcomes — and the credited insight is that the constraint covers far less than silence pretends. Option C maps the boundary precisely: the merger, the numbers, the timeline, and the process are confirmed facts whose concealment protects no one (the team already has them via leak, in distorted form — rumour abhors a vacuum and fills it with worse), while individual outcomes are the actual restricted zone. The update *rhythm* matters as much as content: "nothing new, next update Friday" is information — it tells people the channel is alive and they need not mine the rumour mill. Pressing to compress the limbo attacks the harm at its source, since pre-consultation dead time is where organisations bleed exactly the people option B panics about. And B's panic is the sophisticated trap: private safety assurances to favourites are promises you may not be able to keep (outcomes are undecided — that was the true part of the constraint), are corrosive when discovered by the unreassured (and they are always discovered), and convert a fair process into a pre-judged one, creating the legal risk A feared by a different route. Option A is the compliance-shaped abdication: legally cautious, humanly absent, and practically self-defeating as delivery and retention degrade in the silence. Option D buys a moment of solidarity by spending your credibility on a fight the design has already settled — and when the posts go anyway, you have taught the team that your fighting words carry no force. Scored behaviours: honest communication inside real constraints, equal treatment under uncertainty, working the system upward (compressing limbo) while stabilising downward, and the leadership discipline of being present with incomplete answers rather than absent with perfect ones.

**Question 11 (hard) — Ranking: the Monday morning portfolio**

At 08:30 Monday, four items compete: (1) overnight, a supplier's engineer applied an emergency fix to the payments platform outside change control — it worked, service is stable, and the supplier's account manager has already emailed asking to "regularise the paperwork quietly"; (2) the casework service's authentication latency trend you have been watching has crossed its warning threshold, with the pattern that preceded last year's three-day degradation; (3) a director you have been cultivating for a strategic partnership has offered a rare 30-minute slot at 11:00 today to discuss it; (4) your monthly compliance attestation — personally certifying your portfolio's patching and configuration status — is due to the risk committee by 17:00, and two platform reports needed for it have not arrived. Rank the order in which you address them:

- A) 2, 1, 4, 3
- B) 1, 2, 3, 4
- C) 2, 4, 1, 3
- D) 3, 2, 1, 4

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Rank by irreversibility, window, and what only you can do. Item 2 leads: a recognised precursor pattern crossing threshold is a closing prevention window — engaging the resolver capability *now*, while degradation is incipient, is the difference between a tuning intervention and last year's three-day event; an hour's delay is potentially the whole prevention margin, and your proactive-ownership skill exists for precisely this moment. Item 1 is second, and its urgency is subtler than it looks: the technical risk is stable (the fix worked), but an uncontrolled change to a *payments* platform sits unrecorded — configuration integrity — and the account manager's "quietly" is a live integrity probe: the longer it stands unanswered, the more your silence resembles assent. The response — retrospective emergency-change record through the proper route, review of the fix, and a clear signal that "quietly" is not how this portfolio works — takes twenty minutes and sets a precedent either way. Item 4 is third: it has a hard 17:00 deadline, but the *action* is chasing two reports (delegable, morning) and refusing to certify what you cannot see — if the reports fail to arrive, the attestation goes in qualified, on time, with the gaps named; an accurate qualified attestation beats a complete guessed one, and arranging that takes a fraction of the day. Item 3 is last not because the director is unimportant but because it is the only item that is *reschedulable without loss* — a cultivated relationship survives "I have a developing situation on a citizen-facing service; may I have your next slot?", and indeed a strategic partner watching you prioritise a service risk over their meeting learns exactly what you want a partner to know about you. Option D inverts this insight; option B mistakes the loudest item for the most time-critical; option C undervalues the integrity clock running on item 1. Scored behaviours: prevention over reaction, integrity signalling at speed, honest attestation, and relationship management that trusts relationships to survive right priorities.

**Question 12 (hard) — The consensus against the evidence**

You sit on a cross-government design board deciding the support model for a shared platform serving eleven departments. Ten departments' representatives have aligned on a fully-centralised support model — it is cheapest and administratively cleanest, and the chair wants unanimous sign-off today after months of negotiation. Your department's user research, the only substantive research conducted by any participant, shows the centralised model's single generic front door performed poorly for the two departments (yours and one other) whose users have complex, regulated casework needs — the research recommends a thin specialist triage layer for those cases, adding modest cost. The other affected department's representative has not read the research and is ready to sign. What is the most effective action?

- A) Sign — ten of eleven departments aligned after months of negotiation is a consensus your single study should not disturb
- B) Refuse to sign and formally dissent, protecting your department's position on the record
- C) Before the vote: brief the other affected department's representative on the research findings as they concern *their* users, then table the evidence to the board with a specific, costed, minimal amendment — the specialist triage layer — framed as protecting the model's success for the hardest cases rather than reopening the model; if the board still declines, seek an explicit review trigger (post-launch performance data for complex-casework users) in the sign-off rather than a bare dissent
- D) Sign, but implement an unofficial specialist triage arrangement within your own department afterwards

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The scenario compresses your entire senior skill set into one room: championing research for all users, influencing without authority, building consensus that deserves the name, and knowing what to do when evidence and momentum collide. Option C's sequence is the craft. Briefing the other affected representative first is the highest-leverage move available — it converts your lone voice into two *affected* voices, transforms "one department's objection" into "the two departments this concerns, aligned on evidence", and is owed to them anyway: their users are in your findings and they are about to sign unread. Tabling the amendment as *specific, costed, and minimal* respects the months of negotiation — boards accept surgical amendments far more readily than reopened designs, and "a thin triage layer that protects the model where it is weakest" is framed as completing the consensus, not defeating it. The fallback — an explicit, data-triggered review clause — is the senior alternative to noble defeat: it converts your evidence into a tripwire the platform's own performance data will pull, so the question reopens itself with accumulating proof if you are right. Option A discards the only research in the room for social proof — ten unresearched agreements weigh less than one studied objection, and complex-casework users (the users least able to absorb a poor front door) pay the difference; your skill set says *represent users internally* precisely for rooms like this. Option B protects your record and abandons the outcome — dissent without the pre-work is how being right becomes irrelevant. Option D is the quiet defection: it fixes your users, abandons the other department's identical users, undermines the shared model you signed, and surfaces eventually as bad faith. Scored behaviours: evidence-based influence, coalition-building with the genuinely affected, amendment craft over veto reflex, review triggers as institutionalised persistence, and user championship that extends beyond your own boundary.

### Preparation tips

- **Reread the Civil Service behaviours and your organisation's values through a leadership lens.** At senior level the scored behaviours include developing others, candour upward, and standards under pressure — recognise them inside options, especially when they carry short-term cost.
- **Audit your own escalation reflex.** Before any assessment, review your last five difficult situations: how many did you escalate that influence could have resolved, and how many did you absorb that should have gone up with options attached? The SJT tests exactly this calibration, in both directions.
- **Practise the option-generation move.** The credited answers repeatedly refuse binary framings: before positioning on any dilemma, list three intermediate options. Rehearse until it is reflexive — it is the single most transferable senior SJT skill.
- **Study your own worst case honestly.** Recall a real situation you handled imperfectly — a softened finding, a delayed hard conversation, a premature escalation. Write what the best version would have been. That reflection is the highest-fidelity SJT preparation that exists.
- **For ranking questions, rank by irreversibility, closing windows, and integrity clocks.** What is lost forever if delayed an hour? What window is closing? What silence is being read as assent? Diary items reschedule; precursor patterns and integrity probes do not.
- **In least-effective formats, hunt perception management.** At senior level the fatal option rarely looks lazy — it looks prudent while managing appearances instead of realities: the amber that means red, the reassurance that pre-judges, the delayed submission awaiting better data.
- **Trust the values, not the guessed key.** Consistent, values-based answering outscores tactical second-guessing — and produces the leader the assessment is trying to find anyway.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Escalating before influencing.** Directors are for decisions that need their authority, delivered with options and numbers — not for difficulties your role exists to resolve. The credited path runs understand → shrink → resource → escalate-with-evidence.
- **Softening or delaying truth to protect standing.** The amber-that-means-red, the flattering baseline, the postponed submission — each buys a week and mortgages every future report. Boards forgive red; they never again trust discovered amber.
- **Reassuring selectively under uncertainty.** Private promises to favourites during restructures and reductions are promises you cannot keep, unfairness you cannot undo, and legal risk you created while avoiding it.
- **Confusing a strategy's default with its destination.** "Digital by default" and equivalent commitments govern the general case; senior judgement exists for the exceptions, argued with evidence and costed options — not exemption requests or quiet defection.
- **Weaponising information given in confidence.** Early warnings are for acting on through legitimate channels, not for confronting their subjects. Burn a source once and early warnings stop arriving.
- **Letting sunk costs argue.** "The breakthrough may be close" after 40% of budget and zero output is hope structured as analysis. Checkpoint on evidence, rebalance on findings.
- **Protecting results by hoarding work.** Your own or your best manager's — excellence that develops no one and survives no holiday is a liability register entry, not an asset.
- **Winning the record while losing the outcome.** Formal dissents, documented blame, and unanswered directors protect your file and abandon your users. The credited answers fight for the outcome first, and paper the record only when the fight is genuinely lost.
