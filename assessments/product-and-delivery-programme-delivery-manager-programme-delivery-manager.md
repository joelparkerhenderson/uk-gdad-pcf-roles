# Programme Delivery Manager - Psychometric Assessment Resources

## Introduction

Welcome! This guide has been written specifically for you as a programme delivery manager working within the UK Government Digital and Data profession. Whether you are preparing for an internal assessment centre, applying for a new post at a higher grade, benchmarking your own capability against the framework, or coaching your delivery managers through their own assessment preparation, this document will help you practise, reflect, and build confidence.

Psychometric assessments are structured, standardised exercises that measure the mental capabilities and behavioural tendencies that predict success in a role. For a programme delivery manager, that means far more than abstract puzzles. The assessments described here are job-specific: they use the kinds of artefacts you genuinely handle — programme plans, dependency maps, RAID logs, burn-up charts, budget forecasts, supplier contracts, governance papers, and escalation emails — and the kinds of decisions you genuinely make, such as sequencing delivery across multiple teams, resolving high-risk commercial issues, rebalancing people and resources as priorities shift, and moderating difficult conversations between technical specialists and senior non-technical stakeholders.

Why do these assessments matter for your role in particular? You are accountable for the delivery of complex products and services delivered by multiple teams, often carrying high technical or political risk. That accountability demands sustained, high-quality reasoning under pressure: holding a web of dependencies in mind while a governance board asks pointed questions; reading a budget variance table and knowing within seconds whether the programme is genuinely in trouble; extracting the one material fact from a dense assurance report; and choosing a defensible course of action when two directors want incompatible things by Friday. Assessments of cognitive ability, numerical reasoning, verbal reasoning, and situational judgement map directly onto those demands. Practising them sharpens exactly the muscles your role depends on — and, because you support and coach delivery managers, it also equips you to guide others through the same experience with empathy and technique.

This document is organised into four main assessment sections, each following the same pattern: an overview of what the assessment measures and why employers use it for programme-level delivery roles, a mapping of the assessment dimensions to the specific named skills in your capability framework, a substantial set of practice questions that progress from easy through moderate to hard with full worked explanations, practical preparation tips, and common pitfalls to avoid.

Here is how to get the most from it. First, read each "About this assessment" section so the format holds no surprises. Second, attempt every practice question honestly — commit to an answer in writing before you read the explanation, because the act of committing is where the learning happens. Third, treat every explanation as a mini-lesson: even when you answer correctly, the worked reasoning will deepen your technique, and several explanations deliberately model the kind of thinking you can pass on when coaching your delivery managers. Fourth, notice the numeric questions in particular: they show the arithmetic step by step, and at programme level the discipline of showing your working is also the discipline of making your reporting auditable. Finally, use the preparation tips and pitfalls sections for self-reflection — they connect assessment performance back to your professional development as a leader who oversees the delivery of a programme director's vision while keeping many teams unblocked and moving.

Take your time, be kind to yourself, and enjoy the practice. Let's begin.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive ability assessment measures the exact mental processes your role demands, using practical workplace scenarios rather than abstract shapes and sequences. Instead of asking you to rotate an unfamiliar figure, it presents you with the everyday materials of programme delivery — dependency maps, RAID logs, release plans, resource matrices, governance schedules — and asks you to reason quickly and accurately about them.

The typical format is an online, timed test lasting 15 to 30 minutes, containing somewhere between 15 and 30 questions covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective, comparing your responses against a norm group of people at a similar level, and many modern platforms adapt question difficulty based on your previous answers — so a test that feels progressively harder is usually a sign you are doing well, not badly. Employers typically receive a breakdown of speed versus accuracy rather than a single number, which means working both quickly and carefully matters. You will normally be offered short, ungraded practice questions before the real test begins, so you can settle into the interface without pressure.

For a programme delivery manager, cognitive assessment is particularly relevant because your core professional value is structured thinking at scale. You manage dependencies of varying complexity across multiple teams, potentially feeding into larger programmes and portfolios; you predict, prevent, mitigate and manage risks at different phases of the life cycle; and you are the initial escalation point when something goes wrong. Each of those duties is, cognitively, an exercise in holding many moving parts in mind at once, spotting the inconsistency or the critical path, and deducing what follows when one element changes. A well-designed cognitive assessment simulates precisely those demands in miniature — which is why employers trust it as a predictor of performance in multi-team delivery leadership.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The assessment dimensions map directly to the named skills in your capability framework:

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **Maintaining delivery momentum** and **Planning**: optimising the delivery flow of teams means noticing recurring signals across sprint data, dependency maps and risk logs — the team whose cycle time is quietly lengthening, the dependency shape that has caused slippage twice before, the risk that resurfaces in a new disguise each quarter.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Planning** and **Life cycle management**: leading a continual planning process in a very complex environment requires you to reason validly from constraints — sequencing rules, team availability, governance gates — to their consequences, and to predict risks before they materialise at each phase of the life cycle.
- **Error checking** maps to **Making a process work** and **Financial management**: identifying and challenging organisational processes that are unnecessarily complicated, and managing complex budgets across a programme view, both depend on spotting the inconsistency — the process step that contradicts the policy, the forecast line that cannot be reconciled with the burn rate.
- **Prioritisation** maps to your duty to **balance objectives and redeploy people and resources as priorities change**, and to your position as **the initial escalation point for the programme**: when four issues arrive at once, ranking them by impact-weighted urgency is the difference between a programme that self-corrects and one that thrashes.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Maintaining delivery momentum** and **Community collaboration**: actively addressing the most complicated risks, issues and dependencies — including those where ownership sits outside the team or no clear ownership exists — and solving issues between teams or departments at the highest level, are both exercises in decomposing a tangled situation, modelling it, and finding the innovative unblocking move.

Because you also **support and coach delivery managers**, several questions below place you in a reviewing or coaching position: checking someone else's plan, spotting the flaw in a team's process, or deciding what feedback will most improve another person's reasoning. That is deliberate — at your level, cognitive skill is something you exercise on your own work and transmit through others' work.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern recognition in a RAID log**

Your programme's RAID log uses a fixed identifier convention: items are coded with a letter for type (R = risk, A = assumption, I = issue, D = dependency), then the two-digit team number, then a sequential three-digit number — for example, R-04-017 is the seventeenth risk raised by team 4. Reviewing entries added this week by a new delivery manager, you see:

1. R-04-018
2. I-11-003
3. D-7-102
4. A-09-001
5. I-11-004

Which entry breaks the convention?

- A) Entry 2
- B) Entry 3
- C) Entry 4
- D) Entry 5

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The convention requires a two-digit team number. Entry 3 uses "7" rather than "07", so it breaks the pattern; every other entry follows letter, two digits, three digits. This looks trivial, but identifier conventions are what make a programme-level RAID log filterable and reportable — if team 7's items are split between "7" and "07", your dependency reports silently undercount, and the programme board sees a cleaner picture than reality. Spotting small structural inconsistencies quickly is the foundation of the error-checking discipline that the harder questions in this section build on, and it is exactly the kind of gentle, specific feedback you would give a new delivery manager while coaching them into programme ways of working.

**Question 2 (easy) — Logical deduction from a governance rule**

Your programme board's terms of reference state: "Any change with a forecast cost impact above £100,000 must be approved by the programme board before implementation, unless the programme director has granted emergency authority in writing." A delivery manager tells you: "We implemented a change last month with a cost impact of £150,000, and it never went to the programme board." Which one of the following conclusions must be true?

- A) The change breached the terms of reference.
- B) The programme director granted emergency authority in writing.
- C) Either the programme director granted written emergency authority, or the terms of reference were breached.
- D) The delivery manager should be formally disciplined.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The rule is a conditional with exactly one permitted exception. From the facts given you cannot tell whether written emergency authority exists, so neither A nor B must be true on its own — each is merely possible. D is a judgement about consequences that the facts do not support at all. The only conclusion guaranteed in every case consistent with the facts is the either/or statement in C. Assessments reward this discipline of deducing only what the information guarantees — and so does your job. As the initial escalation point for the programme, the same restraint stops you accusing a team of a governance breach before checking whether an exemption exists, which protects both your logic and your relationships. Verify first, then act.

**Question 3 (easy) — Prioritisation of incoming demands**

It is 9:05 on Monday morning. Four items are waiting for you:

1. A delivery manager reports that a critical third-party API used by two teams went down over the weekend and is still down; both teams are blocked.
2. The programme director asks for talking points for a ministerial briefing on Thursday.
3. A finance business partner needs your budget reforecast by the end of the week.
4. A delivery manager asks you to review their draft team health-check survey, no deadline given.

What should you address first?

- A) Item 2 — the programme director is your most senior stakeholder
- B) Item 1 — an active blocker stopping two teams outranks everything with later deadlines
- C) Item 3 — financial deadlines are immovable
- D) Item 4 — quick reviews should be cleared first to empty the queue

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Item 1 is the only item that is both urgent and currently costing delivery capacity: every hour the API stays down, two teams burn time they cannot recover, and removing blockers is the heart of your role. Items 2 and 3 have deadlines of Thursday and Friday — important, but schedulable. Item 4 has no deadline and modest impact. Option A confuses seniority with urgency; the programme director would expect you to unblock teams first and would be alarmed if you drafted talking points while delivery stood still. Option C treats a Friday deadline as if it were today's. Option D is the "small tasks first" trap, which feels productive but spends your highest-leverage hour on your lowest-leverage item. The pattern to internalise: rank by impact-weighted urgency, not by seniority, deadline proximity alone, or ease.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Dependency deduction across teams**

Your programme's plan contains these rules: (i) Team Alpha's payments service cannot go to public beta until Team Beta's fraud-check API is live; (ii) the fraud-check API cannot go live until the data-sharing agreement with another department is signed; (iii) security assurance of the payments service must be completed no earlier than two weeks before its public beta, or it must be repeated. The data-sharing agreement will be signed at the end of week 6. The fraud-check API needs two weeks from signature to go live. Security assurance of the payments service was completed in week 3. If public beta happens at the earliest possible date, what follows?

- A) Public beta occurs in week 8 and the week 3 security assurance remains valid.
- B) Public beta occurs in week 8 and security assurance must be repeated.
- C) Public beta occurs in week 6 and security assurance must be repeated.
- D) Public beta cannot occur until security assurance is repeated, so its date cannot be determined.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Chain the dependencies forward. The agreement is signed at end of week 6; the API needs two further weeks, so it is live at end of week 8; public beta can therefore occur at the earliest in week 8. Now test the assurance rule: assurance is valid only if completed no earlier than two weeks before beta, i.e. in week 6 or later for a week 8 beta. It was completed in week 3, which is more than two weeks before week 8, so it must be repeated. Note that D overreaches: the repeat assurance is a task that can be scheduled inside the existing window (weeks 6-8); nothing says it delays the beta, so the earliest date is still determinable. This is the everyday logic of managing dependencies of varying complexity: sequence the hard constraints first, then test the conditional rules against the resulting dates — and be alert to validity windows, which are the constraint programme plans most often get wrong.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Error checking a milestone report**

A delivery manager's monthly report to you includes this table:

| Milestone | Baseline date | Forecast date | Variance (weeks) | RAG |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Private beta start | 1 June | 15 June | +2 | Amber |
| Security assessment | 1 July | 1 July | 0 | Green |
| Public beta start | 1 August | 22 August | +3 | Amber |
| Service assessment | 1 September | 8 September | +1 | Green |

The report's narrative states: "All variances are consistent and no milestone slips more than three weeks." You know the programme standard is that a variance of more than two weeks must be rated Red. How many distinct errors are in the table and narrative combined?

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Work systematically, row by row, then check the narrative. Row 1: 1 June to 15 June is 2 weeks; variance +2; Amber is permitted (not more than two weeks) — consistent. Row 2: no variance, Green — consistent. Row 3: 1 August to 22 August is 3 weeks; the stated variance +3 matches the dates, but a variance of more than two weeks must be Red, and it is rated Amber — error one. Row 4: 1 September to 8 September is 1 week, variance +1, Green — consistent. Narrative: "no milestone slips more than three weeks" is technically true (the largest slip is exactly three weeks), but "all variances are consistent" is false, because row 3's RAG rating contradicts the programme standard — error two. Total: 2. The trap answers count row 3 as two errors (it is one inconsistency described two ways) or treat the "+3" as a date arithmetic error (it is not — check before you flag). At programme level you consume dozens of such reports; the skill is finding the minimal set of genuine defects quickly, because every false positive you raise costs a delivery manager an hour of rework and a little trust.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Pattern recognition in team delivery data**

You review four teams' last six sprints. Each team's completed story points per sprint:

- Team A: 40, 42, 38, 41, 40, 39
- Team B: 35, 36, 34, 28, 22, 15
- Team C: 55, 30, 58, 27, 60, 29
- Team D: 20, 24, 28, 32, 36, 40

Which team's pattern most warrants immediate investigation as a delivery risk?

- A) Team A
- B) Team B
- C) Team C
- D) Team D

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Read each shape. Team A is stable around 40 — healthy. Team D is rising steadily — plausibly a maturing team; worth a light-touch check that scope quality is holding, but a positive trend. Team C alternates high/low — a striking oscillation, but a regular one; the classic causes (a fortnightly release overhead landing in alternate sprints, or carry-over accounting) are process quirks rather than accelerating danger, and the team's average is stable. Team B shows a sustained, steepening decline: 35 to 15 is a 57% fall, and the drops are growing (1, 2, 6, 6, 7). A monotonic worsening trend is the signature of a compounding problem — attrition, mounting technical debt, an unresolved blocker — and it will not fix itself. Optimising the delivery flow of teams starts with knowing which anomaly is a curiosity and which is a trajectory; assessments test whether you can rank the two under time pressure. Note also the coaching angle: the right first move with Team B is an open question to its delivery manager, not a conclusion.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Making a process work**

Your organisation's change process requires every production release to pass through five gates: team review, cross-team integration check, security sign-off, change advisory board (CAB), and release scheduling. Analysis shows: team review catches 60% of all defects found in the process; integration check catches 30%; security sign-off catches 9%; CAB catches 1% and adds an average of 6 working days' delay; release scheduling catches nothing and adds 2 days. Teams release fortnightly. You want to improve flow without materially increasing risk. Which change is the strongest candidate to propose first?

- A) Remove team review, since teams should build quality in rather than inspect it
- B) Make CAB exception-based (only changes meeting defined risk criteria attend), retaining the other gates
- C) Remove security sign-off, since it catches under 10% of defects
- D) Add a sixth gate to catch the defects the process currently misses

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Compare each gate's defect yield against its delay cost. CAB has the worst ratio in the data: 1% of catches for 6 days of delay — the longest delay in the chain for the smallest yield. Making it exception-based preserves scrutiny for genuinely risky changes (so risk is not materially increased) while removing the delay for the routine majority. Option A removes the highest-yield step on an ideological slogan; the aspiration to build quality in is right, but you would sequence that improvement before removing the inspection that currently catches 60% of defects. Option C confuses low volume with low value — security defects are low-frequency but high-severity, so yield percentage alone is the wrong metric for that gate. Option D adds process to a flow problem, which is the opposite of identifying and challenging organisational processes that are unnecessarily complicated. The technique to remember: evaluate process steps on yield versus cost, but weight severity, not just count — and prefer proportionate redesign over deletion when risk is concentrated.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Resource redeployment logic**

You have three workstreams and must cover an urgent two-sprint task requiring two senior developers. Constraints: (i) Workstream X has three senior developers but is four weeks from a legally mandated deadline; (ii) Workstream Y has two senior developers and is mid-discovery, with its alpha not starting for six weeks; (iii) Workstream Z has two senior developers, one of whom is the only person who understands the legacy payments integration that the urgent task concerns; (iv) no workstream may lose more than one person. Which combination best satisfies the constraints?

- A) Two senior developers from X
- B) One from Y and one from Z (the payments specialist)
- C) Two from Y
- D) One from X and one from Y

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Apply the constraints as filters before weighing preferences. Constraint (iv) eliminates A and C immediately — each takes two people from a single workstream. That leaves B and D, both of which are constraint-legal, so now optimise. The urgent task concerns the legacy payments integration, and Z holds the only person who understands it; a two-sprint task staffed without that knowledge risks failing at the point of the whole exercise. D draws from X, which is four weeks from a legally mandated deadline — the worst possible place to remove capacity, even one person. B takes one person from Y, which is in discovery with six weeks of slack before alpha (the most absorbable loss), plus the specialist from Z, keeping every workstream within the one-person limit. This mirrors real redeployment decisions: hard constraints first, then optimise for knowledge fit and deadline risk. In the workplace you would also confirm Z can backfill or pause the integration work the specialist leaves behind — an assessment cannot test that follow-through, but your programme depends on it.

**Question 9 (hard) — Multi-constraint scheduling**

You must schedule three events in one working week (Monday to Friday, one event per day at most): a programme increment planning session (P), a supplier contract negotiation (N), and a go/no-go review (G). Constraints: (i) P must occur before G, with at least one clear working day between them for teams to finalise plans; (ii) the commercial lead is available only Tuesday and Friday and must attend N; (iii) the senior responsible owner must attend both P and G, and is unavailable Wednesday; (iv) N must not be on the day immediately after P, because the planning outputs need a day's commercial analysis before negotiation. On which day must G take place?

- A) Wednesday
- B) Thursday
- C) Friday
- D) It cannot be determined

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Work the constraints together, pinning the most restricted event first. From (ii), N is Tuesday or Friday. Test N on Tuesday: then (iv) forbids P on Monday (the day before N is fine — the rule forbids N immediately after P, so P Monday with N Tuesday is the banned shape); P cannot be Tuesday (N occupies it); (iii) forbids Wednesday for P; so P would be Thursday at the earliest, and (i) then pushes G to Saturday or later — impossible. So N must be Friday. With N on Friday, G cannot be Friday (day taken) and cannot be Wednesday (SRO unavailable), leaving Monday, Tuesday or Thursday. G on Monday fails: no day exists before it for P. G on Tuesday fails: P would need to sit before Monday to leave a clear day between P and G. G on Thursday works: P on Monday (Tuesday and Wednesday clear) or Tuesday (Wednesday clear) satisfies (i); (iii) holds for both; and (iv) holds because N is Friday, never the day immediately after a Monday or Tuesday P. Every constraint is satisfied only when G is Thursday. Finish with an explicit verification pass of the surviving schedule — in timed multi-constraint questions a first pass can feel complete while quietly skipping a case, and the same elimination-then-verify habit is how you schedule real governance events across diaries without a board discovering the clash for you.

**Question 10 (hard) — Deduction under uncertainty at escalation**

As initial escalation point, you receive three statements about a missed integration milestone. The delivery manager of Team One says: "Our part was ready; Team Two's API changed without notice." The delivery manager of Team Two says: "We published the API change four weeks ago in the change log; any team consuming it was notified via the standard feed." The technical architect says: "The standard feed has been broken for six weeks; no notifications have been delivered to any team." Assuming all three statements are true, which conclusion is best supported?

- A) Team Two failed to publish the change and is primarily at fault.
- B) Team One failed to read the change log and is primarily at fault.
- C) Both teams followed their local process, and the failure lies in a broken shared mechanism neither team owns.
- D) The technical architect is mistaken, because Team Two says notifications were sent.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Take the statements as given and look for the consistent picture. Team Two published to the change log and relied on the standard feed — that is their process followed. Team One relied on receiving notifications — reasonable if the feed is the standard mechanism. The architect's statement explains how both can be true simultaneously: the change was published but never delivered, because the shared notification mechanism has been silently broken for six weeks. A eliminates itself (publication happened); B assumes teams are obliged to poll the change log manually, which the scenario does not state; D wrongly treats Team Two's belief that notifications were sent as evidence against the architect's direct statement about the feed. C is the classic programme-level finding: the most complicated issues are those where ownership exists outside the teams or no clear ownership exists at all — a broken shared feed is precisely such an orphan. The follow-through in real life: assign an owner to the feed, add a detection check so it cannot fail silently again, and steer the retrospective away from inter-team blame — solving issues between teams at the highest level means fixing the system, not adjudicating the argument.

**Question 11 (hard) — Applied problem solving on the critical path**

Your programme's critical path runs: data migration (4 weeks) → integration testing (3 weeks) → security assessment (2 weeks) → public launch. The launch date is fixed by a ministerial announcement 8 weeks from today. Options: (a) integration testing can start when migration is 75% complete, saving 1 week of sequence time, but adds a 20% chance of 2 weeks' rework; (b) security assessment can run in parallel with the final week of integration testing at a cost of £40,000 in additional assessor time, saving 1 week with no quality impact; (c) migration can be compressed to 3 weeks by adding contractors at £60,000, with no quality impact. The sequential plan takes 9 weeks — 1 week over. Which option, alone, gives the most reliable path to the launch date at the lowest cost?

- A) Option (a), because it is free
- B) Option (b)
- C) Option (c)
- D) Options (b) and (c) combined

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** You need exactly 1 week of savings. Compare the single options. Option (a) saves 1 week but carries a 20% chance of 2 weeks' rework — an expected loss of 0.4 weeks, and in the bad case the programme lands 1 week late anyway (9 − 1 + 2 = 10 weeks becomes… work it through: 8-week plan, 20% chance it becomes 10). "Free" is illusory once risk is priced. Option (b) saves 1 week for £40,000 with no quality impact — the plan becomes a reliable 8 weeks. Option (c) also saves 1 week reliably but costs £60,000. Both (b) and (c) deliver the date; (b) does so £20,000 cheaper. Option D buys 2 weeks of savings when only 1 is needed, spending £100,000 to arrive a week early against a fixed announcement — over-recovery is waste. The reasoning pattern is one you use whenever you manage risks, commercials and budgets together: convert every option into the same currencies (time saved, reliability, cost), meet the constraint exactly, and never pay twice for a week you only need once. Answer: B.

**Question 12 (hard) — Coaching another's reasoning**

A delivery manager you coach presents this argument: "Every sprint where we've had the full team available, we've hit the sprint goal. Next sprint the full team is available. Therefore we will hit the sprint goal, so I've told stakeholders the release is certain." Which is the most accurate assessment of the reasoning, and the most useful coaching point?

- A) The reasoning is deductively valid; the only issue is stakeholder tone.
- B) The reasoning is a reasonable induction wrongly presented as a certainty; the coaching point is to communicate probabilistic confidence, not guarantees.
- C) The reasoning is worthless because past performance never indicates future results.
- D) The reasoning is flawed because sprint goals should never be shared with stakeholders.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Separate the logic from the communication. The inference "full team available has always coincided with hitting the goal, so it likely will again" is induction: genuinely useful evidence, never a guarantee — team availability is one of many conditions (unchanged scope, no new dependencies, no external blockers) and the sample of past sprints is finite. So the reasoning is not valid deduction (eliminating A) but nor is it worthless (eliminating C, which overcorrects into a slogan). D invents a rule that contradicts transparent ways of working. The defect is the leap from "likely" to "certain", compounded by transmitting that false certainty to stakeholders, where it becomes a commitment the team may not control. The coaching move — and this is the part assessments at your level probe — is not to rewrite the delivery manager's message yourself but to teach the distinction: strong evidence supports high confidence, stated as high confidence ("on track, main risks are X and Y"), preserving trust when the 10% case happens. Managing stakeholder expectations about high-risk topics starts with never manufacturing certainty you do not have — and coaching others out of the same habit multiplies that discipline across the programme.

### Preparation tips

- **Practise with your own artefacts.** Ten minutes with yesterday's RAID log or plan-on-a-page, actively hunting for one inconsistency, is better training than an hour of abstract puzzles — it builds the same muscles on the material the test imitates.
- **Rehearse constraint problems on paper.** For scheduling and dependency questions, write the constraints as a short list and eliminate options against them one at a time. The habit of externalising constraints frees working memory for reasoning, in the test and in programme planning alike.
- **Time-box, don't rush.** In a 20-question, 25-minute test, give each question a soft budget of about 70 seconds; flag and move on rather than sinking four minutes into one item. This is triage — the same skill as your Monday-morning inbox.
- **Verify the surviving answer.** After eliminating options, spend ten seconds checking the one you have left actually satisfies every condition. Question 9 above shows why: a confident first pass can still be wrong.
- **Sleep and settings matter.** Do the test rested, on a proper screen, with notifications off. You would not run a go/no-go review from a phone on a train; give your own assessment the same professional conditions.
- **Coach someone else beforehand.** Explaining a practice question to one of your delivery managers is the fastest way to deepen your own technique — and it turns your preparation into their development too.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Answering from experience instead of the stem.** Senior people lose marks by importing knowledge the question does not give ("in my organisation, CABs also do X"). Assessments score reasoning from the stated facts only — treat each stem as the whole world.
- **Confusing seniority with priority.** A director's undated request does not outrank an active blocker. Several questions are designed to catch exactly this reflex.
- **Flagging symptoms as separate errors.** When one defect produces two visible inconsistencies, count the defect. Over-flagging costs time in tests and credibility in report reviews.
- **Ignoring validity windows and side conditions.** Rules like "no earlier than two weeks before" or "not the day immediately after" are where most marks are dropped. Read conditionals twice.
- **Treating a striking pattern as the risky one.** Oscillation is eye-catching; monotonic decline is dangerous. Rank anomalies by trajectory and consequence, not visual drama.
- **Certainty inflation.** In deduction questions, prefer the conclusion that must be true over the one that is probably true. The disjunction ("either X or Y") often is the right answer, however unsatisfying it feels.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures your ability to interpret, manipulate and draw sound conclusions from quantitative information presented the way it actually arrives in your job: budget tables, burn-up charts, resource forecasts, supplier invoices, velocity data and risk exposure figures. It is not a mathematics exam. The arithmetic itself rarely goes beyond percentages, ratios, averages, rates and simple projections — the difficulty lies in reading the data accurately, choosing the right operation, and resisting plausible-looking wrong answers under time pressure.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 20 to 30 minutes with 15 to 25 questions, each based on a table, chart or short data extract. Calculators are usually permitted, and many platforms are adaptive. Employers receive accuracy and speed measures against a norm group. For senior delivery roles the questions lean towards interpretation and decision relevance: not "what is 15% of 80,000" in isolation, but "given this variance table, which workstream's forecast should worry you".

For a programme delivery manager, numeric reasoning is not a supporting skill — it is load-bearing. You influence or create complex budgets across an organisation, programme or product view; you manage the budget you are given and make it work; you act as the escalation point for large or high-risk commercial issues; and you report delivery status to boards who will make funding decisions on the numbers you present. A misread variance, a percentage calculated on the wrong base, or a trend extrapolated naively can misdirect a programme worth millions. Employers use this assessment because it predicts, cheaply and fairly, whether a candidate can be trusted to do that quantitative reading correctly at speed.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Percentages and variances** map to **Financial management**: managing the budget you are given means constantly computing spend against profile, variance against baseline, and percentage complete against percentage spent — and knowing which base each percentage is calculated on.
- **Rates and burn calculations** map to **Maintaining delivery momentum** and **Planning**: burn rate, velocity, cycle time and run-rate projections are the arithmetic underneath "will we make the date", which is the question your continual planning process must keep answering.
- **Ratios and proportions** map to your duty to **balance objectives and redeploy people and resources as priorities change**: rebalancing people across teams is ratio arithmetic with consequences, as is comparing supplier day rates and blended team costs.
- **Reading tables and charts** maps to **Communicating between the technical and non-technical**: you translate quantitative delivery data for boards, ministers' offices and directors — you cannot simplify a chart honestly unless you first read it precisely.
- **Trend interpretation and projection** maps to **Life cycle management** and your **Commercial management** escalation duty: predicting and preventing risks at different phases means projecting current data forward and knowing the limits of that projection, and resolving commercial issues means testing a supplier's numbers rather than accepting them.

The questions below use the data your role genuinely handles: programme budgets, team velocity, supplier invoices, defect rates, and contingency draw-down. Every explanation shows the arithmetic step by step, because at your level the discipline of auditable working is part of the professional standard you set for others.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Percentage of budget spent**

Your programme's annual budget is £4,800,000. At the end of month 6, actual spend is £2,640,000. What percentage of the annual budget has been spent?

- A) 52%
- B) 55%
- C) 58%
- D) 60%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Divide spend by budget: 2,640,000 ÷ 4,800,000 = 0.55. Multiply by 100 to express as a percentage: 55%. A quick sanity check: half the budget would be £2,400,000, and £2,640,000 is a little more than half, so an answer slightly above 50% is right — 55% fits, 60% would be £2,880,000. Note what the number means before you report it: at the halfway point of the year you have spent 55% of budget, i.e. 5 percentage points ahead of a straight-line profile. Whether that is a problem depends on whether spend was profiled evenly — which is exactly the follow-up question a good finance business partner will expect you to have asked before the board meeting.

**Question 2 (easy) — Team velocity average**

A delivery team completed the following story points over five sprints: 34, 38, 30, 42, 36. What is the team's mean velocity per sprint?

- A) 34
- B) 35
- C) 36
- D) 38

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Sum the points: 34 + 38 = 72; 72 + 30 = 102; 102 + 42 = 144; 144 + 36 = 180. Divide by the number of sprints: 180 ÷ 5 = 36. The mean is 36 points per sprint. When you use this figure for forward planning, remember its spread: the sprints range from 30 to 42, so quoting "36" to a stakeholder without a range invites a commitment the team will miss roughly half the time. A programme-level habit worth building: whenever you compute an average for planning, note the minimum alongside it — "typically 36, never below 30 in the last five sprints" is a far more honest planning statement.

**Question 3 (easy) — Supplier day-rate comparison**

Supplier A offers a senior developer at £680 per day. Supplier B offers an equivalent developer at £600 per day plus a fixed monthly management fee of £2,400. You need one developer for one month of 20 working days. Which option is cheaper, and by how much?

- A) Supplier A, by £800
- B) Supplier B, by £800
- C) Supplier B, by £1,600
- D) They cost the same

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Cost each option fully, including fixed fees. Supplier A: £680 × 20 = £13,600. Supplier B: (£600 × 20) + £2,400 = £12,000 + £2,400 = £14,400. Supplier A is cheaper by £14,400 − £13,600 = £800. The trap is comparing day rates alone — £600 looks better than £680, which is exactly what distractor B rewards — while the fixed fee reverses the conclusion at this volume. The commercial insight is scaling behaviour: the fee is fixed, so at higher volume Supplier B improves. At three developers, A costs £680 × 60 = £40,800 while B costs £600 × 60 + £2,400 = £38,400 — now B wins by £2,400. When you coach others in appropriate commercial management, "never compare rates without total cost, and always ask how the cost scales with volume" is the lesson this question encodes.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Burn rate and runway**

Your programme has £1,860,000 remaining for the financial year, which ends in 31 weeks. The current burn rate is £72,000 per week. A planned team expansion in 6 weeks will raise the burn rate to £84,000 per week from that point. Will the remaining budget last to year end, and what is the approximate surplus or shortfall?

- A) Yes, with roughly £128,000 to spare
- B) No, with a shortfall of roughly £672,000
- C) No, with a shortfall of roughly £228,000
- D) Yes, with roughly £28,000 to spare

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Split the year into two phases at the step change. Phase 1: 6 weeks at £72,000 per week = £432,000. Phase 2: the remaining 31 − 6 = 25 weeks at £84,000 per week = £2,100,000. Total forecast spend: £432,000 + £2,100,000 = £2,532,000. Compare with the remaining budget: £2,532,000 − £1,860,000 = £672,000 shortfall, so the budget does not last and the answer is B. The distractor C (roughly £228,000) is what you get if you transpose the phases — applying the higher rate to the short phase and the lower rate to the long one (6 × £84,000 + 25 × £72,000 = £504,000 + £1,800,000 = £2,304,000; £2,304,000 − £1,860,000 = £444,000 — or from similar phase confusions at speed). Distractor A comes from using the current rate throughout (31 × £72,000 = £2,232,000, £372,000 over — still a shortfall, so "to spare" should ring alarm bells immediately). The programme lesson: a burn-rate step change dominates the forecast when it applies to the longer phase, which is why you model expansions before approving them, not after. If this forecast were real, you would be taking options to the programme director now — descope, delay the expansion, or secure additional funding — because a £672,000 problem discovered in week 30 is a crisis, while the same problem discovered today is a decision.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Defect rate percentage change**

Across your programme, defects found in production per month were: January 40, February 46, March 58. What was the percentage increase from January to March?

- A) 18%
- B) 31%
- C) 45%
- D) 58%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Percentage increase = (new − old) ÷ old × 100. From January to March: (58 − 40) ÷ 40 = 18 ÷ 40 = 0.45, so 45%. The distractors are the classic errors: 18% is the absolute increase (18 defects) misread as a percentage; 31% is roughly the February-to-March change calculated on the wrong pair ((58 − 46) ÷ 46 ≈ 26%, or (58 − 40) ÷ 58 ≈ 31% — the increase computed on the new base rather than the old); 58% is the raw March figure. Always anchor a percentage change to its stated base period and divide by the old value. In delivery reporting this matters doubly, because a stakeholder hearing "defects up 45%" and one hearing "18 more defects a month" will react very differently to the same fact — you are responsible for choosing the framing that informs rather than alarms, and for being able to defend the arithmetic behind either.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Weighted programme RAG from team data**

Your programme rolls up delivery confidence from four workstreams, weighted by budget share. The workstreams report: Alpha (40% of budget) at 90% confidence; Beta (30%) at 70%; Gamma (20%) at 60%; Delta (10%) at 40%. What is the budget-weighted average confidence for the programme?

- A) 65%
- B) 69%
- C) 73%
- D) 75%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Multiply each confidence by its weight and sum. Alpha: 0.40 × 90 = 36. Beta: 0.30 × 70 = 21. Gamma: 0.20 × 60 = 12. Delta: 0.10 × 40 = 4. Sum: 36 + 21 + 12 + 4 = 73, so 73%. The unweighted mean, (90 + 70 + 60 + 40) ÷ 4 = 65%, is distractor A — the error of ignoring the weights. Notice what the weighted figure conceals: Delta sits at 40% confidence, a workstream more likely to fail than not, yet it barely dents the headline because it is only 10% of budget. When you communicate this to a board, the honest presentation is the weighted average plus the outlier — "73% overall, but Delta needs intervention" — because averages are where small crises hide. Budget weight is also not the only defensible weighting: if Delta is on the critical path, its true weight on the programme's success is far more than 10%, and part of your value is knowing when the standard formula understates the risk.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Resource ratio rebalancing**

Your programme has 60 engineers split across three services in the ratio 3:2:1 (Casework : Payments : Reporting). A strategy shift requires the split to become 2:2:1 with the total headcount unchanged. How many engineers move out of Casework?

- A) 6
- B) 10
- C) 12
- D) 24

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Compute both allocations. Current ratio 3:2:1 has 6 parts; 60 ÷ 6 = 10 engineers per part, giving Casework 30, Payments 20, Reporting 10. Target ratio 2:2:1 has 5 parts; 60 ÷ 5 = 12 engineers per part, giving Casework 24, Payments 24, Reporting 12. Casework moves from 30 to 24: 6 engineers move out (4 to Payments, 2 to Reporting). The distractor 12 comes from assuming the part size stays at 10 (2 × 10 = 20, so 30 − 20 = 10 — which is distractor B) or from other part-size confusions; the essential step is recognising that changing the ratio changes the size of a "part" because the total is fixed. When you redeploy people as priorities change, this arithmetic is the easy half; the hard half — knowledge transfer, team morale, and not moving the only payments specialist — is what the surrounding questions in the situational judgement section address. Do the arithmetic first so the people conversation starts from correct numbers.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Reading a burn-up chart**

A burn-up chart for a programme increment shows: total scope rising from 400 points (week 0) to 460 points (week 6, after a scope addition in week 4); completed work rising steadily from 0 (week 0) to 300 points (week 6). Completion has been linear. At the current completion rate, in which week will the team complete the current 460-point scope?

- A) Week 8
- B) Week 9
- C) Week 9.2, so during week 10
- D) Week 12

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Completion rate = 300 points ÷ 6 weeks = 50 points per week. Remaining work = 460 − 300 = 160 points. Time to complete = 160 ÷ 50 = 3.2 weeks. Current week 6 + 3.2 = week 9.2, which lands during week 10 (the team passes the finish line partway through the tenth week). Distractor B rounds 9.2 down to "week 9", which quietly promises delivery before the arithmetic supports it; distractor A uses the original 400-point scope (400 ÷ 50 = 8); distractor D misreads the remaining work as 300. Two professional notes: first, always project against current scope, not original — burn-up charts exist precisely to make scope growth visible, and using the stale total repeats the error the chart was designed to prevent. Second, when you communicate "week 9.2" to a non-technical stakeholder, say "during week 10" — rounding in the honest direction — because a date given to one decimal place implies a precision your data does not have.

**Question 9 (hard) — Contingency draw-down analysis**

Your programme started the year with a £900,000 contingency fund. Draw-downs so far: Q1 £180,000; Q2 £275,000; Q3 £310,000. It is the start of Q4. The programme director asks: "If Q4 draw-down follows the trend of the last three quarters, will contingency run out, and roughly how large is the gap?" Which answer is most defensible?

- A) Yes — the average draw is £255,000 against £135,000 remaining: a gap of roughly £120,000
- B) Yes — the trend suggests a Q4 draw of roughly £375,000 against £135,000 remaining: a gap of roughly £240,000
- C) Yes — the gap is £765,000
- D) No — contingency exactly covers Q4

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Step 1, remaining contingency: £900,000 − (180,000 + 275,000 + 310,000) = £900,000 − £765,000 = £135,000. Step 2, project Q4. The question says "follows the trend", and the draws are rising: Q1→Q2 up £95,000; Q2→Q3 up £35,000. A simple trend continuation adds something like the average of those increments (£65,000) to Q3's figure: £310,000 + £65,000 = £375,000 — a reasonable single estimate, with any defensible trend reading landing in the £345,000–£405,000 range. Step 3, the gap: £375,000 − £135,000 = £240,000. Option A answers the wrong question — it computes the flat average (765 ÷ 3 = £255,000), which is what "average", not "trend", would ask for; note that even this understated method still shows contingency running out. Option C confuses the total already spent (£765,000) with the gap. Option D is arithmetic hope. The senior-level point: distinguish average from trend. A flat average (£255,000) already exceeds the remaining £135,000, so the qualitative answer is "yes, it runs out" under any reasonable projection — which is what makes B defensible even though the £375,000 is an estimate, not a fact. Boards need the direction and the order of magnitude now; a precise figure can follow. And a projected contingency breach is a textbook trigger for your escalation duty: this goes to the programme director with options attached, today.

**Question 10 (hard) — Percentage points versus percentages**

Two statements appear in a draft board paper about your programme's automated test coverage: (i) "Coverage improved from 60% to 75%, an increase of 15%." (ii) "The defect escape rate fell from 8% to 6%, a reduction of 25%." Which statement uses its terminology correctly?

- A) Both
- B) Only (i)
- C) Only (ii)
- D) Neither

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Statement (i): moving from 60% to 75% is an increase of 15 percentage points; as a percentage increase it is (75 − 60) ÷ 60 = 0.25 = 25%. Calling it "an increase of 15%" conflates points with percent — wrong as written. Statement (ii): from 8% to 6% is a fall of 2 percentage points, and as a percentage reduction it is (8 − 6) ÷ 8 = 0.25 = 25% — exactly what the statement claims, so (ii) is correct. Hence C. This distinction is one of the most common genuine errors in government reporting, and at your level you are the last check before a board reads it. It also cuts both ways rhetorically: "coverage up 25%" and "coverage up 15 percentage points" describe the same fact with different force, and a supplier's glossy report may choose whichever flatters them. When you act as the escalation point on commercial issues, recomputing a percentage claim from its base numbers is a thirty-second habit that regularly pays for itself.

**Question 11 (hard) — Supplier invoice reconciliation**

A supplier invoices £63,412.50 for a four-week period, itemised as: "3 senior developers × 19 days × £750 = £42,750; 2 developers × 20 days × £600 = £24,000; gross £66,750; less 5% volume discount = £63,412.50." You check the contract: the rates are correct, but the 5% volume discount applies only when gross monthly fees exceed £100,000, and then only to the portion above £100,000. What should the invoice total be?

- A) £63,412.50 — the invoice is correct as submitted
- B) £66,750 — no discount applies because gross fees are below £100,000
- C) £65,081.25 — the discount should be 2.5%
- D) £61,750 — the discount should also apply to expenses

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Step 1, verify the gross from its components. Seniors: 3 × 19 = 57 days; 57 × £750 = £42,750 — matches. Developers: 2 × 20 = 40 days; 40 × £600 = £24,000 — matches. Gross: £42,750 + £24,000 = £66,750 — matches. Step 2, test the discount against the contract: the 5% discount is triggered only when gross monthly fees exceed £100,000, and £66,750 is below the threshold, so no discount applies at all. The correct invoice is £66,750, and the supplier has undercharged by £66,750 − £63,412.50 = £3,337.50. Options C and D invent discount mechanics found nowhere in the contract — a reminder that in commercial questions, every adjustment needs a clause to stand on. The subtle senior-level point is that an error in your favour still gets corrected: an undercharge unwinds later as a disputed catch-up invoice, distorts your accruals and your budget forecast in the meantime, and quietly teaches the supplier that nobody on your side reads the arithmetic. Acting as the escalation point for large or high-risk commercial issues often begins exactly here — with one person reproducing an invoice from its own stated basis and testing every line against the contract, in both directions.

**Question 12 (hard) — Benefits versus run-cost projection**

A business case claims a new service will save £120,000 per quarter in staff time once live, against run costs of £45,000 per quarter, and one-off build costs of £600,000. The service goes live at the start of Q3 (build spend is spread evenly over Q1 and Q2). Counting from the start of Q1, at the end of which quarter does the service first reach cumulative break-even?

- A) Q5
- B) Q8
- C) Q10
- D) Q12

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Model the cumulative cash position. Q1: build spend £300,000; cumulative −£300,000. Q2: build spend £300,000; cumulative −£600,000. From Q3 the service is live and the net benefit per quarter is the saving minus the run cost: £120,000 − £45,000 = £75,000. Recovering £600,000 at £75,000 per quarter takes 600,000 ÷ 75,000 = 8 quarters of operation. Eight live quarters starting at Q3 are Q3 through Q10, so cumulative break-even lands at the end of Q10 — answer C. Now name the distractors, because each encodes a real business-case error. A (Q5) divides £600,000 by the gross saving of £120,000 (5 quarters) and forgets both the run costs and the two build quarters — the most flattering possible reading. B (Q8) uses the correct £75,000 net rate but counts 8 quarters from Q1, forgetting that nothing is recovered while you are still building. D (Q12) double-counts the build by treating it as £600,000 per quarter of delay. The examinable lesson: always net run costs against gross savings before projecting break-even — the £120,000 headline is not the recovery rate; £75,000 is — and anchor the recovery clock at go-live, not at first spend. In a real investment board, a benefits case built on the Q5 logic overstates the speed of the investment by five quarters; catching that before the money moves is precisely why programme delivery managers are in the room.

### Preparation tips

- **Rebuild your own programme's numbers.** Take last month's finance report and recompute one variance, one percentage and one projection by hand. Practising on live data trains the same operations the test uses, and occasionally finds a real error — a double return on preparation time.
- **Drill the three bases.** Most numeric errors are base errors: percentage change on the old value, weighted averages on the correct weights, projections on current (not original) scope. Before calculating, say to yourself: "what is the base?"
- **Write the model before the arithmetic.** For multi-step questions, jot the structure first ("phase 1 cost + phase 2 cost vs budget") and only then compute. Structure errors are expensive; arithmetic errors are cheap and catchable.
- **Use the calculator, verify by estimate.** Calculators are usually allowed — use one, but sanity-check each result against a rough mental estimate ("a bit more than half", "roughly 8 quarters") so a keying slip cannot survive.
- **Practise percentage points aloud.** The points-versus-percent distinction should be so automatic that you catch it in others' slides in real time. Explaining it to a delivery manager is the fastest way to make it permanent.
- **Time-box per question.** In a 20-question, 25-minute test, budget roughly 75 seconds each; multi-step questions earn a little more by borrowing from the easy ones. Never let one reconciliation absorb five minutes.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Computing on the wrong base.** Percentage change divides by the old value; a weighted average uses the stated weights; a projection uses current scope. The distractors are built from the wrong bases — that is how test writers design them.
- **Averaging when the question says trend.** A rising series projected flat understates the forecast. Read whether the stem asks for average, trend, or worst case; they are three different calculations and often three different options.
- **Ignoring step changes.** A burn rate or headcount that changes mid-period must be modelled in phases. Applying one rate across a period with a known change is the single most common error in runway questions — and in real reforecasts.
- **Rounding in the flattering direction.** 9.2 weeks is "during week 10", not "week 9". In tests this loses a mark; in board papers it loses credibility when reality arrives a week after your promise.
- **Forcing an answer that matches no working.** If your careful model matches no option, recheck the stem for a skipped term first; if the working survives, pick the option whose generating error you can identify — and never abandon verified arithmetic for a familiar-looking number.
- **Trusting a stated total.** Invoices, business cases and slide decks state totals; your job is to reproduce them from their own components. If the basis cannot reproduce the total, the document — not your arithmetic — is wrong.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately you extract meaning from written material under time pressure, and how well you distinguish between what a text states, what it implies, and what it simply does not address. The materials mirror your working reading diet: governance papers, assurance reports, policy extracts, supplier correspondence, escalation emails, service standard guidance and board minutes.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 15 to 25 minutes with 15 to 30 questions. The commonest question type presents a short passage followed by statements you must classify as True (definitely follows from the passage), False (definitely contradicted by the passage), or Cannot Say (the passage provides insufficient information either way). Other question types test inference strength, identifying an author's main point, and judging which of several rewrites communicates most clearly. Scoring is objective against a norm group, and the cardinal rule is that you answer from the passage alone — not from your professional knowledge, however extensive.

For a programme delivery manager, verbal reasoning is core infrastructure. You are responsible for understanding, managing and communicating to complex stakeholder groups; you mediate between technical and non-technical people, which means translating precisely in both directions; you moderate difficult discussions about high-risk topics within constrained timescales, where a misread sentence can become a misbriefed director; and you speak on behalf of your community to large audiences, where the standard of your language sets the standard for the programme. You also consume enormous volumes of text — assurance reports, contracts, ministerial correspondence — where the difference between "must", "should" and "may", or between "has decided" and "is minded to", carries real delivery consequences. Employers assess verbal reasoning because it predicts whether a candidate reads these documents as carefully as the role demands.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Comprehension of dense passages** maps to **Communicating between the technical and non-technical**: you cannot mediate between people or translate a technical assurance finding for a director unless you have first extracted its meaning exactly — including its hedges and conditions.
- **True/False/Cannot Say discipline** maps to your role as **the initial escalation point for the programme**: when an escalation lands, acting on what the email actually says rather than what you assume it means is the difference between a proportionate response and an own goal. It also underpins **Commercial management**, where contract words mean precisely what they say.
- **Inference judgement** maps to **understanding, managing and communicating to complex stakeholder groups**: stakeholder letters and board minutes are written diplomatically; your job is to detect what is supported by the text and what you are merely tempted to read into it, and to brief others on the difference.
- **Clear-writing judgement** maps to **speaking on behalf of, and representing the community to, large audiences** and to **supporting and coaching delivery managers**: you set the writing standard for status reports and board papers across the programme, and you improve others' writing through feedback rather than rewriting it yourself.
- **Identifying the main point** maps to **having an awareness of the bigger picture** and **supporting the programme director by overseeing the delivery of their vision**: a twelve-page assurance report has one message the programme director needs; finding it fast, without distorting it, is a daily act of verbal reasoning.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — True/False/Cannot Say on a policy extract**

Passage: "Departments must complete a service assessment before a service moves from private beta to public beta. Assessments are carried out by a panel of assessors who are independent of the service team. A service that does not meet the Service Standard at assessment may be reassessed once the panel's recommendations have been addressed."

Statement: "A service that fails its assessment can never move to public beta."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage directly provides the mechanism that contradicts the statement: a service that does not meet the standard "may be reassessed once the panel's recommendations have been addressed" — so a failed assessment is recoverable, and "can never" is definitely false. The discipline here is matching the strength of the statement against the strength of the text: absolute words like "never", "always" and "all" in a statement need absolute support in the passage to be true, and are contradicted by any stated exception. As someone who guides teams through assessments and governance gates, you will also recognise the real-world importance: a delivery manager who believes a failed assessment is terminal will make very different (and worse) decisions than one who knows the reassessment route exists.

**Question 2 (easy) — Reading an escalation email precisely**

Passage (email from a delivery manager): "The supplier has told us the API gateway upgrade is delayed by at least two weeks. This puts our integration test window at risk. We can hold the current go-live date if the upgrade lands within three weeks, but only by compressing regression testing. I haven't yet told the service owner, as I wanted your steer first."

Statement: "The go-live date has been missed."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The email says the date can still be held if the upgrade lands within three weeks — the date is at risk, not missed, so the statement is contradicted by the passage and is false. Notice the ladder of certainty in the email, because your reply depends on reading it exactly: the delay is a fact ("has told us… at least two weeks"), the risk is a fact, the miss is conditional and not yet realised, and the mitigation (compressing regression testing) carries its own unstated risk. A hasty reader who reports "we've missed go-live" upward has manufactured a crisis; one who reports "no risk to go-live" has hidden one. The role-relevant skill is transmitting exactly the certainty the source text supports — no more, no less — which is what managing stakeholder expectations on high-risk topics means in practice.

**Question 3 (easy) — Identifying the main point**

Passage (from an assurance review): "The programme demonstrates strong delivery discipline at team level, with mature agile practices and reliable reporting. However, dependencies between teams are tracked inconsistently, and no single view of cross-team dependencies exists. While individual teams are likely to meet their local milestones, the review team considers that the absence of a programme-level dependency view presents the most significant threat to the integrated go-live date."

What is the review's main message?

- A) The programme's agile practices are mature.
- B) Individual teams will miss their milestones.
- C) The biggest risk to the integrated go-live date is the lack of a programme-level dependency view.
- D) The programme's reporting is unreliable.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage is built on a classic "praise, however, verdict" structure, and the main point lives after the "however" — signposted explicitly by "the review team considers that… the most significant threat". Option A is true but subordinate: it is context, not the message. Option B contradicts the passage ("likely to meet their local milestones"). Option D contradicts it too ("reliable reporting"). Finding the load-bearing sentence in a politely structured report is a daily skill: when you brief the programme director on this review, the first sentence of your summary should be C, with the praise as reassurance afterwards — not the other way round. And note the professional irony the question smuggles in: the finding itself concerns dependency management, your own specialist domain, which is a reminder that assurance reviews are most useful when someone senior reads them as instructions rather than as criticism.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Cannot Say discipline on a supplier letter**

Passage (from a supplier's letter): "Following the change in scope requested on 14 May, we have re-baselined our delivery plan. The revised plan extends the delivery timeline by six weeks. We consider that the scope change constitutes a compensation event under clause 12.3, and we will submit a quotation for the additional costs within ten working days."

Statement: "The supplier's additional costs have been approved."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage says the supplier considers the change a compensation event and will submit a quotation. It says nothing about anyone approving anything — approval is a future event that has not happened in the text, but neither does the text say approval has been refused. So the statement is neither supported nor contradicted: Cannot Say. The tempting error is False, on the reasoning "a quotation hasn't even been submitted yet, so costs can't be approved" — but that is your commercial knowledge filling a gap the passage leaves open; strictly, the passage does not state the approval status at all. This discipline has a sharp commercial edge: a supplier's letter asserting that something "constitutes a compensation event" is a claim, not a fact, and the correct programme response is to log it, neither accepting nor rejecting it by silence. Reading claims as claims — in tests and in contract correspondence — is a core part of acting as the escalation point for high-risk commercial issues.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Inference from board minutes**

Passage (board minutes extract): "The board noted the programme's amber-red status. The senior responsible owner reminded members that the spending review settlement remains provisional. The board agreed that recruitment to the three vacant delivery manager posts should proceed, but that offers above the standard pay range would require her explicit approval. The next deep-dive on the payments workstream was brought forward from November to September."

Which inference is best supported?

- A) The board has lost confidence in the programme.
- B) The board has increased its scrutiny of the payments workstream.
- C) The three delivery manager posts will be filled by September.
- D) The spending review settlement has been cancelled.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Bringing a deep-dive forward by two months is a concrete act of increased scrutiny — B is directly supported by a stated decision. A overreaches: amber-red status plus tighter pay controls might hint at concern, but "lost confidence" is far stronger than anything the minutes state, and the board simultaneously approved recruitment, an act of continued investment. C converts an approval to recruit into a prediction of successful recruitment by a specific date — nothing in the text supports the timeline. D distorts "provisional" into "cancelled", a plain misreading. The skill being tested is calibrated inference: choosing the conclusion at the exact strength the evidence supports. In your role this is how you read governance signals — a rescheduled deep-dive is a message, and briefing your payments delivery manager that "the board is looking harder at us in September, let's be ready" is the correctly calibrated response, while announcing "the board has lost confidence" would damage morale on evidence that does not support it.

**Question 6 (moderate) — True/False/Cannot Say on standards guidance**

Passage: "Service teams should deploy to production frequently — many teams deploy multiple times a day. Teams must ensure that deployments do not cause downtime for users. Where a team cannot yet deploy without downtime, deployments should be scheduled during the service's lowest-usage periods, and the team should have a plan to reach zero-downtime deployments."

Statement: "Teams are required to deploy multiple times a day."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Track the modal verbs, because government standards are written in them. "Should deploy frequently" is a recommendation; "many teams deploy multiple times a day" is an observation about practice, not a requirement; the only "must" in the passage attaches to avoiding user downtime. The statement claims a requirement ("are required") for a specific frequency that the passage presents as neither required nor even universally recommended — the passage's own framing shows multiple-daily deployment as something many (not all) teams do. Because the passage's actual requirement structure contradicts the claimed one, the answer is False rather than Cannot Say. The must/should/may hierarchy is load-bearing across everything you read — service standards, security policies, contracts — and across everything you write: a status report that says "teams must adopt the new process" when governance only agreed "should" will start a conflict you then have to mediate. Precision with modality is cheap; repairing its absence is not.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Judging the clearest rewrite**

A delivery manager drafts this for a programme board paper: "It should be noted that, in the event that the aforementioned dependency on the legacy data platform is not resolved in a timely manner by the responsible parties, there exists a possibility that the go-live date may potentially be subject to slippage." As their coach, which rewrite would you commend as clearest while preserving the meaning?

- A) "The go-live date will slip because the legacy data platform dependency is unresolved."
- B) "If the legacy data platform dependency is not resolved by [date], the go-live date is at risk."
- C) "Slippage to the go-live date may potentially occur due to unresolved dependency issues in a legacy context."
- D) "The legacy data platform team must resolve their dependency or they will be responsible for delay."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The original buries one conditional risk statement under six hedges ("should be noted", "in the event that", "aforementioned", "timely manner", "possibility", "may potentially"). B strips the padding and — crucially — sharpens "timely manner" into an explicit date placeholder, converting vague anxiety into a monitorable condition, while preserving the conditional nature of the risk. A changes the meaning: it converts a conditional risk into a settled fact ("will slip"), which the original does not claim. C keeps the fog and merely rearranges it. D converts a risk statement into a blame assignment, which both changes the meaning and poisons the cross-team relationship you would then have to repair. Two lessons for your role: boards act on conditions and dates, not on hedges; and when coaching a delivery manager's writing, the best feedback names the technique ("one hedge maximum; replace 'timely' with a date") so they can apply it without you next time.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Comprehension of a technical explanation for translation**

Passage (from a technical lead's note): "Our current architecture routes all payment confirmations through a single message queue. Under normal load this is fine, but during peak periods the queue depth grows faster than consumers can drain it, adding up to 40 minutes' latency to confirmation emails. The fix — partitioning the queue by region — is straightforward but requires a coordinated change with the notifications team, who own the consumer service."

You must brief a non-technical director in one sentence. Which briefing is accurate?

- A) "Payment confirmations are failing during peak periods and the fix requires a full re-architecture."
- B) "During busy periods, confirmation emails can be delayed by up to 40 minutes; there is a known fix, but it needs joint work between two teams."
- C) "The notifications team's service is broken and they need to fix their consumer."
- D) "There is a minor technical issue with no user impact, being handled within the team."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Test each candidate against the source. A inflates twice: delays are not failures (emails arrive, late), and a "straightforward" partitioning change is not a re-architecture. C misassigns both the problem (the queue design, not the consumer, is the stated cause) and the ownership (the fix is explicitly coordinated, not one team's job). D minimises a real user impact (40-minute delays are user-visible) and hides the cross-team coordination need — the one thing a director might actually help unblock. B preserves every load-bearing fact at the right strength: the trigger condition (busy periods), the user impact (delays up to 40 minutes), the tractability (known fix), and the dependency (joint work). This is the essence of communicating between the technical and the non-technical: translation is compression without distortion, and the facts you keep should be the ones the audience can act on. A director hearing B knows exactly what to ask next week: "has the joint work started?"

**Question 9 (hard) — Cannot Say under diplomatic language**

Passage (from a letter by another department's director general): "We remain committed to the data-sharing partnership and recognise the benefits it will bring to citizens. Our legal team continues to examine the draft agreement, and we anticipate being able to respond formally once that work concludes. We note the programme's proposed go-live date and will keep it in view as our internal processes proceed."

Statement: "The other department has agreed to the proposed go-live date."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Read what the letter actually commits to: continued commitment to the partnership in general, ongoing legal examination, a formal response at an unspecified future point, and — on the date — only that they "note" it and will "keep it in view". Noting a date is neither agreement nor refusal; the letter carefully avoids both. So the statement is not supported (no agreement is expressed) and not contradicted (no objection is expressed either): Cannot Say. The hard part is that experienced readers correctly sense the letter's cool temperature — "keep it in view" is the diplomatic register for "do not assume us into your plan" — and are tempted to answer False. But the test asks what the text establishes, not what its subtext suggests. In the workplace you need both readings simultaneously: log the fact (no commitment given) for the record, and act on the subtext (escalate the dependency risk to the programme director, because a partner who meant yes would have said yes). The letters you exchange at programme level are drafted this carefully on purpose; read them the same way.

**Question 10 (hard) — Evaluating argument strength in a governance debate**

Passage: "At the design authority, the head of security argued: 'Every major incident this year has involved a change that skipped the full assurance path. Therefore the expedited change route should be closed.' The head of delivery responded: 'Ninety-four per cent of expedited changes this year completed without incident, and the expedited route exists precisely because the full path takes eleven days. Closing it would not have prevented the incidents — it would have driven more changes into emergency releases, which have the worst incident rate of all.'"

Which is the most accurate analysis of the exchange?

- A) The head of security's argument is conclusive: the incident correlation proves the expedited route is unsafe.
- B) The head of delivery has shown that the expedited route is safe and should be expanded.
- C) The head of security infers causation from a correlation, without base rates; the head of delivery supplies a base rate and a displacement argument, but neither speaker has yet shown what would actually reduce incidents.
- D) Both arguments are equally weak because both use statistics.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Dissect each argument. The security argument observes that incidents share a feature (skipped full assurance) and concludes the route enabling that feature should close — but without knowing how many changes used the expedited route safely (the base rate), the correlation cannot carry a causal conclusion; by analogy, every incident also involved a change that was deployed, yet nobody proposes closing deployment. The delivery response adds the missing base rate (94% incident-free) and a displacement mechanism (closure pushes changes into worse-governed emergency releases) — genuinely stronger reasoning. But strictly, the delivery head has argued that closure is a bad remedy, not that the current route is optimally safe, and neither speaker has identified what would reduce incidents (for instance, tightening the criteria for expedited eligibility, since 6% of expedited changes apparently do cause incidents). So B overreads and A underreads; D is lazy equivalence. C captures the exact state of the argument. Chairing exactly this kind of exchange — crediting the strongest version of each side, naming the gap, and commissioning the missing analysis — is what moderating difficult discussions about high-risk topics means at programme level.

**Question 11 (hard) — Reading a contract clause against a scenario**

Passage (contract clause): "The Supplier shall provide a monthly service report no later than the fifth working day of the following month. Where the Buyer identifies a material error in a service report, the Buyer may reject the report by written notice within ten working days of receipt. A rejected report shall be corrected and resubmitted within five working days of the notice. Service credits shall be calculated from accepted reports only."

Scenario: The March report arrived on the third working day of April. On the twelfth working day after receipt, your team found a material error understating downtime. Which statement about the situation is accurate?

- A) The supplier breached the contract by submitting the report late.
- B) The rejection window has closed, and service credits for March will be calculated from a report you believe is materially wrong, unless the supplier agrees to correct it voluntarily.
- C) You may still reject the report, because material errors extend the rejection window.
- D) Service credits for March are automatically forfeited.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Test each option against the clause text. A fails: the report arrived on the third working day, within the "no later than the fifth" requirement — no lateness breach. C invents an extension mechanism; the clause grants ten working days from receipt with no exception for late-discovered errors, and you discovered the error on day twelve. D invents a forfeiture; the clause says credits are calculated from accepted reports, and this report — unrejected within the window — stands as accepted, so credits will be calculated, just from flawed data. B states exactly the uncomfortable position: the contractual remedy has lapsed, leaving only negotiation. The two professional lessons are precisely why this question exists. First, contract deadlines are operational deadlines: "review the supplier report" needs to sit in someone's calendar inside the ten-day window, every month — a process you would now institute. Second, when the contractual position is against you, the escalation conversation changes register: you are asking the supplier to correct the record as a matter of good faith and relationship, while noting the error formally so a pattern is documented. Knowing which register you are in — entitlement or negotiation — before you open the conversation is a hallmark of senior commercial management.

**Question 12 (hard) — Synthesising conflicting written accounts**

Passage: Three documents about the same slipped milestone. (1) The delivery team's retrospective: "The milestone slipped five days because the test environment was unavailable for a week; we flagged this to the environments team twice before the outage." (2) The environments team's report: "Environment maintenance was published in the annual schedule in January; teams were asked to plan around the March window. We received no change requests from the programme." (3) The PMO log: "Two emails from the delivery team to the environments shared mailbox, dated 3 and 10 March, requesting the maintenance window be moved; no reply recorded. The maintenance window appears in the January schedule."

Which conclusion do the three documents, taken together, best support?

- A) The delivery team failed to plan around a schedule published two months in advance, and its account is false.
- B) The environments team ignored two direct requests, and its account is false.
- C) Both accounts are largely accurate from their own vantage points: the window was published in January, the delivery team did send two requests, and a channel failure (an unmonitored mailbox or unlogged requests) let each team believe the other was informed; the process gap, not either account's honesty, is the finding.
- D) The PMO log should be disregarded because it conflicts with both teams.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Lay the claims side by side and look for the reconciliation before choosing a victim. The PMO log corroborates part of each account: the January schedule existed (supporting the environments team) and two requests were sent (supporting the delivery team). The apparent contradiction — "we received no change requests" versus two dated emails — dissolves if the shared mailbox was unmonitored or its contents never reached the report's author: "received" is doing ambiguous work, and neither team need be lying. A and B each accept one account wholesale and convict the other on evidence that actually part-vindicates both; D discards the only independent source, which is backwards — the PMO log is the document that reconciles the other two. This synthesis pattern is the verbal core of your job as escalation point: escalations arrive as competing narratives, each internally coherent, and the senior move is to find the reading that explains all the evidence, then fix the mechanism (a monitored request channel with acknowledgement) rather than adjudicate the blame. Note also the retrospective phrase "we flagged this twice" — true, and yet insufficient, because a flag no one acknowledges is not a managed dependency. That distinction belongs in your coaching conversation with the delivery manager, delivered with the same care the question demands.

### Preparation tips

- **Practise the passage-only discipline daily.** Take one paragraph from a real assurance report or policy page, write one True, one False and one Cannot Say statement about it, and check yourself. Ten minutes of this a day for two weeks builds the core habit the test rewards.
- **Highlight modality as you read.** For a week, mentally flag every must/should/may/anticipate/note in your inbox. The test's hardest items turn on these words — and so do your contracts and standards.
- **Read the statement before re-reading the passage.** In True/False/Cannot Say items, read the passage once for structure, then read each statement and hunt for its specific evidence. This is faster and more accurate than holding the whole passage in memory.
- **Rehearse compression without distortion.** Take a technical note from one of your teams and write a one-sentence director briefing; then check every load-bearing fact survived at the right strength. This is simultaneously test preparation and your actual job.
- **Calibrate your inference reflex.** When you catch yourself concluding something from a document, ask "which sentence supports that?" If the answer is "none, but it's obvious", you have found exactly the reflex the test penalises — and the one that misbriefs directors.
- **Time-box and return.** Verbal items reward a steady 45-75 seconds each; if a passage resists, flag it and return. Comprehension degrades sharply when you force it under frustration.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Importing professional knowledge.** You know how compensation events and service assessments really work; the passage may not say. Answer from the text alone — the test is measuring precisely your ability to separate the two.
- **Mistaking subtext for text.** Diplomatic letters and board minutes carry strong subtext. Cannot Say questions punish readers who report the subtext as fact — even when the subtext is real and important.
- **Matching keywords instead of meaning.** Wrong options often reuse the passage's exact words in a distorted claim ("provisional" becoming "cancelled"). Verify the claim's logic, not its vocabulary.
- **Absolutism asymmetry.** Statements with "never/always/all" need total support to be true and only one exception to be false. Statements with "may/some" are much easier to support. Match strength to strength.
- **Letting the strongest personality win the argument analysis.** In argument-evaluation items, assess the reasoning, not the role or confidence of the speaker. Heads of security and heads of delivery can both commit fallacies.
- **Rushing the final synthesis items.** Multi-document questions sit late in tests when you are tired; they carry the same marks. Budget time so the last three questions get your full reading, not your leftovers.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement test (SJT) presents realistic workplace dilemmas and asks you to judge the effectiveness of possible responses. Formats vary: you may be asked to pick the most effective response, to pick both the most and the least effective, to rank all options, or to rate each option independently on an effectiveness scale. There are rarely absolute right answers in the way a numeric item has one; instead, scoring reflects the judgement of experienced practitioners and the employer's professional behaviour framework — for GDAD roles, behaviours such as user focus, collaboration, ownership, transparency, proportionate escalation, and inclusion.

The typical format is 12 to 25 scenarios in 25 to 40 minutes, usually untimed per item but timed overall. Unlike the other assessments, an SJT measures judgement and values in action: what you believe good looks like when goods conflict — pace versus quality, candour versus diplomacy, team autonomy versus programme consistency.

For a programme delivery manager, the SJT is arguably the most role-relevant assessment of the four, because your work is a continuous stream of exactly these dilemmas. You are the initial escalation point, so ambiguous problems arrive with your name on them. You balance objectives and redeploy people as priorities change, which means disappointing someone weekly and doing it well. You moderate difficult discussions about high-risk topics, coach delivery managers whose mistakes you must address without deflating, resolve issues between departments where no one owns the problem, and support a programme director whose vision you must sometimes challenge in private while representing it faithfully in public. Employers use SJTs at this level because technical delivery skill without sound judgement is precisely what causes senior hires to fail — and because the scenarios reveal how you think about people, power and candour before an employer entrusts you with all three.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Escalation and ownership scenarios** map to being **the initial escalation point for the programme** and to **Maintaining delivery momentum**: knowing what to absorb, what to fix, what to escalate upward, and how to act when ownership is unclear — including addressing risks whose owners sit outside your teams entirely.
- **Stakeholder and communication scenarios** map to **Communicating between the technical and non-technical** and your duty to **understand, manage and communicate to complex stakeholder groups**: delivering unwelcome messages early, moderating conflict between senior people, and keeping trust while saying no.
- **Coaching and people scenarios** map to **supporting and coaching delivery managers** and **Community collaboration**: developing people through their mistakes, building community across teams, and handling team dynamics and conflict with fairness and inclusion.
- **Commercial and financial scenarios** map to **Commercial management** and **Financial management**: acting as the escalation point for large or high-risk commercial issues, and making honest budget choices under pressure.
- **Process and methodology scenarios** map to **Agile and Lean practices** and **Making a process work**: challenging unnecessarily complicated processes without vandalising necessary governance, and tailoring approaches rather than imposing dogma.
- **Priority and integrity scenarios** map to **balancing objectives and redeploying people and resources as priorities change**, and to supporting **the programme director's vision** — including the moments when supporting the vision means privately challenging it.

In each explanation below, the reasoning references these professional behaviours explicitly, because SJT preparation is less about learning answers and more about internalising the value hierarchy behind them: users and delivery outcomes first, transparency over comfort, fix the system before blaming the person, escalate facts early and solutions alongside them.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — A delivery manager brings you a hidden slip**

A delivery manager tells you privately: "My team will miss the milestone by two weeks, but I haven't put it in the report yet — I think we can catch up, and I don't want the board asking questions." The board pack is due tomorrow. What is the most effective response?

- A) Agree to keep it out of the report this once, since the delivery manager knows their team best.
- B) Insist the slip and the recovery plan both appear in tomorrow's report, and offer to work through the recovery plan together today so the report shows the problem and the response.
- C) Report the slip yourself directly to the board and let the delivery manager explain themselves at the meeting.
- D) Tell the delivery manager to move people from another team to catch up before anyone notices.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** B pairs the two behaviours this scenario is testing: transparency (delivery data in reports must be true — once a programme's reporting is known to be curated, every green becomes suspect) and coaching (helping build the recovery plan turns a concealment instinct into a learning moment, and a report that shows problem-plus-response builds board confidence rather than inviting panic). A makes you complicit in misreporting and teaches your delivery managers that reports are negotiable. C fixes the report but destroys the relationship: the delivery manager came to you voluntarily, and ambushing them at board level guarantees you hear about the next slip only when it is undeniable — the exact opposite of the trust-based early warning a programme runs on. D compounds concealment with an unexamined resource decision made for optics rather than delivery. The senior principle: protect the truth-telling channel above the comfort of any single report, and make honesty the easier path by meeting it with help rather than punishment.

**Question 2 (easy) — Credit in front of the programme director**

At a programme showcase, the programme director praises "your turnaround of the payments workstream". In fact, the turnaround plan was designed and driven by one of your delivery managers, who is in the room. What is the most effective response?

- A) Accept the praise gracefully and thank the delivery manager privately afterwards.
- B) Redirect the credit immediately and specifically: name the delivery manager as the person who designed and drove the plan, and let them speak to it.
- C) Say "it was a team effort" and move the agenda on.
- D) Accept the praise, since as programme delivery manager you are accountable for the workstream's results.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** B is a small act with large systemic effects. Publicly and specifically crediting the delivery manager — in front of the programme director — develops their visibility and career (coaching is not only feedback; it is sponsorship), signals to every delivery manager watching that good work under you is seen and named, and costs you nothing real, since accountability and credit are different currencies: you remain accountable for the workstream whether or not you take the applause. A captures the benefit for yourself and pays the delivery manager in private currency worth far less. C is better than A but launders a specific person's achievement into an anonymous collective, which is a common way organisations quietly under-recognise people — an inclusion behaviour worth noticing. D confuses accountability with credit exactly backwards: senior people absorb blame downward-facing and pass credit downward too. The habit of specific public credit is one of the cheapest culture-building tools you have as a community leader.

**Question 3 (easy) — A process everyone tolerates**

You discover that every release requires a form to be completed for an "architecture review board" that was disbanded eighteen months ago; a governance administrator files the forms, and nobody reads them. Teams spend perhaps an hour per release on it. What is the most effective first action?

- A) Tell teams to stop completing the form immediately — dead process is waste.
- B) Check briefly who, if anyone, still relies on the form or its data (audit, security, the administrator's records), and if genuinely orphaned, get its retirement confirmed with the governance lead and announce the change to all teams.
- C) Raise a paper for the next quarterly governance review proposing a working group on process simplification.
- D) Leave it — an hour per release is minor, and challenging governance processes creates friction.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This is Chesterton's fence applied to governance: the form is probably waste, but "probably" is not "certainly", and dead-looking processes sometimes feed audit trails or regulatory records invisibly. B does the proportionate check (a day's asking, not a quarter's committee), then kills the process properly — with the governance lead's confirmation so the retirement is legitimate, and a broadcast so every team benefits simultaneously and knows why. That last step matters: identifying and challenging unnecessarily complicated processes is a named part of your role, and doing it visibly coaches the whole organisation to inspect and adapt its own processes. A risks breaking an invisible dependency and, worse, models "seniors can skip governance unilaterally", which your delivery managers will imitate on processes that are actually alive. C is proportionality inverted — a working group to remove one form — and delays a free win by months. D tolerates compounding waste (an hour per release across every team, forever) and abandons a core duty of the role. The pattern: verify cheaply, retire decisively, communicate widely.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Two directors, one team**

Two directors each believe their priority has claim over the same specialist data engineering team for the next quarter. Each has emailed you separately implying the matter is settled in their favour. The team's delivery manager is caught in the middle and morale is dropping. What is the most effective response?

- A) Allocate the team to the priority you judge more valuable and inform both directors of your decision.
- B) Split the team 50/50 between both priorities so neither director loses face.
- C) Prepare a short options paper with the delivery impact of each allocation (including a split), get the two directors and the programme director into one conversation, and drive it to an explicit, recorded decision — then communicate it to the team the same day.
- D) Ask the team's delivery manager to balance the two demands as best they can while you monitor the situation.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The real problem is an unmade decision living rent-free in a team's morale, and the test is whether you resolve conflicts at the right level with the right machinery. C does four senior things at once: it converts assertions into comparable evidence (an options paper with delivery impact — including honestly costing the split option, which usually shows 50/50 delivering neither priority well); it forces the conflict into one room, because contradictory bilateral promises persist precisely while the parties never meet; it involves the programme director appropriately, since prioritisation between directors' objectives is a call at or above your level to ratify even when you broker it; and it closes the loop with the team same-day, because the people most damaged by the ambiguity should be first to hear its resolution. A oversteps: deciding between directors' priorities unilaterally spends authority you may not have and guarantees one director works against the decision. B is the classic anti-pattern C's paper would expose — splitting a specialist team usually halves throughput on both fronts. D delegates the conflict downward to the person with the least power to resolve it, which is the opposite of removing blockers. Behaviours in play: proportionate escalation, evidence over advocacy, and protecting teams from ambient political noise.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Supplier performance and a friendly relationship**

A supplier's delivery has degraded for three consecutive months: missed sprints, rising defects, key people quietly rotated off the account. The supplier's account director — with whom you have an excellent relationship — asks you to "keep this between us while we fix it; formal escalation would trigger our internal red-account process and make everything slower." Your contract requires performance issues to be recorded in the monthly service review. What is the most effective response?

- A) Agree to keep it informal for one more month in exchange for a written recovery plan.
- B) Record the performance facts in the service review as the contract requires, tell the account director you are doing so and why, and offer to work the recovery plan jointly — being clear that the relationship and the record are separate tracks.
- C) Escalate immediately to contract termination discussions to signal seriousness.
- D) Record only the least serious issues to protect the relationship while creating some paper trail.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The scenario tests whether you can hold warmth and governance simultaneously — and whether you recognise the request for what it is. Suppressing contractually required performance records is not a favour between professionals; it is the first step of every commercial dispute that later founders on "but the service reviews were clean". B keeps the record true (protecting your organisation's remedies — service credits, and the evidentiary basis for any future dispute all flow from the recorded history), does it without ambush (telling the account director openly preserves the relationship's honesty), and stays collaborative on the fix (a joint recovery plan is genuinely better than an adversarial one). Note that the supplier's own red-account process existing is a reason to record, not to hide: it is the supplier's mechanism for adding senior attention to a failing account. A trades your organisation's contractual position for an unenforceable promise and makes you personally the reason the record has a hole in it. C is disproportionate — termination talk before recovery attempts torches value and relationships governance exists to protect. D is the worst of both: a falsified record that fails as evidence and as candour. Senior commercial behaviour: the record is the record, the relationship is the relationship, and people who conflate them end up with neither.

**Question 6 (moderate) — A blocked programme dependency nobody owns**

Your programme depends on a reference-data service owned by a central team outside the programme. That team has deprioritised the API change you need; their head of unit says "it's not in our quarter's objectives." Your go-live in eleven weeks depends on it, and two of your teams will hit the blocker in about four weeks. What is the most effective response?

- A) Instruct one of your teams to build a workaround now, so the dependency disappears.
- B) Escalate the conflict to the programme director immediately and let seniors resolve it.
- C) Work the problem at your level first: quantify the impact with dates, explore with the central team's head what would make the change feasible (a smaller change, your engineers contributing under their review, a later integration date), and set a named date on which you will escalate jointly with facts if no path exists.
- D) Raise the risk in the RAID log with a high severity rating and review it weekly.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** This is the signature programme-delivery-manager scenario — actively addressing risks and dependencies where ownership exists outside your teams — and the options map the maturity curve. D is necessary but nowhere near sufficient: a RAID log entry is a memory, not an action, and "review weekly" watches the four-week fuse burn. B escalates prematurely: you spend the programme director's political capital before establishing that the problem cannot be solved at your level, and you arrive without the facts that make senior intervention effective. A may eventually be right, but as a first move it spends your own teams' capacity on duplicating central infrastructure — creating permanent maintenance cost — before cheaper options (a smaller change, loaned engineers, resequencing) are explored; it also teaches central teams that ignoring programmes is consequence-free. C does the senior sequence: quantify (heads of unit respond to "two teams blocked on this date, go-live at risk on that date" very differently than to "we need this"), negotiate creatively (contributing your engineers under their code review is the classic unlock — it converts their capacity problem into a review task), and pre-commit to a joint escalation date, which keeps escalation proportionate, predictable and non-hostile. The behaviours: ownership of the outcome, collaboration before escalation, escalation with facts and a proposed solution rather than a complaint — and always with a date, so working-at-your-level cannot quietly become waiting-at-your-level.

**Question 7 (moderate) — The struggling delivery manager**

One of your delivery managers is visibly struggling: their reports are late and thin, two sprint reviews were cancelled, and their team's flow metrics are deteriorating. A well-meaning peer suggests you "quietly take over the team's planning for a few weeks to steady things". What is the most effective response?

- A) Take over the planning temporarily as suggested — delivery comes first, development later.
- B) Have a direct, private conversation with the delivery manager: share the specific observations without accusation, ask what is going on, and agree a support plan — which might include pairing on planning, temporarily reducing their load, or addressing anything personal or systemic underneath.
- C) Raise the performance concerns with HR to start a formal process, since the pattern is well evidenced.
- D) Wait a month to gather more metrics before intervening, to be fair to them.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The evidence pattern (lateness, cancellations, deteriorating metrics) says something is wrong; it does not say what — overload, a team conflict they are absorbing, a skills gap, a health or caring situation, or a systemic problem affecting their team that they have not felt safe to raise. B is the only option that finds out. Its architecture matters: specific observations ("two cancelled reviews") not judgements ("you're struggling"), genuine inquiry before solutions, and a support plan that treats coaching as the first tool — pairing on planning develops them, whereas A's takeover, however kindly meant and "quiet", develops nothing, humiliates them in front of their team, and leaves you doing two jobs; note that quiet takeovers are never quiet. C reaches for formal process before a single conversation has established what the problem is — disproportionate, trust-destroying, and often unfair, since half these patterns have causes a support conversation resolves in weeks. D confuses fairness with delay: the metrics are already clear enough to warrant care, and a month's wait means a month of the team deteriorating and the delivery manager feeling secretly watched. Supporting and coaching delivery managers means being the person who notices early and asks well — inclusion here means remembering that struggle is usually situational, and that the goal is a recovered leader, not a documented one.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Agile dogma versus delivery reality**

A newly arrived delivery manager, strongly certified in one agile framework, insists their team abandon its current kanban-style flow (which has excellent, stable metrics) and adopt the full ceremony set of their preferred framework, "because that's proper agile". The team is resistant and has asked you to intervene. What is the most effective response?

- A) Back the delivery manager publicly — undermining a new leader's first initiative would damage their authority.
- B) Instruct the team to keep kanban since its metrics are good, and tell the delivery manager to stand down.
- C) Coach the delivery manager privately: anchor on the principle that methods serve delivery outcomes, review the team's actual flow data together, and steer them towards evolving the team's approach through experiments the team co-designs — while making clear that imposing ceremonies on a well-functioning team is not what expertise in agile looks like.
- D) Have the team vote on which methodology to use.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** As a recognised expert and advocate for agile and lean approaches, your authority in this scenario comes from holding the principle above any framework: create and tailor the right approach for a team, evaluating and iterating it — which means a team with excellent, stable flow has earned evidence-based deference, and change should come as co-designed experiments, not imposed ceremony. C coaches rather than overrules: reviewing the team's data with the delivery manager respects their professional standing while re-anchoring them on outcomes over orthodoxy, and it converts a brewing authority conflict into a development conversation — exactly the "challenge, evaluate, iterate" behaviour you are meant to model. It also protects their future authority better than A, because backing a mistake publicly means a bigger, more public failure later. B wins the argument but loses the leader: overruling a new delivery manager in their first month, at their team's request, teaches the team to route around them permanently. D abdicates: methodology choice is not majoritarian; it is an evidence-and-outcomes question the delivery manager should facilitate, and a vote entrenches the two camps you are trying to dissolve. The wider culture point: how you handle the first dogmatist shapes whether your programme's community treats methods as tools or as tribes.

**Question 9 (hard) — The programme director's optimistic reporting**

You support a programme director you respect. Reviewing the draft quarterly report to the investment board, you find the delivery confidence stated as "amber, improving" — but your own analysis of dependency and burn data says "amber-red, deteriorating", and two delivery managers privately agree with you. The programme director wrote the assessment personally and the report goes out tomorrow. What is the most effective response?

- A) Say nothing — the confidence assessment is the programme director's call, and your job is to support their vision.
- B) Raise your analysis with the programme director privately today: present the data, state plainly that you believe the report overstates confidence, recommend the wording change, and if they still disagree, ask that the report at least include the specific risks and data so the board sees the evidence base.
- C) Contact the investment board secretariat directly to flag your concerns before the report lands.
- D) Persuade the two delivery managers to raise it jointly with you in tomorrow's programme board, so the challenge has weight.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Supporting a programme director's vision has never meant supporting their errors into the record — the most valuable thing a senior deputy provides is private, evidenced challenge before public commitment. B has the right anatomy: today (challenge before the deadline is help; after it is blame), private (public challenge would force the director to defend rather than think), data-led (a confidence rating is a judgement, so you contest it with the dependency and burn evidence, not with counter-assertion), and with a fallback that preserves both integrity and their authority — if they hold their view, putting the underlying data in the report lets the board apply its own judgement, which converts a disagreement about adjectives into shared transparency about facts. That fallback matters: the director may also hold context you lack, and B is the only option that lets both analyses meet. A is the courtier's failure mode — loyalty performed by silence — and if the programme later slips, the record shows you knew. C is a serious breach of trust and hierarchy while a direct route remains untried; it would end the relationship's candour permanently and is not proportionate escalation, which goes around a person only after going to them has failed on something material. D turns private doubt into an orchestrated ambush, spending your delivery managers' safety on a confrontation you should have had one-to-one. Behaviours: candour upward, evidence over deference, escalate through people before around them.

**Question 10 (hard) — Redeployment with a human cost**

A strategic priority change means you must move four engineers from Team Echo — which has spent a year becoming your highest-performing team — to accelerate a politically urgent workstream. Echo's delivery manager tells you the move will "destroy the best team culture in the programme" and that one of the four, a specialist, has told them they may resign rather than move. The redeployment decision is confirmed and within your authority; how you implement it is yours. What is the most effective approach?

- A) Delay implementation quietly for a month, hoping the political urgency subsides.
- B) Implement immediately by emailing the four engineers their new assignments, since prolonging uncertainty is worse than bad news.
- C) Implement without the specialist — swap in a different engineer — to remove the resignation risk.
- D) Move fast but through people: brief Echo's delivery manager fully on the why, speak with the four engineers directly (the specialist first, exploring what would make the move workable for them), agree with both teams how knowledge transfer and Echo's rebuild will work, and set a review point to check both teams' health.
- E) Escalate back to the programme director asking for the decision to be reversed given the resignation risk.

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** The decision is made and within authority; the test is implementation judgement — balancing objectives and redeploying people as priorities change is your named duty, and the phrase hides its hardest part, which is doing it without breaking what you built. D moves at the required pace while treating every human in the chain properly: the delivery manager hears the strategic why first (they must carry the message credibly, and learning of it second-hand would undercut them exactly as in Question 8's authority dynamics); the four engineers hear it from you in person, because reassignment-by-email converts a career event into a notification and is where resignations are manufactured; the specialist conversation happens first and as an inquiry — "what would make this workable" surfaces the actual objection (commute? technology? team bonds? career direction?), which is often addressable once known; and the review point plus rebuild plan treats Echo's culture as an asset being consciously risked, not collateral damage. A is a quiet insubordination that spends your credibility when discovered and wastes the urgency the redeployment exists to serve. B is right about uncertainty and wrong about medium — speed and humanity are not alternatives. C optimises around one risk by damaging the mission (the specialist is presumably needed precisely for the urgent work) and teaches everyone that resignation threats steer assignments. E re-litigates a confirmed decision on first contact with difficulty, which is sometimes right — but not before D's conversations have tested whether the difficulty is real; if the specialist conversation genuinely fails, escalating that fact then is proportionate. Behaviours: ownership of hard messages, respect for the management chain, inclusion (people affected hear first and in person), and protecting long-term team health while delivering the change.

**Question 11 (hard) — Budget pressure and a quality corner**

Finance requires a 10% in-year reduction to your programme budget. After honest options analysis, the least-damaging package includes pausing one low-priority workstream and slowing another. A senior stakeholder proposes an alternative: "Keep everything running — just cut the automated testing and assurance activity across all teams; it's 12% of spend and users never see it." Some of your delivery managers, anxious about their workstreams, are sympathetic to the idea. What is the most effective response?

- A) Adopt the stakeholder's proposal — it keeps all commitments visible to users and stakeholders intact.
- B) Refuse in the meeting on principle: quality is non-negotiable and the proposal shows the stakeholder doesn't understand delivery.
- C) Bring evidence to the decision: show what testing and assurance spend actually buys (defect escape rates, incident costs, rework time — and what happened historically when it was cut), recommend the pause-and-slow package with the reasoning visible, and make the quality-cut option's real cost explicit in the options paper so the trade-off is decided honestly at the right level.
- D) Accept a compromise of cutting half the testing spend, since programme management is the art of the possible.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The stakeholder's proposal contains a real observation (assurance spend is invisible to users in the short term) attached to a false conclusion (therefore cutting it is free). The senior move is not to assert the conclusion is false but to show it: testing and assurance spend converts directly into defect escape rates, incident response costs, and rework — a currency conversion you can usually evidence from your own programme's history, and once "saves 12% now" is placed next to "cost 30% in rework and incidents when tried in year one", the option tends to retire itself. C also gets the decision made at the right level with honest inputs: your job is to make the options paper truthful, including the tempting option's real price, not to pre-censor options you dislike — that is what influencing complex budgets across a programme view means, and it is also what managing the budget you are given and making it work looks like when the budget shrinks. A buys short-term stakeholder comfort with compounding delivery debt and an incident that will eventually be very visible to users indeed. B may be substantively right but is relationally ruinous and evidence-free — "non-negotiable" without data reads as professional territorialism, and it humiliates a senior stakeholder in public, violating the same moderation-of-difficult-discussions duty you are being assessed on. D is compromise as reflex: half a bad idea is a smaller bad idea, adopted without the analysis that C would produce — and note that your anxious delivery managers are watching whether evidence or anxiety steers programme decisions. Behaviours: evidence over assertion, transparency in options, protecting long-term delivery health, and disagreeing with seniors without disrespect.

**Question 12 (hard) — An inclusion problem inside a high-performing team**

Your best-performing team's retrospectives have gone quiet. A junior engineer from the team asks to speak with you confidentially and describes a pattern: the team's two most senior engineers dismiss ideas from newer members, interrupt them in ceremonies, and route real decisions through private conversations between themselves. The team's delivery manager "doesn't seem to see it". The junior engineer is visibly anxious and asks you not to make it obvious they raised anything. What is the most effective response?

- A) Thank them, but explain that team dynamics are the delivery manager's responsibility and they should raise it there first.
- B) Attend the team's next several ceremonies yourself to observe directly, then act on what you see — telling no one why you are there.
- C) Protect the confidence while acting on the pattern: coach the team's delivery manager onto the general territory (facilitation balance, decision transparency, retro health — observable things they can look for themselves), create structural changes that help regardless of individuals (round-robin facilitation, written decision logs, retro formats that equalise voice), and check back with the junior engineer; treat it as a formal matter only if the pattern persists or worsens.
- D) Raise the specific behaviours with the two senior engineers directly, without naming sources, so the problem is addressed at its origin.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Two duties are in tension: acting on a real inclusion and team-health problem (quiet retrospectives in a high-performing team are a leading indicator — "high-performing" metrics can coast for months on accumulated trust while the engine that built them corrodes), and protecting a junior person who took a career risk by telling you. C threads it deliberately. Coaching the delivery manager onto observable territory — "how balanced is airtime in your ceremonies? where do decisions actually get made?" — equips them to discover the pattern themselves, which fixes the "doesn't seem to see it" problem at its root and keeps the intervention inside the management chain without exposing the source. The structural moves (rotating facilitation, decision logs, equalising retro formats) improve voice and transparency for everyone whether or not any individual is "guilty", which is both good design and good cover: they cannot be traced to a complaint. The check-back honours the person who trusted you and tests whether the pattern is actually improving — and C's final clause keeps the escalation path alive rather than promising confidentiality beyond what a worsening situation could honour. A is technically correct about ownership and practically a door closed in an anxious junior colleague's face; they came to you because the normal channel is the problem's blind spot. B gathers first-hand evidence but seniors appearing unannounced in ceremonies changes the behaviour being observed, and several sessions of silent observation delays action while the source wonders what happened. D acts fastest but "without naming sources" barely protects a junior engineer in a team small enough that the seniors can infer who spoke — and it treats as an individual disciplinary matter what may be fixable as culture and structure. Behaviours: inclusion as a delivery concern, protecting psychological safety, coaching over commanding, and fixing systems before prosecuting people.

### Preparation tips

- **Study your organisation's behaviour framework first.** SJTs are scored against a value hierarchy. For GDAD roles, re-read the relevant Civil Service behaviours and your framework's leadership expectations; the "most effective" option is the one those documents describe.
- **Practise separating decision from implementation.** Many senior scenarios (like the redeployment question) are not about what to do but how. When two options share a conclusion, compare their treatment of people, sequence and communication.
- **Rehearse the escalation ladder.** Work at your level with facts and creativity first; escalate on a pre-declared date with evidence and a proposed solution; go around a person only after going to them has failed. Most SJT distractors are rungs skipped in one direction or the other.
- **Notice the "record versus relationship" pattern.** Several commercial and reporting dilemmas turn on keeping records truthful while keeping relationships warm. Practise phrasing that does both, because you will need it verbatim in real service reviews.
- **Debrief real dilemmas weekly.** After each week, pick one live judgement call you made and ask which SJT option you actually chose — including the times you chose B-quality actions with C-quality timing. Honest self-review is the highest-yield preparation.
- **Answer as the professional you are, consistently.** Do not try to guess a "test personality". Modern SJTs include consistency signals, and the role you are practising for is one you already hold — your calibrated professional judgement is the asset being measured.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Choosing decisive-looking over effective.** Unilateral action (taking over the planning, overruling the new delivery manager, building the workaround) reads as leadership but scores as skipped collaboration. Effectiveness usually routes through other people.
- **Confusing loyalty with silence.** Options that "support" a senior person by not challenging them are consistently scored low. Candour delivered privately, early and with evidence is the loyal behaviour.
- **Escalating too early or too late.** Immediate escalation spends capital and skips your level's job; pure risk-logging watches the fuse burn. The scored pattern is almost always: work it at your level, with a declared date and facts ready for escalation.
- **Protecting people from information.** Withholding slips from boards, keeping supplier failures off the record, delaying redeployment news — the kindly motivated concealments are the most heavily penalised options in senior SJTs.
- **Solving the stated problem and stepping on the systemic one.** Watch for the second layer: the ambush that fixes a report but kills a truth-telling channel, the swap that removes a resignation risk but rewards threats. Effective options fix the instance without damaging the system.
- **Forgetting the people who are watching.** At your level every choice is also a broadcast: delivery managers learn from how you treat the struggling one, the honest one, and the junior engineer who spoke up. When torn between options, ask which one teaches the programme the lesson you want taught.

You lead complex delivery across many teams, through risk, ambiguity and competing priorities — and you have just spent several thousand words demonstrating to yourself that you can reason carefully under exactly those conditions. Keep practising, keep coaching, and good luck.
