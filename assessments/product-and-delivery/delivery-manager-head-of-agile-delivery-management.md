## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures reasoning with the actual material of the role. At head-of-profession level, the material is organisational: community capability data, workforce structures, cross-departmental dependency systems, governance architectures, and strategy documents. The cognitive operations remain pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving — but the objects they operate on are systems of teams and organisations rather than teams themselves.

Format expectations are familiar: online, timed (15 to 30 minutes), objectively scored against a norm group of comparable seniority, frequently adaptive, with accuracy and speed profiled separately. At this level the discriminating items are engineered around organisational reasoning traps: aggregation effects that reverse at different levels of analysis, structural explanations masquerading as individual ones, plausible strategies that fail a single quantitative check, and rules whose interactions produce conclusions no single rule states.

Employers use cognitive assessment at head-of level because the role's distinctive value is seeing what position-holders inside the system cannot. A head of delivery management looks at twelve teams' data and sees the organisational design flaw producing all twelve patterns; looks at a failing recruitment round and sees the funnel stage that is actually broken; looks at a cross-government proposal and sees the constraint interaction that makes it undeliverable as drafted. That altitude of reasoning — abstract enough to see structure, concrete enough to check arithmetic — is precisely what these assessments sample.

A note on stance as you practise: at your level the assessment also measures restraint — knowing what the data cannot support is tested as rigorously as knowing what it can, because your conclusions are repeated across departments with your authority attached.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Pattern recognition** maps to your accountability to lead the community of practice and build capability and excellence: seeing systemic patterns across many teams' data — capability gaps, recurring failure modes, structural bottlenecks — is how a head of role knows what the community needs before individual teams do.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Team dynamics and collaboration** at its head-of grade ("lead on strategy for an entire organisation, joining up business needs with innovative analysis") and to **Planning** ("lead a continual planning process in a very complex environment"): strategy is sustained inference under organisational constraints.
- **Error checking** maps to **Financial management** and **Commercial management** at escalation grade: business cases, workforce plans, and large commercial positions reach you for exactly one reason — to be checked by the person whose sign-off carries the profession's credibility.
- **Prioritisation** maps to your accountability for making and justifying decisions characterised by high levels of risk, impact and complexity: at head-of level, prioritisation is the allocation of your finite attention and authority across an entire organisation's demands.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Making a process work** ("identify and challenge organisational processes of increasing complexity") and to changing organisational structures to fixable and sustainable designs: the problems that reach you are ones where the system, not any actor within it, is the defect.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern recognition in community capability data**

Your annual capability review covers 24 delivery managers across four directorates. The proportion assessed as "working at or above level" by directorate: A: 9 of 10; B: 5 of 6; C: 2 of 5; D: 2 of 3. Directorate C's programmes are also the department's most legacy-constrained, and C is the only directorate whose delivery managers have no senior delivery manager above them. What is the soundest first reading?

- A) Directorate C recruits weaker delivery managers; prioritise replacing them.
- B) C's out-of-line result (40% versus 80–90% elsewhere) coincides with two structural differences — hardest context and no senior support layer — so investigate structure before capability; the data cannot yet distinguish "weaker people" from "harder context, less support."
- C) The variation is random noise across small samples; do nothing.
- D) Directorates A and B are marking too generously.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The pattern is real — 40% against a cluster at 80–90% is not subtle — but the question is what it is a pattern of, and the stem supplies two confounds that any causal reading must pass through: C holds the hardest delivery context (capability assessments are always partly context assessments — the same person looks weaker against legacy constraints) and C uniquely lacks the senior layer that elsewhere provides coaching, escalation cover, and calibration. Option B reads the evidence at exactly its strength: the signal warrants investigation, the confounds forbid attribution, and the structural hypotheses are testable (compare individuals who moved between directorates; examine what the missing senior layer does elsewhere). Option A fires the people the system may be failing — and at your level that error replicates, because your diagnosis becomes the workforce plan. Option C hides behind sample size selectively; 2 of 5 versus 16 of 19 elsewhere is beyond casual noise even if formal significance is arguable. Option D invents a marking hypothesis with no supporting feature in the stem — note that it, like A, conveniently locates the problem in people rather than in the structure you own. Building capability and excellence across a community begins with this discipline: structure first, people second — because you are the only person positioned to fix the structure.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction from an organisational design rule**

Your target operating model states: (i) every multidisciplinary product team must include a dedicated delivery manager; (ii) a senior delivery manager may cover at most two teams, and only where both teams are in the same service area; (iii) heads of role do not count as team delivery managers. The Casework service area currently runs five product teams, staffed by three dedicated delivery managers and one senior delivery manager covering two of the teams. A reorganisation will split one covered team into two teams in different service areas. After the split, what is the minimum additional delivery management resource required to satisfy the model?

- A) None; the senior delivery manager can stretch across three teams temporarily.
- B) One delivery manager: the split team's new out-of-area half needs its own cover, which the senior delivery manager cannot provide across service areas.
- C) Two delivery managers, one per post-split team.
- D) The model must be suspended during reorganisation.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Track the coverage arithmetic through the change. Before: five teams; three have dedicated DMs, two are covered by one SDM — legitimate under rule (ii) since both are in Casework. The split converts one covered team into two teams, one of which now sits in a different service area. The SDM's coverage of that team fails rule (ii)'s same-service-area condition — so the out-of-area team needs new provision: one delivery manager (rule (i)). The Casework-resident half of the split can remain under the SDM (still two Casework teams: the untouched covered team plus the resident half — check: yes, two teams, same area, permitted). So the minimum addition is exactly one DM — option B. Option A violates two rules at once and dresses it as pragmatism — "temporarily" is how operating models die. Option C overstates: one half remains legitimately covered. Option D treats a solvable staffing consequence as a derogation trigger. This is the daily reasoning of being responsible for the recruitment of the right people to the right teams: organisational changes arrive as org-chart diagrams, and the head of role is the person who runs the coverage rules against them before the gap becomes a vacancy discovered in production.

**Question 3 (easy) — Error checking a workforce plan**

A draft workforce plan for your profession states: "Current establishment: 30 delivery management posts, 26 filled. Attrition assumption: 15% annually (4 leavers). Growth: 6 new posts funded from Q3. Recruitment target for the year: 8 hires, restoring full establishment by year end." What is wrong with the recruitment target?

- A) Nothing; 4 vacancies plus 4 leavers equals 8.
- B) The target ignores the 6 new posts: full establishment by year end is 36 posts, requiring 4 (current vacancies) + 4 (attrition) + 6 (growth) = 14 hires, not 8.
- C) The attrition assumption is too high for the civil service.
- D) The target double-counts leavers.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Reconcile the plan's own numbers. Year-end establishment: 30 + 6 = 36 posts. Sources of demand for hires: existing vacancies (30 − 26 = 4), expected attrition (15% of 26 filled ≈ 3.9, the plan's own 4), and the 6 new posts. Total: 4 + 4 + 6 = 14. The stated target of 8 covers the first two components and silently omits the growth the same paragraph announces — an internal inconsistency, the highest-yield error class in composite planning documents, and exactly the kind that survives because each sentence is separately true. Option A is the arithmetic of the error itself. Option C is an opinion the document's internal logic neither needs nor supports (and 15% is unremarkable for digital roles). Option D names a defect the plan does not have. The consequence of missing this is concrete and yours: recruitment pipelines are long, so an under-target discovered in Q3 becomes six unfilled funded posts by year end — capability your organisation paid for and never received. Heads of role sign workforce plans; the signature is worth what the reconciliation was.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Prioritisation at head-of-role altitude**

Monday morning presents four demands:

1. A permanent secretary's office invites you to present your profession's capability strategy to the executive committee — a slot in three weeks that could unlock the funding case you have built for a year.
2. Two of your senior delivery managers are in open, escalating conflict over ownership of a shared platform team; their teams are starting to take sides.
3. A cross-government working group you co-chair needs your review of a draft delivery standard by Friday; your profession's position will be represented by whatever you submit.
4. A journalist has contacted your press office about an FOI-released document mentioning delays on a programme in your area; press office needs a factual briefing by 15:00 today.

Which allocation of your week is most defensible?

- A) 1, 3, 2, 4 — the executive committee opportunity outranks all noise.
- B) 4 today (bounded, deadlined, factual); begin 2 this week personally (escalating conflict between your seniors is culturally corrosive and only you can mediate it); protect real time for 3 by Friday (your profession's position is at stake); schedule dedicated preparation for 1 across the three weeks.
- C) 2, 1, 3, 4 — internal conflict always comes first.
- D) 4, 3, 1, 2 — external deadlines strictly before internal matters.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Head-of-level prioritisation allocates attention across a week, not a queue — each item has a different deadline structure, delegability, and consequence-of-neglect curve. Item 4 is due at 15:00 and is bounded: an hour assembling accurate facts for press office, done today, because inaccurate or absent briefings compound into public misinformation. Item 2 has no deadline, which is exactly why option D's rule fails: escalating conflict between senior leaders is the item whose cost curve steepens silently — teams taking sides means the dispute is already propagating into the culture you are accountable for, and mediation between your own seniors is genuinely undelegable. Item 3's Friday deadline carries representational weight — whatever you submit becomes your profession's position across government, so it needs your considered attention, not a Thursday-night skim. Item 1 is the largest opportunity and the least urgent this week; three weeks of scheduled preparation beats a panicked fortnight — and note option A's tell, calling everything else "noise": the executive slot is won or lost partly on the organisational health (item 2) and cross-government credibility (item 3) it would be spent describing. The pattern to internalise: sequence by deadline where deadlines are real, by cost-curve steepness where they are not, and reserve yourself for what only you can do.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Aggregation reversal in delivery data**

Across your organisation's two divisions, you compare this year's on-time milestone delivery against last year's. Division X: last year 90 of 100 on time (90%); this year 28 of 30 (93.3%). Division Y: last year 10 of 20 (50%); this year 54 of 100 (54%). Both divisions improved. What happened to the organisation's overall on-time rate, and what is the lesson?

- A) It improved, since both divisions improved — component improvement guarantees aggregate improvement.
- B) It stayed flat at about 83%.
- C) It fell from 83.3% (100 of 120) to 63.1% (82 of 130): both divisions improved, yet the volume mix shifted heavily toward the lower-performing division, dragging the aggregate down — so aggregate and component trends can point in opposite directions, and an honest report states both levels.
- D) The comparison is invalid because volumes changed.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Compute all three levels.

Step 1: Division X: 90 ÷ 100 = 90% last year; 28 ÷ 30 = 93.3% this year — improved.
Step 2: Division Y: 10 ÷ 20 = 50% last year; 54 ÷ 100 = 54% this year — improved.
Step 3: Aggregate last year: (90 + 10) ÷ (100 + 20) = 100 ÷ 120 = 83.3%.
Step 4: Aggregate this year: (28 + 54) ÷ (30 + 100) = 82 ÷ 130 = 63.1%.

Both components rose; the aggregate fell twenty points. This is Simpson's paradox, driven by the mix: last year 83% of the volume sat in high-performing X; this year 77% sits in lower-performing (though improving) Y, so the aggregate is now dominated by the weaker division. Option A states exactly the intuition the paradox refutes — component improvement does not guarantee aggregate improvement when volume shares move. Option D throws away a comparison that is not invalid but two-layered: the changed volumes are not a nuisance to be excluded, they are the explanation to be reported. The head-of-role consequence is direct: an executive dashboard showing only the aggregate would report your profession's delivery "collapsing" in a year when every division improved — while a community update showing only division-level rates would miss that the organisation's work has shifted toward its harder ground, which is itself the strategic fact worth interrogating (why did Y's volume quintuple? was X wound down deliberately?). Leading on strategy for an entire organisation, "joining up business needs with innovative analysis" as your skill framework puts it, means reporting both levels and the mix shift that connects them — because whichever single level you publish alone, someone will make a wrong decision from it.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Deduction across funding and structure rules**

Rules governing a proposed new delivery hub: (i) the hub may be established only if at least 8 delivery professionals transfer into it at formation; (ii) no more than 2 professionals may transfer from any single existing programme, to protect running delivery; (iii) programmes in their final quarter are exempt from rule (ii); (iv) formation must complete before the new financial year. You have five programmes: P1 and P2 each willing to release 2; P3 willing to release 3 but only 2 permitted; P4, in its final quarter, willing to release 3; P5 willing to release 1. Can the hub form, and with what margin?

- A) No — only 7 can transfer.
- B) Yes — exactly 8: 2+2+2+1+1.
- C) Yes — 10 can transfer (2 from each of P1, P2, P3; 3 from P4; 1 from P5), a margin of 2 over the minimum.
- D) Cannot be determined without knowing the financial year date.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Apply the cap rule programme by programme, watching for the exemption. P1: 2 (willing 2, cap 2). P2: 2. P3: willing 3, capped at 2 by rule (ii) — takes 2. P4: willing 3, and rule (iii) exempts final-quarter programmes from the cap — takes all 3. P5: 1. Total: 2+2+2+3+1 = 10 ≥ 8 — the hub can form with a margin of 2. Option A forgets the exemption entirely (capping P4 at 2 gives 2+2+2+2+1 = 9, which is still ≥ 8 — so option A's "7" also miscounts; it is doubly wrong, a reminder that distractors need checking too, not reverse-engineering). Option B undercounts P3 and P4. Option D manufactures indeterminacy: rule (iv) is a completion constraint on timing, not on the count, and nothing in the question puts the deadline in doubt. The structure of this item is the structure of real organisational design under rules: caps, exemptions, and thresholds interact, and the head of role who computes the true feasible margin — rather than the cautious undercount or the hopeful overcount — is the one whose proposal survives both the workforce panel and reality. Margin matters beyond feasibility: forming at exactly 8 leaves the hub one resignation from breaching its own founding condition; forming at 10 does not.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Error checking a benefits narrative at portfolio level**

A portfolio summary for the executive committee claims: "Our delivery profession's improvement programme has driven a 30% reduction in average time-to-live across the portfolio (from 40 weeks to 28), saving an estimated £2.4m annually. Additionally, the three programmes that adopted the new delivery playbook averaged 26 weeks, versus 31 weeks for non-adopters — evidence the playbook works. We recommend mandating the playbook portfolio-wide." Which is the most important analytical weakness?

- A) The 30% arithmetic is wrong.
- B) Playbook adoption was voluntary, so adopters are self-selected — likely the teams already most capable or least constrained — and the 26-versus-31 comparison cannot distinguish the playbook's effect from the adopters' pre-existing characteristics; the mandate recommendation rests on the weakest link.
- C) The £2.4m saving should be net of the improvement programme's costs.
- D) Time-to-live is the wrong metric for a delivery profession.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Triage the weaknesses by how much load each bears. The 30% claim is arithmetically sound (40 → 28 is a 12-week reduction; 12 ÷ 40 = 30%) — option A fails. Option C is a fair audit note (gross savings should be netted) but adjusts the size of a benefit, not the validity of the decision being recommended. Option D is a debatable preference, and time-to-live is in fact a reasonable outcome metric. The recommendation — mandate the playbook — rests entirely on the adopter/non-adopter comparison, and that comparison is built on self-selection: teams that voluntarily adopt new practice differ systematically from teams that do not (maturity, headroom, leadership) in ways that independently predict faster delivery. The 5-week gap may be entirely, partially, or not at all the playbook — the data cannot say, and mandating on it risks imposing costs on constrained teams for benefits that may not transfer. That is option B, and note the professional consequence at your level: this paper goes out under your profession's credibility, and the executive committee's analysts will find the selection effect if you do not. The constructive amendment is modest — pilot the playbook in a few non-adopter teams, or compare adopters against their own pre-adoption baseline — and recommending that amendment is precisely the "innovative analysis" your **Team dynamics and collaboration** skill's strategy language demands. Advocacy for your own profession's programme makes this check harder and more necessary: the head of role must be most sceptical exactly where they are most invested.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Problem solving: the process nobody owns**

Every programme in your department complains about the same three-stage approvals pipeline (business case → spend control → commercial sign-off), which averages 9 weeks. Your analysis: each stage takes about 2 weeks of actual work; the other 3 weeks are hand-off queues between stages, because the three approving teams report to three different directors, batch their intakes monthly, and each requires the previous stage fully complete before opening the file. Individual escalations routinely compress single cases to 4 weeks, but the pipeline always reverts. What is the structurally correct intervention?

- A) Establish a permanent expedite route so important programmes can always escalate.
- B) Address the design, not the cases: propose to the three directors a joint redesign — aligned intake cadences (or continuous intake), parallel provisional review of near-final material, and a shared service-level target owned across the three teams — using the escalation data (4 weeks is achievable) as proof of feasible turnaround.
- C) Instruct your programmes to submit everything earlier to absorb the 9 weeks.
- D) Publish a league table of the three teams' turnaround times to create pressure.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The diagnostic detail decides everything: work content is 6 of 9 weeks — the pipeline's tax is queuing between stages, produced by three specific design choices (monthly batching, serial completion requirements, fragmented ownership). Escalations succeed per case and always revert because escalation removes a case from the queues without changing the queue-generating design — which is also why option A fails structurally: a permanent expedite route institutionalises queue-jumping, degrades the standard route further (expedited cases consume the same review capacity), and converts a design flaw into a political economy of access. Option B intervenes on each design choice: cadence alignment attacks batching, parallel provisional review attacks the serial constraint, and a jointly owned service-level target attacks fragmented accountability — with the escalation data deployed as the feasibility proof ("your own teams turn cases in 4 weeks when asked; the design can too"). This is your **Making a process work** skill at head-of grade — identifying and challenging organisational processes of increasing complexity — executed through the only viable route, since the three teams answer to none of your programmes: consensus among the owning directors, which your role's cross-organisational influence exists to build. Option C surrenders the 9 weeks and hides the cost in every programme's plan. Option D applies pressure without design change — the teams will optimise the measured number (rejecting incomplete files faster, redefining start dates) rather than the system, as measured-but-unredesigned processes always do.

**Question 9 (challenging) — The strategy that fails its own arithmetic**

A draft profession strategy proposes: "We will reduce reliance on contractors from 40% of our 60 delivery posts to 10% within two years by converting contractor posts to permanent roles. Permanent recruitment in this specialism runs at approximately 6 successful hires per year for our department, and our permanent attrition is 10% annually. The conversion will save £1.8m over the two years." Setting aside the savings figure, is the headcount plan feasible on its own stated assumptions?

- A) Yes: 18 conversions over two years is well within reach.
- B) No: reaching 10% contractors requires the permanent base to grow from 36 to 54 (+18), while attrition costs roughly 4 permanents per year (about 8 over two years) — so total permanent hiring needed is about 26 against a stated capacity of 12; the plan exceeds its own recruitment assumption by more than double.
- C) Yes, if contractors convert directly without open competition.
- D) The plan is infeasible because attrition makes any growth impossible.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Run the plan's own numbers against its own target.

Step 1: Current mix: 60 posts, 40% contractor = 24 contractors, 36 permanents.
Step 2: Target mix: 10% contractor = 6 contractors, 54 permanents. Required permanent growth: 54 − 36 = 18.
Step 3: Attrition drag: 10% of the permanent base annually ≈ 3.6 rising toward 5 as the base grows — call it roughly 8 leavers over two years, each requiring a replacement hire just to stand still.
Step 4: Total hires needed ≈ 18 (growth) + 8 (replacement) ≈ 26.
Step 5: Stated capacity: 6 per year × 2 = 12. Shortfall: ~14 hires, more than half the requirement.

The strategy fails its own arithmetic by a factor of two — option B. Option A counts conversions as if recruitment capacity were unconstrained, but the plan itself states the constraint. Option C gestures at a real lever (direct conversion routes, where rules permit them, can bypass the standard funnel) — but the plan as drafted asserts none of that, and "yes, if" concedes the draft is infeasible as written. Option D overcorrects: growth is possible; this pace of growth is not. The head-of-role duty here is precise: strategies for your profession go forward under your name, and the feasibility check — target arithmetic versus stated capacity, with attrition's compounding drag included — is the review only you are positioned and obliged to perform. The constructive redraft options fall straight out of the arithmetic: extend the timeline, raise recruitment capacity (the actual binding constraint, worth its own investment case), use permitted conversion routes explicitly, or accept a 20% interim target. A strategy that survives its own numbers is rarer, and more valuable, than an ambitious one.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Reading a cross-departmental commitment matrix**

Four departments have signed a memorandum for a shared delivery capability. The commitment matrix: Department A contributes 4 FTE and chairs governance; B contributes 3 FTE contingent on its spending review settlement; C contributes 2 FTE and the hosting platform, but its FTE are committed elsewhere for the first two quarters; D contributes 3 FTE unconditionally. The capability needs 9 FTE from launch to be viable, launching next quarter. What is the true launch position?

- A) 12 FTE committed — comfortably viable.
- B) Launch-quarter firm commitment is 7 FTE (A's 4 + D's 3); B's 3 are conditional on an external event and C's 2 are unavailable for two quarters — the capability is 2 FTE short of viability at launch on firm commitments, and the memorandum's headline total conceals this.
- C) 9 FTE — exactly viable.
- D) The capability cannot launch for two quarters.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Distinguish committed from firm-at-launch, the distinction memoranda are built to blur. A: 4, unconditional — firm. D: 3, unconditional — firm. B: 3, contingent on a spending review settlement — a genuine external condition that may resolve either way, and not before launch: not firm. C: 2, real but explicitly unavailable for the first two quarters: not present at launch. Firm launch-quarter total: 7 against a viability threshold of 9 — option B, including its sting: the headline "12 FTE" is what the signing announcement said, and the gap between announced and firm is where shared capabilities quietly fail in their first quarter, each department believing the others' numbers. Option D overcorrects — the launch is not impossible; it is 2 FTE short, which is a solvable, nameable gap (interim loan from A or D's departments, phased viability threshold, launch delayed one quarter, or conditional-commitment acceleration if B's settlement lands early). Options A and C read the matrix at face value with and without generosity. At your level this reading is the job: effectively building consensus between organisations — your named skill — includes being the person who makes conditionality explicit before signature, because consensus built on blurred commitments is deferred conflict, and the head of role who says "we have 7 firm, 9 needed — let's close the gap now" at the memorandum stage saves the capability from being one more joint initiative that launched underwater.

**Question 11 (challenging) — The reorganisation with a hidden single point of failure**

A proposed reorganisation consolidates delivery management of six product teams under three senior delivery managers (two teams each), removes the separate release-management function by distributing its duties to the six teams, and creates one platform team on which all six product teams will depend for deployment infrastructure. Modelling capacity, all the numbers work. As head of delivery management, what is the most important structural observation?

- A) The design is sound: capacity balances and the hierarchy is clean.
- B) Two changes interact dangerously: distributing release management removes the organisation's concentrated release expertise at exactly the moment every team's deployments come to depend on a single new platform team — creating a shared single point of failure operated by six teams that each hold only a fragment of the released function's knowledge. The design needs an explicit release-capability plan (platform-team ownership of release standards, or a retained release practice) before implementation.
- C) Three senior delivery managers cannot manage six teams.
- D) Platform teams are an anti-pattern and the dependency should be removed.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Each change is separately defensible — release-management duties are often healthily devolved to teams; platform consolidation is often sound engineering economics — and the capacity model, checking each change alone, passes. The structural risk lives in their interaction: devolution assumes the receiving teams can absorb the expertise, while the platform consolidation simultaneously routes every team's deployment path through one new, unproven team — so the moment of maximum release-process fragility (expertise scattered into fragments) coincides with the moment of maximum release-path concentration (one shared dependency). A deployment incident in month two finds six teams with partial knowledge and one overwhelmed platform team with none of the released function's institutional memory. Option B both names the interaction and supplies the repairable design: relocate release standards into the platform team (concentrating expertise where the dependency concentrates) or retain a small release practice through transition. This is "change organisational structures to fixable and sustainable designs" — your skill's exact words — where sustainable means resilient to the failure modes the org chart cannot show and fixable means the flaw is correctable before implementation rather than after the incident. Option A audits components and misses the coupling, the signature failure of capacity-led reorganisation review. Option C asserts a ratio problem the model contradicts (2:1 is within your own operating rules from Question 2). Option D applies a slogan where a design judgement is required — the dependency is not the flaw; the undefended dependency is.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Deciding with incomplete, asymmetric evidence**

You must recommend this week whether your department joins a cross-government shared delivery-assurance service, replacing your internal assurance function. Evidence available: the shared service's two pilot departments report 20% cost reductions (self-reported, methodology unpublished); your internal function's costs are precisely known and its quality is well regarded but unbenchmarked; joining is contractually reversible after two years, but rebuilding the internal function after disbandment is estimated (by that function) at 18 months and significant cost; declining this round means the next joining window is in two years. What is the soundest reasoning?

- A) Join: a 20% saving across two pilots is compelling evidence.
- B) Decline: never trade a known-good function for unverified claims.
- C) The decision's structure matters more than either headline: the evidence is asymmetric (their savings self-reported and unverified; your quality unbenchmarked — neither side's case is actually proven) and the reversibility is asymmetric too (contractual exit exists, but the 18-month rebuild means the practical option value of exit is far lower than the contract implies, even discounting the estimate's self-interested source). The soundest recommendation is the one that buys information before surrendering irreversibility: negotiate joining with the internal function retained through a verified transition benchmark, or a partial scope entry — and if no such structure is negotiable, the unverified savings do not yet justify the near-irreversible step; decline and benchmark internally before the next window.
- D) Delay the recommendation until the shared service publishes its methodology.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Strip the decision to its structure, because both headline options select on unproven claims. The pilots' 20% is self-reported without methodology — advocacy-grade, not evidence-grade; your function's quality reputation is real but unbenchmarked — also advocacy-grade, and note the symmetry discipline: the same scepticism you apply to their savings applies to your own side's 18-month rebuild estimate, produced by the function whose existence it defends (discount, but do not dismiss: rebuild frictions are real even when their estimators are interested). The pivotal asymmetry is reversibility: the contract's two-year exit is nominal — a disbanded function's people, knowledge, and relationships do not wait for the exit clause — so joining-with-disbandment is a near-irreversible step taken on unverified numbers, exactly the transaction structure a head of role exists to catch. Option C's recommendation follows from the structure: create an option-preserving path (retained function through verified transition, or partial scope) that converts the decision from bet to experiment; failing that, the irreversible step waits and the missing evidence (internal benchmarking) gets built before the next window — making the two-year cost a price paid for information, stated honestly. Option A buys irreversibility with someone else's press release. Option B is the mirror error, elevating incumbency to principle — "never trade" would also refuse a genuinely verified 20%. Option D fails the stem's own constraint (a recommendation is due this week) and mistakes delay for diligence: your recommendation can be made now, about structure, without the methodology — that is what "making and justifying decisions characterised by high levels of risk, impact and complexity" means when the evidence is not going to improve before the deadline. Justification under irreducible uncertainty is the head-of competency: not the right answer, but the right reasoning, on the record.

### Preparation tips

- **Check the arithmetic inside authoritative prose.** At your level the dangerous document is fluent, well-structured, and wrong by one calculation — strategy targets versus recruitment capacity, headline commitments versus firm ones, aggregate rates recomputed from components. Recompute before you concur.
- **Hunt interactions, not just components.** Reorganisations, rule sets, and funding structures fail where two individually sound changes couple. For any proposal, explicitly ask: which pairs of changes touch the same capability, dependency, or person?
- **Practise structure-first diagnosis.** For every performance pattern (a directorate, a team, a recruitment round), list the structural explanations before the people explanations — then design the test that distinguishes them.
- **Rehearse restraint as an answer.** "The data cannot yet distinguish X from Y, and here is the test that would" is a full-credit answer at head-of level, in assessments and executive committees alike.
- **Time-box strategy documents into evidence and advocacy.** Practise marking each sentence of a real paper as verified, self-reported, or aspirational; the discipline transfers directly to the assessment's argument-evaluation items.
- **Set questions for others.** Turning your organisation's real dilemmas into practice questions for your community is the deepest form of this preparation — it forces you to articulate the reasoning standard you are implicitly assessing everyone else against.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Locating problems in people when you own the structure.** Firing the symptoms of your own organisational design is the head-of error with the longest half-life; the assessment plants it as the decisive-sounding option.
- **Accepting headline totals across organisations.** Committed ≠ firm ≠ present-at-launch. Read every matrix for conditionality and timing before believing its sum.
- **Auditing components and missing couplings.** Capacity models pass while designs fail; the flaw lives between the boxes on the chart.
- **Letting advocacy-grade evidence carry decision-grade weight** — including your own side's. Self-reported savings, self-interested estimates, and unbenchmarked reputations all get the same discount.
- **Treating nominal reversibility as real.** Exit clauses do not rebuild disbanded capability. Weigh the practical option value, not the contractual one.
- **Mistaking delay for diligence.** When evidence will not improve before the deadline, the competency is reasoning about structure under uncertainty — on the record — not deferring until certainty that is not coming.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures fluency with the numbers your role genuinely handles — and at head-of-profession level, those numbers describe organisations: workforce plans and recruitment funnels, pay and contractor cost structures, portfolio budgets and their variances, community capability metrics, and the trend arithmetic that underwrites strategy. The mathematical operations remain accessible — percentages, ratios, rates, weighted averages, compounding — but the stimuli are organisational datasets and the traps are structural: wrong denominators across mixed populations, funnel stages confused, compounding mistaken for linearity, and headline figures that dissolve under decomposition.

Typical format: online, timed (20 to 30 minutes, 15 to 25 questions), calculator usually permitted, objective scoring profiled for accuracy and pace, senior norm groups. Expect multi-step items where selecting and structuring the right figures is the test and the arithmetic is merely the audit trail.

Employers weight numeric assessment at your level because your numbers allocate other people's futures. You negotiate, influence or set budgets in complex environments; your workforce plans determine who gets hired and where; your business cases move investment between capabilities; your community metrics shape which delivery managers get developed, promoted, or supported. A head of role whose recruitment funnel arithmetic is wrong under-hires for a year; one whose budget decomposition is sloppy loses the negotiation with finance; one who misreads a compounding trend writes a capability strategy for the wrong future. The assessment compresses these stakes into twenty-five minutes.

As you work the questions, note that every worked answer shows its steps — and that at your level the visible method has a second function: your figures are challenged in boards and negotiations, and the figure that arrives with its derivation attached is the one that ends the argument.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Workforce and recruitment arithmetic** maps to your accountability for the recruitment of the right people to the right teams: establishment maths, attrition drag, funnel conversion rates, and time-to-fill are the quantitative core of building your community.
- **Budget and cost-structure calculations** map to **Financial management** ("negotiate, influence or set budgets in complex environments; write or input into business cases"): decomposing envelopes, projecting run rates, and pricing workforce mixes are negotiation instruments.
- **Commercial arithmetic at escalation grade** maps to **Commercial management** ("act as the escalation point and resolve large or high risk commercial management issues; coach others"): the disputes that reach you turn on numbers — and on teaching others to check them.
- **Community and capability metrics** map to leading the community of practice and supporting its professional development: participation rates, capability distributions, and progression data are how you know whether the community is actually being built.
- **Trend and distribution analysis** maps to **Team dynamics and collaboration** at head-of grade ("lead on strategy for an entire organisation, joining up business needs with innovative analysis") and **Planning** ("lead a continual planning process in a very complex environment"): strategies stand on trends read correctly.
- **Presenting organisational numbers** maps to being credible and influential across departments: the profession's case, in funding rounds and cross-government forums, is only as strong as its most checkable figure.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Community participation rate**

Your delivery community of practice has 85 members. Last month's sessions drew attendances of 42, 38, and 47 (members may attend multiple sessions; these are attendance counts, not unique attendees). The three sessions' unique-attendee count was 61. What percentage of the community attended at least one session?

- A) 49.8%
- B) 61%
- C) 71.8%
- D) 84.7%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Use the unique count, not the attendance counts.

Step 1: Unique attendees = 61.
Step 2: Participation rate = 61 ÷ 85 = 0.7176…
Step 3: 0.7176 × 100 ≈ 71.8%.

About 72% of the community attended at least once. Option A is the average attendance (42.3) divided by 85 — a different (also useful) metric measuring typical session reach, not community coverage. Option D is what you get by summing attendances (127), capping at 85 implicitly and dividing wrongly. Leading a community of practice means measuring it honestly, and this distinction — attendance versus unique participation — is the first honest cut: 72% coverage with 50% average reach tells you the community is broad but attendance is selective; the improvement conversation differs entirely from a community where 40% of members generate all the attendance. Define the metric before quoting the number; your community reports travel.

**Question 2 (easy) — Establishment gap after growth**

Your profession's funded establishment grows from 48 to 60 posts next year. Currently 44 posts are filled. Expected attrition is 12.5% of current staff. How many hires are needed to reach full establishment by year end?

- A) 16
- B) 21 or 22 (16 for vacancies and growth, plus 5–6 attrition replacements)
- C) 12
- D) 27

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Sum the three demand sources.

Step 1: Current vacancies: 48 − 44 = 4.
Step 2: Growth: 60 − 48 = 12 new posts.
Step 3: Attrition: 12.5% of 44 = 5.5 — expect 5 to 6 leavers requiring replacement.
Step 4: Total hires ≈ 4 + 12 + 5.5 = 21.5, i.e. 21 or 22.

Options A and C each omit a demand source (A drops attrition; C counts only growth). Option D double-counts by applying attrition to the full future establishment including unfilled posts — empty posts do not resign. The presentation point matters as much as the sum: a recruitment plan stated as "22 hires: 4 backfill, 12 growth, 6 attrition cover" survives finance scrutiny and lets each component be challenged separately, whereas a bare "22" invites the response "but you're only growing by 12." Being responsible for the recruitment of the right people to the right teams begins with a hiring number that decomposes on demand.

**Question 3 (easy) — Contractor cost differential**

Your profession includes 10 contractors at an average of £720/day (assume 220 paid days/year) and you are building a case to convert 6 of them to permanent posts at a fully loaded average of £82,000/year. What is the gross annual saving from the 6 conversions?

- A) £158,400
- B) £458,400
- C) £492,000
- D) £950,400

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Cost each side of the conversion.

Step 1: Contractor annual cost each: £720 × 220 = £158,400.
Step 2: Six contractors: 6 × £158,400 = £950,400.
Step 3: Six permanents: 6 × £82,000 = £492,000.
Step 4: Gross saving: £950,400 − £492,000 = £458,400.

About £458k gross per year. The distractors are the intermediate figures — one contractor's cost (A), the permanent cost (C), the six-contractor cost (D) — a reminder that multi-step questions plant every station of the journey as an option. For the real business case, "gross" carries weight: recruitment costs, time-to-fill during which contractor cover continues, and any capability differences all net against the headline, and a head of role who presents £458k without the netting invites finance to do it for you, on their assumptions. The strong version of this case states gross, deductions, and net — which is what "write or input into business cases" means at your grade.

**Question 4 (easy) — Budget share under a cut**

Your directorate's £8.4m budget faces a 7.5% reduction, applied proportionally. The delivery profession's current share is £1.6m. What is the profession's budget after the cut?

- A) £1.48m
- B) £1.52m
- C) £1.54m
- D) £1.6m — professions are usually protected

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** A proportional cut applies the same percentage to each share.

Step 1: Reduction on £1.6m = 7.5% × 1.6 = £0.12m.
Step 2: Post-cut budget = 1.6 − 0.12 = £1.48m.

£1.48m. (Cross-check via the whole: £8.4m × 0.925 = £7.77m, and 1.48 ÷ 7.77 ≈ 19.05%, matching the original share 1.6 ÷ 8.4 = 19.05% — proportionality preserved, which is the definition.) Option D is not arithmetic but negotiation smuggled into an answer choice — and that is the real lesson inverted: protection is argued for, not assumed. The competent sequence is to compute the proportional position first (£1.48m is your baseline if you do nothing) and then negotiate deviations from it with a case — which activities would stop, what capability would be lost, what the cost of re-building later exceeds the saving now. Negotiating and influencing budgets in complex environments starts from knowing precisely what the default outcome is.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Recruitment funnel diagnosis**

Your last campaign for delivery managers: 240 applications → 60 passed sift (25%) → 36 attended interview (60% of sifted) → 9 passed interview (25% of interviewed) → 6 accepted offers (67% of passes). Sector benchmarks for comparable campaigns: sift pass 30%, interview attendance 85%, interview pass 45%, offer acceptance 80%. Which funnel stage most deviates from benchmark, and what does it suggest?

- A) The sift (25% vs 30%) — advertise more.
- B) Interview attendance (60% vs 85%) — a quarter of sifted candidates are lost before interview, suggesting slow scheduling or better competing offers in the gap; fix speed and candidate contact between sift and interview.
- C) Interview pass (25% vs 45%) — either interviewing is miscalibrated or the sift passes weak candidates; investigate jointly with attendance.
- D) Offer acceptance (67% vs 80%) — improve the offer.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Compute each stage's deviation as a ratio of its benchmark.

Step 1: Sift: 25 ÷ 30 = 0.83 of benchmark.
Step 2: Attendance: 60 ÷ 85 = 0.71 of benchmark.
Step 3: Interview pass: 25 ÷ 45 = 0.556 of benchmark — the largest shortfall.
Step 4: Acceptance: 67 ÷ 80 = 0.84 of benchmark.

The interview pass rate, at barely half of benchmark, deviates most; attendance (0.71) is second. Option C also draws the sound diagnostic link: a low pass rate indicates either miscalibrated interviewing or a sift passing weak candidates — and the abnormal attendance loss compounds it, because candidates with competing offers who drop out pre-interview are disproportionately the strong ones, further depressing the pass rate. Option B is the deliberately planted temptation of this item: it is written persuasively, its fix (scheduling speed, candidate contact) is genuinely worthwhile, and 0.71 is a real shortfall — but persuasive framing is not the largest deviation, and the assessed skill is computing all four ratios before choosing. The strong head-of response addresses both linked stages: fix the sift/interview calibration and the scheduling speed. In real campaign retrospectives, run every stage against benchmark as ratios first — the stage that feels broken and the stage that is most broken differ more often than not, and being responsible for recruiting the right people to the right teams means diagnosing the funnel by arithmetic, not anecdote.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Weighted capability score**

Your capability review scores delivery managers 1–4. Results: 3 people scored 4; 10 scored 3; 8 scored 2; 3 scored 1. What is the mean capability score, and what share of the community scores 3 or above?

- A) Mean 2.5; 50% at 3+
- B) Mean 2.54; 54.2% at 3+
- C) Mean 2.63; 54.2% at 3+
- D) Mean 2.54; 62.5% at 3+

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Weight scores by counts.

Step 1: Total people = 3 + 10 + 8 + 3 = 24.
Step 2: Weighted sum = (3×4) + (10×3) + (8×2) + (3×1) = 12 + 30 + 16 + 3 = 61.
Step 3: Mean = 61 ÷ 24 = 2.5416… ≈ 2.54.
Step 4: At 3 or above = 3 + 10 = 13 people; 13 ÷ 24 = 0.5416… ≈ 54.2%.

Mean 2.54, with 54.2% at level 3+. The near-miss options mix a correct figure with an error: option C's mean of 2.63 comes from averaging the four level-values (4+3+2+1)÷4 = 2.5 — no, from a weighting slip; option D's 62.5% counts 15 people (adding the 8 at level 2 partially or miscounting) — in both cases the paired-figure format is the trap, because one familiar-looking number lulls the check on the other. Verify both components independently. For community development, the pair tells a policy story the mean alone hides: a 2.54 mean with 46% below level 3 says your development investment belongs in the 8-person level-2 cohort (the largest and most movable group), which is exactly the "build capability and excellence" allocation decision the review exists to inform.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Portfolio budget variance triage**

Mid-year, your four programmes report against annual budgets: Alpha budget £2.0m, forecast outturn £2.3m; Bravo budget £3.5m, forecast £3.4m; Charlie budget £1.2m, forecast £1.5m; Delta budget £4.8m, forecast £4.9m. Which programme has the largest percentage overspend, which the largest absolute overspend, and what is the portfolio's net position?

- A) Charlie largest by percentage (+25%); Alpha and Charlie tie in absolute (+£0.3m each); portfolio net +£0.6m over.
- B) Alpha largest by percentage (+15%); Charlie largest absolute; portfolio net +£0.5m over.
- C) Delta largest on both measures; portfolio net +£0.7m over.
- D) Charlie largest by percentage (+25%); Delta largest absolute (+£0.1m); portfolio balanced.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Compute each variance both ways.

Step 1: Alpha: +£0.3m; 0.3 ÷ 2.0 = +15%.
Step 2: Bravo: −£0.1m; −2.9%.
Step 3: Charlie: +£0.3m; 0.3 ÷ 1.2 = +25%.
Step 4: Delta: +£0.1m; 0.1 ÷ 4.8 = +2.1%.
Step 5: Portfolio net: +0.3 − 0.1 + 0.3 + 0.1 = +£0.6m over (on £11.5m, about +5.2%).

Charlie leads by percentage (+25%), Alpha and Charlie tie in absolute terms (+£0.3m each), and the net is +£0.6m — option A. The dual measure is the point: percentage variance finds the programme whose control is weakest (Charlie is running a quarter over — something is structurally wrong with its plan or its discipline), while absolute variance finds where the money is (Alpha's £0.3m matters as much to the envelope as Charlie's). Bravo's underspend partially offsets — legitimate at portfolio level, but never silently: netting overspends against underspends without saying so is how portfolio reports earn distrust. The head-of conversation differs by programme: Charlie gets a plan-integrity intervention, Alpha a scope conversation, Bravo a check that the underspend is efficiency rather than slippage, Delta routine monitoring. One table, three different management actions — that is what reading variance dually buys.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Time-to-fill and its cost**

Your average time-to-fill for delivery manager vacancies is 14 weeks. Each vacant week costs the organisation an estimated £1,900 in contractor cover premium (contractor day rate above the permanent equivalent). A proposed recruitment improvement — dedicated sourcing plus pre-approved panels — costs £30,000 per year and is projected to cut time-to-fill to 9 weeks. With 8 vacancies arising per year, does the investment pay back, and by how much?

- A) No: it costs £30,000 and saves £15,200.
- B) Yes: saving = 5 weeks × £1,900 × 8 vacancies = £76,000 per year, against £30,000 — net benefit £46,000, a 2.5:1 return.
- C) Yes: saving = £1,900 × 9 × 8 = £136,800 net.
- D) Break-even, roughly.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Value the reduction, not the remainder.

Step 1: Time saved per vacancy = 14 − 9 = 5 weeks.
Step 2: Saving per vacancy = 5 × £1,900 = £9,500.
Step 3: Annual saving = 8 × £9,500 = £76,000.
Step 4: Net = £76,000 − £30,000 = £46,000; ratio 76 ÷ 30 ≈ 2.5:1.

The investment clears its cost two-and-a-half times over. Option C prices the 9 remaining weeks instead of the 5 saved — the classic wrong-difference error; option A prices one vacancy's saving against the whole cost. Presentation matters for the decision this case must win: the benefit rests on two estimates (the £1,900 weekly premium and the 5-week reduction), so the robust case shows the break-even sensitivity — the scheme pays for itself if it saves just 30,000 ÷ (1,900 × 8) ≈ 2 weeks per vacancy, well inside the projection — which converts "trust my forecast" into "the forecast has 60% margin for error." Recruitment infrastructure is unglamorous spend, and heads of role win it with exactly this arithmetic.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Compounding attrition of community knowledge**

Your profession has 40 members. Annual attrition is 15%, and each leaver is replaced (headcount constant). Roughly what fraction of today's members will still be in the profession in three years, and what does this imply for how the community's knowledge is held?

- A) 55% remain (100% − 3×15%); knowledge is safe in people's heads.
- B) About 61% remain (0.85³ = 0.614); roughly 4 in 10 of today's members — and their tacit knowledge — will be gone within three years, so community knowledge must be institutionalised (playbooks, communities, documented practice) rather than held in individuals.
- C) 85% remain; attrition only applies once.
- D) 45% remain; attrition compounds upward each year.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Retention compounds multiplicatively.

Step 1: Annual retention = 1 − 0.15 = 0.85.
Step 2: Three-year retention = 0.85 × 0.85 × 0.85 = 0.6141.
Step 3: About 61% of today's members remain; about 39% will have left (roughly 15–16 of 40 people).

Option A subtracts linearly (45% lost), overstating the loss because each year's 15% applies to an already-reduced surviving cohort; option D compounds in the wrong direction. The strategic implication is the question's real payload: with headcount constant but 4 in 10 faces changing over a strategy's three-year horizon, any capability that lives only in individuals — assessment craft, supplier history, the reasons behind the operating model — decays at the same compounded rate. This is the quantitative case for exactly the activities your role lists: leading the community of practice, building capability and excellence, and supporting professional development are not cultural niceties but the knowledge-retention infrastructure that outlives the 39%. A head of role who can put the 0.85³ arithmetic under the community budget line defends it in terms finance respects.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Decomposing a pay-gap headline**

A workforce report headline reads: "Contractors in the delivery profession earn 2.1 times the daily cost of permanent staff." You decompose: contractors average £760/day. Permanent staff daily cost was computed as average salary £52,000 ÷ 260 working days = £200/day, then quoted as "£360/day fully loaded" in a footnote using a 1.8 loading factor. Which is the defensible comparison, and what is the true ratio?

- A) 2.1:1 as headlined — £760 versus £360 is close enough.
- B) £760 ÷ £360 = 2.11, so the headline is right and defensible.
- C) Neither: the comparison also needs alignment of the day bases (contractors are paid per worked day, typically ~220/year; permanents' £52,000 spreads across ~220 productive days too, not 260 calendar working days) — on aligned bases, permanent fully loaded ≈ £52,000 × 1.8 ÷ 220 ≈ £425/day, giving a true ratio of about 1.8:1. Still material, but the headline overstates by inflating the permanent denominator with non-productive days.
- D) The ratio cannot be computed without knowing contractor pension arrangements.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Audit both numerator and denominator for basis alignment.

Step 1: The £200/day permanent figure divides salary by 260 (52 weeks × 5 days) — but nobody works 260 days: leave (~30 including public holidays), sickness, and training reduce productive days to roughly 220, the same basis on which contractor day rates are quoted (contractors bill only worked days).
Step 2: Aligned permanent cost: £52,000 × 1.8 (loading) ÷ 220 = £93,600 ÷ 220 = £425.45/day.
Step 3: True ratio: £760 ÷ £425 ≈ 1.79 ≈ 1.8:1.

The honest comparison is about 1.8:1, not 2.1:1 — the headline gained 17% of its drama from mismatched day bases. The finding still supports action (an 80% premium is material), which is precisely why the correction matters: a 2.1 headline that a challenged analyst reduces to 1.8 in front of the executive committee discredits the whole case, including its valid core; the 1.8 figure, presented with its basis stated, is unassailable. Option D is the fastidiousness trap — pension treatment is inside the 1.8 loading factor's remit; refinements exist but do not bar computation. As the escalation point for large commercial issues and a voice that is credible across departments, your ratios get quoted upward without you attached; basis-alignment is the hygiene that keeps them survivable. The generalisable check: whenever two populations are compared per-unit, ask whether the units were counted the same way on both sides.

**Question 11 (challenging) — The savings target and the double count**

A departmental efficiency programme asks your profession for £900k of savings over two years, and the draft return lists: (1) contractor conversions, £458k/year from year 1 (from Question 3's case); (2) recruitment improvement net benefit, £46k/year (from Question 8); (3) reduced time-to-fill "saving £76k/year in contractor cover" (also from Question 8); (4) shared assurance service joining saving, £120k/year from year 2, contingent on an unapproved business case. What is the defensible two-year savings total?

- A) £1,404k — sum everything across two years.
- B) £1,008k: items 1 and 2 across two years (916 + 92), excluding item 3 (already inside item 2's net figure — counting both double-counts the £76k gross) and item 4 (contingent on an unapproved case) — with item 4 listed separately as pipeline, not banked savings.
- C) £1,160k — include item 4 because the business case will probably pass.
- D) £916k — only item 1 is real.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Audit the list for double-counting and contingency before summing.

Step 1: Item 1: £458k × 2 = £916k. Firm (assuming the conversions proceed).
Step 2: Item 2: £46k × 2 = £92k. Firm — but note its derivation: £76k gross saving minus £30k cost.
Step 3: Item 3 is item 2's gross component listed again as a separate line: including both books the £76k twice (once gross, once inside the net). Exclude.
Step 4: Item 4 rests on an unapproved case: pipeline, not savings. Exclude from the banked total; disclose as upside.
Step 5: Defensible total: £916k + £92k = £1,008k over two years — comfortably above the £900k target, and honestly composed.

The double-count in step 3 is the signature error of savings returns assembled from separate initiative papers: each paper's figures are individually true, and the aggregation invents money. The contingent item is the second signature error — banking unapproved cases converts a savings return into a wish list, and the first audit deflates it publicly. The strategic point is that honesty here is also tactically superior: at £1,008k firm you exceed the target with headroom and hold £240k of disclosed pipeline in reserve, whereas option A's £1,404k invites a higher target next year calibrated to a number that was never real. Writing business cases and resolving high-risk financial positions at your level is substantially this: sum only once, bank only the approved, and disclose the rest as what it is.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Choosing the honest trend story**

Applications to your delivery manager campaigns over five successive campaigns: 310, 265, 228, 196, 170. A colleague drafts: "Applications have fallen by 28 per campaign on average; at this rate we will have ~86 applications in three more campaigns and zero within six." What is the soundest analytical response?

- A) Endorse it: the average decline is (310−170) ÷ 5 = 28 per campaign, and linear projection is standard practice.
- B) The decline is better described as multiplicative — each campaign draws about 86% of the previous (265/310 = 0.855; 228/265 = 0.860; 196/228 = 0.860; 170/196 = 0.867) — so the honest projection is decay toward a floor (~126 after three more campaigns at 0.86³ ≈ 0.64 of 170 — check: 170 × 0.64 ≈ 109), not a march to zero; and either way the projection is secondary to diagnosing the cause of a five-campaign 45% decline in the profession's applicant pool.
- C) Both descriptions are equally valid; choose whichever supports the funding case better.
- D) Four data points of decline are too few to say anything.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Test the two models against the data.

Step 1: Successive differences: −45, −37, −32, −26 — the absolute fall shrinks each time, which linear decline (constant differences) cannot produce.
Step 2: Successive ratios: 0.855, 0.860, 0.860, 0.867 — nearly constant, the signature of multiplicative decay at ≈ 14% per campaign.
Step 3: Projection under the better model: 170 × 0.86³ = 170 × 0.636 ≈ 108 applications after three more campaigns — declining, but nowhere near the linear story's 86-then-zero trajectory. (Also note the average-decline arithmetic in the draft divides by 5 when there are 4 intervals: (310−170) ÷ 4 = 35 per campaign — the draft's own model is miscalculated.)
Step 4: The analytical priority: model choice refines the projection, but a 45% decline across five campaigns is a strategic alarm under either model — employer brand, market conditions, salary competitiveness, or campaign reach — and diagnosis outranks extrapolation.

Option B does all of this. Option A endorses a model the differences refute and an arithmetic slip besides. Option C is the integrity failure dressed as pragmatism — choosing models by advocacy is how professions lose the credibility your role exists to build across departments. Option D wastes a clear, consistent signal. The head-of habit: before projecting any organisational trend, compute both differences and ratios; the one that is stable is the model the data is proposing — and then ask why the trend exists, because the strategy answers the why, not the extrapolation.

### Preparation tips

- **Decompose every headline before repeating it.** Ratios, savings totals, capability means: rebuild each from components, aligning bases (day counts, loading, populations) on both sides. Your quoted numbers are quoted onward with your credibility attached.
- **Sum demand sources completely in workforce arithmetic:** vacancies + growth + attrition-on-filled-posts. The omitted component is the planted error in nearly every establishment question — and every under-resourced year.
- **Compute both absolute and percentage variance, always.** They identify different problems (where the money is versus where control is weakest) and different management actions.
- **Test trends as differences and as ratios.** Stable differences → linear; stable ratios → compounding. Retention, attrition, applicant pools, and growth are almost always the latter, and the model choice changes the strategy.
- **Audit savings and benefits lists for double counts and contingencies** before summing: gross figures reappearing inside nets, and unapproved cases banked as certainties, are the two failures that audits find first.
- **State sensitivity with every investment case:** the break-even version of your assumption ("pays back at just 2 of the projected 5 weeks") is the single most persuasive line available to a numerate advocate.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Mismatched bases in comparisons.** Per-day, per-head, per-campaign figures computed on different denominators (260 vs 220 days; attendance vs unique members) manufacture drama or hide it. Align first.
- **Linear projection of multiplicative processes** — and its cousin, subtracting compound attrition linearly. Both misprice the future your strategy is written for.
- **Choosing the persuasive stage over the computed one.** Funnel diagnosis, variance triage, capability gaps: run every ratio before naming the biggest problem; the narrative-friendly answer and the arithmetic answer diverge regularly, including in this section's own Question 5.
- **Banking contingent money and double-counting gross-within-net.** Savings returns and business cases fail audits on these two errors more than all others combined.
- **Quoting paired figures with only one checked.** Mean-and-share, total-and-ratio options pair a correct number with a wrong one; verify both independently.
- **Advocacy-driven model selection.** Never choose the analysis that "supports the case better" — at head-of level the discovery of one motivated model reprices every figure your profession has ever published.

### Practice questions

Passage for Questions 1–4 (a spend-control instrument):

"Digital and technology spend proposals exceeding £1m must receive central approval before commitment. Proposals between £100,000 and £1m may be approved departmentally, provided the department holds a current delegated approval agreement and the proposal is recorded in the quarterly return. Delegated approval agreements are granted for twelve months and may be revoked at any time where the centre judges departmental controls inadequate. Contract extensions are treated as new proposals at the value of the extension, except where the original approval explicitly covered the extension option, in which case no further approval is required. Nothing in this instrument removes a department's obligations under procurement law."

**Question 1 (easy) — True, false, or cannot say**

Statement: "A £750,000 proposal in a department with a current delegated approval agreement requires central approval."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, because all digital spend needs central approval

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Locate the governing band: £750,000 sits between £100,000 and £1m, where the instrument permits departmental approval "provided the department holds a current delegated approval agreement and the proposal is recorded in the quarterly return." The department in the statement holds a current agreement, so the delegated route is available and central approval is not required — the statement is false. Note what the correct reasoning did not skip: the proviso has two limbs (agreement and recording), but the statement asks only whether central approval is required, and the recording obligation is a condition of the delegated route's proper use, not a trigger that re-imposes central approval in advance. Option D asserts the blanket rule the banding structure exists to replace. This is the bread-and-butter reading your cross-departmental credibility rests on: banded instruments are misquoted in both directions — over-centralised by the cautious, under-controlled by the hopeful — and the head of role is the person whose citations match the text.

**Question 2 (moderate) — True, false, or cannot say**

Statement: "A £400,000 contract extension always requires a new approval."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, because extensions are treated as new proposals

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The extension rule has an explicit exception: extensions are treated as new proposals at the extension's value, "except where the original approval explicitly covered the extension option, in which case no further approval is required." The statement's "always" collides with that exception — an extension whose option was explicitly covered in the original approval needs nothing further — so the statement is false. Option D quotes the rule's first half as though it were the whole rule; the "always" is defeated by the text's own "except." The practical layer is worth teaching your community: this exception is precisely why well-drafted original approvals name their extension options — a habit that converts a future approval cycle into a nullity — and why, when coaching others in appropriate commercial management (your named **Commercial management** skill), the first question about any extension is "what did the original approval say?" Universal quantifiers — always, never, all — are the most efficiently falsifiable words in these assessments: one textual exception defeats them, and the drafters plant the exception deliberately.

**Question 3 (moderate) — True, false, or cannot say**

Statement: "A department whose delegated approval agreement is revoked loses its ability to approve proposals under £100,000."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, because revocation removes all delegation

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Trace what the instrument actually regulates. It governs proposals over £1m (central approval) and the £100,000–£1m band (delegated route). About proposals under £100,000 it says nothing — no approval requirement, no delegation structure, no prohibition. Whether sub-£100,000 spend requires any approval, and whether revocation touches it, are questions this text does not address; other instruments might, but the passage is the universe. Cannot say. The trap options resolve the silence in opposite directions: option B ("false") would require the text to affirm that sub-threshold approval survives revocation — it is silent; option D extends revocation's effect to a domain the instrument never delegated in the first place. Reading regulatory silence as silence — neither permission nor prohibition — is among the most consequential senior reading skills: heads of profession are routinely asked "does the control catch this?", and the credible answer distinguishes "the instrument requires X," "the instrument forbids X," and "the instrument does not speak to X; check what else applies." The third answer, delivered precisely, is what being consulted across departments sounds like.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Strongest inference**

Which is best supported by the instrument?

- A) The centre distrusts departments.
- B) The instrument is designed to make delegation conditional and reversible: time-limited agreements, revocation on judged inadequacy, and mandatory recording all keep departmental approval under continuing central oversight rather than granting it outright.
- C) Most departments have inadequate controls.
- D) Procurement law takes precedence over this instrument.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Test each option against textual features. Option B is assembled entirely from the drafting: twelve-month grants (delegation expires by default), revocation "at any time" on the centre's judgement (delegation is continuously contingent), and quarterly returns (delegation is observed). Together these are the architecture of conditional, reversible delegation — a design inference, drawn from what the clauses do rather than from anything they merely say. Option A converts design into psychology: oversight mechanisms are compatible with trust, prudence, statutory duty, or audit findings, and "distrust" is a motive the text cannot evidence. Option C infers a factual distribution from a power's existence — the revocation clause implies nothing about how often its condition obtains. Option D misreads the savings clause: "nothing in this instrument removes obligations under procurement law" states non-interference (both regimes apply), not precedence — a hierarchy the passage does not establish. Design-level inference — reading what a text's machinery accomplishes — is the head-of reading skill in its purest form: it is how you predict how an instrument will behave in edge cases the drafters never listed, and how you brief your community on the spirit accurately rather than folklorically.

Passage for Questions 5–8 (a cross-departmental memorandum):

"This memorandum records the agreement between the four participating departments to establish a joint delivery profession curriculum. Each department will nominate two members to the curriculum board by 30 September. The board will agree the core syllabus by December; departments may supplement the core with local modules, provided local modules do not duplicate or contradict core content. Funding of £160,000 for the first year will be met equally by the participating departments, subject to each department's internal approvals. Should any department withdraw before the first cohort completes, its funding share becomes payable in full unless withdrawal results from a machinery of government change. The curriculum will be reviewed after the first cohort, and continuation requires the agreement of at least three departments."

**Question 5 (easy) — True, false, or cannot say**

Statement: "Each participating department will contribute £40,000 in the first year, subject to its internal approvals."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, and the contribution is unconditional

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Two textual facts combine: funding of £160,000 "met equally by the participating departments," and the memorandum's opening establishes four participants. £160,000 ÷ 4 = £40,000 each — and the statement carries the text's own qualifier, "subject to each department's internal approvals," which is what makes it true as stated. Option D strips that qualifier and thereby fails: the memorandum makes the contribution explicitly conditional, and a version of the statement asserting unconditional commitment claims more than the text grants. This tiny item models a large habit: when relaying financial commitments from any multi-party document, the qualifier is part of the number. "Four departments at £40k each" and "four departments at £40k each, subject to internal approvals" price differently — the second contains the risk that the memorandum's own drafters saw fit to record, and the head of role who drops it in the retelling has silently upgraded a hope into a commitment across four organisations.

**Question 6 (moderate) — True, false, or cannot say**

Statement: "A department that withdraws after the first cohort completes must still pay its full funding share."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) False, because withdrawal is not permitted after the first cohort

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Read the clause's temporal scope exactly: the full-payment consequence attaches to withdrawal "before the first cohort completes." About withdrawal after completion the memorandum is silent — it neither imposes the payment obligation (so "true" fails) nor releases the departing department from it (the first-year share may already have been paid, or other terms may govern — the text does not say, so "false" also fails). The continuation clause (three departments must agree) governs whether the curriculum persists, not what a late-withdrawing department owes. Cannot say. Option D invents a prohibition from the existence of a consequence — a clause that prices early withdrawal does not forbid later withdrawal, and reading penalties as prohibitions is a recurrent senior misreading (the same error reads liquidated-damages clauses as bans on delay). The boundary discipline — noting exactly which period, population, or event a clause covers, and refusing to extend it by vibe — is what makes your reading of shared agreements the one the other three departments accept as neutral, which is consensus-building's quiet precondition.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Applying a compound condition**

A department proposes a local module: "Advanced stakeholder communication for delivery professionals," covering ground the core syllabus addresses in an introductory module but at substantially greater depth and with different case material. Under the memorandum, is the local module permitted?

- A) Yes, clearly — greater depth is not duplication.
- B) No, clearly — the topic already exists in the core.
- C) The memorandum's test is duplication or contradiction, and an advanced treatment of a core topic sits in the test's unresolved middle ground: it plausibly neither duplicates (different depth, different material) nor contradicts, but "duplicate" is undefined — so the defensible route is a board determination, which the curriculum board exists to give.
- D) Yes, because departments may supplement the core without any restriction.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Apply the actual test: local modules are permitted "provided local modules do not duplicate or contradict core content." Contradiction is not alleged. Duplication turns on what "duplicate" means — same topic, or same content? The module shares a topic with the core but differs in depth and material, which is the paradigm hard case: under a content reading it does not duplicate; under a topic reading it might. Options A and B each resolve the ambiguity by fiat in opposite directions, and both overclaim certainty the text cannot fund — note their twin structure, "clearly," which is the giveaway. Option D deletes the proviso wholesale. Option C does what a head of profession actually does with genuinely ambiguous instrument language: identifies the test, applies it as far as it goes, names the residual ambiguity honestly, and routes the determination to the body constituted to make it — establishing, incidentally, the precedent that will govern the next twelve local-module questions. The meta-skill is knowing the difference between text that answers a question (apply it), text that is silent (say so), and text that is ambiguous (decide who resolves it) — three verdicts that lazy reading collapses into one.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Summary for four audiences**

You must summarise the memorandum for your own community. Which summary is most faithful and most useful?

- A) "We've joined a cross-government curriculum — details to follow."
- B) "Four departments are jointly building a delivery curriculum: core syllabus agreed by a joint board (our two nominees by 30 September), local additions allowed if they don't duplicate or contradict core, £40k first-year contribution pending internal approval, early withdrawal carries full funding liability, and continuation after cohort one needs three of four departments. What this means for you: nominations open now; module suggestions to the board via me."
- C) "We're locked in for £40k with penalties if we leave, and a committee will decide what we teach."
- D) "A new curriculum is coming which will replace all local training."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Audit each against fidelity, completeness, and usefulness. Option B carries every operative element — governance (board, nominees, deadline), content rules (the actual duplication/contradiction test), money with its qualifier (pending approvals), the withdrawal consequence, and the continuation threshold — and closes with the two things a community summary must add: what it means for the reader and the action channel. Option A relays enthusiasm and nothing operative; communities cannot nominate, suggest, or plan on it. Option C is factually recoverable but framed as grievance — "locked in," "penalties," "a committee" — and a head of role's framing is contagious: the community will meet the curriculum with exactly the stance your summary models, which is why fidelity of tone is part of fidelity. Option D asserts a replacement the memorandum not only omits but contradicts (local modules are expressly permitted). Communicating the value of work to your community and beyond — the representational half of your role — is mostly this: compression that preserves decisions, conditions, and doors left open, plus the reader's next step. If your summary would surprise a signatory of the original, it is not a summary; it is a position.

Passage for Questions 9–10 (an internal audit finding):

"The audit examined delivery governance across the department's twelve major programmes. Ten programmes evidenced regular, minuted governance with appropriate senior attendance. In two programmes, governance boards had not met for over a quarter, and decisions traceable in correspondence had been taken outside any minuted forum. The audit notes that both programmes had recently lost their senior responsible owners and that interim arrangements were unclear. The audit rates delivery governance as 'moderate' overall and makes two recommendations: first, that interim SRO arrangements be documented within ten working days of any SRO departure; second, that the head of delivery management profession review whether delivery managers in the two affected programmes escalated the governance lapses appropriately."

**Question 9 (moderate) — True, false, or cannot say**

Statement: "The audit found that delivery managers in the two affected programmes failed to escalate the governance lapses."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, since the audit ordered a review of their escalation

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Read the recommendation's verb precisely: the audit asks the head of profession to "review whether delivery managers... escalated the governance lapses appropriately." A commissioned review of whether X happened is not a finding that X failed to happen — the review's outcome could be full vindication (they escalated correctly and were not heard), partial, or adverse; the audit text takes no position. So the statement can be neither confirmed nor denied: cannot say. Option D commits the exact inference the item exists to catch — treating the commissioning of an inquiry as its verdict — and it is worth naming why this matters beyond test technique: you are the addressee of that recommendation, and how you receive it determines whether two delivery managers begin your review pre-convicted by a misreading. If the head of profession relays "audit found our people failed to escalate," the community hears a verdict, morale and candour absorb the damage, and the actual review is prejudiced before it starts. The accurate relay — "the audit asks me to review the escalation record; no finding has been made" — protects both the review's integrity and the people in it. At your level, precision about what has not been concluded is a duty of care.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Weighing the finding fairly**

Which reading of the audit extract best reflects its evidential weight?

- A) Governance is failing across the department: the audit rated it only "moderate."
- B) Ten of twelve programmes evidence sound governance; the two lapses cluster with a specific, named structural trigger (SRO departure with unclear interim arrangements), which the first recommendation directly addresses — so the finding primarily indicts the succession gap, and secondarily asks (without concluding) whether the profession's escalation practice caught the lapses as it should.
- C) The audit exonerates delivery managers, since the lapses were caused by SRO departures.
- D) The "moderate" rating shows the audit was lenient; the true position is worse.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Weigh what the text actually distributes. The base picture: 10 of 12 sound — a majority finding option A's "failing across the department" inverts by generalising from the exceptions. The two lapses are not random: the audit itself supplies the covariate (both lost SROs; interim arrangements unclear) and aims its first recommendation squarely at that gap — the textual signature of a structural diagnosis, which option B reads at face value. The second recommendation keeps a genuine question open about escalation practice, and option B carries it as open — neither the conviction of Question 9's trap nor option C's mirror-image error: identifying a structural trigger does not exonerate the humans in the system, because the escalation question is precisely whether the profession's practitioners compensated for the structure's failure as their role expects; the audit asks, and so must you. Option D goes beyond the text in the cynical direction — second-guessing the rating without evidence is the same sin as inflating it. Note the head-of pattern in option B's shape: majority position stated, exceptions explained by named mechanism, remedy matched to mechanism, open questions held open. That is also, not coincidentally, the shape of the briefing you would give your executive committee about this audit — fair reading and fair representation are the same competency at different tables.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Argument evaluation: the consolidation case**

A paper to the digital leadership board argues: "Five departments each run separate delivery management professions with separate standards, curricula, and capability frameworks. This quintuplication wastes effort and confuses suppliers who work across departments. A single cross-government delivery profession, centrally governed, would eliminate duplication, give suppliers one interface, and raise standards to the level of the best. We estimate £600k annual savings in duplicated profession-management effort. The five heads of profession have been consulted and none produced evidence that departmental differences add value. We recommend the board approve consolidation."

Which is the most incisive critical assessment?

- A) The argument is sound: duplication is waste, and no evidence for local value was produced.
- B) The savings estimate needs an auditable basis, and beyond it the argument has two structural gaps: it assumes uniformity raises standards to the best (centralisation can equally lock in the average, and the mechanism selecting "the best" is unspecified), and it treats "no evidence produced that differences add value" as evidence of no value — an absence-of-evidence move, weighty only if a genuine search for such evidence was conducted, which the paper does not describe. The recommendation may still be right; the case as written does not yet establish it.
- C) The argument fails because cross-government initiatives never work.
- D) The argument fails because the heads of profession are conflicted and their consultation should be discounted.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Grade the argument's load-bearing joints. The waste claim is plausible but rests on an unevidenced £600k. The standards claim contains an unexamined mechanism: consolidation guarantees uniformity, not excellence — whether the unified profession adopts the best department's practice, the average, or the politically feasible depends entirely on governance the paper never specifies. The consultation claim is the subtle one: "none produced evidence that differences add value" shifts the burden — it reports an absence without describing any inquiry that would have surfaced presence (were the heads asked for such evidence? given time? are the differences even documented?), and absence-of-evidence carries weight in proportion to the diligence of the search, which is unstated. Option B catches all three and — the mark of senior evaluation — separates the case's quality from the conclusion's truth: consolidation might well be right, and the board's correct move is to demand the missing basis, mechanism, and search, not to reject the direction. Option A accepts the burden-shift at face value. Option C is a slogan where analysis is required. Option D discounts testimony by interest alone — the heads' incentives are real, but interested parties can still be factually right, and their consultation is evidence to be weighed, not disqualified; at your level you will sit on both sides of this exact table, as evaluator of such papers and as a consulted head whose evidence deserves better than presumptive discount. Making and justifying high-impact decisions — your skill's language — is substantially the craft of option B: naming precisely what would make the case sound, so the decision, whichever way it goes, stands on the record.

**Question 12 (challenging) — True, false, or cannot say across instrument and practice**

Text 1 (workforce policy): "Fixed-term appointments may be extended once, for up to twelve months, where a business case is approved by the departmental workforce board. Second extensions require Cabinet Office consent."
Text 2 (email from an HR business partner): "Re your delivery manager on the fixed-term contract: good news, the workforce board approved the business case, so we can extend her again — this is the second extension, but board approval covers it since the first extension was only six months."

Statement: "The proposed extension complies with the policy as stated."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, because the workforce board approved the business case

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Chain the texts and refuse the email's arithmetic of convenience. The policy's structure is ordinal, not cumulative: one extension may be board-approved; "second extensions require Cabinet Office consent" — full stop. The email concedes this is the second extension, then invents a mitigation ("the first was only six months") that the policy nowhere provides: the twelve-month figure caps the length of the first extension, it does not create a duration budget that unspent months carry into a second. On the texts as given, the proposed extension is a second extension without Cabinet Office consent, which the policy as stated does not permit: false — the statement is contradicted, not left open, so "cannot say" under-reads what the two texts jointly settle. Option D repeats the email's error with the email's confidence, and that confidence is the item's real subject: the misreading arrives from a professional whose domain this is, wrapped in "good news," and agreeing is the path of least resistance. Heads of profession live at this junction — responsible for recruitment and for the community's compliance, credible across departments precisely because their sign-offs survive checking. The move the item rehearses is the gentle correction with the text in hand: "I think this needs Cabinet Office consent as a second extension — can we check before we commit to her?" — which protects the individual (an unlawful extension unwinds most painfully for its beneficiary), the department, and the HR partner too. Authority is not evidence; the instrument is the evidence, and at your level you are the person others trust to have read it.

### Preparation tips

- **Read quantifiers and boundaries as load-bearing.** Always/never, before/after, first/second, once — senior instruments do their work in these small words, and every item (and every misquotation you will ever correct) turns on one.
- **Practise the three verdicts on silence and ambiguity.** Text answers → apply; text silent → say so and name what else might govern; text ambiguous → identify who resolves it. Drill the distinction on real instruments until the collapse into false certainty stops feeling natural.
- **Trace design, not just content.** For any instrument, ask what its machinery accomplishes (conditional delegation, reversibility, oversight) — design-level reading is what lets you answer edge cases the text never lists, accurately.
- **Separate commissioned questions from concluded findings.** Reviews ordered, consultations held, concerns noted — none are verdicts. Relay them at their actual evidential grade, especially when your own community is their subject.
- **Grade arguments joint by joint.** Evidence for each claim, mechanism for each promised effect, diligence behind each absence-of-evidence move — and always distinguish "this case is not yet made" from "this conclusion is wrong."
- **Audit your own output with the same instruments.** Take your last community update or board summary and check it against its source as an assessor would: every decision, every condition, every qualifier, tone included. Your writing is someone else's passage.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Extending a clause beyond its stated scope** — temporal, population, or value boundaries. Penalties are not prohibitions; first-extension rules are not duration budgets; pre-completion consequences say nothing about post-completion.
- **Resolving textual silence by instinct.** The instrument that does not speak to a case neither permits nor forbids it; the head of role who says "the control doesn't catch this" when the text is merely silent has legislated by accident.
- **Treating commissioned reviews as adverse findings.** The costliest relay error available to you, because it pre-convicts your own people and prejudices the review you will conduct.
- **Accepting authority as evidence.** HR partners, finance colleagues, and central teams misread instruments confidently; check the text before adopting the reading, and correct with the text, not with rank.
- **Framing drift in summaries.** Grievance framing, enthusiasm framing, and replacement claims all distort as surely as factual error — your community inherits your stance along with your summary.
- **Discounting interested testimony wholesale — or accepting burden-shifts uncritically.** Both are evaluation shortcuts; weigh what was actually searched, asked, and evidenced, and hold "the case is unproven" apart from "the conclusion is false."

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement test (SJT) presents realistic role scenarios and asks you to identify the most effective response, the least effective, best-and-worst pairs, or to rate every option. Scoring reflects expert consensus on effective professional behaviour, calibrated to the role's seniority — and at head-of-profession level, that calibration changes the test's character fundamentally. The scenarios are no longer about managing delivery; they are about stewarding a profession: recruitment and succession, community health, standards versus relationships, cross-departmental influence, and the exercises of authority where every choice you make is also a precedent, a signal, and a piece of culture.

Typical format: online, 20 to 35 minutes, 10 to 20 scenarios, generously timed. Expect dilemmas with no clean option, senior counterparties, and — the head-of signature — second- and third-order consequences that outweigh the first-order ones: what this response teaches the community, what it does to the profession's credibility, what norm it sets for the next hundred cases you will never personally see.

Employers weight SJTs decisively at this level because judgement is what the role is for. You exemplify what good looks like across the delivery roles — which means your responses are the community's reference implementations. You make and justify decisions characterised by high levels of risk, impact and complexity; you resolve the commercial escalations and organisational conflicts that defeated the tiers below; you build consensus between organisations that owe you nothing. An SJT samples exactly these calls, in miniature and on the record.

One orientation note for practice: at head-of level, the recurring test beneath the tests is whether you can hold two goods simultaneously — standards and relationships, innovation and guardrails, care for individuals and duty to the community, your department and your profession — without sacrificing either. The strong options almost always refuse the choice the scenario appears to force.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Community stewardship scenarios** map to leading the community of practice, building capability and excellence, and supporting professional development and continuous improvement: the SJT tests whether your responses grow the community or merely manage it.
- **Recruitment and succession scenarios** map to your responsibility for the recruitment of the right people to the right teams: judgement about fit, fairness, pipeline, and the long game of capability.
- **Standards-versus-relationships scenarios** map to exemplifying what good looks like and championing the role across government and industry: what you tolerate becomes the standard; what you police becomes the culture.
- **Cross-organisational scenarios** map to **Team dynamics and collaboration** at head-of grade: building consensus between organisations or highly independent stakeholders, and solving issues within teams or departments at the highest level.
- **Commercial escalation scenarios** map to **Commercial management**: acting as the escalation point for large or high-risk commercial issues, and coaching others in appropriate commercial management.
- **Conflict and mediation scenarios** map to understanding the psychology of a team, strong mediation skills, and coaching an organisation on team dynamics and conflict resolution.
- **Organisational design scenarios** map to changing organisational structures to fixable and sustainable designs — including the judgement of when, how fast, and with whose consent.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — The community star and the community standard**

Your most visible senior delivery manager — a superb practitioner, popular conference speaker, and informal deputy in community matters — has for months been dismissive in community sessions toward less experienced members: interrupting, correcting with visible impatience, and once describing a junior's question as "the kind of thing the framework exists so we don't have to discuss." Attendance by early-career members is declining. What is the most effective response?

- A) Accept it as the cost of brilliance; the community benefits from their expertise overall.
- B) Address it privately, directly, and specifically — the observed behaviours, the attendance data, the standard expected of senior members in your community — while affirming their value; make clear the visibility that makes them valuable is exactly what makes the behaviour costly; and follow through with structural moves (junior-friendly formats, rotating speaking roles) that rebalance the room regardless.
- C) Stop inviting them to community events until the behaviour improves.
- D) Raise it openly at the next community session so everyone hears the standard.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Compute what the scenario actually threatens: not one person's manners but the community's function — a community of practice whose juniors stop attending has stopped building capability, which is the accountability the role exists for. Option B pairs the two necessary interventions. The private conversation is direct and evidence-borne (behaviours plus attendance data, not vibes), and its pivotal sentence is the visibility point: senior members are the standard in a community — what your star models is what your community learns is acceptable, which is why the same behaviour costs more from them than from anyone else. The structural half matters equally: formats that protect early-career voice fix the room, not just the offender, and signal to the silent majority that the decline was noticed. Option A prices brilliance wrongly — expertise delivered in a way that empties the room of learners has negative community value whatever its technical worth. Option C is escalation without conversation: it punishes before it explains, forfeits the development of a valuable person, and reads to the community as politics. Option D humiliates publicly to teach respect — self-refuting, and it converts a correctable colleague into an opponent. Exemplifying what good looks like includes exemplifying how leaders correct their stars: privately, specifically, with the standard stated and the structure fixed.

**Question 2 (easy) — The recruitment shortcut**

A director urgently needs a delivery manager for a politically sensitive programme and asks you to appoint their preferred candidate — a capable contractor already on the programme — directly into the vacant permanent post, "given the urgency, we can regularise it later." Fair-and-open competition rules apply to the post. What is the most effective response?

- A) Appoint as asked; urgency is real and the candidate is genuinely capable.
- B) Refuse and report the director's request to HR as an attempted breach.
- C) Hold the line on the rules while solving the director's actual problem: the post must be filled by fair and open competition (in which the contractor may well compete and win), and separately offer legitimate speed — an expedited campaign, interim cover arrangements, or the contractor continuing in role via the proper commercial route until the competition concludes.
- D) Appoint the contractor on a fixed-term basis to avoid the rules applying.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Separate the director's need (continuity and speed on a sensitive programme — legitimate) from the proposed means (bypassing fair-and-open competition — not yours to grant, and "regularise later" is the phrase that precedes every appointment that ends up in an employment tribunal or an internal audit finding, with your signature as head of profession on it). Option C refuses the means and serves the need, which is the head-of pattern this entire section rehearses: an expedited but proper campaign, interim cover, or continued contractor engagement through the correct commercial channel each solve the actual problem at a small cost in convenience; and the candid footnote — the preferred candidate can compete, and strong incumbents often win open competitions fairly — usually dissolves the pressure entirely. Option A trades the profession's recruitment integrity, which you personally warrant, for one director's calendar; and every delivery manager who competed fairly for their post learns what the rules are worth. Option B escalates a request as though it were an act: the director has asked, not appointed, and the first response to an improper ask is a better path, not a report — reserve escalation for insistence after refusal. Option D is the same breach wearing procurement's clothes, and fixed-term routes have their own rules (as this guide's verbal section rehearsed). Being responsible for recruiting the right people to the right teams means being the person for whom "the right way" and "the fast way" are made, wherever possible, the same way — and where they cannot be, the right way wins.

**Question 3 (easy) — The failing delivery manager nobody warned**

Conducting your annual capability review, you discover that a delivery manager rated "partially meets" this year had received "exceeds" ratings from their line manager for two prior years — while, you now learn from multiple sources, the performance concerns were well known and simply never documented or raised with the individual. The delivery manager is shocked by the new rating and feels ambushed. What is the most effective response?

- A) Stand by the accurate rating; the truth had to land eventually.
- B) Soften the rating back toward "meets" to be fair about the ambush.
- C) Keep the honest rating but own the institutional failure that surrounds it: acknowledge explicitly to the individual that the profession failed to give them the feedback they were owed, build a properly supported development plan with realistic time to respond, and separately address the line management failure — the manager who rated "exceeds" over known concerns — as its own performance and coaching issue.
- D) Quietly move the delivery manager to a less demanding team.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Two failures are present and only one has a name on it. The rating is accurate and must stand — option B corrects an injustice by committing a fresh one, re-falsifying the record and guaranteeing this same ambush repeats in a future review — but the ambush is real: this person was denied two years of feedback they were owed, and the denial was the system's, not theirs. Option C holds both truths, and its sequencing is the craft: the acknowledgement ("we failed to tell you; that was our failure, and here is how we will support you now") converts a career-threatening shock into a recoverable development chapter, and is also simply true. The second half is the head-of obligation the scenario hides in plain sight: a line manager who rated "exceeds" over known, discussed concerns has failed at the core act of management — honest feedback — and leaving that unaddressed teaches every manager watching that kind ratings are cheaper than hard conversations, which is precisely how this case was manufactured. Option A delivers truth without justice; accuracy that arrives two years late, unaccompanied by institutional ownership, reads as persecution and poisons the community's trust in the review process itself. Option D relocates the problem, unrated and unexplained — the move without the conversation is the original sin repeated at team scale. Supporting professional development and continuous improvement of your community — your role's words — begins with a feedback culture whose failures you treat as seriously as its successes.

**Question 4 (moderate) — The cross-government working group stalemate**

You co-chair a cross-government group drafting a shared delivery standard. Four departments support the draft; one — the largest, whose adoption matters most — objects through its head of delivery, your counterpart, who argues the standard's assurance cadence is unworkable for their scale and has stopped attending, sending a junior observer instead. The publication deadline you all announced is six weeks away. What is the most effective response?

- A) Publish on schedule with four supporters; the fifth will follow eventually.
- B) Go direct to your counterpart, one-to-one: understand the objection's substance (scale concerns from the largest department are plausibly informative, not merely obstructive), explore whether a tiered or scale-adjusted cadence preserves the standard's intent while resolving their constraint, and re-engage them as co-author of the amendment rather than dissenter from the draft — using the deadline as shared pressure, moving it only if a genuinely better standard needs the time.
- C) Escalate to your counterpart's director general, noting the disengagement.
- D) Water the assurance cadence down to whatever the objecting department will accept; adoption is everything.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Read the disengagement as information: counterparts stop attending when they conclude the forum will not move — the junior observer is a placeholder that says "wake me when something changes." Option B changes something, through the only channel that reliably reopens a peer-level stalemate: direct, private, substantive engagement. Its components each carry weight. Taking the objection seriously is not concession — the largest department's scale problem may be the standard's first real stress test, and a standard that breaks at scale is not rescued by publication. The tiered-cadence exploration is the consensus-builder's core move: separate the standard's intent (assurance happens, proportionately) from its mechanism (one cadence for all) and flex the mechanism. Co-authorship is the psychological unlock — people adopt what they helped write and resist what they were outvoted on, and "highly independent stakeholders," your skill's phrase, are precisely those over whom you hold no vote worth casting. Option A publishes a "shared" standard that the most consequential department has not agreed to: the deadline is met, the standard is dead on arrival where it matters most, and cross-government forums learn your signature means four-fifths of a consensus. Option C escalates over a peer's head while their objection remains unanswered on the merits — the fastest way to convert a substantive disagreement into a permanent institutional grudge; escalation has a place, but only after the merits conversation has genuinely failed. Option D purchases adoption with the standard's substance, setting the assurance bar at the least willing participant's comfort — a standard defined by its floor. Building consensus between organisations is the named skill; its practice is option B's shape: substance first, mechanism flexible, authorship shared, escalation last.

**Question 5 (moderate) — The commercial escalation with a personal edge**

A £3m supplier dispute has escalated to you: the supplier claims scope creep and demands £400k; your senior delivery manager — who managed the contract and whom you rate highly — insists the work was in scope and is visibly invested in "winning." Reviewing the record, you find the truth is genuinely mixed: change requests were handled informally, some extra work was plausibly ordered by your side's emails, and documentation on both sides is poor. Commercial colleagues advise a negotiated settlement around £150–200k is achievable and defensible. What is the most effective response?

- A) Back your senior delivery manager fully; conceding anything undermines them and invites future claims.
- B) Settle in the advised range, and handle your senior delivery manager separately and honestly: walk the record with them, distinguish loyalty to them from endorsement of a weak position, frame the informal change-handling as the real lesson — and follow through with contract-management coaching for them and the wider community, since the failure pattern (informal variations, thin documentation) is rarely unique to one contract.
- C) Fight the claim to demonstrate the department cannot be pushed around, whatever the record shows.
- D) Settle quietly and tell the senior delivery manager it was purely a commercial decision with no reflection on their management.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The record is the decision: informal change-handling and emails plausibly ordering extra work mean the department's litigation position is genuinely weak in parts, and a £150–200k settlement against a £400k claim with mixed evidence is the outcome your commercial advisers exist to identify — resolving large or high-risk commercial issues, your named skill, means resolving them on the merits, not on morale. The scenario's real test is the second half: what you do with your senior delivery manager. Option B's separation — "I back you as a professional; I cannot back this position, and here is the record's reason" — is the only version of loyalty that survives contact with evidence, and the coaching turn converts an expensive dispute into the profession's cheapest available lesson: every delivery manager watching learns that variations get documented, and your skill's second clause ("coach others in appropriate commercial management") gets its curriculum from the case. Option A spends £3m of relationship and possibly £400k of settlement to protect a colleague from feedback they need. Option C litigates for reputation with a weak file — the outcome that actually teaches suppliers the department can be pushed around, by discovery. Option D settles correctly and lies about why: the delivery manager learns nothing, the informal-variation pattern continues on their next contract, and when they eventually read the settlement rationale — settlements are documented — they learn something worse about you. The head-of pattern: settle on the record, coach on the record, and let the community inherit the lesson instead of the liability.

**Question 6 (moderate) — The reorganisation you inherit mid-flight**

Arriving as the new head of delivery management, you inherit a half-implemented reorganisation designed by your predecessor: delivery managers are being moved from stable, long-standing team assignments into a central pool allocated to programmes quarterly. Three moves in, the evidence is discouraging — two strong delivery managers have resigned citing the churn, programme leaders complain of lost continuity, and the pool's promised flexibility benefits have not appeared. Your predecessor's design has visible executive sponsorship. What is the most effective response?

- A) Complete the implementation as designed; reversing course three moves in looks chaotic and disloyal.
- B) Reverse everything immediately and publicly restore the old model.
- C) Pause further moves on the evidence, evaluate honestly — what problem was the pool solving, is that problem real, has the design's cost (attrition, continuity loss) exceeded its benefit — and take a revised recommendation to the executive sponsor: amend, complete, or unwind, with the evidence attached; protect the people mid-move either way.
- D) Continue the moves but slow the schedule, letting the design quietly fade without a decision.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Separate three things the scenario entangles: the design (a pooling model, now generating evidence), the sponsorship (executive capital invested in it), and your accountability (organisational structures that are — your skill's exact words — "fixable and sustainable designs"). Option C honours all three. The pause is evidence-triggered, not preference-triggered: two resignations of strong people plus absent benefits is a signal no responsible steward implements through. The evaluation asks the question inheritances rarely get — what problem was this solving? — because the pool may address a real allocation rigidity that a lighter mechanism (secondments, an internal gig market, annual rebalancing) could solve without the churn; "amend" is often the answer, and it preserves the sponsor's intent while fixing the mechanism. Taking the recommendation to the sponsor with evidence is the loyalty that matters: executives are better served by "your investment is not returning; here are three options" than by completion of a failing design in their name — and making that case is exactly "make and justify decisions characterised by high levels of risk, impact and complexity." Option A implements loyalty to a plan over loyalty to its purpose, at the price of the next resignations. Option B reverses on arrival without evaluation — replacing one evidence-free structural change with another teaches the organisation that structure follows whoever last held the pen. Option D is the genuinely corrosive choice: an undecided organisation, moves neither completed nor reversed, people mid-transition indefinitely, and a decision everyone can see being avoided by the person hired to make it. Sustainable design includes sustainable decision-making about design.

**Question 7 (moderate) — The conference invitation and the vendor's shadow**

A major commercial conference invites you to keynote on government delivery transformation — prestigious, genuinely useful for championing the profession across industry, and your director supports it. Preparing, you learn the conference's headline sponsor is a supplier currently shortlisted in a live procurement your department is running, and the sponsor's marketing team has asked whether you would join a "fireside chat" on their stand and be quoted in their post-event materials. What is the most effective response?

- A) Decline the keynote entirely; the risk is not worth it.
- B) Keynote as invited — championing the role in industry is your job — but decline the sponsor's stand appearance and quotation outright, notify your commercial team of the contact given the live procurement, and have your talk's content reviewed against the procurement's boundaries so nothing you say could be read as signalling.
- C) Do the keynote and the fireside chat; refusing the sponsor would be rude, and everything is public anyway.
- D) Ask the sponsor to make a written commitment that the appearance will not influence the procurement.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Disaggregate the invitation, because its components carry entirely different risk. The keynote is your role performed in public — "represent and champion the role within your department, across government and in industry" is close to a literal description — and option A abandons a duty to avoid managing a boundary, which is not what heads of profession are for. The sponsor's asks are different in kind: a stand appearance and quotable endorsement from a senior official of a department currently evaluating that supplier is marketing material with procurement implications — it hands the supplier implied favour, hands losing bidders a challenge ground, and hands you a conflict-of-interest file, all for zero professional benefit. Option B draws the line exactly where the risk changes: perform the public duty, refuse the private capture, notify commercial colleagues (the contact itself is procurement-relevant information — evaluated suppliers approaching senior officials mid-procurement is something your commercial team must know), and pre-clear content, because innocent remarks about "transformation partners" read differently in a challenge letter. Option C's "everything is public anyway" is the tell — publicity is precisely what gives the endorsement its value to the supplier and its danger to the procurement. Option D asks the beneficiary of an impropriety to certify its absence: a written commitment from the sponsor binds nothing that matters and would itself become disclosure material. The head-of pattern: visibility is the job; capture is the risk; the discipline is knowing which parts of an invitation are which, and telling the commercial team either way.

**Question 8 (moderate) — The community survey you commissioned**

You commissioned your profession's first community survey. Results: strong scores on pride and mission; poor scores on progression ("no visible path from DM to SDM"), workload sustainability in two directorates, and — pointedly — "leadership listens" at 38% favourable. A quarter of respondents left written comments, many raw. Your people team suggests "presenting the positives prominently and handling the rest through line management." What is the most effective response?

- A) Follow the people team's advice; morale needs protecting and line management owns the issues.
- B) Publish the results to the community in full candour — strengths and failures, including the 38% — commit to two or three specific, visible actions with dates (a progression framework; a workload review in the two named directorates), explicitly not promising what you cannot deliver, and report back on those actions on schedule, making the survey-to-action loop the community's new expectation.
- C) Re-run the survey with better-designed questions before acting; the raw comments suggest methodological noise.
- D) Publish everything and invite the community to self-organise solutions; imposed fixes would contradict the listening message.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The 38% is the question answering itself: a community that doubts leadership listens is watching what you do with the first evidence it has ever handed you — the survey's handling is the listening score's next data point. Option B closes the loop that builds communities: full candour (publishing your own worst number is the only credible proof the exercise was real), selective commitment (two or three actions with dates beats ten aspirations — over-promising to a sceptical audience converts the next survey into a referendum on broken promises), and scheduled report-back, which converts "listening" from a sentiment into a mechanism. The named-directorate workload review matters specifically: generalised responses to localised problems read as evasion. Option A — leading with positives and privatising the failures — is the exact behaviour a 38% listening score predicts, performed on schedule; the community's next survey response is silence, and silence is how heads of profession lose their communities without a single resignation letter. Option C re-litigates the instrument to defer the findings — methodological refinement is legitimate for next year, but raw comments are signal, not noise, and asking again before acting once is asking the community to repeat themselves. Option D abdicates in listening's clothing: progression frameworks and directorate workload sit in leadership's gift, not the community's — inviting self-organisation on problems only you can fix is participatory theatre, and the community knows which problems are whose. Leading the community of practice and supporting its development — the role's core — is this loop, run visibly, until "leadership listens" is a boring result because it is assumed.

**Question 9 (challenging) — The head-of-peer whose team is failing your people**

Delivery managers embedded in programmes under a fellow head of profession (product) report a pattern to you: product managers in that community routinely exclude delivery managers from planning decisions, re-scope mid-sprint without consultation, and in two documented cases have represented delivery managers' concerns to programme boards inaccurately. Your peer — the head of product — is well regarded, defensive about their community, and you sit together on the departmental digital leadership team. What is the most effective response?

- A) Raise it peer-to-peer with the head of product, evidence in hand, framed as a joint professional-practice problem — "our two communities' operating pattern is failing in these specific ways; let's fix the interface together" — proposing joint interventions (shared ways-of-working guidance, paired coaching for the worst cases, a joint session for both communities), and agreeing how the two documented misrepresentation cases will be addressed; escalate to the digital leadership team only if the peer route fails.
- B) Coach your delivery managers to assert themselves; fighting their battles infantilises them.
- C) Raise the pattern at the digital leadership team first, so it is on the record before any private conversation.
- D) Issue guidance to your community documenting how to escalate product-manager overreach, creating a paper trail.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The stem describes an interface failure between two professions — and interfaces are jointly owned, which is why option A's framing ("our pattern," "let's fix the interface") is load-bearing rather than diplomatic decoration: a defensive peer met with "your people are mistreating mine" defends; met with a joint problem and joint interventions, they can engage without conceding their community is bad, which is the face-saving room consensus requires. The evidence matters (patterns and documented cases, not vibes), and the misrepresentation cases need explicit handling — inaccurate representation of a colleague's professional concerns to a governance board is conduct, not culture, and letting it dissolve into general ways-of-working work would fail the two delivery managers concerned. The escalation clause is sequenced correctly: peers first, leadership team if the peer route fails — with the leadership team's involvement then framed by the attempt that preceded it. Option B contains a truth (delivery managers should be equipped to hold their ground, and your coaching should build that) deployed as an evasion: individuals cannot coach their way out of a structural pattern backed by another profession's norms, and heads of profession exist precisely for the failures that exceed individual remedy. Option C escalates before conversing — putting a peer's community on the record in front of their leadership peers as your opening move converts a fixable interface problem into a permanent alliance problem; "on the record first" is protection you do not yet need at the price of a relationship you permanently do. Option D arms your community for a war instead of ending it: escalation guidance as first resort institutionalises the conflict, teaches both communities that the interface is adversarial, and guarantees the head of product reads it — because documents like that always travel. Solving issues between departments at the highest level, and coaching organisations on conflict resolution, means modelling the sequence: evidence, peer conversation, joint fix, escalation last.

**Question 10 (challenging) — The minister's office and the delivery truth**

Your department's flagship service is six weeks from a publicly committed launch date. Your honest assessment, based on the delivery teams' evidence, is that launching on the date carries a high probability of a visible service failure; a six-week delay would allow the fixes. The programme's SRO — under pressure from the minister's office, which has briefed the date to media — asks you, as head of delivery profession, to "get the teams to find a way," and hints that a head of profession who cannot deliver a date might be the story instead. What is the most effective response?

- A) Get the teams to find a way; ministerial commitments outrank delivery preferences, and your role depends on being seen to deliver.
- B) Give the SRO the professional assessment in writing — the specific failure probabilities, what "finding a way" would actually require and cost, and the launch-shaping options that genuinely exist (phased launch, reduced-scope launch, soft launch with controlled volumes, or six weeks) — and make clear you will support whichever informed choice the accountable owners make, while declining to alter the assessment itself; treat the personal hint as noise, not input.
- C) Go around the SRO to the permanent secretary with your concerns about political interference.
- D) Publicly align with the launch date while privately telling your delivery managers to document everything for the inevitable inquiry.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Locate the boundary the scenario is built to blur: the launch decision belongs to the SRO and ministers — accountable owners are entitled to accept delivery risk for political reasons; that is what accountability means — but the assessment of that risk belongs to the profession, and altering it under pressure is the one move that destroys the assessment's value to everyone, including the minister. Option B polices exactly that line. The written form is not defensiveness; it is decision-quality: verbal assessments soften in transmission, and an SRO carrying hard news to a minister's office needs the evidence in carriable form. The options work is the professional half of the answer — "no" is an assessment, but "here are four real versions of yes and their costs" is the profession doing its job; phased and reduced-scope launches frequently dissolve exactly this standoff by giving the political layer a date and the delivery layer a survivable scope. Supporting the informed choice, whatever it is, completes the boundary: you are not the decision's owner, and saying so protects your ability to be believed next time. The hint is treated correctly — engaging with it (negotiating your assessment against your standing) would corrupt the assessment as surely as complying. Option A converts the profession's assessment function into an amplifier of pressure: the launch fails visibly, and the inquiry finds the head of profession assessed it would and said otherwise. Option C escalates past the accountable owner before the owner has decided anything — the SRO has asked and hinted, not yet overridden; premature escalation on anticipated wrongdoing spends your gravest card on a hand not yet played. Option D is the worst construction available: public assent, private dissent, and a documentation trail prepared for a failure you are simultaneously endorsing — the inquiry-proofing that guarantees the inquiry reads it. Making and justifying high-risk decisions includes knowing precisely which decisions are yours: the assessment, its integrity, and its delivery in usable form — on the record, with options attached.

**Question 11 (challenging) — The succession you don't want to need**

You learn confidentially that you are likely to be asked to lead a two-year cross-government programme, leaving your head-of post within six months. Honest stocktake: your two most senior delivery managers are both strong but neither is ready — one is technically superb but avoids the political layer; the other is politically adept but has never run the community or the recruitment machinery. No succession plan exists on paper; the community's institutional knowledge lives substantially in you. What is the most effective response?

- A) Say nothing until the move is confirmed; premature succession talk destabilises, and the move may not happen.
- B) Start succession work now, framed as institutional resilience rather than departure planning (which is simply true — heads of profession should always be one bus away from continuity): document the role's machinery, deliberately develop both candidates against their specific gaps — the technical one into political exposure, the political one into community and recruitment leadership — create structures that hold knowledge institutionally (deputies, documented processes, a community leadership group), and brief your own director on the resilience work without disclosing the unconfirmed move.
- C) Pick your preferred successor now and begin transferring the role to them quietly.
- D) Recommend the department recruit your successor externally, since neither internal candidate is ready.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The scenario's trap is treating succession as an event to be timed rather than a capability to be built — option A's logic ("may not happen") concedes that the profession currently depends on one person's continued presence, which is an organisational design flaw whether or not you leave; the confidentiality constraint is real, but it constrains disclosure of the move, not work on resilience, and option B's framing resolves the tension honestly because bus-factor resilience is a defensible, indeed overdue, initiative on its own merits. The development design is the head-of craft: each candidate's gap is specific and closable in six months of deliberate exposure — the technical SDM chairs the next cross-departmental negotiation with you observing; the political SDM runs this year's recruitment campaign and community programme — and developing both is not indecision but option preservation plus a stronger profession regardless of who succeeds (the "loser" of a two-candidate development race, treated well, is the successor's deputy; the loser of a quiet anointment, discovered, is a resignation). Institutionalising the knowledge — deputies, documentation, a community leadership group — attacks the deeper dependency: your role summary's own accountabilities (community, recruitment, capability) should survive any individual, including you, and building that is exemplifying what good looks like at the organisational scale. Option C converts succession into patronage: unadvertised transfer of a senior role pre-empts fair competition (the Question 2 principle, now applied to yourself) and gambles everything on one unready person. Option D outsources the answer before attempting development — external recruitment may ultimately be right and the eventual competition will test it, but recommending it while declining to develop two strong internals is a verdict on your own capability-building, delivered by you, about the community you lead. The through-line of this entire assessment, surfacing one last time: the head of profession's product is not their own performance but the capability that remains when they leave the room — succession is simply that product, audited.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Choosing least effective: the profession under pressure**

A departmental efficiency review proposes cutting your profession's central functions — community programme, capability development, recruitment support — by 60%, on the argument that "delivery managers deliver in programmes; the centre is overhead." You have four weeks to respond. Which response is LEAST effective?

- A) Build the evidence case: quantify what the central functions produce (time-to-fill and its contractor-premium cost, capability progression, attrition against benchmark, the knowledge-retention arithmetic of a 15%-attrition profession) and present the 60% cut's specific consequences in the review's own financial language.
- B) Mobilise legitimate allies: brief the programme directors who consume your profession's output on what the cut means for their delivery, so the review hears from the centre's customers, not only its owner.
- C) Accept the framing privately and concentrate on protecting your own position and team assignments in the restructured organisation, since reviews of this kind are usually decided in advance.
- D) Engage the review's premise directly and constructively: concede what is genuinely true in it (any centre accumulates activities worth pruning), offer a self-designed 20–25% reshaping that cuts the weakest activities and protects the highest-value ones, and contest the remaining 35–40% with the evidence.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Least-effective format: three options form the complete playbook for defending organisational capability, one abandons the field. Options A, B, and D are the triad in proper sequence — evidence in the review's own currency (A: this guide's numeric section, deployed for its true purpose — the £46k recruitment return, the 0.85³ knowledge-decay arithmetic, the funnel diagnostics are precisely the "overhead" being priced), third-party validation (B: a centre defended only by its head is presumed self-interested; the same case from programme directors who consume the capability is disinterested testimony, and briefing them is advocacy through legitimate channels, not lobbying — they are genuinely affected parties), and strategic concession (D: the most sophisticated move in the set, because "overhead" reviews expect wholesale resistance, and a head of profession who arrives having already cut the defensible 20% has purchased credibility for defending the rest — conceding the premise's true fraction is what makes contesting its false fraction believable). Option C fails on every axis at once: it mistakes fatalism for realism (reviews are decided in advance exactly as often as nobody contests them competently), it converts the head of profession into a private negotiator for their own chair while the community's capability — the thing the role exists to steward — is dismantled unresisted, and it teaches the community, which will learn how this went, the precise lesson that ends professions: that their leadership defends itself and not them. Note, finally, what the option's quiet privacy signals: every other option in this section has been performed in the open, on the record, with evidence — the consistent grammar of the head-of role. The least effective option in the entire guide is the one performed in private, for an audience of one. Representing and championing the role within your department — the first accountability on your role's list — is never more literal than in the four weeks this scenario describes.

### Preparation tips

- **Judge every option at three orders.** First order: does it solve the scenario? Second: what does it teach the community, the peer, the supplier watching? Third: what norm does it set for the hundred cases you will never see? Head-of scoring lives at the second and third orders.
- **Practise refusing forced choices.** The strongest options repeatedly hold two goods at once — standards and the relationship, the honest rating and the institutional apology, the keynote and the boundary. When a scenario offers you a sacrifice, look for the option that declines it.
- **Sequence before selecting.** Peer conversation before leadership-table escalation; evidence before settlement; development before external recruitment; conversation before report. Many wrong options are right actions taken out of order.
- **Separate the decision from the assessment, and know which is yours.** The SRO owns the launch; you own the assessment's integrity. The director owns the urgency; you own the recruitment rules. Boundary clarity is the head-of competency the hardest scenarios test.
- **Rehearse the strategic concession.** "Here is what is true in your criticism, here is what I have already acted on, and here is the evidence against the rest" — practise this structure until it is available under pressure; it is the most consistently underused senior move.
- **Use your real inbox as the item bank.** Every escalation, invitation, review, and survey in your actual role is one of these questions with the options unwritten. Draft the four options yourself, including the seductive wrong ones — the ability to write the distractor is the proof you will not choose it.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Protecting individuals at the community's expense — or the reverse.** The star's behaviour, the unready successor, the ambushed delivery manager: every case demands both the person and the system be served, and options sacrificing either are planted as tests.
- **Escalating past the conversation.** Reporting the director's ask, board-tabling the peer's community, going over the SRO's head — each skips the direct engagement that resolves most cases and poisons the relationship that survives all of them.
- **Purchasing adoption, calm, or approval with substance.** Watered standards, softened ratings, altered assessments, positives-first survey handling: the currency is always integrity, and the exchange rate is always ruinous.
- **Quiet moves in a role defined by visible ones.** Unadvertised appointments, fading reorganisations, private self-protection, undisclosed transfers: at head-of level, the option performed in private for convenience is almost always the trap.
- **Treating structural failures as individual ones** — coaching people to survive a broken interface, rating people caught in a feedback vacuum, blaming programmes for a pool design. Fix the structure you own; then develop the people in it.
- **Forgetting you are the precedent.** Every response in this section is watched, repeated, and cited by a community that calibrates on its head of profession. The recurring question behind every stem — answer it before choosing — is: what happens when everyone does what I am about to do?

## Conclusion

Congratulations on completing this guide — the most demanding in this series, worked at the altitude where your profession's decisions are made.

Consider what you have practised. In the cognitive section, you diagnosed structure before people, ran coverage rules through reorganisations, reconciled workforce plans against their own arithmetic, caught Simpson's-paradox reversals and self-selection effects, read commitment matrices for what was firm rather than announced, and reasoned about irreversibility when the evidence would not improve before the deadline. In the verbal section, you read instruments at design level, held regulatory silence as silence, kept commissioned reviews distinct from adverse findings, weighed audit language fairly, and corrected confident misreadings with the text rather than with rank. And in the situational judgement section, you rehearsed the stewardship calls themselves: correcting stars privately while fixing rooms structurally, refusing recruitment shortcuts while solving the urgency, settling on the record and coaching from it, holding the assessment line under political pressure, and building the succession that makes your own presence optional.

That last phrase is the guide's true through-line, and the role's. At every previous level of this profession, the practitioner's product is delivery. At yours, the product is capability that persists — a community that develops its own people, standards that survive their authors, knowledge that outlives the 15% who leave each year, and a reference implementation of professional judgement that dozens of delivery managers calibrate against without your knowing. Every question here was secretly about that: what remains when you leave the room.

Three uses for what you have done. First, revisit in a month and attempt the hardest items cold — the least-effective stems and the boundary questions especially; spaced retrieval is as true for judgement as for arithmetic. Second, deploy the instruments on live work this quarter: run the funnel ratios on your next campaign, the double-count audit on your next savings return, the design-level read on the next instrument that crosses your desk, and the strategic concession the next time your centre is called overhead. Keep assessment itself in proportion, as you would counsel anyone in your community: a test samples reasoning on one day, and everything it samples is trainable — that is the premise you have just spent many thousands of words confirming.
