# Content Designer - Psychometric Assessment Resources

## Introduction

Welcome. This guide is written for you as a content designer in the UK Government Digital and Data profession — someone responsible for creating, evaluating, updating and reviewing content at all stages of the end-to-end user journey. Your role description says you are comfortable using evidence, data and research to make content decisions, that you build relationships across government to focus on user needs and influence stakeholders, that you contribute to and use style guides and content patterns, and that you engage with the cross-government content community. That is a role defined by judgement and influence as much as by craft.

You may be preparing for a formal assessment as part of a recruitment or promotion process, or you may simply want to sharpen the reasoning your job depends on. Either way, it is worth being precise about what psychometric assessment is at this level. It is not an intelligence test and it is not a knowledge quiz. It is a standardised sample of the thinking a role demands, and for a competent practitioner that thinking has a particular character: you are rarely choosing between a right answer and a wrong one, and usually choosing between defensible options under incomplete information, competing constraints and real consequences.

Three features of your role shape the questions here. First, you are comfortable using evidence to make content decisions — which means the assessment will test whether you know what a piece of evidence supports, not merely whether you can read it. Second, you influence stakeholders across government, which is a negotiating skill resting on your evidence being unassailable and your reasoning being explicable. Third, you work across the end-to-end journey, which means the unit you are responsible for is rarely the page in front of you: it is the sequence of things a person reads, including the letter, the email, the error message and the thing the contact centre says.

There is a discipline-specific point worth naming. Content design is routinely mistaken for writing, and much of what you actually do is deciding what should exist. The strongest content decisions are often deletions, reorderings, or the recognition that a page should not exist because the question it answers should never have arisen. Several items below turn on that, because it is where content design earns most of its value and where it is most often prevented from doing so.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the reasoning your role genuinely uses, presented through practical materials rather than abstract puzzles. At your level the materials get messier and the reasoning less mechanical. You will meet evidence supporting two incompatible readings, constraints that turn out to be negotiable and others that do not, and prioritisation problems with no dominant option.

The format is usually an online test of fifteen to thirty minutes covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective against a norm group at a comparable level. Many platforms are adaptive, so a test that feels hard is usually one you are doing well on, and acuity reports distinguishing speed from accuracy reward precision.

Two things distinguish this section at your level.

The first is that you are expected to reason about causes rather than only symptoms. A page generating phone calls, a search term appearing constantly, a step where people abandon — each is a symptom, and the useful question is what produces it. Frequently the answer is not in the content at all: it is in a policy that cannot be explained simply because it is not simple, in a form asking for something people do not have, or in a letter sent three weeks earlier that set the wrong expectation. Content designers who can trace a symptom to its source solve problems that rewriting cannot touch.

The second is comfort with irreducible uncertainty. Several questions have no clean answer, only a best one, and the discriminating factor is whether you can identify the strongest reading of ambiguous evidence and state its limits.

You need to memorise nothing. What you need is the discipline not to let familiarity substitute for examination.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Auditing against a standard**

Your standard requires every transactional page to: use words users would use, say what happens next, state how long it will take, avoid asking for information already held, and offer a route to help. A page uses internal terminology, says the case will be assessed, gives no timescale, re-requests a held reference number, and has a "contact us" link in the footer. How many requirements are unmet?

- A) 2
- B) 3
- C) 4
- D) 5

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Work through all five. Internal terminology fails the first. "The case will be assessed" satisfies the second, minimally. No timescale fails the third. Re-requesting held information fails the fourth. A "contact us" link in the footer does provide a route to help, so the fifth is met — thinly, since a footer link is not the same as help offered at the point of difficulty, but the requirement as written is satisfied. Three unmet, giving B. Option C typically comes from rejecting the footer link, which is a defensible content judgement and the wrong audit judgement: the discipline is to apply the standard as written, and if a footer link satisfies your standard then the standard is what needs changing. That distinction matters when you are contributing to a style guide, because it tells you which problems are compliance problems and which are standard problems.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction from a publishing rule**

Your rule states: "Content may be published without second review only if it is a factual correction approved by the content lead." A colleague says: "I published a page this morning without second review." Which must be true?

- A) The rule was breached.
- B) It was a factual correction approved by the content lead.
- C) Either it was an approved factual correction, or the rule was breached.
- D) The page contains an error.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The rule is conditional with a stated exception, and the facts do not tell you whether the exception applies. Options A and B each assume a branch. Option D imports a claim nothing supports. Only C is guaranteed. Note the slightly different construction from the usual pattern: this rule is phrased as "only if", which makes the exception the *sole* route to publishing without review rather than one exception among several — so the disjunction is exactly two branches with no third possibility. Recognising the logical shape of "only if" as distinct from "unless" is worth a moment, because both appear in governance prose and they place the exception in different positions.

**Question 3 (moderate) — Tracing a symptom to its cause**

A page explaining eligibility generates the highest contact volume in the service. It is well written, follows the style guide, and tested well in research. What is the strongest analysis?

- A) The page must have a problem the research missed.
- B) A page that reads well and still generates calls is often answering the question correctly for a rule that is genuinely complicated, or arriving after users have formed an expectation elsewhere — so the useful next step is to find out what callers actually ask, which may point at the policy, at an earlier letter, or at a search result that brings people here with the wrong question.
- C) The contact centre should be given a script.
- D) The page should be rewritten more simply.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The most informative fact here is the combination: well written, well tested, and still generating calls. That combination is what rules out the obvious explanations and points at something outside the page. Option B names the realistic candidates and, crucially, identifies the cheapest way to distinguish them — asking what callers actually say, which the contact centre already knows and almost nobody asks. If callers ring to ask whether their specific situation qualifies, the rule is too conditional to be explained in prose and needs a different content form entirely. If they ring having read a letter that said something different, the fix is in the letter. Option A assumes the research was wrong before checking anything cheaper. Option C treats the symptom. Option D proposes rewriting a page that already tests well, which is where a great deal of content effort is wasted.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Prioritising by what can change**

You have capacity for one substantial piece of work this quarter:

1. A new service's core journey content is being written next month and will set the pattern for eight related pages.
2. A live page has generated steady contact volume for a year.
3. A policy team wants exploratory work on a concept for next year.
4. The team wants to revisit a page you wrote six weeks ago after minor design changes.

Which is strongest, and why?

- A) Item 2, because it affects users now.
- B) Item 1, because the decisions are imminent, will set a pattern for eight pages, and content input is the only thing that could still shape them.
- C) Item 3, because early work has the highest leverage.
- D) Item 4, because unverified changes are a risk.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The right frame is how much a decision changes as a result of your input, and whether there is time. Item 1 scores highest on both, with a multiplier the others lack: content decisions made once and repeated across eight pages establish either a good pattern or a bad one eight times, and undoing an established pattern later is far harder than getting it right now — partly because eight pages need changing and partly because people will have started defending it. Item 2 matters and has been stable for a year, so deferring costs comparatively little, though it should be next. Item 3 has no deadline pressure. Item 4 is weakest. Option A ranks by current impact and misses the compounding effect, which is precisely the strategic thinking your role names.

**Question 5 (moderate) — What the evidence supports**

Nine of twelve participants did not understand a phrase on a page. Which conclusion is best supported?

- A) 75% of users do not understand it.
- B) The phrase is a substantial problem, evidenced across most participants, and should be replaced with wording that says specifically what is meant — with a follow-up check on whether the replacement is understood.
- C) The phrase should be deleted.
- D) The page is badly written.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Nine of twelve is a strong qualitative signal, strong enough to act on, and B states it at that strength while specifying what acting looks like — including the follow-up, which is what distinguishes a change from an improvement. Option A converts twelve participants into a population rate, the commonest overreach in content reporting and the one most likely to be quoted back at you. Option C proposes deletion without asking whether the information is needed; a phrase can be badly worded and load-bearing. Option D generalises from one phrase to the whole page, which is both unsupported and unhelpful to whoever has to act on it. Note the precision available in B: participants did not understand the phrase, which points at replacement wording, whereas "the page is confusing" points nowhere.

**Question 6 (moderate) — A pattern across sources**

Search on the service shows "how long" as the top term. Contact centre data shows "when will I hear" as the top call reason. Research participants asked about timescales unprompted in seven of nine sessions. The service does publish a timescale, on a page two clicks from the journey. What is the strongest conclusion?

- A) The timescale needs to be more prominent.
- B) Three independent sources converge on the same need, and the timescale exists but is not where users look — so the fix is placement rather than creation, and the strongest version puts it at the moment the question arises rather than on a page users must seek out.
- C) The research is unnecessary given the analytics.
- D) The timescale page should be promoted in navigation.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Three sources of different kinds converging is unusually strong evidence, and the fourth fact — that the content exists — is what makes the problem tractable, because a placement problem is far cheaper to fix than a content one. Option B says both and adds the substantive refinement: prominence is not the same as placement, and the effective answer is usually to put the timescale in the confirmation message, the email and the letter, at the moment the user forms the question, rather than to make a separate page easier to find. Option A is close and stops short of that distinction. Option D proposes a navigation change, which is the weakest version of prominence — users experiencing a wait do not navigate, they search or ring. Option C dismisses the qualitative source that told you people ask unprompted, which is the fact establishing that the question is spontaneous rather than prompted by a confusing page.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Designing under a constraint**

A legal team requires the phrase "you may be liable to a financial penalty" to appear verbatim. Research shows users do not understand what it means or when it applies. What is the strongest approach?

- A) Use the phrase and accept the outcome.
- B) Establish whether the requirement is that the phrase appears or that nothing else appears, since those are different — then keep the phrase and immediately explain it in plain words, giving the amount and the trigger.
- C) Replace it with plain language.
- D) Link to the legislation.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The strongest approach separates two things that get conflated: a requirement that certain wording appears, and a prohibition on saying anything else. Legal requirements are almost always the first and almost never the second, which means the apparent deadlock usually dissolves once somebody asks — and asking costs one email. Option B does that and specifies what the explanation must contain: the amount and the trigger, because "you may be liable to a financial penalty" leaves a user unable to judge either how much or what would cause it, which is the entire information content of the sentence. Option A publishes something demonstrably not understood. Option C proposes overriding a legal requirement, which will be refused and will cost you credibility for the constraints that are negotiable. Option D helps nobody who cannot read legislation.

**Question 8 (harder) — Scoping a flaw**

You are relying on a study whose participants were all recruited through the service's own online panel. Four findings: (i) participants did not understand "relevant supporting evidence"; (ii) participants found the page easy to navigate; (iii) participants completed the journey in under ten minutes; (iv) participants did not notice a link in the footer. Which findings are most compromised by the recruitment?

- A) All four.
- B) None; the sample is adequate.
- C) Findings (ii) and (iii) are most compromised, because a panel recruited through the online service consists of people already comfortable with it, which flatters navigation and speed — while (i) and (iv) concern comprehension of a phrase and noticing an element, which are less dependent on prior familiarity though not wholly independent of it.
- D) Only (i).

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The skill is scoping precisely rather than treating a sampling flaw as total or negligible. A panel recruited through the online service consists of people who have used it and volunteered afterwards — a group filtered twice for digital confidence and goodwill. That most affects the findings about ease and speed, which are exactly what familiarity produces. Comprehension of a specific phrase is less dependent on familiarity: an unfamiliar legal term is unfamiliar to most people regardless. The footer link is intermediate, since experienced users may know it is there. Option C says all of this, including the honest qualifier that (i) and (iv) are not wholly unaffected. Option A discards findings that survive. Option B leaves two flattered findings in circulation, and flattered findings are more dangerous than absent ones because they generate confidence.

**Question 9 (harder) — Content that should not exist**

A page exists solely to explain how to correct a mistake users commonly make on the preceding form. It is well used and well rated. What is the strongest analysis?

- A) The page is doing its job and should be maintained.
- B) A well-used page explaining how to fix a common mistake is evidence of a defect upstream: the form is producing the mistake, and the highest-value content work is on the form itself, after which this page should shrink or disappear.
- C) The page should be made more prominent so more people find it.
- D) The page should be merged into the form.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This is the reasoning that most distinguishes content design from writing, and it is uncomfortable because it means the correct recommendation is to make your own successful page unnecessary. A page that exists to help people recover from a mistake is a symptom; the mistake is the problem. Heavy use is not evidence the page is succeeding — it is a measure of how often the upstream defect occurs. Option B names that and points the work where it belongs. Option A maintains the symptom indefinitely. Option C makes the recovery route easier while leaving the failure rate untouched, which is a real if second-best intervention and is frequently mistaken for a fix. Option D is closer, and merging recovery content into a form usually makes the form longer without preventing the mistake; the actual fix is normally a clearer label, a better input format, or accepting the input people naturally give.

**Question 10 (harder) — Two metrics moving apart**

After a rewrite, average time on a page fell from 90 to 50 seconds. Contact centre calls about that step rose 22% and completion was unchanged. What is the strongest analysis?

- A) The rewrite succeeded on its metric; the calls need separate investigation.
- B) The pattern is consistent with content having been removed that users needed, pushing them to telephone for the explanation — an apparent efficiency that is probably a cost transfer, with unchanged completion suggesting no benefit at all; the right measure is end-to-end effort including contact.
- C) The call increase must have another cause.
- D) Report both without interpretation.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Three facts fit together: the page got shorter, calls rose, and completion did not move. The most economical explanation is that removed content is content users now obtain by telephone, so the saving has been transferred rather than realised — and the unchanged completion is decisive, because a genuine improvement should have moved it. Option B names the mechanism and the measurement error beneath it. This matters particularly in content design because "shorter is better" is a widely held and mostly correct heuristic that is wrong in a specific case: shortening by removing information users need is not simplification, it is omission, and it looks identical in a word count. Option A accepts a metric because it was agreed. Option C asserts an alternative cause without evidence. Option D abdicates the interpretation your role exists to supply.

**Question 11 (harder) — Conflicting evidence**

A satisfaction survey of 1,200 users reports 81% positive about a service's guidance. Your ten sessions found seven participants struggling to understand it. The contact centre reports guidance queries as its second-highest call driver. How should you synthesise this?

- A) Trust the survey; 1,200 responses outweigh ten sessions.
- B) The sources see different populations and measure different things: satisfaction surveys are typically served at the end of a journey, so people who gave up rarely see them, and satisfaction with an outcome is not comprehension of guidance — while contact centre volume is behavioural evidence at scale that corroborates the sessions. The picture is guidance most people get through, many find hard, and a substantial minority need help with.
- C) The sources conflict irreconcilably.
- D) The guidance is performing well with minor issues.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The conflict dissolves once you note who each source sees and what it measures. End-of-journey satisfaction surveys reach only people who reached the end, and asking whether someone is satisfied is different from asking whether they understood. Your sessions observed comprehension. The contact centre data is the pivot and the most useful element for a content designer: high call volume on guidance is behavioural evidence, at scale, that the guidance is not doing its job — and it is expressed in a currency, cost per call, that sceptical colleagues find credible in a way they do not always find seven of ten participants. Option A defers to scale over relevance. Option C abandons an available reconciliation. Option D is the comfortable reading and ignores the contact centre.

**Question 12 (harder) — A flaw in your own work**

Three weeks after a rewrite you supported, you realise the research behind it recruited only users who had completed the service successfully. What should you do?

- A) Note it as a limitation next time.
- B) Tell the team and decision-makers now, scope precisely which conclusions are affected and which stand, and propose targeted follow-up with users who did not complete — since those are the people the study could not see and the ones the content most needs to serve.
- C) Quietly commission follow-up and correct the record if findings differ.
- D) Say nothing; the rewrite was probably an improvement anyway.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A sample of successful completers cannot see the failures the content was meant to address, and a rewrite has already gone live on that basis. Option B does the three things that matter: it tells the people who acted on the finding, it scopes the damage precisely rather than minimising or catastrophising, and it proposes the fix. The scoping matters — conclusions about how successful users read the page may remain sound, and saying so is accuracy rather than defensiveness. Option A defers past usefulness. Option C conceals a known flaw while the content stands. Option D is least defensible: reasoning that the rewrite was probably an improvement substitutes your guess for the evidence you relied on, and if it emerges later, the concealment rather than the sampling error is what people will remember.

### Preparation tips

- **Ask what produces a symptom.** A page generating calls may be a policy problem, a letter problem, or a form problem.
- **Ask who could not have appeared in this evidence.** Panel recruitment is the version of that question you will meet most.
- **Scope flaws precisely.** Say which findings a sampling problem compromises and which survive.
- **Notice when a page exists because something upstream is broken.** Heavy use of a recovery page measures the failure rate.
- **Weight decisions by repetition.** Content setting a pattern for eight pages is worth eight times the attention.
- **Watch for metrics moving in opposite directions.** Shorter is usually better and is not always simpler.
- **Separate "this wording must appear" from "nothing else may appear".**
- **Read the stem's final sentence twice.**

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Converting participants into percentages.** "Nine of twelve participants" must never become "75% of users".
- **Rewriting a page that already tests well.** The problem is probably elsewhere.
- **Applying a standard as you would have written it.** If a footer link satisfies it, change the standard.
- **Treating heavy use as success.** Sometimes it measures how often something upstream fails.
- **Accepting "shorter is better" without checking what was removed.**
- **Deferring to survey scale over relevance.** Who answered matters more than how many.
- **Proposing deletion of a badly worded phrase that is load-bearing.**
- **Overriding a legal requirement rather than asking what it actually requires.**

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A numeric reasoning assessment measures how accurately you handle the quantitative material your role produces and consumes. At your level the emphasis shifts from calculating to interrogating. You will still need to compute rates correctly, but the harder items ask whether a figure means what somebody claims — which is, not coincidentally, the question you are often the only person in the room positioned to ask.

The format is typically a timed online test of fifteen to thirty minutes, each item presenting a table, chart or paragraph of figures with a multiple-choice question. Calculators are usually provided and the arithmetic is deliberately tractable. Time per item is tight, so the operative skill is locating what matters and disregarding what does not.

There is a professional stake worth naming. Quantitative evidence carries unearned authority: a number in a slide is treated as fact and a content argument as a matter of taste, regardless of the rigour behind either. Content designers who cannot interrogate numbers confidently lose arguments they should win — and the arguments they lose are frequently about whether content work is worth doing at all, because content improvements are hard to attribute and easy to dismiss. Your role names being comfortable using evidence and data to make content decisions, and that comfort has to extend to challenging somebody else's numbers as well as reading your own.

Four habits carry the load. Establish what the question asks before looking at the data. Identify the denominator and the population it represents. Estimate, calculate, compare. And sanity-check against the world.

Distractors are built from a predictable catalogue: wrong denominator, percentage confused with percentage point, new value used as the base in a percentage change, rates averaged without weighting, raw counts compared across unequal groups.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The numerical skills tested map onto your named skills:

- **Reading analytics, search and contact data** maps to **User focus (content design)**, where insights from evidence drive content decisions.
- **Comparing rates fairly** maps to **User-centred content design**, where deciding which content to fix first depends on comparing pages honestly.
- **Measuring whether a change worked** maps to **Agile working (content design)**, where responsiveness to feedback means knowing whether the feedback loop closed.
- **Quantifying scale** maps to **Strategic thinking (content design)**, where making the case for content work depends on expressing its value in numbers other people recognise.
- **Presenting figures at defensible strength** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management (content design)**.
- **Detecting misleading metrics** maps to **User focus**, where a wrong measure sends content work to the wrong page.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Feedback rate**

Of 3,600 users who answered a feedback prompt, 900 said they did not find what they needed. What percentage?

- A) 20%
- B) 25%
- C) 30%
- D) 40%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 900 ÷ 3,600 = 0.25, so 25%, and the answer is B. Estimate first: 900 of 3,600 is a quarter. Options A and C come from dividing by 4,500 and 3,000. Worth noting what this figure is: the percentage of people who *answered the prompt*, not of everyone who saw the page. Feedback prompts are answered disproportionately by people with a strong reaction in either direction, which makes the absolute level unreliable and the comparison between pages far more useful — a habit worth carrying into how you report it.

**Question 2 (easy) — Comparing pages fairly**

| Page | Views | Negative feedback |
|---|---|---|
| A | 20,000 | 600 |
| B | 4,000 | 480 |
| C | 10,000 | 300 |

Which page has the highest negative rate, and what is it?

- A) Page A, 3%
- B) Page B, 12%
- C) Page C, 3%
- D) Page A, 30%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Convert to rates. Page A: 600 ÷ 20,000 = 3%. Page B: 480 ÷ 4,000 = 12%. Page C: 300 ÷ 10,000 = 3%. Page B is four times worse, giving B. The raw counts tell the opposite story — Page A has the most negative responses — which is the trap. Both readings answer different questions and a good report gives both: Page A disappoints more people in total, Page B disappoints a much higher proportion of those who reach it. If you can fix one, the choice depends on whether you are reducing total harm or fixing the page that is most clearly broken, and saying so explicitly is more useful than picking one silently and being challenged later.

**Question 3 (easy) — Reading a contact log**

A month of contact centre calls about a service: "when will I hear" 2,400, "what documents" 1,500, "how do I cancel" 400, "fee query" 400, "other" 1,300. What percentage of calls are about timescales?

- A) 30%
- B) 35%
- C) 40%
- D) 45%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Find the total first: 2,400 + 1,500 = 3,900, plus 400 = 4,300, plus 400 = 4,700, plus 1,300 = 6,000. Then 2,400 ÷ 6,000 = 0.4, which is 40%, giving C. The step people skip is computing the total, because the question supplies a list rather than a sum. The finding is worth dwelling on: two in five calls to this service are asking when something will happen, which is a content problem with a one-sentence solution, and quantifying it that way is how a sentence gets prioritised over a redesign.

**Question 4 (easy) — Scale of a content fix**

A service handles 400,000 applications a year. Adding a timescale to the confirmation page could plausibly remove the 40% of contact volume that asks about timescales. If the service receives 6,000 calls a month, how many calls a year might that avoid?

- A) 2,400
- B) 28,800
- C) 72,000
- D) 240,000

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 40% of 6,000 monthly calls is 2,400 a month, and 2,400 × 12 = 28,800 a year, giving B. Option A stops at the monthly figure — the single most common error in this question type, because the calculation feels finished before the annualisation. Option C multiplies the total call volume by twelve without applying the 40%. The reason this matters is that 28,800 calls a year is a figure a finance colleague can put a unit cost against, which converts a content change into a business case. Content work is chronically under-prioritised because its value is hard to express in numbers other people use; this arithmetic is the cheapest available remedy.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Did the change work?**

Before a rewrite, 1,200 of 3,000 users answered a feedback prompt negatively. After, 900 of 3,600 did. Did the page improve, and by how much in percentage points?

- A) Yes, by 300 responses
- B) Yes, by 15 percentage points
- C) Yes, by 25 percentage points
- D) No, it got worse

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Convert to rates because the totals differ. Before: 1,200 ÷ 3,000 = 40%. After: 900 ÷ 3,600 = 25%. The improvement is 15 percentage points, giving B. Option A compares raw counts across different-sized groups, and note the direction of the distortion here: more people answered the prompt after the change, so the raw fall of 300 understates the improvement rather than overstating it. That is worth registering, because the usual warning about raw counts assumes they flatter, and they can equally conceal a genuine win — which is a good reason to convert every time rather than only when you suspect a problem.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Percentage change**

Views of a guidance page fell from 60,000 to 45,000 a month after a form was simplified. What is the percentage decrease, and is it necessarily bad?

- A) 25% decrease, and it is bad
- B) 25% decrease, and it may be good — if the form no longer produces the confusion the guidance existed to resolve, fewer people needing it is the intended outcome
- C) 33% decrease, and it is bad
- D) 33% decrease, and it may be good

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The difference is 15,000, and 15,000 ÷ 60,000 = 0.25, a 25% decrease, so options C and D miscalculate by dividing by the new figure. Between A and B, the interpretation decides it. A fall in views of guidance that exists to resolve confusion is exactly what you would expect if the confusion has been removed, and treating page views as a success measure for that kind of content gets the sign backwards. Option B says so. This is worth holding on to because content designers are routinely asked to justify their work in traffic terms, and for a significant class of content the honest answer is that success looks like decline — which needs saying before the numbers arrive, not after.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Working back to a base**

A colleague says 15% of users abandon at the evidence question, and that this is 5,400 people a month. How many reach the evidence question monthly?

- A) 6,210
- B) 8,100
- C) 36,000
- D) 81,000

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** If 15% equals 5,400, the total is 5,400 ÷ 0.15 = 36,000, giving C. Stepwise: 15% is 5,400, so 5% is 1,800, and 100% is 36,000. Verify by reversing — 15% of 36,000 is 5,400. Option A adds 15%; option B is 5,400 × 1.5. This matters because colleagues quote percentages far more readily than bases, and 15% of 36,000 is a different problem from 15% of 360. Establishing the base in ten seconds lets you judge whether a figure deserves the weight it is being given, and it is one of the fastest ways to be useful in a meeting without knowing anything nobody else knows.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Choosing the denominator**

A journey shows 50,000 start, 40,000 reach the evidence page, 30,000 reach the declaration, 28,500 submit. What percentage of those who reached the declaration submitted?

- A) 57%
- B) 60%
- C) 71%
- D) 95%

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** The stem names users who reached the declaration, so the denominator is 30,000 and the part is 28,500: 28,500 ÷ 30,000 = 0.95, giving D. Option A uses 50,000, answering a whole-journey question nobody asked. This is the most common trap in funnel data and it has a real consequence for where content effort goes: 95% of people who reach the declaration submit, so the declaration is not the problem. The losses are 10,000 between the start and the evidence page and 10,000 between evidence and declaration, and those two stretches are where content work belongs.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Weighted comparison**

Three pages in a guide have negative feedback rates of 5% on 20,000 views, 12% on 5,000 views, and 8% on 5,000 views. What is the overall negative rate across all three pages?

- A) 6.7%
- B) 7.5%
- C) 8.3%
- D) 9%

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Convert to counts before combining. Page one: 5% of 20,000 = 1,000 negative responses. Page two: 12% of 5,000 = 600. Page three: 8% of 5,000 = 400. Total 2,000 across 30,000 views, so 2,000 ÷ 30,000 = 0.0667, which is about 6.7% and the answer is A. Option C is the unweighted mean of 5, 12 and 8, which is 8.33 and over-weights the two small pages against the 20,000-view page that should dominate. Always return to counts when combining rates, because the unweighted average answers a question nobody asked — what the average *page* does, rather than what the average *user* experiences. Those two figures diverge whenever traffic is unevenly distributed, which is almost always, and the second is nearly always the one a decision needs.

**Question 10 (harder) — A metric that hides a split**

Average time on a guidance page is 40 seconds. Your sessions showed participants either glancing for a few seconds or reading carefully for two to three minutes. What is the most useful response?

- A) The average is wrong.
- B) The average is probably correct and uninformative: it sits between two clusters and describes neither, so ask for the distribution — which will show whether the page has two audiences, and a single change may help one while harming the other.
- C) The participants were unrepresentative.
- D) The page should be shorter.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** When behaviour splits into two clusters the mean lands in the gap and describes nobody. Forty seconds is entirely consistent with half the users spending five seconds and half spending two and a half minutes, and those are different problems: glancers may be skipping content they need, while careful readers may be struggling with it. Option B asks for the distribution and names the consequence that matters for content work — a single edit may serve one group at the other's expense, which is the argument for structure rather than length. Shortening the page helps the glancers and removes what the readers needed; adding a summary at the top serves both. Option A disputes a figure that is probably correct. Option C blames the research. Option D is the change that looks obvious and may be exactly wrong.

**Question 11 (harder) — Percentage points and relative change**

A page's usefulness score rose from 45% to 54% after a rewrite. Which statement is accurate?

- A) A 9% increase.
- B) A 9 percentage point increase, equivalent to a 20% relative increase.
- C) A 20 percentage point increase.
- D) A 16.7% increase.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The arithmetic gap is 54 − 45 = 9 percentage points. The relative increase is 9 ÷ 45 = 0.2, or 20%. Option B labels both correctly. Option A is ambiguous and generally read as relative. Option C misapplies the percentage-point label. Option D divides by the new value. The professional stake is unusually direct for content designers, because you frequently write the sentence these numbers appear in. That puts you in the position of choosing which framing a report carries, and the honest practice is to give both — partly because it is accurate and partly because a colleague who later discovers the flattering figure was chosen will discount everything else in the document.

**Question 12 (harder) — What a small comparison can establish**

Your team wants to know whether a rewrite improved completion. Baseline is 60%, and a 5 percentage point improvement would be meaningful. A colleague proposes 20 moderated sessions before and 20 after. What is the strongest response?

- A) Agree; 40 sessions is reasonable.
- B) Explain that a 5 point difference on 20 per group is one participant and well inside ordinary variation, so the comparison cannot detect it — recommend measuring the rate with live analytics across all users, and spending the 40 sessions on understanding why people succeed or fail, which sessions do uniquely well.
- C) Increase to 40 per group.
- D) Run it and report the difference with caveats.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** With 20 per group a five point difference is a single participant, indistinguishable from chance; detecting a difference that small reliably needs hundreds per group. Option B says so and redirects rather than merely objecting: analytics can measure the rate across the whole user base at no recruitment cost, while moderated sessions are the only method that explains why. That division of labour is the substantive recommendation. Option A endorses a design that cannot answer its own question. Option C moves towards adequacy without approaching it. Option D is the most damaging, because a reported difference from an underpowered comparison invites the team to act on noise — and for content work specifically, it establishes a precedent that small session counts can measure content effectiveness, which will be cited against you the next time a rewrite is judged on twenty people.

### Preparation tips

- **Find the denominator first.** Underline the group the question names.
- **Calculate the total when given a list.** Search and contact logs rarely hand you one.
- **Annualise before you report.** A monthly figure understates a case by a factor of twelve.
- **Return to counts before combining rates.** The unweighted average of page rates answers the wrong question.
- **Convert percentages into people, and then into calls or pounds.** That is how content work gets prioritised.
- **Ask whether a decline is a failure.** For content that exists to resolve confusion, decline is success.
- **Ask for the distribution.** A mean between two clusters describes nobody and invites the wrong edit.
- **Know what samples can detect.** Twenty per group cannot see five points.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Dividing by the wrong total.**
- **Comparing raw counts across pages with different traffic.**
- **Averaging page-level rates without weighting.**
- **Stopping at a monthly figure.**
- **Treating page views as a success measure for problem-solving content.**
- **Confusing percentages with percentage points.** You are frequently the person writing the sentence.
- **Shortening a page whose real problem is structure.**
- **Endorsing an underpowered comparison.** It sets a precedent that will be used against content work.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately you read. For most disciplines that is a proxy skill; for yours it is close to the job itself, which makes this the section where you should score best and where a careless habit costs most.

The classic format presents a short passage of dense workplace prose, typically three to six sentences, followed by statements to classify as True, False or Cannot Say. True means the statement follows necessarily. False means the passage contradicts it. Cannot Say means the passage neither establishes nor excludes it. Alongside these come comprehension items, main-point items, and questions asking which of four summaries is most accurate.

The governing rule is that you answer from the passage alone. Your professional knowledge must be set aside — and this becomes harder, not easier, with experience. The more policy prose you have translated, the more readily you supply the clause you expect and feel confident doing it.

Cannot Say deserves particular attention because people avoid it. It feels evasive, so candidates reach for True or False to appear decisive. In practice it is frequently correct.

Small words carry the meaning. "All" is not "most". "May" is not "must". "Should" is not "will". "Consider whether" is not "recommend". "Unless", "provided that" and "where practicable" govern everything around them.

The payoff for a content designer is unusually direct. Your job frequently consists of being handed a paragraph of legislation or policy and asked what it actually requires, so that you can write the plain-language version. Getting that wrong in either direction is serious: understate a requirement and users miss something that matters; overstate it and you have published an obligation government does not impose, which is both wrong and, occasionally, legally significant. The main-point and summary items here are the closest any assessment comes to testing that exact task.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The verbal skills tested map onto your named skills:

- **Reading precisely** maps to **User-centred content design**, where using style guides and content patterns means applying them as written.
- **Separating statement from inference** maps to **User focus (content design)**, where reasoning from evidence must not add to it.
- **Identifying the main point** maps to your core task of turning dense source material into content that says what it means and no more.
- **Handling qualified language** maps to **Strategic thinking (content design)**, where knowing whether something is required or recommended tells you how much room you have.
- **Reading for absence** maps to **User focus**, where noticing that a source never states a timescale is what stops you publishing a page without one.
- **Reading stakeholder correspondence accurately** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management (content design)**.

### Practice questions

*Passage A — for Questions 1 to 4*

"All content published on the service must be reviewed against the style guide before publication. Where content is a factual correction of published material, it may be published immediately and reviewed within five working days. Content that includes legal wording supplied by the legal team must not be altered without the legal team's agreement, although surrounding explanatory content may be edited freely. All published content is reviewed annually."

**Question 1 (easy)** — Statement: "Explanatory content around legal wording may be edited without the legal team's agreement." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The third sentence states that legal wording must not be altered without agreement, "although surrounding explanatory content may be edited freely". The statement restates the second half, so True. This is worth internalising beyond the test, because it is the fact that unlocks most content design arguments about legal text: the constraint attaches to the legal wording itself, not to the page containing it, which means you can almost always explain what the wording means even where you cannot change it.

**Question 2 (moderate)** — Statement: "A factual correction may be published before it has been reviewed against the style guide." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The second sentence permits factual corrections to be published immediately and reviewed within five working days, which is exactly what the statement describes. True. The item rewards holding two sentences together: the first establishes review before publication as the general rule, and the second reverses the order for a defined case. A reader who absorbs the general rule and stops will mark this False. The exception exists for a good reason worth noticing — a published page containing a factual error is doing harm every hour it stands, and a rule requiring review first would keep the error live while the review was arranged.

**Question 3 (moderate)** — Statement: "Legal wording may never be altered." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The passage prohibits altering legal wording *without the legal team's agreement*, which means alteration with their agreement is permitted. The statement's "never" drops the condition and asserts an absolute prohibition the passage does not impose, so it is False. This is a distinction with real consequences in practice: content designers who believe legal wording is untouchable never ask, and legal teams are frequently more willing to agree a clearer formulation than anyone assumes — particularly when you bring evidence that the current wording is not understood, since incomprehensible warnings serve their purposes no better than yours.

**Question 4 (harder)** — Statement: "A page containing legal wording is reviewed annually." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The final sentence states that all published content is reviewed annually, without exception. A page containing legal wording is published content, so it falls within that requirement, and the statement follows. True. The item tests whether you notice that the annual review sentence carries no carve-out for legal content — a reader who has just absorbed the strong constraint on altering legal wording may assume such pages are treated differently throughout, which is a reasonable expectation and not what the passage says. Reviewing is not altering, and the passage keeps them separate.

*Passage B — for Questions 5 to 8*

"The review of the guidance found that users who read the eligibility page before applying were more likely to complete an application. The review notes that these users differed in other respects, including whether they had applied before. The review did not assess whether the eligibility page was accurate. It recommends that the team consider whether the eligibility page should be more prominent."

**Question 5 (easy)** — Statement: "Users who read the eligibility page were more likely to complete an application." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The first sentence states this directly and the statement restates it without addition. True. Note the passage's careful "more likely to complete" rather than "usually completed", and the statement respects it. Answer quickly and bank time for the harder items.

**Question 6 (moderate)** — Statement: "Reading the eligibility page causes users to complete applications." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The passage reports an association and explicitly flags that the groups differed, including in whether they had applied before. Previous applicants are more likely to complete for reasons unrelated to the page — they know what is needed and have their documents — and may also be more likely to look at the eligibility page out of familiarity, which reverses the causal arrow entirely. So the passage neither asserts nor rules out a causal role. Cannot Say. The content consequence is direct: if the page causes completion, prominence helps; if experienced users simply read more pages, prominence achieves nothing and adds a step for everyone else.

**Question 7 (moderate)** — Statement: "The eligibility page is accurate." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The passage says explicitly that the review did not assess accuracy. Declared silence establishes nothing, so Cannot Say. Candidates sometimes reason that a page associated with higher completion must be doing its job, which is the inference this item catches — and the failure mode it protects against is a real one. An inaccurate eligibility page that encourages people to apply when they are not eligible would correlate with higher completion while causing considerable harm, and it would look like a success in exactly the data this review reports.

**Question 8 (harder)** — Statement: "The review recommends making the eligibility page more prominent." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The review recommends that the team *consider whether* the page should be more prominent — a recommendation to evaluate, including the possibility of concluding it should not be. The statement asserts a recommendation to act, which was not made, so False rather than Cannot Say: the passage tells you what was recommended and it was something different. This pervades real review documents, and content designers are unusually likely to be the person writing the sentence that records what a review said, which makes reading it precisely a direct professional obligation rather than a test technique.

*Passage C — for Questions 9 to 12*

"Content must be written so that it can be understood by users with a reading age of 9 where the subject matter allows. Where technical or legal terms cannot be avoided, they must be explained at first use. Content must not rely on colour alone to convey meaning. Headings must describe the content that follows and must be used in order without skipping levels. These requirements apply to public-facing content and to internal guidance used by more than fifty staff."

**Question 9 (easy)** — Statement: "All content must be understandable by users with a reading age of 9." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The requirement is qualified: "where the subject matter allows". The statement's "all" drops that, and the passage explicitly contemplates subject matter where the standard cannot be met, so it is False. This qualifier is frequently misquoted in both directions. It is not a general excuse — most content claiming difficult subject matter could be far simpler — and it does acknowledge genuine cases. A content designer who cites the rule without the qualifier will eventually be corrected by a colleague who has read it, which is an avoidable way to lose an argument you were otherwise winning.

**Question 10 (moderate)** — Statement: "Technical terms must be avoided." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The passage says that *where technical or legal terms cannot be avoided*, they must be explained at first use. That wording presupposes such cases exist and permits the terms, subject to explanation. The statement asserts an absolute prohibition the passage does not impose, so False. Note the structure: a rule addressing what to do in a situation implicitly accepts the situation occurs. Reading it as a prohibition produces content designers arguing that a term must be removed when the actual requirement is that it must be explained — and losing, because the term is often genuinely necessary.

**Question 11 (moderate)** — Statement: "These requirements apply to internal guidance used by twenty staff." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The final sentence extends the requirements to internal guidance used by more than fifty staff. Guidance used by twenty does not meet that threshold, so the extension does not apply — but the passage does not say the requirements are *excluded* for smaller audiences, only where they do apply. Silence about the twenty-user case leaves it undetermined, so Cannot Say rather than False. This is a fine and common distinction in scoping clauses: stating where a rule applies is not stating where it does not, and a reader who converts the first into the second will confidently tell a team they are exempt when nobody has said so.

**Question 12 (harder) — Main point**

Which best captures the main point of Passage C?

- A) Content must be written for a reading age of 9.
- B) Public-facing content and widely used internal guidance must be comprehensible and navigable without specialist knowledge or reliance on visual cues — through plain language where the subject allows, explanation where terms are unavoidable, meaning carried in more than colour, and headings that describe and are correctly ordered.
- C) Headings must be used in order.
- D) Technical terms must be explained at first use.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The main point must cover the whole passage without adding anything. Option A drops the qualifier and ignores four other elements. Options C and D are each true of one sentence and silent on the rest. Option B captures all four requirements, the scope clause, and the logic joining them: each exists so content can be understood and navigated by someone who does not share the author's knowledge and may not perceive the page as the author does. Note that B preserves both qualifiers that A discarded — "where the subject allows" and "where terms are unavoidable" — and is the only option mentioning scope, which matters because a summary omitting it implies the requirements are universal. Writing a summary that keeps every qualifier and adds nothing is, more or less exactly, your job.

### Preparation tips

- **Point at the words.** Before marking True, identify the phrase that makes it true. Experience makes this harder.
- **Trust Cannot Say.** If you are supplying a link, that is the diagnostic.
- **Circle every qualifier.** All, some, may, must, should, unless, provided that, where the subject matter allows, more than.
- **Notice rules that presuppose a situation.** "Where X cannot be avoided" permits X.
- **Distinguish a prohibition from a conditional prohibition.** "Not without agreement" is not "never".
- **Distinguish stating where a rule applies from stating where it does not.**
- **Separate "consider whether X" from "recommend X".** You may be writing the record.
- **Practise on real policy prose.** Take a paragraph of legislation and write three statements — one True, one False, one Cannot Say.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Answering from professional knowledge.**
- **Avoiding Cannot Say.**
- **Dropping a qualifier.** Citing a rule without it is how a good argument gets corrected.
- **Reading a conditional prohibition as absolute.** "Not without agreement" means you can ask.
- **Reading a conditional rule as a prohibition.** "Where terms cannot be avoided, explain them" is not "avoid terms".
- **Converting a scope statement into an exemption.**
- **Treating association as causation.**
- **Inferring from what a review did not examine.**

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment presents realistic workplace scenarios and asks what you would do. There is no passage to parse and no arithmetic. You get a dilemma of the kind your role produces, four or five candidate responses, and a question asking which is most effective and often which is least.

This section measures whether your judgement matches what the profession considers good practice at your level. Your role description places you as someone responsible for content across the end-to-end journey, comfortable using evidence to make content decisions, building relationships across government and influencing stakeholders, contributing to style guides and patterns, and engaging with the cross-government community. The strongest answers involve exercising real professional judgement, holding a position under pressure when the evidence warrants it, and knowing which decisions genuinely belong to someone else.

Four principles carry most scenarios.

Users come first, and in content design that specifically means users who will be misled, delayed or financially harmed by what a page does or does not say. Content failures are quiet — nobody rings to say they misunderstood something and gave up — which puts a particular obligation on you to represent the people who are not in the room.

Protect the accuracy of what is published. You are frequently the last person to read something before it reaches the public, and the only person whose job it is to ask whether it is true, complete and comprehensible. Options that let something inaccurate or materially incomplete go live are close to automatically wrong.

Ask what a request is for. Stakeholders very often ask for a specific sentence when what they need is a specific outcome, and the two are frequently satisfiable in ways nobody has considered. This is the highest-yield move available to a content designer and it appears throughout this section.

Fix the instance, then the source. Content problems usually recur, because the thing producing them — a policy that cannot be summarised, a form that generates a mistake, a letter that sets the wrong expectation — is still there.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The judgement dimensions map onto your named skills:

- **Protecting accuracy and comprehensibility** maps to **User-centred content design** and **User focus (content design)**.
- **Influencing stakeholders** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management (content design)**, where building relationships across government to focus on user needs is named explicitly.
- **Using evidence in argument** maps to **User focus**, where being comfortable using evidence to make content decisions is your defining capability.
- **Working in cycles** maps to **Agile working (content design)**.
- **Exploring how content might work** maps to **Content concepts and prototyping**.
- **Seeing beyond the page** maps to **Strategic thinking (content design)**, and engaging the cross-government community is named at your level.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — A stakeholder asks for specific wording**

A policy colleague asks you to publish a paragraph in the language of the regulation. Research shows users do not understand it. What is the most effective response?

- A) Publish it as requested.
- B) Ask what the paragraph needs to achieve — user understanding, legal protection, or the appearance of a specific form of words — because those three needs have different solutions and only one requires that exact wording.
- C) Refuse and publish a plain-language version.
- D) Publish both versions.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** This is the characteristic content design conversation, and the productive move is to establish what the request is for before deciding how to satisfy it. If the need is user understanding, plain language serves it better and the colleague will usually agree once that is said. If the need is legal protection, the exact wording may have to appear and can be explained alongside. If the need is that the policy team's contribution is visible, that is a different conversation. Option B distinguishes them. Option A is least effective, publishing something demonstrably not understood while believing there was no choice — and it also teaches the colleague that requests of this kind are simply granted, which makes the next one harder to question. Option C overrides a stated requirement without understanding it. Option D may be the answer and proposes it before establishing whether it is needed.

**Question 2 (easy) — A live inaccuracy**

You find that a live page states a fee that changed two months ago. What is the most effective response?

- A) Raise a ticket for the content backlog.
- B) Correct it immediately if you have the authority and the correct figure, tell your lead, and check whether the same figure appears on other pages, in the letters, or in the confirmation email.
- C) Correct the page.
- D) Ask the policy team to confirm the new fee before doing anything.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A wrong fee is a live harm — users may be budgeting for the wrong amount or deciding not to apply — so speed matters, and option B moves quickly while doing the thing that distinguishes a content designer from a proofreader: checking where else the figure appears. A fee is almost never stated once. It will be in the guidance, the start page, the confirmation email and probably a letter, and correcting only the page you happened to see leaves the contradiction in place, which is arguably worse than a consistent error because users now see two figures. Option C does half the job. Option A treats a live financial inaccuracy as backlog work. Option D is prudent if you are genuinely unsure and, where you have the correct figure documented, delays a fix that costs a minute.

**Question 3 (easy) — Feedback on your work**

A senior colleague returns your draft heavily rewritten with the comment "too wordy". What is the most effective response?

- A) Accept the rewrite and move on.
- B) Compare the versions carefully, work out what was cut and why, then ask a couple of targeted questions about the changes you cannot account for.
- C) Ask them to explain every change.
- D) Point out that some removed detail was requested by the policy team.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A heavily rewritten draft with a one-line comment is a considerable amount of free teaching, provided you do the work of extracting it. Option B does that: comparing line by line explains most of the changes on its own — qualifiers removed, sentences split, passives turned around — leaving a short list of genuinely puzzling edits worth asking about, which is a far better use of a colleague's time than option C. Option A is least effective, because accepting a rewrite without extracting the pattern means making the same choices next time. Option D raises a legitimate point and leads with a defence, which makes the conversation about who was right; the same point lands better as one of your targeted questions, where it may reveal that the colleague did not know the detail was requested.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Pressure to publish**

A delivery manager asks you to publish before the second check your process requires, because a campaign launches tomorrow. What is most effective?

- A) Publish; the deadline is real.
- B) Explain the check and ask what is possible — whether someone can review in the next hour, or whether the page could go live first thing tomorrow — and escalate if no route works.
- C) Refuse and cite the process.
- D) Publish and arrange the check afterwards without telling anyone.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** The deadline is genuine and the check exists for a reason, and option B treats both as real. Asking what is possible usually resolves it — a second check on one page takes twenty minutes — and naming the alternative of tomorrow morning tests whether "today" is a constraint or a preference. Option D is least effective: publishing outside the process and concealing it means nobody knows the page is unchecked, so an error will not be found, and you have made yourself solely responsible for a decision that was not yours. Option A skips the process without asking. Option C cites a rule without engaging with the deadline, which is technically defensible and makes you harder to work with than you need to be, at a cost you will pay in later negotiations.

**Question 5 (moderate) — A pattern you cannot fix locally**

You notice that four services in your organisation each explain the same benefit rule differently, and at least two are inconsistent. What is most effective?

- A) Fix the two that contradict.
- B) Document all four with their differences, establish through the policy owner which is correct, then propose a single agreed form of words and offer it to the cross-government content community if the rule is not unique to your organisation.
- C) Make all four match the clearest version.
- D) Raise it in your own team's retrospective.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** Four explanations with at least two contradicting means at least one is wrong and nobody currently knows which, so establishing correctness precedes establishing consistency. Option B does them in that order and then does the thing your role names — engaging the cross-government content community — because a benefit rule explained four ways in one organisation is almost certainly explained forty ways across government, and a single agreed form of words is the kind of contribution that outlasts the individual fix. Option C is least effective and is the tempting one: making pages consistent looks like a fix and could propagate a wrong rule to four pages, which is worse than the inconsistency you started with, because inconsistency at least signals that something is unresolved. Option A does the same on a smaller scale. Option D raises a cross-service problem in a single team's forum.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Evidence a stakeholder does not accept**

You present research showing users do not understand a key term. A stakeholder says the participants were unrepresentative and the term is standard in the sector. What is most effective?

- A) Concede and keep the term.
- B) Acknowledge the sample's limits honestly, then point out that the sector-standard argument concerns people in the sector while the page is for members of the public — and propose a cheap test: check whether the term appears in the site's search log, since users searching for it would suggest familiarity and users searching for a different word would not.
- C) Defend the research and insist on the change.
- D) Escalate to your lead.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** The stakeholder has made two claims of very different quality, and separating them is the whole move. The sample objection is partly fair and worth conceding, which costs nothing and buys credibility. The sector-standard claim is a category error — a term being standard among practitioners says nothing about members of the public, and is in fact weak evidence in the opposite direction, since specialist vocabulary becomes standard by being used among specialists. Option B says both and then proposes a test that is cheap, quick and uses data the organisation already holds, which converts a disagreement into a question with an answer. Option A is least effective, conceding on the strength of an objection that does not survive examination. Option C wins nothing without new evidence. Option D escalates a conversation you are equipped to have.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Content that belongs to another team**

The letter sent after an application contradicts the timescale on your confirmation page. Letters belong to another team. What is most effective?

- A) Ignore it; letters are not yours.
- B) Establish which timescale is correct, correct whichever of the two you control, and raise the other with the owning team — noting that the discrepancy means one of them has been telling users something untrue.
- C) Change your page to match the letter.
- D) Raise it with your lead and leave it there.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Two different timescales for one thing means at least one is wrong, and a user reading both has no way to know which — which produces a phone call at best and a missed deadline at worst. Option B does the necessary things in the right order: establish the truth first, fix what you control, and escalate the rest with the point that makes it urgent, which is that this is a factual error rather than an inconsistency of style. Option A is least effective: "not mine" describes an org chart while the user experiences one service contradicting itself, and your role is explicitly defined across the end-to-end journey including offline channels. Option C matches your page to a half that may be the wrong one. Option D reports the problem without fixing the half you can.

**Question 8 (harder) — Content that may cause harm**

Two days before launch you realise a page does not mention that applying may reduce a benefit the user already receives. Adding it needs policy sign-off and may delay launch. What is most effective?

- A) Launch and add it in the next update.
- B) Raise it immediately with your lead and the service owner, stating specifically that users may reduce their income without knowing, ask for an explicit recorded decision, and offer a form of words for policy to approve so the sign-off is as fast as possible.
- C) Add a sentence based on your understanding of the interaction.
- D) Launch and note the gap in the backlog.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** A page omitting a financial consequence may cause real users real harm, and weighing that against a launch date is a decision for people with the authority to make it — though raising it, precisely and now, is entirely yours. Option B does everything in your remit, and two details make it strong: stating the consequence in terms of income prompts action in a way that "the page is missing something" does not, and drafting the wording for policy to approve removes the most common cause of delay, which is that nobody has written anything for them to react to. Option C is least effective and is the conscientious person's trap: publishing your own account of how two benefits interact, unapproved, risks telling people something wrong about their money. Options A and D launch with a known harm.

**Question 9 (harder) — Being asked to soften a message**

A stakeholder asks you to change "you must tell us within 28 days or you may have to pay a penalty" to "please let us know as soon as you can", saying the original sounds threatening. What is most effective?

- A) Make the change; tone matters.
- B) Agree that the tone can be improved, and explain that the sentence carries two things users need — the deadline and the consequence — so removing both leaves people at risk of a penalty they were never warned about; then offer a revised version that keeps both and sounds less punitive.
- C) Refuse and keep the original.
- D) Change it to "please tell us within 28 days".

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** The concern about tone is legitimate — a great deal of government content reads as threatening, with real costs in trust and engagement — and the proposed solution is not, because it removes the information the sentence exists to convey. Option B separates the concern from the solution, concedes the valid half, and offers to do the work, which is what makes it persuasive rather than obstructive. Something like "Tell us within 28 days. If you tell us later, you may have to pay a penalty" keeps both facts and drops the confrontational framing. Option A is least effective: the softened version leaves a user who tells you on day 40 facing a penalty they were never warned of, which is a far worse experience than a stern sentence. Option C wins the point and forgoes the improvement. Option D keeps the deadline and drops the consequence, which is the part that makes the deadline matter.

**Question 10 (harder) — A page that should not exist**

A page explaining how to correct a common form error is heavily used and well rated. What is the strongest recommendation?

- A) Maintain the page; it is working.
- B) Recommend fixing the form that produces the error, on the reasoning that heavy use of a recovery page measures how often the upstream defect occurs rather than how well the page works — while keeping the page until the form is fixed.
- C) Make the page more prominent.
- D) Merge the page into the form.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** This is the reasoning that most distinguishes content design from writing, and it is uncomfortable because the correct recommendation makes your own successful page unnecessary. A page existing to help people recover from a mistake is a symptom; the mistake is the problem, and heavy use measures the failure rate rather than the page's quality. Option B names that and includes the practical qualifier — keep the page meanwhile, because users are making the mistake today. Option A is least effective, maintaining a symptom indefinitely and treating good ratings as evidence the situation is fine, when what users are rating is being rescued from a problem they should never have had. Option C makes recovery easier while leaving the failure rate untouched, which is a real if second-best intervention. Option D usually makes a form longer without preventing the mistake.

**Question 11 (harder) — A mistake of your own**

You realise a page you published last week states a deadline of 14 days when the correct period is 28. What should you do?

- A) Correct it quietly.
- B) Correct it immediately, tell your lead, check every other place the deadline appears, and raise whether users who acted on the wrong figure may need contacting — since a shortened deadline may have caused people to rush or to believe they had missed it.
- C) Correct it and mention it at stand-up.
- D) Check with policy whether 28 is right before doing anything.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A deadline stated as half its actual length is a consequential error in a specific direction: users may have believed they had less time than they did, and some may have concluded they had already missed it and not applied. Option B fixes the live error, tells the person who needs to know, checks the other places the figure appears, and raises the question of contacting affected users — which is not your decision and is your responsibility to surface. Option A is least effective: correcting silently leaves the consequences unexamined, and if it emerges later the concealment rather than the error is what will be remembered. Option C delays disclosure. Option D is prudent if you are unsure and, where you know 28 is correct, delays a fix while users continue to see the wrong figure — the right sequence is to correct first and confirm in parallel.

**Question 12 (harder) — An assumption in the room**

In a planning session someone says "we don't need to worry about people who can't read this — they can always ring up". The contact centre is open 9 to 5 on weekdays and the service is used heavily by shift workers. What is most effective?

- A) Let it go.
- B) Bring the specific facts — that the line is 9 to 5 on weekdays and many users work shifts — and ask what should happen to someone who cannot read the page and cannot ring in those hours, so the decision is made knowingly.
- C) Argue that all content must be accessible to everyone.
- D) Raise it privately afterwards.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** The comment rests on an assumption that a phone line is a universal fallback, and assumptions collapse against specific facts rather than principles. Option B supplies two facts and asks a question, which keeps the conversation about the design rather than about who is right and leaves room for the team to have a good answer. It is also the strongest form of challenge available, because you are contributing information rather than an opinion. Option A is least effective: a decision made on an assumption that is factually wrong leaves real people with no route at all, and you had the facts. Option C states a true principle in a way that invites a debate about principles, which rarely changes a room. Option D is a reasonable backup and misses the moment.

### Preparation tips

- **Ask what a request is for.** The highest-yield move available to a content designer.
- **Establish which version is correct before making things consistent.**
- **Check where else a figure appears.** A fee or a deadline is never stated once.
- **State the consequence, not the fault.** "Users may reduce their income" prompts action; "the page is incomplete" gets listed.
- **Draft the wording for the person who has to approve it.** It removes the commonest cause of delay.
- **Concede the valid half of an objection.** It costs nothing and buys the argument you actually need.
- **Ask what heavy use is measuring.** Sometimes it is a failure rate.
- **Read the least-effective question separately.** The worst option usually conceals something or lets a harm continue.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Publishing something demonstrably not understood because it was requested.**
- **Making pages consistent before establishing which is right.**
- **Writing your own account of something you have not confirmed.** Especially about money or entitlement.
- **Softening a message by removing the information in it.**
- **Accepting "it's standard in the sector" as evidence about the public.** It is weak evidence the other way.
- **Treating good ratings on a recovery page as success.**
- **Publishing outside a process and not saying so.**
- **Delaying a correction while you confirm.** Correct first, confirm in parallel.

## Conclusion

You have reached the end, and it is worth pausing over what that involved: forty-eight questions across four assessment types, with the reasoning worked through on each. That is a substantial piece of professional preparation and rather more than most people do.

Look back at the ground. In the cognitive section you traced a well-written page's call volume to something outside the page, weighted a content decision by the eight pages that would repeat it, recognised a heavily used recovery page as a measure of an upstream failure rate, separated "this wording must appear" from "nothing else may appear", and scoped a panel-recruitment flaw precisely enough to say which findings survived it. In the numeric section you compared pages by rate rather than count, annualised a monthly figure before reporting it, returned to counts before combining rates, recognised that a fall in views can be a success, and declined to endorse a comparison too small to detect what it was built to find. In the verbal section you kept qualifiers that others drop, read a conditional prohibition as conditional, refused to convert a scope statement into an exemption, and caught "consider whether" pretending to be a recommendation. And in the situational judgement section you asked what a policy request was actually for, declined to make four pages consistent before knowing which was right, conceded the valid half of a stakeholder's objection while dismantling the rest, and drafted the wording that made a policy sign-off fast.

Notice what unites almost all of it. Every section, in its own idiom, was testing whether you know the limits of what you have and will say so — the limits of a sample, of a metric, of a constraint somebody has asserted, of a page that reads well and is answering the wrong question. That is what makes a content designer worth listening to on decisions that are not about wording, which is where most of the value in the discipline actually sits.

A word about your level. Being responsible for content across an end-to-end journey means the unit you are accountable for is rarely the page in front of you. It is the sequence, including the letter, the email, the error message and the thing the contact centre says — and much of the strongest content work consists of noticing that the page is fine and the problem is three steps earlier. That reframe is available to you now in a way it was not as a trainee, and using it is what distinguishes a content designer from a very good writer.

If you want to build on this, a few things repay effort more than rereading. Read your service's site search log for a month; it is the cheapest user research available and almost nobody looks at it. Spend an hour listening to contact centre calls and find the pages that should have answered them. Read every letter and email your service sends, in order, as a user would receive them. Take a legal phrase you have accepted for a year and ask the legal team what the requirement actually is. And practise stating findings at the right strength — "nine of twelve participants", not "most users" — until the accurate version is the one that comes naturally.

Finally, for the day itself. An assessment samples your reasoning on one morning under artificial conditions. It is not a verdict on your judgement or your career. Read the stem twice, find the denominator, trust Cannot Say, keep the qualifiers, and remember that the strongest answer is usually the one honest about uncertainty rather than the one that sounds most decisive.

Good luck. Go carefully, ask what things are for, and keep reading the letters.
