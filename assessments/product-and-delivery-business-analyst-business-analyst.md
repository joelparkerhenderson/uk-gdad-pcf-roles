## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the mental operations your role performs daily — pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving — using realistic work material rather than abstract puzzles. Instead of number series and shape rotations, you meet a requirements set that contradicts a policy constraint, a dependency network to sequence, an options matrix with an inconsistency buried in it, or a week of competing demands to order defensibly.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 15 to 30 minutes containing roughly 15 to 30 multiple-choice questions. Modern platforms are frequently adaptive — difficulty adjusts to your performance, so a test that feels progressively harder is usually going well — and scoring is norm-referenced, comparing your accuracy and speed against a relevant candidate population. Employers often receive speed and accuracy as separate signals, which rewards a measured, methodical approach over a rushed one. Unscored practice questions are almost always offered before the timer starts; take them, since interface familiarity is free performance.

Why do employers use cognitive assessment for business analysts at your level? Because independent delivery raises the stakes on unaided thinking. A trainee's reasoning is checked by a supervisor; yours frequently is not — your problem statements, models, and recommendations often go straight into team plans and governance papers. Employers therefore want evidence that your reasoning is fast, accurate, and self-correcting: that you spot the inconsistency before the stakeholder does, deduce only what the evidence entails, and rank competing demands by impact rather than by noise. The assessment compresses those demands into minutes; your career distributes them across every working day.

### How this assessment maps to your role

Each cognitive dimension corresponds to named skills in your role:

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **Business modelling** and **Business process improvement**: modelling elements of the business with limited direction, and analysing processes to identify problems and opportunities, both rest on recognising recurring structures — duplicated steps, convention breaches, outlier behaviours — across large, untidy information sets.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Context, problem and option analysis** and **Requirements definition and management**: defining problem statements, evaluating options, and challenging requirements all require valid reasoning from rules, constraints, and evidence to their actual consequences — no more, no less.
- **Error checking** maps to **Requirements definition and management** and **Testing (business analysis)**: identifying, analysing, challenging and validating requirements, and reviewing prototypes, test plans and outcome reports, are systematic error detection across artefacts that must agree with each other.
- **Prioritisation** maps to your role level and **Adapting to delivery methodologies**: leading small to medium projects while supporting larger ones means sequencing your own attention defensibly, and adapting what you produce to your team's methodology so that you deliver value rather than documents.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Systems analysis** and **Business process improvement**: analysing IT system capabilities, developing models with minimal supervision, and designing process improvements all mean decomposing ambiguous situations into examinable parts — the analyst's signature move.

### Practice questions

The questions progress from easy to hard. Commit to an answer before reading each explanation.

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern recognition in a requirements catalogue**

Your requirements catalogue convention states: every functional requirement is phrased "The system must/should/could [verb] [object]", with the modal verb reflecting its MoSCoW priority. Reviewing a colleague's additions, you see:

1. The system must validate the applicant's National Insurance number.
2. The system should generate a monthly exceptions report.
3. The system must be user friendly.
4. The system could archive cases closed for more than seven years.

Which requirement most clearly breaches good practice, and why?

- A) Requirement 2 — "should" requirements do not belong in a catalogue.
- B) Requirement 3 — it is untestable as written, with no measurable definition of "user friendly".
- C) Requirement 4 — "could" priorities should not be recorded.
- D) Requirement 1 — validation rules are design detail, not requirements.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Test each option against what the convention and good practice actually demand. Requirements 2 and 4 use legitimate MoSCoW modals — "should" and "could" priorities absolutely belong in a catalogue, so A and C fail. Requirement 1 states a checkable behaviour at an appropriate level, so D fails. Requirement 3 follows the surface pattern — modal verb present, sentence well-formed — but breaks the deeper rule: a requirement must be verifiable, and "user friendly" names no observable criterion. No test can pass or fail it; no developer knows when it is done. The fix is to decompose it into measurable statements (task completion rates, error rates, compliance with a design system, accessibility conformance). The professional point: at your level, challenging requirements is a named part of your skill set, and the most valuable challenges are often to requirements that look tidy — pattern-compliant on the surface, unverifiable underneath. Surface convention checks are necessary; testability checks are where an experienced analyst earns their keep.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction from a policy constraint**

A policy constraint states: "Applications may be processed automatically only if the applicant is over 18, resident in the UK, and no fraud indicator is present. All other applications require manual review." You are told that Application X was processed automatically. Which one of the following must be true?

- A) Application X was processed correctly.
- B) If the rules were followed, the applicant is over 18, UK resident, and free of fraud indicators.
- C) The applicant is over 18, so the application cannot involve fraud.
- D) Application X would have failed manual review.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Note the question's precision: it asks what must be true, and option B is carefully conditional — "if the rules were followed". The constraint makes the three conditions jointly necessary for automatic processing; so if the system behaved as specified, all three held for Application X. Without that conditional, nothing is guaranteed: the fact of automatic processing alone cannot establish the conditions held, because the system might have a defect — which is why option A overreaches (correct processing is exactly what cannot be assumed) and why B's phrasing is the only defensible deduction. Option C confuses a screening indicator with ground truth — absence of a fraud indicator is not absence of fraud. Option D speculates about a counterfactual the rule says nothing about. This distinction — "the rule guarantees X" versus "the system's behaviour proves X" — is one you use constantly when developing system requirements and reviewing test outcomes: specifications describe intended behaviour, and only testing connects intention to reality.

**Question 3 (easy) — Prioritising a delivery week**

You lead the analysis on a medium-sized project. Monday morning presents:

1. The delivery manager needs your revised acceptance criteria for three stories entering the sprint on Wednesday.
2. A director's office has asked for a one-page summary of your options appraisal "this week" for a briefing pack.
3. A junior analyst you mentor has asked you to review their first stakeholder map — no deadline, but they are blocked on next steps until you respond.
4. Your requirements catalogue needs its routine monthly tidy-up.

Which sequencing is most defensible?

- A) 2, 1, 3, 4 — the director outranks everything
- B) 1, 3, 2, 4 — sprint-blocking work first, then unblock your mentee, then the summary, then routine maintenance
- C) 1, 2, 3, 4 — strict order of stated urgency
- D) 3, 1, 2, 4 — mentoring always comes first

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Weigh impact and urgency together, and account for who is blocked. Item 1 is both urgent (Wednesday) and blocking a whole team's sprint — it anchors the early week. The interesting judgement is items 2 and 3. The director's summary matters, but "this week" gives slack, and the material already exists — condensing an appraisal is bounded work you can schedule confidently for mid-week. Your mentee, meanwhile, is blocked now: a short review today costs you little and returns them to productive work immediately — unblocking others cheaply is one of the highest-leverage uses of a senior person's hour, which is why 3 edges ahead of 2 once 1 is under control. Option A over-weights seniority against a sprint deadline; C treats stated urgency as the only variable, ignoring the asymmetry between a blocked person and a flexible deadline; D turns a good instinct into an absolute rule. Note what B does not do: nothing is dropped, and the routine tidy-up correctly comes last. Defensible prioritisation is rarely about heroic choices — it is about sequencing that you could explain, without embarrassment, to everyone on the list.

**Question 4 (easy) — Error checking across artefacts**

A user story reads: "As an applicant, I want to save a partially completed application and return to it later, so that I don't lose my work." Its acceptance criteria include: "1. A signed-in applicant can save progress at any step. 2. Saved applications are retained for 28 days. 3. An applicant returning within 28 days can resume from where they left off." The service's published privacy notice states that incomplete application data is deleted after 21 days. What is the most important problem?

- A) Criterion 1 wrongly restricts saving to signed-in applicants.
- B) Criteria 2 and 3 contradict the privacy notice's 21-day deletion commitment — the artefacts disagree about retention.
- C) The story lacks a "so that" clause.
- D) Criterion 3 duplicates criterion 2.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The high-value defect is the cross-artefact contradiction: the criteria promise 28 days of retention while the privacy notice — a public commitment with legal weight under data protection law — promises deletion at 21 days. Building to the criteria would breach the notice; honouring the notice would break criterion 3 for a week's worth of returning applicants. Either the notice or the criteria must change, and that is a decision to surface now, involving whoever owns the privacy notice, not a discovery to make in user support tickets. Option A is a design question worth asking (should saving require sign-in?) but not a contradiction; C is false — the story has its "so that" clause; D misreads the criteria, which cover distinct behaviours (retention versus resumption). The lesson generalises to the core of your validation skill: requirements rarely fail alone. The expensive errors live between artefacts — story versus policy, model versus procedure, criteria versus notice — and checking a requirement means checking its neighbours. A requirements set can be internally immaculate and still be wrong about the world it must live in.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Root cause versus symptom**

A service's completion rate has fallen. Five facts are established: (i) completion fell from 81% to 64% over two months; (ii) the fall began the week a new identity-verification step was added; (iii) drop-off is concentrated at the identity step, where 30% of users now abandon; (iv) users failing identity verification are not told why; (v) call-centre contacts about "being stuck" have tripled. A stakeholder proposes: "Add more call-centre staff." What is the best characterisation of this proposal?

- A) A sensible root-cause fix, since calls have tripled.
- B) A symptom treatment: it addresses consequence (v) while leaving the likely root cause — an identity step that fails users without explanation — untouched.
- C) Wrong, because call volumes are unrelated to the completion fall.
- D) The only feasible option, since the identity step is presumably mandatory.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Assemble the causal chain from the facts: a new identity step (ii) coincides with the fall (i); abandonment concentrates precisely there (iii); users failing it get no explanation (iv); and confused, stuck users then phone (v). The chain runs step → failure without explanation → abandonment and calls. Staffing the call centre expands capacity to absorb a consequence while the generator of that consequence runs on — the definition of symptom treatment, as option B says. Option C errs oppositely: the calls are plainly related, they are just downstream. Option D smuggles in "presumably mandatory" and then surrenders; even a mandatory step can be fixed in the dimension that is failing — explanation and recovery routes for failed verification, which fact (iv) points at directly. And note what a careful analyst would add: the identity step being the cause is strongly indicated but not proven — the timing could conceal a coincident change — so the recommendation is "investigate and fix the failure experience at the identity step, and monitor". Your named skill is understanding problems and root causes before formulating recommendations; this question is that skill in a five-fact miniature, including its honest degree of uncertainty.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Deduction with delivery constraints**

Your team must deliver features F1, F2, F3 and F4. Constraints: (i) F2 depends on F1's data model being finalised; (ii) F3 and F4 both depend on F2's API; (iii) the team can develop at most two features in parallel; (iv) F1 takes one sprint, F2 takes two sprints, F3 and F4 take one sprint each. Development of a dependent feature cannot start until the feature it depends on is fully complete. What is the minimum number of sprints to deliver everything?

- A) 4
- B) 5
- C) 6
- D) 3

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Sequence it forwards. Sprint 1: F1 (nothing else can start — F2 waits on F1, and F3/F4 wait on F2). Sprints 2–3: F2 (two sprints; nothing else is eligible to run alongside it, since F3 and F4 need F2 complete). Sprint 4: F3 and F4 in parallel — two features, within the parallelism limit of (iii), both one sprint. Total: four sprints, option A. The tempting errors: 5 comes from serialising F3 and F4, forgetting the team may run two in parallel; 6 from serialising everything; 3 from starting F2 alongside F1 in sprint 1, which constraint (i) forbids — the dependency chain F1 → F2 → {F3, F4} has a fixed spine of 1 + 2 + 1 = 4 sprints that no parallelism can compress, because the parallelism limit never actually binds (the only parallel moment is sprint 4's pair). Recognising which constraint is binding — here the dependency chain, not team capacity — is the transferable skill: in real planning conversations, effort routinely goes into relaxing constraints that were never the bottleneck. An analyst who can show the critical path in four lines saves that effort.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Inconsistency in an options appraisal**

An options appraisal you are reviewing scores three options against weighted criteria (weights in brackets): Cost (40%), User impact (40%), Delivery risk (20%). Scores out of 10:

| Option | Cost | User impact | Delivery risk | Stated weighted total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A: Enhance existing system | 8 | 5 | 8 | 6.8 |
| B: New build | 3 | 9 | 4 | 5.6 |
| C: Commercial product | 6 | 6 | 6 | 6.4 |

The paper recommends Option A "as the highest-scoring option". Which check most undermines confidence in the paper as it stands?

- A) Option B's total is miscalculated — it should be 5.6.
- B) Option C's total is miscalculated: (6×0.4) + (6×0.4) + (6×0.2) = 6.0, not 6.4 — so at least one total is wrong and all three need re-verification, though A's recommendation happens to survive.
- C) The weights are wrong because delivery risk should always outweigh cost.
- D) Option A cannot be best because Option B has the highest single score.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Verify each total: uniform scores make Option C the easy check — 6 everywhere against weights summing to 1.0 must yield exactly 6.0, yet the paper says 6.4. Check the others: A gives (8×0.4)+(5×0.4)+(8×0.2) = 3.2+2.0+1.6 = 6.8 ✓; B gives (3×0.4)+(9×0.4)+(4×0.2) = 1.2+3.6+0.8 = 5.6 ✓ — so option A of the question fails (B's stated total is already correct). One wrong cell out of three changes the reading of the whole table: the ranking survives (6.8 > 6.0 > 5.6), but an arithmetic error in a decision paper obliges re-verification of everything, because you no longer know the error was unique. That proportionate response — "the recommendation stands, and the paper needs its numbers re-checked before it goes anywhere" — is what option B captures. Option C substitutes a personal weighting philosophy for a check; weights are a stakeholder judgement to be challenged through evidence, not an arithmetic error. Option D misunderstands weighted scoring entirely. At your level you both build and review appraisals like this: the habit of recomputing at least one row — preferably the easiest — before trusting any scored table takes ninety seconds and regularly finds exactly this.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Pattern recognition in operational data**

Reviewing six months of data for a casework process you are analysing, you observe: average daily throughput is stable; the backlog is stable; but the age profile of open cases has shifted — cases open more than 60 days have grown from 4% to 15% of the backlog, while the median open-case age has fallen slightly. What is the most likely explanation of this pattern?

- A) The team has slowed down across the board.
- B) A subset of hard cases is stalling and ageing while the bulk of cases now move slightly faster — the process has bifurcated, which stable averages conceal.
- C) The data must be wrong, since a stable backlog cannot change its age profile.
- D) Nothing needs attention, since throughput and backlog are stable.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Reconcile the three observations instead of averaging them away. Stable throughput and backlog say the process in aggregate is keeping pace. A falling median says the typical case is moving a little faster. Yet the over-60-day tail has nearly quadrupled as a share of the backlog. All three fit one story: the process has split into two populations — most cases flowing slightly better, and a growing minority stuck, ageing indefinitely. That is option B, and it is a pattern every experienced analyst learns to hunt because aggregate metrics are structurally blind to it: the stalled tail is small enough not to move averages, yet it usually contains the highest-stakes cases (complex circumstances, vulnerable users, escalations waiting to happen). Option A contradicts the falling median; option C is false — a backlog can hold volume constant while its composition ages, exactly as a pond can stay full while some water never leaves; option D trusts precisely the aggregates the question shows to be misleading. The actionable next step in real work: segment the stalled cases, find what they share — a missing document type, a dependency on another team, a category of complexity — and you have found your process improvement target. Distributions diagnose; averages anaesthetise.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Choosing the analysis technique**

A programme director asks you to help with a contested question: "Should we replace our legacy grants platform, and if so with what?" Different stakeholders are already championing different answers. As the lead analyst, what is the most appropriate first move?

- A) Run a vendor selection exercise to compare commercial grant platforms.
- B) Establish and agree the problem first: analyse the current situation and its root causes, define an agreed problem statement with success measures, and only then frame options — which may or may not all involve replacement.
- C) Survey staff on which replacement they would prefer.
- D) Draft the business case for replacement, since the director's question implies the decision.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The question as posed — "should we replace, and with what?" — has a solution baked into it, and stakeholders are already anchored to answers. The analyst's first duty in that situation is to reopen the space between problem and solution: what actually hurts (cost? reliability? user experience? policy flexibility?), what causes it, what would "better" measurably look like — and then an options set framed against that problem, which legitimately includes non-replacement options (remediation, re-platforming components, process change) alongside replacement. That is option B, and it is your context, problem and option analysis skill exercised in its correct order: plan the techniques, understand root causes, define the problem statement, then develop and evaluate options against feasibility, benefits and impact. Option A selects among solutions before establishing the problem — comparison criteria without a problem statement are opinions in a table. Option C measures preference, not need; popularity is not analysis. Option D reads the director's phrasing as a decision, converting an open question into a foregone conclusion — precisely the anchoring the analyst exists to counteract, and a disservice to a director who, pressed, almost always wants the real answer rather than their question echoed. The courage to answer "first, let's agree what problem we're solving" — respectfully, with a fast plan attached — is a defining mark of the independent analyst.

**Question 10 (hard) — Multi-constraint scheduling under governance**

You are planning analysis activities for the next two weeks (10 working days, D1–D10) before a gate review on D10. Required: a stakeholder workshop (W, one day, needs the operations lead who is available only D2, D3 and D8); process observation (O, two non-consecutive days sampling different weekly workloads — one day in each of the two weeks); an options evaluation (E, three consecutive days, cannot start until both W and at least the first O day are done); and a findings paper (P, two consecutive days, must finish by D9, cannot start until E is complete). You can do at most one activity per day. Which plan is feasible?

- A) W on D2; O on D3 and D8; E on D4–D6; P on D8–D9
- B) W on D2; O on D1 and D6; E on D3–D5; P on D8–D9
- C) W on D3; O on D4 and D8; E on D5–D7; P on D8–D9
- D) W on D8; O on D2 and D7; E on D9–D10 (compressed); P after the gate

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Check each plan constraint by constraint, eliminating on the first breach. Plan A: P occupies D8–D9, but O also claims D8 — two activities on one day breaches the one-per-day rule. Eliminated. Plan C: E runs D5–D7 and P needs E complete before starting, with P on D8–D9 — fine so far; W on D3 uses an operations-lead day ✓; O on D4 and D8 — first O (D4) precedes E ✓, days fall one in each week ✓ (D4 week one, D8 week two), non-consecutive ✓; but look again at E's precondition: E cannot start until W and the first O day are done — W is D3, first O is D4, E starts D5 ✓. Then P on D8–D9 — but O also claims D8. Eliminated, same collision as A. Plan D: E compressed to two days violates E's three-day requirement, and P after the gate defeats the paper's purpose — eliminated twice over. Plan B: W on D2 (operations lead available ✓); O on D1 and D6 — non-consecutive ✓, one day in each week ✓ (D1 week one, D6 week two); E on D3–D5 — three consecutive days ✓, starts after W (D2) and after the first O day (D1) ✓; P on D8–D9 — consecutive ✓, finishes by D9 ✓, starts after E completes on D5 ✓; no day carries two activities ✓. Feasible — option B. Notice the subtlety that catches skim-readers: O's first day may precede W (nothing forbids it), and plan B exploits exactly that freedom. In real planning as in tests, infeasibilities hide in day-collisions and misread preconditions — and the checking method is identical: walk every constraint against every claim, and grant plans no credit for looking sensible.

**Question 11 (hard) — Evaluating an inference from mixed evidence**

Three evidence sources bear on a proposed simplification of a benefit application:
- Usability testing (8 participants): all 8 completed the simplified prototype; 6 of 8 completed the current form. Median completion time fell from 24 to 11 minutes.
- Analytics (current live form, 3 months): 22% of started applications are abandoned; abandonment clusters at the income-details section — the section the simplification removes by using data already held.
- Caseworker survey (41 responses): 60% predict the simplification will increase downstream verification workload, because income data already held is "often out of date".

Your recommendation must be formulated. Which conclusion best reflects all the evidence?

- A) Proceed without reservation: users complete the simplified form faster and more often.
- B) Do not proceed: caseworkers, who know the process best, predict increased workload.
- C) The user-facing evidence strongly favours simplification, and the caseworker concern identifies a specific, testable risk — recommend proceeding to a limited pilot that measures downstream verification workload and income-data accuracy before full rollout.
- D) The evidence sources contradict each other, so commission more research before any action.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** First, weigh each source for what it can actually support. The usability results are strong directional evidence (complete-versus-struggle at n=8 is meaningful signal, though not a statistic); the analytics independently corroborate them at scale — abandonment clusters exactly where the simplification operates, a convergence that materially strengthens the case. The caseworker survey does not contradict either source: it speaks to a different stage of the process (downstream verification) and a different risk (data currency), and it is prediction rather than observation — weaker as evidence, but pointing precisely where measurement should go. Option C is the only conclusion that uses all three: proceed on the converging user evidence, at pilot scale, instrumented to test the named risk before committing. Option A hears only the favourable sources and carries an unexamined risk into full rollout. Option B inverts the error, treating a workload prediction as a veto on strong observed user benefit. Option D mistakes complementarity for contradiction — the sources disagree about nothing; they illuminate different segments of the same journey — and "more research" without a question is delay wearing analysis as a costume. This is your evidence-synthesis skill under examination: recommendations at your level are rarely "yes" or "no" but "yes, at this scale, measuring this" — and the ability to convert a stakeholder's objection into an instrumented pilot metric is what turns opposition into design input.

**Question 12 (hard) — The flawed improvement inference**

A colleague presents this argument to a programme board: "After we introduced mandatory pre-submission checklists in the north region, rejected applications there fell from 18% to 12% in six months. The south region, without checklists, stayed at 17–18%. Checklists therefore cut rejections by a third, and mandating them nationally will save the equivalent of 9,000 rejected applications a year. Also, the north's caseworkers now rate their workload as more manageable, proving checklists reduce workload." Which is the most accurate critique?

- A) The regional comparison is worthless without a randomised trial.
- B) The rejection-rate evidence is reasonably strong — a comparison region strengthens the causal claim — but the national extrapolation assumes regions are alike, and the workload claim is weakest: "proving" overstates a satisfaction rating that could reflect many concurrent factors.
- C) The argument is sound in all respects; the board should mandate checklists nationally.
- D) Percentages cannot be compared across regions, so the whole argument fails.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Grade each component of the argument separately — the discipline that distinguishes critique from reflex. The core evidence is genuinely decent: a sustained fall in the treated region against a flat comparison region is the practical, non-experimental version of a controlled comparison, and it makes "checklists reduced rejections in the north" a reasonable working conclusion. Two overreaches follow it, though. The national projection ("9,000 a year") silently assumes other regions share the north's caseload mix, staff practices, and baseline error causes — the standard pilot-to-rollout leap, which may hold but must be argued, not assumed. And the workload claim is the weakest link: a satisfaction rating "proves" nothing about causation — seasonality, staffing changes, or simple enthusiasm for a tool people helped introduce could each move it — so the honest verb is "suggests". Option B grades all three components correctly. Option A demands methodological perfection real services rarely permit; comparison-region designs are legitimate evidence, and boards must decide on attainable evidence. Option C swallows the overreach whole. Option D is arithmetically confused. Formulating recommendations "considering feasibility, benefits and impact" — your named skill — includes exactly this calibration: endorse what the evidence supports, size what it merely suggests, and phrase the paper so the board can see which is which. Analysts who over-claim are corrected once, publicly, and their next three papers are discounted; calibration is credibility.

### Preparation tips

- **Practise in short, spaced sessions** — twenty focused minutes several times a week outperforms a single marathon, because pattern recognition and deduction consolidate between sessions.
- **Always take the platform's unscored practice questions**; interface familiarity converts directly into time, and time into accuracy.
- **Verify one row of every table.** Whether the artefact is a weighted appraisal, a traceability matrix, or a test summary, recomputing the easiest row before trusting the whole is a ninety-second habit that catches disproportionate errors — in tests and in governance papers.
- **Identify the binding constraint before optimising.** In scheduling and dependency questions, find the fixed spine (the critical path, the full column, the immovable date) first; most wrong answers come from optimising a constraint that never binds.
- **Separate "the rule guarantees" from "the behaviour proves".** Deduction questions at this level increasingly turn on conditionals — what must be true if the rules were followed — and the same distinction underpins your requirements and testing work.
- **Rehearse on your own artefacts.** Once a week, apply a question's technique to a live document: testability-check a requirement, recompute an appraisal row, segment an aged backlog. The practice compounds, and it generates the examples you will use when mentoring others.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Trusting surface-compliant artefacts.** The requirement with the correct modal verb, the appraisal with tidy columns — pattern compliance is not correctness. Check testability, arithmetic, and cross-artefact consistency beneath the format.
- **Deducing from what behaviour implies rather than what rules entail.** "It was processed automatically, so the conditions held" assumes a defect-free system; the questions — and production incidents — punish that assumption.
- **Averaging away the signal.** Stable means and totals can conceal bifurcated distributions, ageing tails, and definitional changes. When aggregates and instinct disagree, segment before concluding.
- **Solving the stated problem instead of the actual one.** Questions (and directors) sometimes embed a solution in their phrasing; notice when the correct first move is to re-establish the problem.
- **Perfectionism on hard questions.** Ninety seconds without progress means eliminate, choose, move on. Norm-referenced scoring rewards the candidate who banks twenty-five sound answers over the one who perfects eighteen.
- **Speed-reading constraints.** "Non-consecutive", "at most", "cannot start until", "one per day" — each carries exact force, and most infeasible-plan errors are misread constraints rather than faulty logic. Slow down where the words are dense; accelerate where the checking is mechanical.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures how accurately you extract, manipulate, and interpret the quantitative information your role runs on: percentages and percentage points, ratios and rates, weighted averages, trend data, and figures presented in tables and charts. The mathematics never exceeds everyday arithmetic; the difficulty lives in selection and interpretation — choosing the right numbers, the right base, and the right operation, then saying only what the result actually supports.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 20 to 30 minutes with 15 to 20 multiple-choice questions, each built around a data presentation: a two-way table, a described chart, a short passage dense with figures. Calculators are usually permitted (verify for your specific test), rough paper is essential, and adaptive difficulty is common. Employers generally see accuracy and speed separately, so method and composure are rewarded over raw pace.

Why does numeric reasoning carry weight for a business analyst at your level? Because your numbers travel unaccompanied. The percentages in your options appraisal are quoted in the business case; the trend in your findings paper reaches the programme board; the coverage figure in your test summary shapes a go-live conversation. Nobody re-derives your arithmetic downstream — they act on it. Employers therefore want evidence that your quantitative reasoning is reliable without supervision: that your bases are right, your averages weighted, your comparisons like-for-like, and your interpretations no stronger than the data. Just as importantly, your role includes challenging others' numbers — a supplier's benefits claim, a sponsor's headline average, a dashboard's suspicious step-change — and the fastest route to confident challenge is fluent command of exactly the techniques this section practises.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Weighted averages and distributions** map to **User experience analysis** and **Business process improvement**: presenting research findings accessibly, and analysing operational performance, both require combining unequal groups correctly and knowing when an average conceals the story a distribution tells.
- **Ratios and rates** map to **Adapting to delivery methodologies** and **Systems analysis**: velocity, throughput, capacity, and volume projections are rates; sizing and sanity-checking them is routine in planning and in specifying systems.
- **Table and chart interpretation** maps to **Requirements definition and management** and **Testing (business analysis)**: traceability matrices, coverage tables, defect summaries and test outcome reports are dense tabular artefacts you both produce and review — and reviewing means recomputing, not admiring.

### Practice questions

Use a calculator and rough paper. Every explanation shows the full working.

**Question 1 (easy) — Requirements coverage percentage**

Your traceability matrix links 85 requirements to test cases; 68 requirements have at least one test case. What percentage of requirements are covered?

- A) 75%
- B) 78%
- C) 80%
- D) 82%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Percentage = part ÷ whole × 100. Part: 68 covered; whole: 85 requirements. 68 ÷ 85 = 0.8 exactly, so coverage is 80%. Quick verification: 10% of 85 is 8.5, so 80% is 8.5 × 8 = 68 ✓. When a division looks unfriendly, test whether the numbers share a factor — 68 and 85 both divide by 17 (68 = 4 × 17; 85 = 5 × 17), giving 4/5 = 80% without a calculator. Reporting habit worth keeping: quote the pair with the percentage ("80% — 68 of 85") so reviewers can verify instantly, and so the 17 uncovered requirements — the actionable remainder — stay visible in the same sentence.

**Question 2 (easy) — Average with an outlier**

Seven stakeholder interviews lasted 42, 45, 38, 44, 40, 118, and 43 minutes. What is the mean interview length, and what should you note about it?

- A) 47 minutes — a typical interview length.
- B) 53 minutes — representative of the sessions.
- C) 53 minutes — but the 118-minute outlier inflates it; the typical interview ran close to 42 minutes.
- D) 42 minutes — the outlier should be silently deleted.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Mean = total ÷ count. Total: 42+45 = 87; +38 = 125; +44 = 169; +40 = 209; +118 = 327; +43 = 370. Mean = 370 ÷ 7 ≈ 52.9 ≈ 53 minutes. But inspect the data before reporting it: six of seven values cluster tightly between 38 and 45, while one session ran 118 minutes. The mean sits above every value except the outlier — a textbook signal that it represents nobody. The median (order the values: 38, 40, 42, 43, 44, 45, 118 → middle value 43) describes the typical session far better. Option C reports the true mean and interprets it honestly. Option A miscalculates; option B calculates correctly and interprets wrongly; option D deletes data silently — the outlier may be the most interesting session (why did one stakeholder need three times the time?), and silent deletion is a data-integrity failure regardless. The professional pattern: compute the mean, look at the distribution, report the number that answers the actual question — and mention the outlier, because outliers are frequently findings.

**Question 3 (easy) — Percentage change in a KPI**

A service's average end-to-end processing time fell from 12.5 days to 10 days after a process change you designed. What percentage improvement is this?

- A) 20%
- B) 25%
- C) 2.5%
- D) 15%

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Percentage change = change ÷ original × 100. Change: 12.5 − 10 = 2.5 days. Original: 12.5 days. 2.5 ÷ 12.5 = 0.2 → 20% improvement. Option B is the classic wrong-base error: 2.5 ÷ 10 = 25% divides by the new value, overstating your own improvement — an error that is particularly costly when it is your change being evaluated, because an inflated claim discovered by a reviewer discounts the genuine 20%. Option C mistakes the raw day-change for a percentage; option D is unanchored. Two habits: always divide by where you started, and when reporting your own work's benefits, have a colleague re-derive the headline figure — the analyst's equivalent of proofreading, and cheap insurance for your credibility.

**Question 4 (easy) — Reading a two-way defect table**

Defects found in the last release, by component and severity:

| Component | Critical | Major | Minor | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Application form | 1 | 6 | 18 | 25 |
| Payments | 3 | 7 | 5 | 15 |
| Reporting | 0 | 2 | 8 | 10 |

Which component should concern you most, and on what basis?

- A) Application form — it has the most defects in total.
- B) Payments — it has the most critical defects and the highest proportion of critical-plus-major defects (10 of 15).
- C) Reporting — fewest defects means poorest testing.
- D) All equally — totals are similar.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Interrogate severity structure, not just volume. Application form leads on raw count (25) but 18 of those are minor; its critical-plus-major share is 7 of 25 = 28%. Payments carries 3 of the release's 4 critical defects and its critical-plus-major share is (3+7) ÷ 15 = 10/15 ≈ 67% — two-thirds of its defects are serious, in the component where seriousness is least tolerable (public money moves through it). Reporting shows 2 of 10 major (20%), no criticals. Option B reads both the counts and the proportions correctly. Option A is the raw-count reflex this table is built to punish. Option C inverts logic — few defects found is not evidence of poor testing without coverage data (though a mature reviewer might ask about Reporting's test coverage as a follow-up, the table alone cannot convict it). Option D ignores everything except order of magnitude. When you review test outcome reports — a named part of your testing skill — severity-weighted reading is the difference between "25 beats 15" and the correct "Payments first".

**Question 5 (moderate) — Weighted average across channels**

Last quarter, satisfaction scores (out of 10) by channel were: online, 7.8 from 1,500 respondents; phone, 6.2 from 400 respondents; face-to-face, 8.4 from 100 respondents. What is the overall average satisfaction across all respondents?

- A) 7.5
- B) 7.6
- C) 7.4
- D) 7.47

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Weight each channel by its respondent count. Online: 1,500 × 7.8 = 11,700. Phone: 400 × 6.2 = 2,480. Face-to-face: 100 × 8.4 = 840. Combined score: 11,700 + 2,480 + 840 = 15,020 across 1,500 + 400 + 100 = 2,000 respondents. Overall mean: 15,020 ÷ 2,000 = 7.51 ≈ 7.5 — option A. The unweighted trap, (7.8 + 6.2 + 8.4) ÷ 3 = 7.47 ≈ 7.5... note the near-coincidence: option D (7.47) is exactly that unweighted figure, planted to catch method-by-answer-matching — here the two methods land close together only because the deviations happen to offset, and you cannot rely on that. Sense-check: the overall must sit between 6.2 and 8.4, pulled strongly towards online's 7.8 (three-quarters of all respondents) — 7.51 fits. And carry the analyst's observation with the average: phone users, a fifth of respondents, sit 1.6 points below online users. When you present findings "in an accessible and easy to understand way", the channel gap is the sentence that drives action; the 7.5 is merely the sentence that opens the paragraph.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Velocity, capacity and a realistic forecast**

Your team's velocity over the past six sprints: 26, 24, 8, 27, 25, 28 story points (the 8 was a sprint disrupted by a production incident). The remaining release backlog is 130 points. The delivery manager asks for a defensible forecast in sprints. What do you advise?

- A) 130 ÷ 23 = 5.7, so 6 sprints — using the six-sprint average of 23.
- B) Treat the disrupted sprint as unrepresentative: typical velocity is (26+24+27+25+28) ÷ 5 = 26; 130 ÷ 26 = 5 sprints — while noting the incident risk that the outlier represents.
- C) 130 ÷ 28 = 4.6, so 5 sprints — using the best sprint.
- D) 130 ÷ 8 = 16.25, so 17 sprints — planning for the worst case.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Compute both candidate averages. All six sprints: 26+24+8+27+25+28 = 138; 138 ÷ 6 = 23; forecast 130 ÷ 23 ≈ 5.65 → 6 sprints. Excluding the disrupted sprint: 130 ÷ 26 = 5 exactly → 5 sprints. Which is defensible? The 8-point sprint has a known, identified cause (production incident) — it is not noise but a distinct event, and including it quietly converts "our velocity" into "our velocity assuming an incident every six sprints". Option B excludes it transparently and names the exclusion, which is the crucial move: the risk the outlier represents is disclosed, not erased, and the delivery manager can choose to hold contingency for it. Option A is arithmetically sound but analytically lazy — it launders a known disruption into the baseline without comment. Option C plans on the best case, a forecast style that delivers disappointment on schedule. Option D inverts it into paralysis. The general principle for forecasting from operational data: exclude identified anomalies openly, never silently — the difference between the two is the difference between judgement and manipulation, and stakeholders can only trust forecasts whose assumptions they can see.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Percentage points in a benefits claim**

A business case claims: "Digital take-up will rise from 55% to 75%, a 20% increase, reducing per-transaction cost from £8.20 (paper) versus £1.30 (digital)." A board member asks you to check the claim's arithmetic and phrasing. Which correction is right?

- A) The rise is 20 percentage points, which is a 36% relative increase (20 ÷ 55); calling it "a 20% increase" understates it.
- B) The claim is correct as written.
- C) The rise is 20 percentage points, which is a 26.7% relative increase.
- D) The rise should be described as 75 − 55 = 20%, so the phrasing is fine.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Separate the two measures. Percentage-point change: 75 − 55 = 20 points. Relative percentage change: 20 ÷ 55 = 0.3636… ≈ 36%. So the accurate sentence is "a rise of 20 percentage points — a 36% increase in take-up". Describing it as "a 20% increase" conflates points with percent and, unusually, undersells the change (a 20% relative increase from 55% would reach only 66%). Option C divides by the wrong base (20 ÷ 75). Options B and D bless the conflation. Why does this matter beyond pedantry? Because the cost model runs on the point change: each point of take-up shifts a fixed slice of transaction volume from £8.20 to £1.30, saving £6.90 per transaction — so the benefits line is driven by "20 points of volume", and a reader who internalises "20%" will mis-model it. Your role reviews business cases exactly like this; the points-versus-percent check takes ten seconds, catches a disproportionate share of benefits-claim errors in the wild, and — raised courteously — is the kind of precise, constructive challenge that builds a reviewer's reputation.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Ratio reasoning in capacity planning**

A casework function requires caseworkers and quality checkers in the ratio 6:1. The function currently has 78 caseworkers and 11 checkers. A planned expansion adds 24 caseworkers. To restore the exact 6:1 ratio after expansion, how many additional checkers must be recruited?

- A) 4
- B) 6
- C) 17
- D) 3

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Work from the target state, not the increment alone. After expansion: 78 + 24 = 102 caseworkers. Required checkers at 6:1 = 102 ÷ 6 = 17. Current checkers: 11. Additional needed: 17 − 11 = 6 — option B. Option C (17) is the required total mistaken for the increment — read what the question asks for. Option A (4) comes from scaling only the new caseworkers (24 ÷ 6) — but that assumes the current state is on-ratio, and it is not: 78 ÷ 6 = 13 checkers required now versus 11 in post, a hidden existing deficit of 2 that the increment-only method silently perpetuates. Option D undercounts further. The professional lesson hides in that deficit: ratio questions about changes tempt you to ratio the change, and the method fails whenever the baseline is already off-ratio — which real baselines usually are. Computing the full target state first ("what should the whole function look like?") is both the safer arithmetic and the better analysis, because it surfaces existing gaps that incremental thinking conceals. Capacity models you build for options appraisals reward exactly this target-state discipline.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Interpreting a trend with seasonality**

Monthly applications to a scheme: January 4,100; February 3,900; March 4,000; April 5,900; May 6,100; June 6,000. Last year's figures for the same months: 4,000; 3,850; 3,950; 5,800; 6,050; 5,900. A colleague drafts: "Applications surged 50% between March and April — demand is accelerating and we need urgent capacity expansion." What is the fairest assessment?

- A) The draft is right: a 47.5% month-on-month rise demands urgent expansion.
- B) The March-to-April rise is real but seasonal — last year shows the same April step — and year-on-year growth is only around 1.7%; demand is essentially flat, so the "acceleration" framing is wrong.
- C) Applications are falling, since June is below May.
- D) The data is unusable without daily figures.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** First verify the colleague's number: (5,900 − 4,000) ÷ 4,000 = 1,900 ÷ 4,000 = 47.5% — the month-on-month rise is real (the draft's "50%" rounds it). But the causal story fails the comparison test: last year April also leapt (3,950 → 5,800, a 47% rise), so the April step is the scheme's seasonal shape — perhaps a deadline or entitlement cycle — not new demand. The correct growth measure compares like month with like month: April on April, 5,900 ÷ 5,800 ≈ +1.7%; June on June, 6,000 ÷ 5,900 ≈ +1.7%; the scheme is growing at under 2% a year. Option B replaces a 50% panic with a 2% fact. Option A quotes accurate arithmetic in service of a wrong inference — the most dangerous combination, because the sums check out. Option C reads one month's wiggle as a trend. Option D demands granularity the question does not need. The transferable rule: never interpret a month-on-month change in seasonal data without the same-period-last-year comparison. Capacity decisions, staffing cases, and "demand surge" narratives in government regularly rest on exactly this error — and the analyst who asks "what did last April look like?" is worth their salary that afternoon.

**Question 10 (hard) — Two-stage benefits model**

An options paper models a channel-shift benefit. Current state: 180,000 transactions per year; 30% arrive through the assisted channel at £7.50 per transaction; the rest are digital at £1.20. The proposal claims to move one-third of assisted transactions to digital. What annual saving does the proposal deliver, and what is the new assisted-channel share?

- A) £113,400 saving; assisted share falls to 20%
- B) £340,200 saving; assisted share falls to 10%
- C) £113,400 saving; assisted share falls to 24%
- D) £136,080 saving; assisted share falls to 20%

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Stage the model and write intermediates down. Current assisted volume: 30% × 180,000 = 54,000 transactions. Moved to digital: one-third × 54,000 = 18,000 transactions. Saving per moved transaction: £7.50 − £1.20 = £6.30 (the transaction still costs something digitally — subtracting is the step option B skips, since 18,000 × £7.50 = £135,000... check: B says £340,200, which is 54,000 × £6.30 — B actually moves all assisted transactions, misreading "one-third"). Annual saving: 18,000 × £6.30 = £113,400. New assisted volume: 54,000 − 18,000 = 36,000; new share: 36,000 ÷ 180,000 = 20%. Option A carries both figures. Option C computes the saving correctly but botches the share (24% would be a one-fifth reduction); option D applies £6.30 to a wrong volume (21,600 — perhaps one-third of 30% misapplied to total volume then re-percentaged). Model-checking method, applicable to every benefits case you review: name each quantity (volume moved, unit saving, remaining volume), compute in stages, and test the endpoints — if all 54,000 moved, the saving would cap at £340,200, so a one-third move must yield exactly a third of that: £113,400 ✓. Endpoint tests catch wrong-base errors that stage-by-stage reading can miss.

**Question 11 (hard) — Reconciling two reports**

Two reports describe the same service's performance last month. Report 1 (operations): "14,400 cases closed; 82% within the 10-day standard." Report 2 (performance team): "Cases meeting the 10-day standard: 10,800 of 13,500 relevant cases — 80%." A director asks you to explain the discrepancy before the figures are published. Which explanation is most plausible and what should you verify?

- A) One team cannot count; report whichever figure looks better.
- B) The reports use different denominators — 14,400 total closures versus 13,500 "relevant" cases — so the definitions of the case population (and possibly of "within standard") differ; verify what the 900-case difference is (exclusions such as withdrawn or transferred cases) and which definition the published standard requires.
- C) The difference is rounding; 80% and 82% are effectively the same.
- D) Average the two: publish 81%.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Quantify the discrepancy before explaining it. Report 1: 82% of 14,400 = 11,808 cases within standard. Report 2: 10,800 of 13,500 = 80%. The reports differ in both numerator (11,808 vs 10,800 — about 1,000 cases) and denominator (14,400 vs 13,500 — exactly 900 cases), and Report 2's word "relevant" is the tell: some population definition excludes 900 closures — plausibly withdrawn, duplicate, or transferred cases — and the two teams are answering subtly different questions. That is option B, together with the correct verification path: identify the 900, and determine which definition the published performance standard actually specifies — because "which number is right" is strictly a question of which question the publication must answer. Option C fails arithmetic scrutiny: 2 percentage points on ~14,000 cases is ~280 cases even before the definitional gap, not rounding. Options A and D are two styles of malpractice — figure-shopping and figure-splitting — and averaging two differently-defined percentages produces a number that answers no question at all. This scenario is a staple of your working life: most "data discrepancies" between teams are definitional, not computational, and the analyst who reconciles definitions before publication prevents the far worse version of this conversation that happens after publication, in public.

**Question 12 (hard) — Sample sizes and subgroup claims**

A user researcher reports on a survey of 1,200 service users: overall satisfaction 72%; among the 48 respondents who used the service with a screen reader, satisfaction was 46%. A stakeholder drafts two statements for a board paper: (1) "72% of our users are satisfied." (2) "Screen-reader users' satisfaction is 46%, 26 points below average — accessibility must be our top improvement priority." What is the most analytically sound advice?

- A) Both statements are fine as written.
- B) Statement 1 should say "72% of surveyed users" (the survey measures respondents, not all users); statement 2's gap is large and worth acting on, but with n=48 the 46% carries wide uncertainty (roughly ±14 points) — report it as a strong signal warranting priority investigation, and pair it with qualitative evidence rather than presenting the point estimate as precise.
- C) Statement 2 must be deleted: 48 people cannot tell you anything.
- D) Statement 1 is wrong because response bias means true satisfaction is certainly lower.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Two calibrations, one per statement. Statement 1 commits the quiet universal: a survey of 1,200 respondents licenses claims about surveyed users (and, with good sampling, estimates about users generally — the overall figure at n=1,200 is precise to roughly ±2.5 points), but the clean fix is the honest one: say "surveyed users". Statement 2 requires holding two truths simultaneously. The truth that protects action: a 26-point gap is far too large to be sampling noise alone — even at n=48, where a proportion's uncertainty is roughly ±1/√48 ≈ ±14 points, the plausible range for screen-reader satisfaction (about 32% to 60%) sits entirely and substantially below 72% — so the signal is real and the priority call is defensible. The truth that protects credibility: "46%" presented as a precise figure from 48 respondents will be challenged, and the challenge will land; "roughly half, versus nearly three-quarters overall — a gap unlikely to be chance" both survives scrutiny and motivates the same action. Option B holds both, and adds the methodologically right companion: pair the quantitative signal with qualitative evidence (what do screen-reader users actually encounter?), which is where the improvement work will find its content anyway. Option C wields sample-size scepticism as a veto — n=48 constrains precision, not existence; dismissing accessibility evidence because the affected population is small inverts both statistics and public duty. Option D declares a bias direction without evidence. Presenting findings accessibly — your named skill — includes presenting uncertainty accessibly: not as hedging that buries the signal, but as honest sizing that makes the signal robust.

### Preparation tips

- **Drill the core toolkit to fluency:** part ÷ whole; change ÷ original; weighted average via totals; rate × time; target-state before increment. Fluency in these five buys you the test's real currency — attention left over for interpretation.
- **Name the base aloud for every percentage** — "percent of what?" — and name the population for every average. Wrong-base and wrong-denominator errors outnumber all other numeric mistakes combined, in tests and in published papers.
- **Write intermediate values down.** Staged models (volume moved, unit saving, new share) fail in the head and succeed on paper. Rough paper is professional equipment.
- **Use endpoint and benchmark tests.** If all cases moved, what would the saving be? Does the weighted mean sit nearer the biggest group? Is the result inside the data's range? Ten-second tests catch the errors that matter.
- **Interrogate before you calculate.** Footnotes, definition changes, "relevant cases", seasonal shapes, known outliers — the meaning of the numbers is decided before the arithmetic starts, and most trap questions (like most flawed board papers) are traps of meaning, not of computation.
- **Practise challenge as well as calculation.** Take one real dashboard, business case, or performance report each week and re-derive its headline figure. You will find errors surprisingly often; deciding how to raise them courteously is the other half of the skill, and it is also how best practice spreads through a community — something your role is explicitly asked to do.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Dividing by the new value in change calculations** — flattering when it is your improvement, embarrassing when a reviewer catches it. The original value is always the base.
- **Averaging averages across unequal groups.** Group sizes in the question belong in the working. Totals first, then divide.
- **Conflating percentage points with percent** — in both directions: a 20-point rise from 55% is a 36% increase, and a "20% increase" from 55% reaches only 66%. Say "points" when you mean points.
- **Ratioing the increment when the baseline is off-ratio.** Compute the full target state; incremental scaling silently preserves existing deficits.
- **Reading seasonal steps as trends.** Month-on-month changes in seasonal data mean nothing without the same-period-last-year comparison. Ask "what did last April look like?" before drafting the word "surge".
- **Presenting small-sample point estimates as precise** — or dismissing them as meaningless. Both miscalibrations cost credibility; size the uncertainty honestly and let large gaps speak with appropriate confidence.
- **Silently excluding (or including) outliers.** Either choice can be right; making it invisibly is always wrong. Disclose exclusions and the anomaly they represent — forecasts are trusted exactly as far as their assumptions are visible.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment measures the precision with which you read, interpret, and draw conclusions from written material. Each question presents a short passage — for your role, typically a policy extract, a governance procedure, a ministerial or directorial communication, a research summary, or a standards document — followed by statements or questions. The dominant format is **True / False / Cannot say**: True when the passage supports the statement, False when it contradicts it, Cannot say when the passage provides insufficient grounds either way. Complementary formats test inference (what genuinely follows), application (how a documented rule applies to a scenario), and clear-writing judgement (which formulation is most accurate and plain).

The typical format is an online, timed test of 15 to 25 minutes with 15 to 30 questions grouped under passages. One rule dominates the scoring: the passage is the entire universe of admissible evidence. Your domain knowledge — often extensive at your level — must be deliberately set aside; a claim you know to be true in the world is still "Cannot say" if the passage does not establish it.

Why does verbal precision matter enough for employers to test it at your level? Because your role converts language into commitments. You identify, analyse, challenge and validate requirements — and challenge in particular is a linguistic act: it means detecting that "must" has quietly become "should", that a rule governs a different population than the team assumes, that a stakeholder's "leaning towards" is being briefed as a decision. You draft the problem statements, recommendations, and summaries that boards act on, where an ambiguous sentence becomes someone else's confident error. And you communicate with stakeholders whose own words are carefully calibrated — reading their hedges, conditions, and silences accurately is stakeholder management in its most literal form. Verbal reasoning practice drills exactly these disciplines under a timer.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Precise comprehension and application of rules** map to **Requirements definition and management**: validating and challenging requirements against source documents means establishing exactly what a policy requires, permits, forbids, and leaves open — including which population each clause actually governs.
- **True/False/Cannot-say discipline** maps to **Context, problem and option analysis**: formulating recommendations "considering feasibility, benefits and impact" demands strict separation of what evidence establishes from what is merely plausible — the same epistemic hygiene the test format enforces.
- **Inference** maps to **Business modelling** and **Systems analysis**: deriving the consequences of documented rules ("if incomplete referrals are returned, then no incomplete referral reaches a caseworker") is how you verify that models and specifications faithfully implement their sources.
- **Reading stakeholder language** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**: building and reaching consensus requires representing positions exactly — hedges, conditions, and protected concerns included — because stakeholders extend trust precisely to those who quote them accurately.
- **Clear-writing judgement** maps to **Testing (business analysis)** and **User experience analysis**: acceptance criteria must be unambiguous and findings must be accessible; both skills reduce to choosing the shortest faithful formulation of a rule or result — the exact judgement the writing questions rehearse.

### Practice questions

Base every answer solely on the passage given.

**Passage 1 (for Questions 1–3)**

"Spend approval framework (extract): Digital and technology spend above £100,000 requires central assurance approval before contract signature. Spend between £25,000 and £100,000 may be approved by the departmental digital board, provided the proposal demonstrates compliance with the technology code of practice. Spend below £25,000 may be approved at deputy director level, except for any spend on data hosting, which requires digital board approval regardless of value. Approval lapses if the contract is not signed within six months."

**Question 1 (easy) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "A £15,000 proposal for data hosting may be approved at deputy director level."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The value routes it under £25,000, where deputy director approval is the default — but the passage attaches an exception: "except for any spend on data hosting, which requires digital board approval regardless of value." The exception explicitly overrides the value-based rule, so the statement is contradicted: False. The trap is structural: the statement invokes the rule; the passage's exception defeats it, and exception clauses routinely sit at the ends of sentences where skim-readers have stopped reading. In requirements validation, this reading order — rule, then sweep for exceptions, then conclude — is mechanical but essential: a category-based exception ("any spend on data hosting") beats a threshold-based rule wherever they collide, and real approval frameworks generate exactly these collisions.

**Question 2 (easy) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "A £60,000 proposal that does not demonstrate compliance with the technology code of practice cannot be approved by the departmental digital board."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The mid-band rule is conditional: spend between £25,000 and £100,000 "may be approved by the departmental digital board, provided the proposal demonstrates compliance". The "provided" clause is a necessary condition on the board's approval power in this band — remove the condition and the permission it qualifies is withdrawn. A £60,000 non-compliant proposal therefore cannot be board-approved under this framework: the passage supports the statement — True. Worth noticing what the passage does not say: whether such a proposal could be revised, or approved by some route outside this extract, is open — but the statement only claims what the conditional establishes, so it stays True. Conditional permissions ("may X, provided Y") are among the most commonly misread structures in governance documents: "may" reads as discretionary, and readers under-weight that the discretion exists only inside the condition. Your challenge function frequently consists of pointing, courteously, at a "provided" someone has skipped.

**Question 3 (easy) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "Central assurance approval takes longer to obtain than digital board approval."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage allocates approval authorities by spend band and category, and sets a six-month lapse rule — but it says nothing anywhere about how long any approval takes to obtain. The statement is highly plausible (central processes are often slower) and that plausibility is precisely the test: neither supported nor contradicted means Cannot say, however strongly experience nods along. This is the discipline transfer for your level: the more senior you become, the more your domain knowledge volunteers itself as evidence, and the more valuable the habit of asking "does the document say that, or do I?" Every requirements dispute in which two experienced people confidently "remember" different rules is this question, at stake in the real world — and the analyst who reaches for the source document rather than the strongest memory is the one who resolves it.

**Passage 2 (for Questions 4–6)**

"From: Director of Service Transformation. Subject: Casework programme — analysis support. Following yesterday's board, three points. First, the board has approved discovery funding for the casework consolidation; it has not approved the programme itself, and colleagues should avoid language suggesting otherwise. Second, I want the discovery's problem definition led by analysis rather than by the incumbent supplier's roadmap — I have seen early drafts that lean heavily on the supplier's framing, and I expect the discovery to test that framing rather than inherit it. Third, the operations directorate has concerns about consolidation affecting local site roles; these concerns are legitimate, and engagement with operational staff should be built into the discovery plan from the start, not added when findings are challenged."

**Question 4 (easy) — Comprehension**

According to the email, what has the board approved?

- A) The casework consolidation programme.
- B) Discovery funding only — and the director explicitly warns against language implying more.
- C) The incumbent supplier's roadmap.
- D) A restructure of local site roles.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The first point is built as an approval plus a fence: "approved discovery funding... it has not approved the programme itself", with an instruction to avoid language suggesting otherwise. Option B captures both the approval and the fence. Option A claims exactly what the director denies; options C and D promote later discussion topics into approvals — the supplier's roadmap is mentioned only as a framing to be tested, and site roles only as a concern to be engaged. The real-world weight of this question is considerable: "the board approved it" is among the most consequential misquotes in programme life, converting funding-to-investigate into mandate-to-build, and directors write fences like this precisely because the conversion happens by careless repetition. When you manage stakeholder communications on a programme, transmitting approval status with exact scope — and correcting inflation when you hear it — is a core part of using evidence to explain decisions.

**Question 5 (moderate) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "The director believes the incumbent supplier's framing of the problem is wrong."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Read the director's actual epistemic position: drafts "lean heavily" on the supplier's framing, and the discovery should "test that framing rather than inherit it". That is a methodological objection — the framing has been adopted without examination — not a verdict that the framing is false. Testing could vindicate the supplier's framing entirely; the director's words are compatible with that outcome, and equally with the opposite. So the belief attributed by the statement ("is wrong") is neither supported nor contradicted: Cannot say. The distinction is worth savouring because it is the analyst's own creed stated by a stakeholder: the objection to unexamined framing is independent of whether the framing happens to be right — inheriting a correct answer without testing it is still a discovery failure, because you cannot know it is correct. Analysts who report this email as "the director thinks the supplier is wrong" would poison the supplier relationship with an inference the text never makes; the accurate summary — "the director wants the framing independently tested" — is both faithful and actionable.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Inference**

Which is the best inference from the director's third point?

- A) The director considers the operations directorate's concerns an obstacle to be managed.
- B) The director expects consolidation to be abandoned if operational staff object.
- C) The director anticipates that discovery findings may be contested, and wants operational engagement done early and genuinely so that findings are robust to that challenge rather than retrofitted against it.
- D) The director believes local site roles will not be affected by consolidation.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Unpack the third point's final clause — engagement "built into the discovery plan from the start, not added when findings are challenged". The phrase concedes, in passing, that findings may be challenged; and the instruction's logic is that early, genuine engagement produces findings that withstand challenge, whereas late engagement is a defensive patch. Add the explicit "these concerns are legitimate" and option C's reading assembles itself from the text. Option A inverts the tone: "legitimate" plus "built in from the start" is the opposite of obstacle-management framing. Option B fabricates a commitment — taking concerns seriously is not granting a veto, and nothing in the email addresses abandonment conditions. Option D contradicts the premise: concerns about roles being affected only make sense, and are only called legitimate, because effects are possible. The professional pattern beneath C deserves noting: inclusion of affected staff is being framed not merely as courtesy but as analytical robustness — evidence gathered with those affected is harder to dismiss than evidence gathered about them. That principle belongs in every discovery plan you write, and this email shows a director who knows it.

**Passage 3 (for Questions 7–9)**

"Requirements management procedure (extract): Each requirement shall have a single named business owner. Requirements without an identified owner shall not enter the prioritisation process. Where two or more stakeholders claim ownership of a requirement, the lead business analyst shall facilitate agreement; if agreement is not reached within ten working days, the service owner shall determine ownership. A requirement's priority may only be changed at a scheduled prioritisation review, except that the service owner may raise (but not lower) a requirement's priority at any time in response to a legal or safety obligation."

**Question 7 (moderate) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "The service owner may lower a requirement's priority between scheduled reviews if a legal obligation justifies it."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Parse the exception with care: the service owner "may raise (but not lower) a requirement's priority at any time in response to a legal or safety obligation". The parenthesis exists precisely to close the door the statement tries to open: between reviews, lowering is excluded even for the service owner, even with legal justification — the only extra-review power granted is raising. The passage directly contradicts the statement: False. This is drafting worth admiring as well as parsing: the asymmetry is deliberate (legal and safety obligations can make something urgent; they do not make something safely deferrable outside governance), and the parenthetical "(but not lower)" anticipates exactly the misreading being tested. When you operate requirements procedures — and when you draft them — asymmetric powers are the clauses to read twice: "may raise" and "may change" differ by an entire category of authority, and the difference is invisible at skim speed.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Applying the procedure**

A new requirement has two directors both claiming ownership. Twelve working days of facilitated discussion have not produced agreement. Meanwhile the delivery team wants the requirement prioritised urgently. According to the procedure, what is the correct position?

- A) The requirement may enter prioritisation now, since urgency overrides ownership rules.
- B) The lead business analyst should now determine ownership, having facilitated for more than ten days.
- C) The service owner should now determine ownership; until an owner is identified, the requirement cannot enter prioritisation, however urgent.
- D) The two directors should share ownership so prioritisation can proceed.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Chain the applicable clauses. Disputed ownership → the lead business analyst facilitates; agreement not reached within ten working days → "the service owner shall determine ownership" — and twelve days exceeds ten, so that trigger has fired. Separately: "Requirements without an identified owner shall not enter the prioritisation process" — an unconditional gate with no urgency exception anywhere in the passage. Option C assembles both: the resolution mechanism now sits with the service owner, and prioritisation waits for its outcome. Option A invents an urgency override; the procedure's only urgency-flavoured provision (the raise-anytime power) applies to requirements already prioritised, with owners — note how it tempts the misapplication. Option B misassigns the escalation: the analyst facilitates, the service owner determines — the procedure deliberately separates the neutral-facilitator role from the decision authority, and conflating them would compromise both. Option D contradicts "a single named business owner" in the first sentence. Two lessons ride together: procedures are applied by chaining clauses, not by finding one friendly sentence; and the facilitator/decider separation is a design pattern you should recognise — and preserve — because your own role usually holds the facilitation half, and its value depends on not seizing the other half.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Clear-writing judgement**

You are summarising the priority-change rule for a programme wiki. Which formulation is most accurate and clear?

- A) "Priorities can only be changed at prioritisation reviews."
- B) "Priorities change at scheduled reviews. One exception: the service owner can raise — but not lower — a priority at any time, where a legal or safety obligation requires it."
- C) "Priority modification shall be effectuated exclusively within the parameters of scheduled review governance, subject to certain service-owner prerogatives in exigent circumstances."
- D) "The service owner can change priorities whenever legal issues come up; otherwise wait for a review."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Audit each candidate against the source. Option B preserves every load-bearing element in plain words: the default (scheduled reviews), the exception's holder (service owner), its direction (raise only — the parenthetical survives), its timing (any time), and its trigger (legal or safety obligation). A reader can act on it without the source document. Option A states the default and amputates the exception — an over-strict summary that would have someone wrongly blocking a legitimate legal-obligation escalation; over-strict wiki summaries cause real incidents precisely because they read as authoritative. Option D commits the opposite sins: "change" erases the raise-only asymmetry, "legal issues" drops safety and loosens "obligation" into vagueness, and the breezy register invites broad interpretation of a narrow power. Option C preserves the meaning roughly ("certain prerogatives", "exigent circumstances") while transmitting almost nothing usable — officialese is not caution, it is fog with a formal accent, and plain-language guidance across government exists because fog at scale becomes error at scale. The craft standard for your summaries, criteria, and wiki notes alike: the shortest formulation from which a careful stranger could reconstruct the rule — asymmetries, triggers, and all.

**Passage 4 (for Questions 10–12)**

"Evaluation of the assisted digital pilot (summary): The pilot offered face-to-face support for the licensing service at six libraries over four months. 380 users were supported; 74% completed their application during the support session, compared with a 58% completion rate among unsupported applicants with similar starting characteristics over the same period. Users supported at the two rural sites accounted for 61% of all supported users. Session demand grew month on month at every site. The evaluation did not measure whether supported users could complete independently on subsequent occasions, and cost per completed application in the pilot was £31 against £4.80 for standard digital completion. The evaluation team notes that pilot sites were selected partly for high expected demand, and cautions against extrapolating demand levels to other locations."

**Question 10 (moderate) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "Supported users completed applications at a higher rate than comparable unsupported applicants."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The passage states it directly, and — the detail that makes this True rather than a hedge — with the comparison done properly: 74% completion among supported users against 58% "among unsupported applicants with similar starting characteristics over the same period". The similar-characteristics clause matters because it pre-empts the standard objection (perhaps supported users were easier cases): the evaluators matched on starting characteristics, and the statement's own word "comparable" mirrors exactly that. So the passage supports the statement as phrased: True, a 16-point completion advantage. Note the calibration boundary for later questions: the passage supports "completed at a higher rate" — an observed outcome — which is not yet "the support caused the difference" (matching strengthens but does not prove causation) and not "the pilot should be rolled out" (a judgement involving the £31 cost figure and more). Verbal reasoning at your level is largely this: knowing precisely how far each sentence of evidence carries, and stopping there.

**Question 11 (hard) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "The pilot demonstrates that assisted digital support reduces long-term demand for support by teaching users to complete applications independently."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The statement makes two claims and the passage declines both. On independent completion: the evaluation "did not measure whether supported users could complete independently on subsequent occasions" — an explicit measurement gap, which means the teaching-effect claim is neither established nor refuted; the passage tells you it cannot tell you. On long-term demand: the only demand evidence runs the wrong way for the statement — session demand "grew month on month at every site" — but growing pilot demand does not contradict a long-term reduction claim either (growth could reflect awareness spreading rather than repeat users, and "long-term" outruns a four-month window). Unmeasured plus unresolvable equals Cannot say — not False, because contradiction requires the passage to actively refute the mechanism, and it merely fails to test it. This distinction carries a live policy weight: "support teaches independence" is the standard argument for funding assisted digital provision, and it may well be true — but this evaluation, as summarised, provides no evidence for it, and an analyst who lets the plausible mechanism ride into a business case on the pilot's coat-tails has converted a measurement gap into a claimed benefit. The professional phrasing: "whether support builds independent capability was not measured and should be in any next phase." Naming the gap is the analysis.

**Question 12 (hard) — Inference and synthesis**

A programme board asks: "What does this evaluation support saying about rolling assisted digital support out nationally?" Which response best reflects the passage?

- A) "Roll out nationally: support raises completion by 16 points and demand is growing everywhere."
- B) "Do not roll out: at £31 per completion against £4.80, the economics are prohibitive."
- C) "The pilot shows a real completion benefit for supported users, at materially higher cost per completion; demand patterns from these deliberately high-demand sites — especially the rural concentration — cannot be extrapolated, and repeat-usage effects are unmeasured. A targeted rollout hypothesis (rural-weighted, with independence effects measured) is what the evidence supports testing next."
- D) "The evaluation is too flawed to support any statement."

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The board's question is calibration under synthesis: what may be said, at what strength, from these findings alone. Option C earns each of its clauses from the passage: the completion benefit is real (74% vs 58%, matched comparison); the cost differential is material and belongs in the same sentence (£31 vs £4.80 — though note the comparison's limit: £4.80 buys a standard completion, while the £31 buys completions among users who would otherwise fail at meaningful rates, so the figures inform rather than settle the value question); extrapolation of demand is explicitly cautioned against by the evaluators themselves, whose site selection favoured high demand; the rural concentration (61% from two of six sites) suggests where value concentrates without proving it; and the unmeasured independence effect is named as the next thing to measure, not assumed in either direction. Option A quotes only the favourable findings and extrapolates exactly where the passage forbids it. Option B lets one number veto, misusing the cost comparison across non-comparable purchases. Option D mistakes documented limitations for fatal flaws — an evaluation that names its own gaps is more usable, not less, because you know precisely how far to trust it. The shape of option C — benefit sized, cost acknowledged, limits quoted, next test specified — is the shape of a defensible recommendation, and producing that shape from mixed evidence is your role's verbal reasoning in its final, board-facing form.

### Preparation tips

- **Read the passage once, thoroughly, building a structural map** — which clauses are rules, which are exceptions, which populations each governs — before reading any statement. Statement-first reading invites the keyword-matching that traps are built for.
- **Sweep for exceptions and parentheticals last-to-first.** Exception clauses cluster at sentence ends ("except...", "provided...", "(but not lower)"), exactly where attention fades. Make the end-of-sentence sweep a mechanical habit.
- **Hold the True/False/Cannot-say boundary precisely:** False requires active contradiction; silence, however suggestive, is Cannot say. Before choosing between them, ask "does the passage speak to this claim at all?"
- **Calibrate verbs when reading and writing summaries:** "tested" is not "rejected"; "did not measure" is not "found no effect"; "leaning towards" is not "decided"; "supports investigating" is not "supports doing". Most hard questions — and most misbriefings — live in these gaps.
- **Practise on the documents your role already handles.** Weekly, take a live procedure or evaluation and write four statements about it — one True, one False, two Cannot say (one plausible, one implausible). Authoring the Cannot-says teaches the boundary faster than answering ever does, and the exercise doubles as artefact review.
- **Time-box by passage, not by statement.** One careful read plus quick verification per statement beats repeated panicked re-reads. If a statement stalls you, flag, choose your best-supported answer, and move.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Importing domain knowledge.** At your level this is the dominant error: you know how spend controls, discoveries, and pilots usually work, and the test measures whether you can read what this document says instead. Plausibility is not passage support.
- **Stopping at the rule and missing the exception** — or applying an exception beyond its stated trigger and direction. Both errors reverse answers; both reverse real approval decisions.
- **Treating methodological objections as verdicts.** "The framing should be tested" does not mean "the framing is wrong"; "selection favoured high demand" does not mean "the findings are false". Distinguish critiques of process from claims about conclusions.
- **Inflating approval and decision language.** "Discovery funding" is not "the programme"; "concerns are legitimate" is not "the concern decides". Quote scope exactly — in tests, minutes, and corridor summaries alike.
- **Letting one number veto a synthesis** — or letting a strong finding silence its own caveats. Board-grade answers carry benefit, cost, and limitation in the same paragraph, each at its evidenced strength.
- **Confusing "not measured" with "not real" (or with "real").** A measurement gap supports exactly one conclusion: that the next phase should measure it. Anything stronger, in either direction, is your hope talking.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment (SJT) presents realistic workplace scenarios and asks you to evaluate courses of action. The material is interpersonal and organisational rather than technical: a sponsor wants a finding softened, two directors want incompatible things, a mentee's work is not ready, a supplier's claim does not survive scrutiny, a deadline collides with an obligation. Formats vary — choose the **most effective** response, the **least effective**, rank all options, or rate each independently — and there is rarely a flawless choice; options are scored against a profile agreed by experienced practitioners who know how UK government digital and data teams actually work.

The typical format is an online assessment of 20 to 40 minutes across 10 to 20 scenarios, often generously timed because it measures judgement rather than speed. Scoring consistently rewards a recognisable cluster of professional behaviours: user focus, evidence integrity, transparent escalation with options attached, proportionate response, collaboration over heroics, inclusion, and the honest handling of mistakes — your own and others'.

Why do employers weight SJTs heavily for business analysts at your level? Because independence relocates the difficulty of the job. The technical work — modelling, analysis, requirements — has established methods; the judgement work has only principles, applied under pressure, usually with incomplete information and an audience. You manage stakeholder relationships in your own right, which means you personally absorb the pressure that stakeholders used to route around you. You lead projects, which means your handling of a slipping deadline or a flawed sponsor figure sets the tone for the team watching. And you mentor others, which means your judgement propagates: the trainee who watches you handle a data discrepancy learns more from that hour than from any course. An SJT samples these moments cheaply; your reputation samples them expensively. Practising here is rehearsal for both.

When your answer differs from the keyed one, treat the explanation as a seasoned colleague's reasoning laid alongside yours. At your level the gaps are rarely about knowing the right value — they are about weighting competing right values, and that calibration is exactly what comparison builds.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Independent judgement under pressure** maps to your role level: **leading small to medium size projects** and **working independently** means the escalation question changes from "should I ask?" to "what do I decide, what do I recommend, and what genuinely needs a senior decision?" — the axis on which most scenarios turn.
- **Stakeholder pressure and consensus-building** map to **Stakeholder relationship management**: at your level you **build and reach consensus with stakeholders** and **use evidence to explain decisions** — scenarios test whether you can hold evidence steady under sponsor pressure while keeping relationships workable.
- **Evidence and record integrity** map to **Requirements definition and management**, **User experience analysis** and **Testing (business analysis)**: challenging requirements, presenting findings faithfully, and keeping test records truthful are precisely the duties that social pressure targets; several scenarios apply that pressure.
- **Delivery behaviours** map to **Adapting to delivery methodologies** and **Business process improvement**: scenarios about scope, pace, and process shortcuts test whether you protect the purpose of a practice while flexing its weight — the mark of someone who adapts methodology to deliver value rather than ritual.
- **Mentoring and community** map to your duty to **develop the business analysis community by sharing best practice and mentoring others**: scenarios probe whether you build capability in others rather than dependence on yourself, and whether you spend influence on making the profession around you better.

### Practice questions

Commit to an answer before reading each explanation. Every option is something a real professional might do; the differences are in consequences.

**Question 1 (easy) — The sponsor and the inconvenient finding**

You lead the analysis on a process improvement project. Your evaluation shows the sponsor's favoured initiative — a triage checklist the sponsor personally championed — has produced no measurable improvement in error rates, though staff report liking it. Presenting a draft to the sponsor before the programme board, the sponsor says: "Drop the error-rate section; the staff feedback tells the real story." What is the most effective response?

- A) Drop the section — the sponsor owns the paper and staff feedback is genuine evidence.
- B) Keep the section unchanged and present it to the board without further discussion with the sponsor.
- C) Explain that the board needs both findings to decide well; offer to present them fairly — no-measurable-effect alongside genuine staff support, with possible explanations and a proposal for what to test next — but be clear you cannot present an evaluation with the error-rate evidence removed.
- D) Drop the section from the paper but mention the error-rate finding verbally if a board member happens to ask.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Locate what is actually yours to protect: not the paper's every word, but the truthfulness of the evaluation the board will rely on. Option C protects exactly that while giving the sponsor everything legitimate they could want — fair framing, the staff-support finding given full weight, explanations explored (perhaps the checklist needs more time, perhaps it helps morale but not accuracy), and a constructive next step. The firm boundary at the end is stated as a professional obligation, not a confrontation, and most sponsors, met this way, accept it — they wanted a good story more than a false one. Option A makes you co-author of a misleading evaluation; when the error rates surface later (they always do), both your credibility and the sponsor's are spent. Option B protects the evidence but torches the relationship needlessly, ambushing a sponsor you could have persuaded — consensus-building is your named skill precisely for moments like this. Option D is the worst compromise: the written record misleads while your conscience rides on a board member's lucky question. The principle, which recurs at every seniority: negotiate framing generously, never negotiate findings.

**Question 2 (easy) — Two directors, one analyst**

Your project's steering group includes two directors. After the meeting, Director A tells you to prioritise analysis of the fraud-prevention workstream. An hour later, Director B emails: the citizen-experience workstream must come first, "as agreed at steering". Your reading of the minutes supports neither ordering explicitly. You lead the analysis and the plan is yours to propose. What is the most effective response?

- A) Follow Director A's instruction — fraud carries more organisational risk.
- B) Follow Director B's, since it came with a claimed steering mandate.
- C) Draft a short sequencing proposal with the evidence for each ordering (risk exposure, dependency, effort), send it to both directors noting their differing steers, and ask the steering chair to confirm the sequence at or before the next meeting — proposing an interim plan so work continues meanwhile.
- D) Split the team's analysis capacity evenly between both workstreams and tell each director theirs is being prioritised.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** At your level, conflicting steers are not a problem to route upward raw — they are a problem to structure and then route. Option C does the analyst's version of resolution: it converts a status contest into an evidence question (which sequencing does risk, dependency and effort actually favour?), makes the disagreement visible to both principals simultaneously (no private siding, no surprise), engages the legitimate decision forum through its chair, and — the mark of delivery experience — keeps work moving under a declared interim plan rather than stalling for governance. Options A and B each pick a director by private criterion, guaranteeing one principal later discovers you overrode their steer on your own authority — relationship damage that evidence-based process would have avoided entirely. Option D is the seductive disaster: it commits capacity fractionally, ensures both workstreams progress slowly, and — fatally — tells each director a different story; being caught managing stakeholders by contradiction ends trust with both at once. Note what makes C available: you came with a proposal, not just a dilemma. Managing stakeholder relationships at your level means giving decision-makers well-framed decisions, and the framing is your contribution.

**Question 3 (easy) — Your own error, downstream**

Reviewing an old options appraisal you authored — now cited in a live business case — you find an arithmetic error: one option's weighted score was inflated, and correcting it narrows the gap between the recommended option and the runner-up from decisive to marginal. The recommendation might well survive on qualitative grounds, but the numbers no longer speak as clearly. The business case goes to committee in three weeks. What is the most effective response?

- A) Say nothing — the recommendation probably survives, and reopening it risks confusion and delay.
- B) Correct the appraisal, inform the business case owner and your project sponsor promptly, and provide a short note on what changed, whether the recommendation still holds and on what grounds, in time for the committee.
- C) Quietly correct the archived appraisal so future readers see accurate numbers, without alerting the business case team.
- D) Wait to see whether committee scrutiny catches it; volunteer the correction only if asked.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Three weeks is a gift — enough time to correct, re-reason, and re-brief calmly — and option B spends it properly: the artefact is fixed, the people relying on it are told while they can still act, and the accompanying note does the analytical work the correction creates (does the recommendation survive? on what grounds now?) rather than dumping a raw problem on the business case owner. That note matters: a correction without re-analysis is half a job, and the recommendation may genuinely stand on qualitative grounds — but that judgement must now be made in the open, by the people accountable for it, not silently presumed by the person who made the error. Option A gambles the committee's decision on "probably", with your name on the gamble twice — once for the error, once for the concealment. Option C is the archivist's evasion: the historical record improves while the live decision proceeds on the flawed copy — precisely backwards. Option D outsources your integrity to the committee's diligence. Here is the senior-level truth this scenario teaches: at your level, errors are survivable and concealment is not — the analyst who self-reports with a correction and an impact note typically gains standing, because everyone in the room now knows what happens when your numbers are wrong: you find it, you say so, you fix it.

**Question 4 (easy) — The methodology mismatch**

You join a project whose delivery manager runs strict two-week sprints. Your analysis work keeps misfiring against the cadence: discovery-type questions are being forced into sprint-sized stories, and half-researched requirements are declared "done" when the sprint ends. The team is frustrated with analysis; you are new. What is the most effective response?

- A) Insist analysis be moved outside the sprint framework entirely, since discovery does not belong in sprints.
- B) Keep forcing analysis into the sprint shapes — consistency with team process matters more than analysis quality.
- C) Talk with the delivery manager about how analysis can flow with the cadence rather than against it — for example, running analysis a sprint ahead of build, using spike stories with explicit questions as their outcome, and defining "done" for analysis stories as validated findings rather than finished features — and propose trying it for two sprints.
- D) Complain to the product manager that the delivery manager does not understand analysis.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** This scenario is your adapting-to-delivery-methodologies skill in its live form: the named capability is adapting how you work and what you produce to the team's methodology to deliver value — not exempting yourself from it (option A) and not surrendering your discipline's quality to it (option B). Option C's specific proposals are the point: analysis-ahead-of-build respects both the sprint cadence and analysis's need to precede commitment; spike stories give research a legitimate sprint-shaped container with honest outcomes (questions answered, not features shipped); a tailored definition of done stops "sprint ended" masquerading as "analysis complete". Crucially, C routes through the delivery manager as a collaborator — the cadence is theirs to run, and a time-boxed trial ("two sprints") converts a philosophical argument into a cheap experiment, which is how methodology disputes are actually won. Option A stakes a claim to special status that new arrivals rarely hold and that isn't even correct — analysis can thrive inside agile cadences when shaped for them. Option B preserves harmony by shipping half-truths as requirements, and the team's existing frustration shows where that leads. Option D escalates a design problem as a personality problem, spending relationship capital on making an ally look bad. The pattern: when practice and process collide, redesign the interface between them — with the process's owner, on trial terms.

**Question 5 (moderate) — The supplier's number**

A supplier's proposal, favoured by your programme director, claims its document-processing product will "reduce manual handling by 80%, as achieved at a comparable UK public-sector deployment". Your due-diligence analysis finds the referenced deployment processed a far simpler document set, and the supplier's own case study shows 80% applied only to one document category — overall manual handling fell about 35%. The director is preparing to cite the 80% figure at investment committee next week. What is the most effective response?

- A) Let it stand — the director is accountable for what they cite, and 35% is still a good result.
- B) Brief the director promptly and privately with your analysis: the 80% is category-specific and context-dependent, the defensible comparable figure is ~35%, and citing 80% risks committee credibility and a benefits baseline the programme will later be held to — then offer revised wording and to attend as analytical support.
- C) Email the investment committee secretariat directly with your findings, copying the director.
- D) Raise it publicly in the programme's open Slack channel so the record shows you flagged it.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Timing and channel decide this one. The director has a week — enough to adjust the case gracefully — and no interest, once informed, in citing a figure that will embarrass them at committee or, worse, become the programme's contractual benefits baseline (option B's second argument is the sharper one: inflated citations do not just risk challenge on the day, they set the number the programme is later measured against, converting today's optimism into next year's "failed to deliver"). A private, prompt, evidence-attached brief with revised wording and offered support is stakeholder management doing exactly what your skill description says: using evidence to explain decisions, and working to improve relationships while doing it. Option A confuses accountability with information: the director is indeed accountable for what they cite — which is precisely why your analysis must reach them before they cite it; silent analysts make accountable directors fail. Option C detonates the relationship by informing the committee before the director, converting a correctable briefing error into a public ambush — the copy-line does not soften it. Option D performs integrity instead of practising it: the open-channel flag optimises for your visible blamelessness while maximising the director's embarrassment and the supplier dispute's audience. The rule: challenge numbers early, privately, with the corrected version in hand — the goal is a director who walks into committee right, not a record proving they were warned.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Scope pressure at the consensus meeting**

You are facilitating the final prioritisation workshop for a release. Consensus has formed around a scope that defers the reporting module. As the workshop closes, the head of finance — who accepted the deferral reluctantly — says: "To be clear, I'll be telling the executive committee that the analysis team decided reporting was unimportant." The room goes quiet. What is the most effective response?

- A) Let it pass to avoid conflict; correct the record later if it becomes a problem.
- B) Respond immediately and evenly: restate what was actually decided and by whom — the group prioritised against the release capacity using the agreed criteria; reporting was deferred, not judged unimportant; and the deferral was the workshop's consensus, not an analysis-team verdict — then offer to document the decision rationale, including finance's concerns, for exactly such executive conversations.
- C) Reopen the prioritisation discussion so the head of finance can win some scope back.
- D) After the meeting, email the head of finance's line manager about the mischaracterisation.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Two things are being tested in that quiet room: the accuracy of the record, and whether consensus decisions survive their first contact with a dissatisfied loser. Option B defends both, immediately and without heat. The restatement corrects each distortion precisely — the decision's content (deferred ≠ unimportant), its method (agreed criteria against real capacity), and its ownership (the workshop's consensus, not the analysis team's preference; facilitators must never absorb ownership of group decisions, or every future workshop's decisions become the facilitator's to blame). The offer to document rationale-with-concerns is the consensus-builder's move: it gives the head of finance a legitimate channel for their dissatisfaction — their concerns, accurately recorded, in front of the executive — which is usually what the barbed comment was really asking for. Option A lets a public mischaracterisation stand in front of the whole room; by the time it "becomes a problem" it will be executive-level received wisdom, and every workshop participant learned that decisions here can be re-narrated at will. Option C rewards the tactic — teaching all stakeholders that consensus is provisional until someone threatens to misreport it. Option D escalates behind a stakeholder's back over words said to your face; whatever it wins, it is not a working relationship. Facilitation authority is spent in exactly these moments: calmly, factually, in the room.

**Question 7 (moderate) — The mentee's stalled development**

You mentor a junior analyst from another team. Over three months you notice a pattern: their analysis is consistently sound, but they bring you every stakeholder difficulty to resolve — you have twice intervened directly with their stakeholders at their request, and both interventions worked. Today they ask you to attend their next stakeholder meeting "because it goes better when you're there". What is the most effective response?

- A) Attend — the meetings do go better, and their project benefits.
- B) Decline all further help so they learn independence the hard way.
- C) Decline the meeting but restructure the help: prepare with them beforehand (map the stakeholders, rehearse the difficult exchanges, plan their opening), debrief afterwards, and name the pattern honestly — your interventions have been substituting for their skill rather than building it, and the goal now is their capability.
- D) Attend, but sit silently at the back so your presence reassures without participating.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Diagnose before choosing: the mentee's analysis is fine; the gap is stakeholder skill, and the mentoring pattern — two successful direct interventions — has been treating the symptom while feeding the cause. Each rescue worked and each deepened the dependence; "it goes better when you're there" is the dependence describing itself. Option C converts rescue into scaffolding: preparation and rehearsal transfer the actual techniques (stakeholder mapping, difficult-conversation planning — teachable, practisable skills), the debrief converts each real meeting into a lesson, and naming the pattern honestly respects the mentee as an adult who can hear a development need stated plainly — which is itself modelling the stakeholder honesty they need to learn. Option A is kindness compounding the problem: their project gains a meeting; their career loses a capability, and you gain a permanent meeting. Option B has the right destination and a callous route — withdrawal without scaffolding teaches sink-or-swim, and mentees mostly learn "don't ask for help", which is the opposite lesson. Option D is superstition management: silent presence builds reliance on a talisman, the purest form of the dependence you are trying to end. The mentoring principle, which is also your community-development duty in miniature: every intervention should leave the person more capable of not needing it — measure your mentoring by your own growing unnecessariness.

**Question 8 (moderate) — The accessibility finding and the launch date**

Two weeks before a service's public beta launch, your acceptance-criteria review against the team's user research turns up a significant issue: the research included no participants who use assistive technology, and automated accessibility checks — which pass — are the only accessibility evidence. The service standard requires services to work for everyone, and assessment is scheduled post-launch. The delivery manager says: "Automated checks pass, the assessment isn't until later, and two weeks is two weeks." What is the most effective response?

- A) Accept the position — automated checks passing is genuine evidence, and the assessment will catch anything real.
- B) Demand the launch be postponed until assistive-technology research is complete.
- C) Set out the risk precisely and propose a proportionate plan: automated checks catch only a fraction of accessibility barriers, so commission assistive-technology testing now — even a small moderated round in the remaining fortnight — brief the service owner on the gap and the evidence status so the launch decision is informed, and prepare a rapid-fix path for what testing finds.
- D) Log the concern in the risk register and take no further action, since accessibility is ultimately the service owner's accountability.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Separate what is known from what is assumed: automated checks passing is real but thin evidence — such tools catch a minority of the barriers assistive-technology users actually hit (missing context, focus traps, unlabelled dynamic content), so the honest status is "no evidence of failure, and insufficient evidence of success" for a population the service standard explicitly protects. Option C acts on that status proportionately: testing commissioned now (a small moderated round with screen-reader and other AT users is achievable in a fortnight and will find the big barriers), the service owner briefed so the launch decision is made by its owner with the gap visible, and a fix path ready so findings convert to action rather than panic. This is the pattern that beats both extremes: option A treats absence of evidence as evidence of absence and outsources the users' interests to a post-launch assessment — meaning real excluded users are the detection mechanism; option B spends your credibility demanding an outcome that is the service owner's call, on a risk whose size you have not yet measured — and postponement may prove unnecessary if the testing comes back clean. Option D is compliance theatre: a risk register entry that changes no behaviour protects the process record, not the users. Note who never appears in the delivery manager's framing: the people who cannot use the service. Keeping them in the decision — with evidence, through the accountable owner — is what user focus means at your level.

**Question 9 (moderate) — The community contribution**

Your department's business analysis community is patchy: practice varies wildly between teams, new analysts get inconsistent inductions, and a recent quality review criticised requirements artefacts across three programmes. The head of profession, stretched thin, asks for volunteers to improve things. You lead a busy project. What is the most effective response?

- A) Stay focused on your project — community work is discretionary and your delivery commitments come first.
- B) Volunteer to personally write a comprehensive best-practice manual covering all analysis activities.
- C) Offer a bounded, high-leverage contribution: propose the community target the quality review's specific findings first, offer to lead one strand — for example, a requirements-quality peer-review circle where analysts across programmes review each other's artefacts — and share the templates and checking methods your own project uses.
- D) Suggest the head of profession hire an external consultancy to standardise practice.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Your role description makes community development a duty, not a hobby — "develop the business analysis community by sharing best practice and mentoring others" — so option A misreads the job, not just the moment; delivery pressure is real, which is why the right contribution is bounded, not absent. Option C is shaped like contributions that actually work: it targets evidenced need (the quality review's findings — improvement effort aimed at documented weakness beats improvement effort aimed at everything), it is scoped to survive contact with your project workload (one strand, led), and its chosen mechanism — a cross-programme peer-review circle — is high-leverage precisely because it builds the community's capability to improve itself rather than depending on any individual's output. Sharing your own templates seeds it with concrete practice. Option B is generous and doomed: comprehensive manuals written by one busy person arrive late, reflect one team's context, and are read by nobody — the monument model of knowledge sharing, versus C's circulation model. Option D outsources the community's development, which is the one thing that cannot be outsourced: external frameworks can inform practice, but a community's standards take hold through its own members reviewing, arguing, and teaching — the consultancy option purchases documents, not capability. The senior-analyst insight: at your level, "sharing best practice" means designing the mechanisms by which practice spreads, and the best mechanisms are the ones that run without you.

**Question 10 (hard) — Least effective: the failing project you inherited**

You take over as lead analyst on a struggling project. In your first week you find: the requirements baseline was signed off without validation by operational stakeholders; the traceability matrix has not been maintained for two months; and the previous analyst — now on another project in your directorate — appears to have known both facts. The delivery manager wants to "look forward, not back". Which response would be **least effective**?

- A) Assess and triage: quantify the validation gap and the matrix decay, size the rework honestly, and give the delivery manager and project sponsor a costed recovery plan with priorities.
- B) Arrange validation sessions with the operational stakeholders whose review was skipped, framed as "confirming the baseline as we resume", and restore the matrix for the highest-risk requirements first.
- C) Circulate a written analysis to the project board attributing the project's difficulties to the previous analyst's specific failures, so accountability is clear before recovery begins.
- D) Privately and without blame, compare notes with the previous analyst to learn the history — what pressures produced the shortcuts, and what undocumented knowledge they hold — and feed anything relevant into the recovery plan.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Hunt the odd one out among four plausible-sounding moves. Options A, B and D are the recovery toolkit in its natural order: A converts discovered mess into sized, prioritised, costed decisions — the analyst's first duty on inheriting entropy; B repairs the substantive gaps with elegant stakeholder framing ("confirming as we resume" validates without staging a public inquest into the skipped step) and risk-first sequencing; D harvests the one irreplaceable asset the previous analyst holds — context — and its no-blame framing is what makes the harvest possible, while also surfacing the systemic pressures (deadline compression? under-resourcing?) that produced the shortcuts and will produce them again if unaddressed. Option C is least effective on every axis that matters: it spends the project's first-week momentum on retrospective blame; it converts a colleague who holds critical undocumented knowledge into a defensive adversary (destroying option D's value the moment it circulates); it poisons the directorate relationship you will need — the previous analyst still works nearby; and it hands the board a personalised story that obscures the systemic one. Note the trap in C's construction — "so accountability is clear" borrows the language of good governance for an act of blame allocation; real accountability for recovery points forward (who fixes what by when), and if the previous analyst's conduct genuinely needs formal review, that is a line-management conversation through proper channels, not a board paper. "Look forward, not back" is wrong too, incidentally — the past must be examined — but examined for causes and knowledge, as D does, not for culprits, as C does. That distinction is the whole question.

**Question 11 (hard) — Ranking responses: the discovered dependency**

Mid-project, your systems analysis uncovers that a critical integration depends on an API owned by another department, which is scheduled for decommissioning in eight months — before your service's planned go-live. Nobody on your project knew. The other department has offered its replacement API, but documentation is sparse and its capabilities may not cover your use cases. Rank these responses from most to least effective:

1. Analyse the gap now: map your integration requirements against the replacement API's actual capabilities, engaging the other department's technical team directly, and quantify what is covered, what is not, and what is unknown.
2. Raise the dependency at your project's next risk review with what you know, flagging it as potentially plan-changing and recommending the gap analysis as an urgent action.
3. Immediately alert your delivery manager and service owner informally today, so the plan-level implications are known while the gap analysis proceeds.
4. Redesign the integration approach now to avoid the other department's APIs entirely, before analysing the replacement.

- A) 3, 1, 2, 4
- B) 1, 2, 3, 4
- C) 3, 2, 1, 4
- D) 4, 1, 3, 2

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Anchor the extremes. Worst is clearly 4: redesigning before analysing spends major effort on an assumption — the replacement may cover everything, making the redesign pure waste, or cover nothing, in which case the redesign proceeds from ignorance of what it must achieve; solution-first is the anti-pattern your entire discipline exists to prevent. Best is 3, and here is the senior judgement: a discovered plan-threatening dependency has a half-life — every day the delivery manager and service owner plan without knowing it, they compound decisions on a false premise — so a same-day informal alert ("found something potentially plan-changing; analysing now; details by Friday") beats even the excellent option 1 for first place, because 1's gap analysis takes days and information this consequential should not wait for its own completeness. Then 1 before 2: the gap analysis is the substance — direct technical engagement, requirements mapped against real capabilities, the unknown sized — while 2's risk-review entry is the formalisation, much stronger once 1's findings give it content, and dangerously slow as a first move (next risk review may be a fortnight away; option 2 alone parks a burning issue in a queue). So: 3, 1, 2, 4 — option A. The embedded principle for independent analysts: escalate the existence of a threat immediately and informally; escalate its size formally once you have measured it. The two escalations do different jobs, and the common error — waiting to raise anything until the analysis is complete — confuses them.

**Question 12 (hard) — Competing goods: the truth, the team, and the timing**

Your project's show-and-tell to senior stakeholders is tomorrow morning. This afternoon, validating the team's demo data, you discover the headline metric the product manager plans to present — "median application time down from 40 to 12 minutes" — is wrong: the 12-minute figure comes from testing with pre-filled data, and the honest current figure with real user input is about 22 minutes. Still a strong improvement, but not the story on the slides. The product manager is in back-to-back meetings until this evening; the team has worked weeks towards tomorrow. What is the most effective response?

- A) Say nothing until after the show-and-tell, then correct the figure quietly for future presentations — the improvement is real, and the team deserves its moment.
- B) Interrupt the product manager's meetings with a message marked urgent: the headline figure is wrong, here is the correct one (40 → 22 minutes, with the pre-fill explanation), here is proposed revised wording that is still a strong story, and you are available this evening to update the slides together.
- C) Let the presentation proceed but position yourself to interject with the correction if the metric is questioned.
- D) Email the senior stakeholders tonight with the corrected figure so they arrive informed.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Test each option against the two goods in tension — the team's earned moment and the integrity of what stakeholders are told — and notice that option B is the only one that preserves both, because the correction travels with its solution: "40 to 22 minutes with real user input" is still a 45% improvement and an honest, strong story, and an evening's slide revision is a small price for presenting a number that survives scrutiny. The urgency is justified arithmetic: the cost of interrupting meetings is minutes; the cost of a senior audience learning later that the headline was tested-with-pre-filled-data is the presentation's entire credibility, retroactively — and such figures are learned later, because 12 minutes will be quoted, baselined, and eventually tested. Option A prices the team's moment above the stakeholders' truth, and buys the team a delayed embarrassment wearing a celebration's clothes; "correct it quietly later" also assumes the figure stops travelling, which quoted headline metrics never do. Option C is integrity as ambush insurance — it protects you while planning to correct your own product manager live in front of seniors, the worst possible venue, and only if someone happens to ask. Option D bypasses the product manager entirely, informing their audience behind their back and converting tomorrow from their presentation into their defence. The deciding habit: when you find an error on the eve of its publication, the move is always the same — the owner, immediately, with the corrected version and your help attached. Everything else is either concealment or ambush wearing process clothing.

### Preparation tips

- **Internalise the behavioural compass the scoring rewards:** evidence integrity, user focus, early transparent escalation with options attached, proportionate response, consensus built rather than assumed, capability built in others, and mistakes handled by owner-first correction. After each practice question, name which of these the keyed answer served — the naming builds the reflex.
- **Locate ownership before choosing.** Ask "whose decision is this, whose relationship is this, whose error is this?" The strong options consistently route information to owners early and completely; the weak ones bypass, ambush, or silently absorb.
- **Prefer options that carry solutions with problems.** At your level, raising an issue naked is half a job: the keyed answers almost always attach the corrected figure, the revised wording, the recovery plan, or the trial proposal.
- **In "least effective" questions, look past rudeness for structural damage.** The worst option is rarely the bluntest; it is usually the one that destroys information flow, converts allies to adversaries, or spends the team's trust on personal cover.
- **In ranking questions, weigh information half-life.** Options that release plan-critical information immediately usually outrank even excellent analysis that delays it; completeness is a virtue in findings and a vice in alarms.
- **Debrief your real weeks.** Once a week, take one live judgement call you made, generate the three options you did not choose, and rank all four honestly. At your level SJT preparation and professional reflection are the same activity — and the habit is one worth modelling aloud for the analysts you mentor.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Negotiating findings to preserve relationships.** Framing is negotiable, evidence is not — and the options that soften, omit, or delay findings under stakeholder pressure score poorly however diplomatic they sound.
- **Performing integrity instead of practising it.** Public flags, record-protecting emails, and positioned interjections that maximise your visible blamelessness while ambushing colleagues are consistently near the bottom of the key.
- **Absorbing decisions that belong to others.** Facilitators who own the group's decision, analysts who demand launches move, mentors who attend the meeting forever — seizing ownership reads as strength and scores as misjudgement.
- **Waiting for complete analysis before raising plan-critical threats.** Escalate existence immediately, size formally later. The polished paper that arrives after the decision is a monument, not an input.
- **Rescuing instead of building.** Interventions that substitute your skill for a colleague's — however successful each instance — score below scaffolding that transfers the skill. This applies to mentees, teams, and entire communities.
- **Letting the user disappear from the room.** When a scenario's stakeholders are all professionals under deadline, check which option still represents the people the service is for — accessibility gaps, excluded populations, unmeasured harms. The keyed answer almost always keeps them visible through the accountable owner.
- **Choosing your cynical self over your professional self.** Answer as the analyst you are on your best day — that is who the assessment scores, and who your team, your mentees, and your profession are counting on.

## Conclusion

Well done for working through this guide — four assessment disciplines, nearly fifty worked questions, and a considerable amount of honest self-examination. That is a serious investment in your own capability, and it will pay in more currencies than test scores.

Consider what you have actually practised. In the cognitive section: testability checks on tidy-looking requirements, deduction that respects the gap between what rules entail and what behaviour proves, critical-path reasoning, segmented reading of operational data, and the discipline of re-establishing the problem before evaluating solutions. In the numeric section: wrong-base and percentage-point vigilance, weighted averages and target-state ratios, seasonal comparison, staged benefits models with endpoint tests, definitional reconciliation between conflicting reports, and the honest sizing of small-sample evidence. In the verbal section: exception-sweeping, conditional permissions, the precise distance between "test the framing" and "the framing is wrong", and the synthesis of mixed evidence into board-grade sentences that carry benefit, cost, and limitation at their earned strengths. And in the situational judgement section: findings defended and framing negotiated, conflicting directors structured into evidence questions, errors self-reported with impact notes, mentoring that builds capability rather than dependence, and escalation that separates the immediate alarm from the measured analysis.

If there is one thread through all of it, it is calibration. The analyst's craft at your level is not knowing more than everyone else — it is claiming exactly as much as the evidence supports, reading exactly what the document says, escalating at exactly the weight the situation warrants, and helping exactly enough that others grow. Every section of this guide has been calibration practice wearing a different costume, and calibration is also precisely what psychometric assessments measure well — which is why preparing for them and deepening your practice turn out to be the same work.

Three suggestions for what comes next. First, return to this guide after a fortnight and retake the questions that stretched you; the difference you notice is your development made measurable. Second, fold the techniques into your live work deliberately — recompute one appraisal row, sweep one procedure for exceptions, debrief one judgement call — until the checks run without conscious effort. Third, and most in keeping with your role: teach this material. Walk a junior analyst through a worked question; run a lunch-and-learn on percentage points or Cannot-say discipline; add the checking habits to your community's peer-review circle. Your level's named duty is developing the analysis community around you, and explaining a technique to someone else is simultaneously the best way to finish learning it and the best gift you can give the profession's next cohort.

If an assessment is imminent: prepare your environment, take the platform's practice questions, trust your methods, and let no single hard question rattle the remaining twenty. You have prepared properly, and preparation — not brilliance — is what these instruments reward.

You work in a profession that government cannot do without: the people who stand between ambiguity and action, who make sure the thing built is the thing needed, and who keep the citizen visible in every decision room. The capabilities you have sharpened here are the quiet infrastructure of that work. Keep practising them, keep teaching them, and be proud of the analyst you have become — and the ones you are helping to build. Good luck.
