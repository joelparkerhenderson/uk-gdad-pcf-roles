## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the reasoning your role performs daily, using leadership-grade material rather than abstract puzzles: portfolio dashboards, governance rules, transformation plans, prediction outputs, and cross-service evidence sets. The mental operations — pattern recognition, deduction, error checking, prioritisation, applied problem solving — are the same ones assessed at every level; what changes at yours is the structure of the material: more interacting variables, longer causal chains, subtler anomalies, and consequences that arrive through other people's subsequent decisions.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 15 to 30 minutes with 15 to 30 questions, adaptively scored against a senior norm group, and reported to employers as a speed-accuracy profile. Two practical notes for senior candidates: adaptive difficulty means a hard-feeling test is usually a well-going test; and timed conditions reward the disciplined habits you already use in governance — read the rule exactly, check the boundary, demand the mechanism — executed briskly rather than a new bag of tricks.

Cognitive assessment earns its place at head-of level because your defining contributions are structural. "See the bigger picture by taking groups of services and investigating how to get the best of underlying services" is portfolio pattern recognition. Producing "a holistic view of important trends across multiple processes" means detecting signals that only exist across datasets — invisible in any single report. Ensuring "the right actions are taken to investigate, resolve and anticipate problems" means auditing other people's causal reasoning, not just performing your own. The questions below compress each of these into assessable form.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Pattern recognition** maps to your **Service focus** skill — the bigger-picture view across groups of services — and your **IT service reporting** skill: cross-process trend detection is pattern recognition across datasets that were never designed to be read together.
- **Logical deduction** maps to your **Service management framework knowledge** and SLA accountability: governance frameworks, contract schedules, and escalation rules are deductive systems, and at your level you both apply and design them.
- **Error checking** maps to your **Ownership** and **Asset and configuration management** skills: strategy papers, prediction models, and configuration baselines cross your desk for assurance, and the flaw you miss becomes organisational.
- **Prioritisation** maps to your accountability for the whole function: allocating attention, budget, and leadership capacity across operations, transformation, people, and governance is the role's permanent question.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to your **Problem management** skill — coordinating investigation across teams, judging when the right actions are being taken — and to anticipating problems before they surface in any metric.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Cross-service pattern detection**

Your portfolio dashboard shows month-on-month change in contact volume for five services: HR system +2%; finance system +1%; case management +18%; email 0%; intranet −1%. The case management service released a major upgrade three weeks ago. Which reading is most defensible?

- A) Demand across the estate is rising and the desk needs more staff.
- B) The portfolio is stable except case management, whose +18% coincides with its upgrade — investigate the upgrade's support impact specifically before any estate-wide response.
- C) The +18% is within normal variation and needs no attention.
- D) The intranet's −1% shows users are abandoning it.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Read the distribution before the aggregate. Four of five services sit within ±2% — ordinary noise — while one shows +18% with a proximate candidate cause (a major upgrade three weeks prior). The pattern is localised, not systemic, so the response should be too: option A converts one service's spike into an estate-wide staffing case, which misprices the problem and buries its cause; option C waves away an eighteen-fold departure from the portfolio's noise band; option D over-reads a 1% movement — noise — into a narrative. B does what portfolio leadership does: isolate the anomaly, attach the candidate mechanism, and scope the investigation to match. Note the discipline that generalises: at portfolio level, the first analytical act is always separating the distribution's signal from its noise, because estate-wide responses to single-service causes are among the most expensive category errors a head of function can make.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction from a governance framework**

Your service governance framework states: "A service enters enhanced monitoring if it misses any SLA for two consecutive months, or misses three SLAs in a single month. A service in enhanced monitoring returns to standard monitoring only after three consecutive fully-compliant months." The licensing service missed one SLA in April, was fully compliant in May, and missed two SLAs in June. What is its status at the start of July?

- A) Enhanced monitoring — it has missed SLAs in two months out of three.
- B) Standard monitoring — neither trigger has fired: no SLA missed in two consecutive months, and no month with three misses.
- C) Enhanced monitoring — two misses in June exceeds the threshold.
- D) Cannot be determined without knowing which SLAs were missed.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Test each trigger precisely. Trigger one requires misses in consecutive months: April missed, May clean, June missed — the May compliance breaks the sequence, so no two consecutive missing months exist. Trigger two requires three misses within one month: the maximum monthly count is two (June). Neither condition fires; the service remains in standard monitoring. Option A substitutes an invented rule ("two months out of three") for the written one — the most common governance misreading, where the rule's gist replaces its letter. Option C misreads the threshold (three, not two). Option D manufactures dependency on information the rules do not use: the framework counts misses, not identities. The leadership relevance is double: you apply such rules, and you design them — and this question quietly demonstrates a design property worth auditing in your own frameworks: under these triggers, a service can miss an SLA every other month indefinitely without ever entering enhanced monitoring. Precise reading is also how design gaps are found.

**Question 3 (easy) — Error checking a strategy paper's logic**

A draft desk strategy paper contains this argument: "Channel shift is succeeding: portal contacts have risen 40% year on year while phone contacts have fallen 25%. Since portal contacts cost £5 against £14 for phone, the desk's total contact costs have therefore fallen." What is the flaw?

- A) The cost figures are implausible.
- B) The conclusion does not follow: percentage changes on unstated baselines cannot establish total cost movement — if phone volumes were small and portal volumes large, total cost could have risen.
- C) Channel shift is not a legitimate strategy.
- D) Year-on-year comparisons are invalid.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The argument moves from percentage changes to an absolute conclusion ("total costs have fallen") without the baselines that alone could support it. Construct the counterexample that proves the gap — and note it cuts the intended way with realistic numbers reversed: suppose portal contacts were 10,000 (rising 40% adds 4,000 × £5 = £20,000) and phone contacts were 3,000 (falling 25% removes 750 × £14 = £10,500): total cost rises £9,500 while both cited percentages hold. Whether the real conclusion is true depends entirely on volumes the paper omits. Options A, C, and D attack claims the argument does not depend on. The assurance habit this rehearses is central to your role: strategy papers reaching your desk routinely argue from rates to totals, and the head of function's question — "what are the baselines?" — is the cheapest quality gate in governance. Percentages persuade; only volumes conclude.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Prioritisation across a head-of-function's week**

Monday, 08:30. Four items on your desk:

1. Your prediction model flags that at current trend, the desk breaches its contractual SLA floor in about seven weeks — the first time the model has flagged this.
2. A director asks you to join a machinery-of-government working group scoping the absorption of another department's 400-person desk function; first meeting Thursday.
3. One of your service desk managers has submitted their resignation this morning; their notice period is four weeks.
4. This month's board pack is due Wednesday; your analyst has drafted it and asks for your review.

Which order of attention is most defensible?

- A) 4, 1, 2, 3 — nearest deadline first
- B) 3, 1, 4, 2 — the resignation first (retention conversation is most time-critical), then the SLA flag's diagnosis, then the board pack, then Thursday's preparation
- C) 1, 2, 3, 4 — the SLA breach risk dominates everything
- D) 2, 3, 1, 4 — the machinery-of-government work is the most strategic item

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Sequence by what decays fastest and what your involvement uniquely changes. The resignation (3) leads: if retention is possible at all, its probability decays by the hour as the decision hardens and colleagues learn of it — and whether or not you retain, the first conversation shapes handover quality, team stability, and the leaver's advocacy; it needs thirty minutes this morning, not a scheduled slot Friday. The SLA flag (1) is next: seven weeks of runway is exactly why it is urgent now — early diagnosis is what converts a prediction into prevention, and a week's delay spends a seventh of the response window; this is your prediction methodology doing its job, provided you answer it. The board pack (4) has a competent draft and a Wednesday deadline — review fits after the two live items. Thursday's working group (2) is genuinely strategic, and preparing for it Wednesday loses nothing. Option C treats a seven-week flag as a same-hour emergency while a four-week door closes; option D confuses the most senior-sounding item with the most time-sensitive; option A confuses deadlines with decay. The head-of-function principle: strategy items are usually schedulable; people items usually are not.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Anomaly in a prediction model's output**

Your demand forecast model, retrained monthly, has predicted next quarter's contact volume at 52,000 — a 30% jump from this quarter's 40,000, where previous forecasts moved 2-4% per quarter. No major service launches, migrations, or organisational changes are planned. What is the most defensible first response?

- A) Accept the forecast and begin recruiting the additional analysts it implies.
- B) Reject the forecast and reuse last quarter's, since 30% is obviously wrong.
- C) Audit the model's inputs and recent training data before acting — a step-change output without a step-change cause most often signals a data or configuration issue, though a real hidden driver must also be checked for.
- D) Average the new forecast with the old one and plan for 46,000.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A forecasting instrument that has historically moved 2-4% per quarter producing a 30% jump, absent any known driver, presents two hypotheses: the world changed invisibly, or the instrument broke. Both are checkable, and checking precedes acting: input audit (did a data feed duplicate? did a category redefinition inflate counts? did the training window ingest an anomalous month?) and driver search (is something real — a degrading service, a policy change elsewhere — generating genuine early signal?). Option A spends recruitment money on an unvalidated number; option B discards what might be the model's first genuinely valuable warning — instruments are retired for being wrong, not for being surprising; option D is the worst of both: averaging a possibly-broken number with a possibly-stale one produces a figure with no defensible meaning, planning theatre rather than planning. The role connection is literal: your reporting skill is prediction methodologies, and owning a model means owning its failure modes — the leadership discipline is neither credulity nor dismissal toward surprising outputs, but audit. Models earn trust the way analysts do: by being checked.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Deduction across a supplier boundary**

Your second-line contract states: (i) incidents the desk escalates with complete diagnostic packages carry a 4-hour supplier response target; (ii) incidents escalated without complete packages carry an 8-hour target; (iii) the completeness standard is defined in Schedule 3, and disputes over completeness are resolved monthly by joint audit. This month the supplier reclassified 60% of escalations as "incomplete" — historically the figure is 15% — and average response times have lengthened accordingly. Your escalation process has not changed. Which conclusion is soundest?

- A) The supplier is in breach of contract.
- B) Your desk's diagnostic quality has collapsed.
- C) Neither breach nor desk failure is yet established: the reclassification spike, against an unchanged process, makes the completeness judgements themselves the question — which the contract's own joint audit mechanism exists to settle; trigger it with the data.
- D) The response targets should be renegotiated.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Map what is established against what is claimed. Established: reclassification quadrupled; your process is unchanged; response times lengthened in consequence. Not established: whether the reclassified escalations actually fail Schedule 3 (the supplier could be applying the standard newly strictly — legitimately or tactically — or your packages could have degraded through some change you have not detected, such as a tooling update altering attachments). Option A asserts breach before the contractually-defined arbiter (joint audit) has examined a single case; option B accepts the supplier's classification as fact — the precise question at issue; option D proposes renegotiating targets when the dispute concerns classification, a category error that would concede the mechanism. Option C reasons from the contract's own architecture: a quadrupled rejection rate against a stable input is exactly the anomaly the monthly audit exists to adjudicate, and arriving with the data (historical rates, sample packages, timestamps) converts suspicion into process. The senior habit under test: when an interface metric moves sharply, interrogate the measurement boundary before either accusing the counterparty or indicting your own people — most interface disputes are classification disputes, and contracts that anticipate this contain their own resolution machinery. Use it.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Error checking a benefits chain**

A transformation business case argues: "Consolidating our three regional service desks into one virtual desk will standardise processes; standardised processes will raise first-contact resolution; higher FCR will reduce repeat contacts; fewer repeat contacts will cut total demand 15%, funding the programme from savings." Which link deserves the most scrutiny?

- A) Consolidation → standardised processes
- B) Standardisation → higher FCR
- C) Higher FCR → fewer repeat contacts
- D) The quantified leap: fewer repeat contacts → 15% total demand reduction, which requires repeat contacts to be a large, known share of demand and the FCR gain to be a specific, evidenced size — neither is stated.

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Audit each link for its evidential burden. Consolidation enabling standardisation (A) is organisationally plausible and largely within the programme's control. Standardisation lifting FCR (B) is defensible if the three desks' current FCR varies — convergence toward the best performer is a real mechanism, though worth a number. Higher FCR reducing repeats (C) is near-definitional: resolved first contacts do not generate follow-ups. The chain's weight-bearing failure point is D, where qualitative plausibility suddenly becomes a funding-grade quantity: a 15% total demand reduction requires knowing what share of current demand is repeat contact (if repeats are 20% of demand, eliminating half of them yields 10%, not 15%) and what FCR uplift standardisation will actually deliver — the case states neither. This is the benefits-chain pattern your assurance role meets constantly: chains whose every qualitative link holds while the quantification is conjured at the end, because the final number was chosen to fund the programme rather than derived from the mechanism. The head-of-function's question is always the same: "show me the arithmetic from the mechanism to the number" — and self-funding cases deserve it twice.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Pattern across processes**

Three monthly reports cross your desk. Incident management: volumes flat, but median resolution time up 12% over two quarters. Problem management: open problem count up from 14 to 23 over the same period; average age rising. Change management: change volume up 40% following the department's new digital programme; emergency changes doubled. Read together, what is the most coherent holistic interpretation?

- A) Three unrelated trends, each for its process owner to manage.
- B) The digital programme's accelerated change load is plausibly the common driver: more (and more rushed) changes generate harder incidents — slower to resolve — and spawn underlying problems faster than investigation capacity absorbs them; the coherent response addresses change quality and problem capacity together, at programme level.
- C) The incident team has a performance problem and needs management attention.
- D) Problem management should close records faster to reduce the count.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This is your reporting skill's defining sentence — "a holistic view of important trends across multiple processes" — rendered as a question. Separately, each report is ambiguous: slower resolutions might be a skills issue (option C), a rising problem count might be closure discipline (option D). Together they cohere around one mechanism with the right timing signature: a 40% change surge with doubled emergency changes is exactly the upstream event that would produce harder, less familiar incidents (resolution drift), and a bloom of underlying defects (problem accumulation) — while flat incident volume rules out simple demand growth as the driver. Option A is the failure mode holistic reporting exists to prevent: three process owners each locally optimising (coaching the incident team, bulk-closing problems) while the shared cause — change quality under programme pressure — continues unaddressed at its source. B also locates the response correctly: at programme governance level, where change-quality standards and problem-investigation capacity can actually be traded against delivery pace. The tested capability: cross-dataset causal assembly, plus the leadership instinct that when three processes degrade in concert, the cause usually lives upstream of all of them.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Multi-constraint transformation sequencing**

You must sequence four initiatives over four quarters, one per quarter: (T) telephony platform replacement; (K) knowledge base consolidation; (A) password-reset automation; (M) absorbing a merged department's desk (mandated to complete by end of Q3). Constraints: (i) A requires the consolidated knowledge base, so K must precede A; (ii) the merged staff must bed in on stable telephony, so at least two full quarters must separate M from T; (iii) the merged department joins on your existing platform, so M must precede T. Which sequence is valid?

- A) K, M, A, T
- B) M, K, A, T
- C) K, A, M, T
- D) M, T, K, A

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Encode the constraints — K before A; M before T; at least two full quarters between M and T; M by end of Q3 — then test each candidate methodically. Option A (K, M, A, T): K precedes A ✓; M precedes T ✓; M deadline ✓ (Q2); but M in Q2 and T in Q4 are separated by only one full quarter (Q3) — constraint (ii) fails. Option C (K, A, M, T): M in Q3 meets the deadline, but M and T are adjacent — fails (ii). Option D (M, T, K, A): M and T adjacent — fails (ii) immediately, whatever else holds. Option B (M, K, A, T): M precedes T (Q1 before Q4) ✓; two full quarters (Q2, Q3) separate them ✓; K precedes A (Q2 before Q3) ✓; M completes in Q1, two quarters inside its mandated deadline ✓ — the only valid sequence. Notice what made the search efficient: the separation constraint is the most restrictive, and applying it first eliminated three options in seconds — the same anchor-on-the-tightest-constraint method that governs real transformation road-mapping. Notice also B's incidental virtue: the one externally-mandated item lands earliest, holding maximum buffer against the deadline whose overrun cost you do not control. In live portfolio planning, where several sequences often pass the letter of the constraints, that buffer logic — mandated items earliest, slack where the penalties are external — is the tie-breaker to reach for.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Auditing a problem investigation**

A recurring outage has taken a service down three times in six weeks. Your problem manager reports: "Root cause found: each outage followed a memory spike on the application server. We've scheduled weekly server restarts, and there have been no outages in the two weeks since. Closing the problem record." As the accountable head, what is the soundest assessment?

- A) Endorse closure — the fix demonstrably works.
- B) The investigation has found a precursor and applied a suppressant, not found a root cause: what causes the memory spikes remains unknown, weekly restarts mask rather than remove it, and two quiet weeks against a three-in-six-weeks base rate is weak evidence; keep the record open with the restart as an interim workaround, and direct investigation at the spike's cause.
- C) Reject the work and reassign the investigation to the supplier.
- D) Close the record but add a monitoring alert for memory spikes.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Audit the causal claim's depth. "Outages follow memory spikes" identifies a mechanism link one step back — valuable — but "root cause" means the point where intervention removes the phenomenon, and the spikes' own cause (a memory leak in which component? triggered by what workload? introduced by which release?) is unexamined; scheduled restarts are the definitional workaround, treating the symptom on a timer. The evidence claim is also weak: at three occurrences in six weeks, two quiet weeks are roughly what chance provides — the base rate makes the observation nearly uninformative. Option A rewards the most seductive pattern in operational management: a suppressed symptom presented as a solved problem, which then recurs at the workaround's first failure (a restart missed during leave, a spike arriving mid-week) — typically at the worst moment and with the record closed and context dispersed. Option D institutionalises the confusion: monitoring a symptom while closing the search for its cause. Option C punishes genuine progress — the precursor finding is real work, and reassignment discards it along with the team's motivation to investigate thoroughly next time. B does what "ensure that the right actions are taken to investigate, resolve and anticipate problems" — your role summary's exact words — means in practice: distinguishing workaround from resolution in other people's reports, keeping records honest about which one exists, and directing continued investigation without disparaging the interim relief. The question every head of function should ask of a claimed root cause: "if we stopped the workaround, would the problem return?" If yes, the cause is still out there.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Strategic inference from long-run channel data**

Five years of your desk's contact data: phone share has fallen from 70% to 45%; portal share risen from 10% to 35%; chat from 0% to 12%; email stable around 8%. Meanwhile phone contacts' average complexity rating has doubled, and satisfaction by channel shows phone 4.4, portal 3.7, chat 4.1. A board member proposes: "The trend is clear — accelerate phone's retirement and target a portal-first desk within two years." What is the strongest assessment of this proposal?

- A) Agree — five years of consistent migration is decisive evidence of user preference.
- B) The trend is real but the inference is flawed: the migration has been selective, moving simple contacts out of phone and leaving it the channel of complex need — phone's doubled complexity and highest satisfaction suggest it now serves precisely the contacts the portal (satisfaction 3.7) handles worst; retiring phone would force the hardest contacts onto the weakest channel. The strategy should be channel-by-need: grow portal for the simple, protect assisted channels for the complex, and fix portal experience before any further shift.
- C) Disagree — channel strategy should not change while any users prefer phone.
- D) Agree, but extend the timeline to three years.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The data contains a composition story the headline trend conceals. Migration out of phone has not been random: self-serviceable contacts left, complex ones stayed — evidenced by phone's doubling complexity rating — so "phone share is falling" does not mean "phone is becoming unnecessary"; it means phone is specialising into the channel of last and hardest resort, where it earns the estate's highest satisfaction while the portal, at 3.7, underperforms on the simpler work it already has. Projecting the share trend to zero (option A) commits the classic extrapolation error of treating a selection process as a preference process; the trend's continuation to phone-retirement would strand exactly the contacts — and users — least able to self-serve. Option C errs oppositely, freezing strategy on any objection. Option D negotiates the timeline of a flawed inference. B exercises the specific capabilities your role summary names: "demonstrate experience in meeting the needs of users across a variety of channels", "bring insight and expertise in how user needs have changed over time", and "apply strategic thinking to provide the best service for the end user" — the insight being that channel mix is an allocation of contact types to channel strengths, not a race with a single winner. The board-ready reframe: the goal is not portal-first but right-channel-first, and the current data says the portal must earn its next tranche of traffic through experience improvement, not policy compulsion.

### Preparation tips

- Practise decomposition as a first move: for any aggregate trend (shares, averages, totals), ask what selection or mix effect could produce it without the naive story being true. Most senior-level traps are composition effects wearing trend costumes.
- Rehearse boundary-exact rule reading with your own governance documents: triggers, thresholds, and consecutive-month clauses. Then audit them as a designer — what behaviour do they permit that they should catch?
- Demand mechanisms and baselines from every argument you practise on: rates need volumes before they conclude anything; benefit chains need arithmetic at the quantified link; root causes need to survive the "stop the workaround" question.
- For sequencing and allocation questions, encode constraints formally (who before whom, what excludes what), test candidates methodically, and when several pass, rank by slack against the hardest external deadline.
- Treat model outputs in practice as you should in post: audit surprising ones before acting, and distrust your own relief when a forecast flatters.
- Before the assessment itself: rest, use the ungraded warm-ups, and let adaptive difficulty read as progress. Senior candidates lose more marks to rushed reading than to reasoning limits — pace like the chair of a meeting, not its scribe.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Responding at the wrong scope.** Estate-wide answers to single-service anomalies, and single-process answers to cross-process patterns, are the two characteristic scope errors of the level.
- **Substituting a rule's gist for its letter.** Governance triggers are written in exact conditions; "roughly two bad months" is not a condition. Read what fires, not what feels fair.
- **Accepting rates without volumes.** Percentage arguments that conclude in absolute terms are unfinished; the baselines are where the truth lives.
- **Confusing suppression with resolution.** Scheduled restarts, smoothing averages, and closed records with monitoring alerts are all workarounds in resolution's clothing. Ask what returns if the workaround stops.
- **Extrapolating selection processes as preferences.** When the easy cases leave a channel (or a queue, or a team), what remains is harder — falling share plus rising complexity is specialisation, not obsolescence.
- **Trusting surprising model outputs — or dismissing them.** Both credulity and reflex rejection skip the only correct step: audit the inputs, then search for the real driver.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment at head-of-function level measures fluency with the quantitative material on which service leadership decisions rest: multi-year budgets, workforce models, prediction outputs and their confidence limits, portfolio SLA aggregates, benefits cases, and cross-channel cost economics. The underlying mathematics remains percentages, ratios, weighted averages, rates, and projections — but deployed on leadership-shaped problems: several steps, several sources, assumptions that must be surfaced, and answers whose interpretation matters as much as their calculation.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 20 to 30 minutes with 15 to 20 questions built on tables, charts, and short data narratives; an on-screen calculator is standard. Scoring profiles accuracy and speed against a senior norm group, often adaptively.

The role relevance is explicit in your skill set. Your reporting skill is defined as "use prediction methodologies to produce and maintain a holistic view of important trends across multiple processes" — forecasting, trend synthesis, and their honest limits are named competencies, not incidental ones. You manage and monitor SLAs and performance across a portfolio; you commission and assure business cases (automation, consolidation, channel shift); you build workforce and budget models that commit public money; and you are the last numerate checkpoint before figures reach boards, directors, and ministers' offices. The questions below rehearse both directions of that duty: producing decision-grade numbers, and catching decision-corrupting ones.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Forecasting and trend arithmetic** map directly to your **IT service reporting** skill: prediction methodologies begin with measured growth rates, seasonality awareness, and disciplined extrapolation — and end with knowing when extrapolation is no longer honest.
- **Budget and workforce modelling** map to your accountability for the function's resourcing: converting demand into FTE, FTE into cost, and attrition into recruitment pipelines is the recurring arithmetic of headship.
- **Portfolio aggregation** maps to your **Service focus** skill: the bigger picture across services is numerically a weighted-combination discipline, and mix effects are where portfolio truth hides.
- **Benefits and cost-model assurance** map to your **Customer service management** duty to investigate automation and your **Ownership** skill: cases that fund themselves on paper are yours to verify before they spend real money.
- **SLA and performance arithmetic** map to your duty to "manage and monitor service desk service level agreements and performance": floors, buffers, penalty thresholds, and recovery trajectories are quantitative objects, and you own their exact behaviour.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Portfolio SLA aggregation**

Across your three supported channels last month: phone — 5,000 contacts, 4,650 within SLA; portal — 3,000 contacts, 2,850 within SLA; chat — 2,000 contacts, 1,820 within SLA. What was the portfolio within-SLA rate?

- A) 92.4%
- B) 93.0%
- C) 93.2%
- D) 94.0%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Aggregate from counts. Met = 4,650 + 2,850 + 1,820 = 9,320. Total = 5,000 + 3,000 + 2,000 = 10,000. Rate = 9,320 ÷ 10,000 = 93.2%. The channel rates individually are 93.0%, 95.0%, and 91.0%; their unweighted average — 93.0%, option B — is the standing trap, and here it sits one temptingly tidy step from the truth. At portfolio level this discipline is not pedantry: your board figure is the weighted one, and every downstream decision (penalty exposure, channel investment) inherits its accuracy. The head-of-function habit: whenever a "combined" rate reaches you, ask whether it was assembled from counts — and be able to reassemble it yourself in under a minute, as here.

**Question 2 (easy) — Budget share and variance**

Your desk's annual budget is £3.6 million: staff £2.7 million, technology £600,000, and the remainder for training and other costs. Mid-year, technology spend is £340,000 against a straight-line expectation. What share of budget is training-and-other, and is technology over or under its mid-year expectation?

- A) 8.3% share; technology £40,000 over
- B) 8.3% share; technology £40,000 under
- C) 10% share; technology £40,000 over
- D) 12.5% share; technology on track

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Step 1: training-and-other = £3.6m − £2.7m − £0.6m = £300,000; share = 300,000 ÷ 3,600,000 = 8.33%. Step 2: straight-line mid-year expectation for technology = £600,000 ÷ 2 = £300,000; actual £340,000 is £40,000 over. Both parts must be right, and the option set punishes solving only one. The managerial layer: a £40,000 (13%) mid-year overspend on a £600,000 line is a signal worth a question — timing (annual licences front-loaded?) or trend (genuine overrun)? — and the difference decides whether you act. Straight-line expectations are crude instruments; heads of function use them as tripwires for inquiry, not verdicts, but they only trip if someone runs the division. This is the arithmetic floor beneath financial stewardship of public money: shares, phasing, variance — quick, exact, and habitual.

**Question 3 (easy) — Attrition and recruitment pipeline**

Your function has 60 analysts. Historical attrition is 15% per year, and your training pipeline takes a quarter to bring a recruit to productive standard. Approximately how many analysts will leave over the next 12 months, and how many should be in the pipeline at any time to hold steady-state headcount?

- A) 9 leavers; about 2-3 in the pipeline at any time
- B) 9 leavers; 9 in the pipeline at any time
- C) 6 leavers; about 2-3 in the pipeline at any time
- D) 15 leavers; 4 in the pipeline at any time

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Step 1: expected leavers = 60 × 0.15 = 9 per year. Step 2: the pipeline question is a flow calculation, not a stock one — you lose 9 per year, i.e. roughly 2.25 per quarter, and each recruit spends one quarter in training; steady state therefore requires about 2-3 recruits in the pipeline at any moment (one quarter's worth of leavers), not the whole year's 9 (option B — which would overstaff within months). Option D misapplies the 15% to nothing in particular. The distinction being tested — annual flow versus in-flight stock — is the core of workforce planning arithmetic, and getting it wrong in either direction is expensive: pipelines sized to annual attrition overshoot budget; pipelines sized to zero mean every resignation becomes a service gap for a quarter. The refinement your real model would add: attrition is lumpy and correlated (post-bonus, post-restructure), so steady-state 2-3 is a floor with surge provisions, not a fixed rule.

**Question 4 (easy) — Cost per contact across channels**

Channel economics this year: phone — 60,000 contacts at £14.00 each; portal — 50,000 contacts at £4.00 each; chat — 25,000 contacts at £7.00 each. What is the blended cost per contact across the desk?

- A) £8.33
- B) £8.68
- C) £9.00
- D) £9.26

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Compute total spend, then divide by total volume. Phone: 60,000 × £14.00 = £840,000. Portal: 50,000 × £4.00 = £200,000. Chat: 25,000 × £7.00 = £175,000. Total cost = £1,215,000; total contacts = 135,000; blended cost = 1,215,000 ÷ 135,000 = £9.00. Option A (£8.33) is the unweighted average of the three per-contact figures ((14 + 4 + 7) ÷ 3) — the standing trap, which underweights phone despite phone carrying both the largest volume and the highest unit cost, and therefore understates the desk's true cost base by 67 pence per contact, roughly £90,000 a year at this volume. Options B and D are plausible-looking near-misses for candidates who mis-key one multiplication. The blended figure matters at your level because it is the desk's benchmark currency — the number compared across departments and quoted in shared-services conversations — and because its movement over time is dominated by channel mix, a dynamic Question 10 examines directly. Produce it from totals, always, and know which channel's weight is driving it before anyone else in the room asks.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Forecast from a measured growth rate**

Portal contacts have grown steadily: 30,000 two years ago, 36,000 last year, 43,200 this year. If the growth rate continues, what is the two-year-ahead forecast?

- A) 51,840
- B) 57,600
- C) 62,208
- D) 86,400

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Measure the rate first: 36,000 ÷ 30,000 = 1.20; 43,200 ÷ 36,000 = 1.20 — consistent 20% annual growth, which justifies (for now) compound extrapolation. One year ahead: 43,200 × 1.20 = 51,840. Two years ahead: 51,840 × 1.20 = 62,208. Option A stops at year one; option B applies simple (linear) growth of 7,200 per year twice (43,200 + 14,400), understating compounding; option D doubles for drama. Two leadership notes attach. First, verify the rate's stability before compounding — here both observed years say 20%, which is what licenses the method; had the years said 20% then 9%, compounding the average would manufacture false confidence. Second, carry the assumption visibly: "62,208, assuming the observed 20% growth persists" is a forecast; "62,208" alone is a hostage to fortune. Your prediction-methodology skill is precisely this pairing of computation with stated conditions — boards remember the number, so the number must travel with its caveat.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Confidence in a prediction range**

Your demand model forecasts next quarter at 48,000 contacts, with an 80% prediction interval of 44,000 to 52,000. Capacity planning is being finalised. Which use of this output is soundest?

- A) Staff for exactly 48,000 — the central estimate is the model's best guess.
- B) Staff for 52,000 — always plan for the worst case.
- C) Staff core capacity around the central estimate with flexible surge provision (overtime agreements, cross-trained staff, supplier overflow) sized toward the upper bound, explicitly accepting a roughly 10% chance that demand exceeds even that — and document the residual risk.
- D) Ask the model team to narrow the interval before planning.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Read the interval as information, not decoration. An 80% interval of 44,000-52,000 means the model assigns roughly 10% probability to demand above 52,000 and 10% to below 44,000. Option A staffs for the median and converts every above-median month — about half of them — into queues; central estimates are forecasts, not plans. Option B buys certainty at fixed-cost prices: permanently staffing the upper bound wastes budget in the roughly 90% of quarters demand falls lower, and "always plan for the worst case" is not a policy any CFO will fund twice. Option C is what interval-literate planning looks like: fixed capacity where demand is likely, flexible capacity where it is possible, and honest documentation of the tail that remains — because 10% quarters happen, and the difference between a managed exceedance and a scandal is whether the risk was accepted knowingly. Option D misunderstands intervals: their width reflects genuine uncertainty, and a narrower interval on demand cannot be produced by request — only by better data or a quieter world; asking is planning-avoidance dressed as rigour. Your skill definition — prediction methodologies producing a holistic view — includes this literacy: the interval is the view, and using only the centre discards most of what the method produced.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Consolidation benefits arithmetic**

A consolidation case projects merging three regional desks (costing £1.4m, £1.1m, and £0.9m annually) into one virtual desk costing £2.8m annually, after one-off transition costs of £750,000. What is the annual saving, the simple payback period, and the three-year net benefit?

- A) £600,000 saving; 15-month payback; £1,050,000 three-year net benefit
- B) £600,000 saving; 12.5-month payback; £1,150,000 three-year net benefit
- C) £400,000 saving; 22.5-month payback; £450,000 three-year net benefit
- D) £600,000 saving; 15-month payback; £850,000 three-year net benefit

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Step 1: current combined cost = 1.4 + 1.1 + 0.9 = £3.4m; future cost £2.8m; annual saving = £600,000. Step 2: simple payback = one-off cost ÷ annual saving = 750,000 ÷ 600,000 = 1.25 years = 15 months. Step 3: three-year net benefit = (3 × 600,000) − 750,000 = 1,800,000 − 750,000 = £1,050,000. All three figures must reconcile — option D computes the net benefit as if transition cost £950,000; option B shaves the payback with optimistic rounding. The assurance layer is where headship lives: this arithmetic is the case at its most flattering — full savings from day one, transition on budget, no attrition spike or performance dip during merger. Your review should stress each: savings ramping over year one (say, half-year effect) pushes real payback toward 20+ months and trims the three-year net benefit materially. Approving the case may still be right; approving it while believing the 15-month figure is how programmes get remembered as disappointments. Verify the base arithmetic in a minute, then spend your scrutiny on the assumptions it flatters.

**Question 8 (moderate) — SLA penalty exposure**

Your contract with the department includes a service credit regime: in any month where the within-SLA rate falls below the 92% floor, the desk credits back 2% of the monthly charge (£250,000) — that is, £5,000 — per full percentage point below the floor. Your last six months: 94.1%, 93.2%, 92.5%, 91.0%, 90.4%, 89.8%. What credits were incurred over the six months, and if the recent decline continues at its current rate, roughly what exposure does the next quarter carry?

- A) £20,000 incurred; roughly £45,000 next quarter
- B) £20,000 incurred; about £20,000 next quarter
- C) £10,000 incurred; roughly £45,000 next quarter
- D) £30,000 incurred; roughly £60,000 next quarter

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Apply the regime month by month, minding the "full percentage point" convention. The first three months (94.1, 93.2, 92.5) sit above the floor: £0. Month four: 91.0% is 1.0 point below — one full point — £5,000. Month five: 90.4% is 1.6 below — one full point (the 0.6 part-point does not count) — £5,000. Month six: 89.8% is 2.2 below — two full points — £10,000. Total incurred = £20,000. Now the forward view: the last three months are falling 0.6 points per month; continuing, the next quarter runs roughly 89.2% (2.8 below → 2 full points → £10,000), 88.6% (3.4 below → 3 points → £15,000), and 88.0% (4.0 below → 4 points → £20,000) — about £45,000, more than double the entire past half-year's exposure in a single quarter, because the regime's cost accelerates as performance sinks deeper below the floor. Option A alone pairs both figures correctly; option B repeats the past as the future, which is exactly what a deteriorating trend forbids. Two head-of-function lessons attach. First, credit regimes are non-linear in effect: each further point of decline costs more than the last, so recovery investment has a rising payoff — the £45,000 projection is the self-funding case for intervention now. Second, the convention matters materially: had this contract pro-rated part-points, month five alone would differ — so your first act on any credit clause is confirming the rounding convention in writing before the first disputed invoice, not after.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Automation at portfolio scale**

You are prioritising three automation candidates with one year's funding for one: (a) password resets — 21,000 contacts/year, £9 each, automation diverts 75%, platform cost £60,000/year; (b) software requests — 9,000 contacts/year, £16 each, diverts 60%, cost £45,000/year; (c) starter/leaver processing — 4,500 contacts/year, £30 each, diverts 80%, cost £70,000/year. Automated handling costs £1.50 per diverted contact in all cases. Which ranks first on annual net saving?

- A) Password resets — highest volume always wins
- B) Software requests
- C) Starter/leaver processing
- D) Password resets and starter/leaver tie

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Compute each case identically: net = diverted × (unit cost − £1.50) − platform. (a) Diverted = 21,000 × 0.75 = 15,750; saving per diversion = 9 − 1.5 = £7.50; gross = 15,750 × 7.5 = £118,125; net = 118,125 − 60,000 = £58,125. (b) Diverted = 9,000 × 0.60 = 5,400; per diversion = 16 − 1.5 = £14.50; gross = 5,400 × 14.5 = £78,300; net = 78,300 − 45,000 = £33,300. (c) Diverted = 4,500 × 0.80 = 3,600; per diversion = 30 − 1.5 = £28.50; gross = 3,600 × 28.5 = £102,600; net = 102,600 − 70,000 = £32,600. Ranking: (a) £58,125, then (b) £33,300, then (c) £32,600 — password resets first, though not for option A's stated reason: volume does not "always win" (note (c) nearly matches (b) on a fifth of the volume — unit economics fight volume to a near-draw); it wins here because the arithmetic says so, and only the arithmetic can. Two portfolio-level observations complete the analysis a head would present: (b) and (c) are close enough (£700 apart) that estimate error swamps the ranking — next year's funding decision between them should be made on strategic grounds (user experience, error rates, fraud risk in starter/leaver) rather than a spurious £700; and the diversion percentages are the cases' softest inputs, so sensitivity-test them: at 60% diversion instead of 75%, option (a)'s net falls to £34,500 and the ranking compresses. Deciding on computed nets, then interrogating the inputs the ranking is sensitive to, is exactly the "investigate which processes can be automated" duty performed at your altitude.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Mix effect in a headline improvement**

Your board pack shows average cost per contact falling from £9.20 to £8.40 over a year — presented as an efficiency gain. Underlying data: phone cost rose from £13.00 to £14.00 per contact, and portal cost rose from £4.00 to £4.20; but portal's share of volume grew from 40% to 55%. Which statement should accompany the headline?

- A) No caveat needed — the blended figure fell, which is what matters financially.
- B) The blended fall is entirely channel mix: both channels became more expensive per contact, and the headline improvement reflects users migrating to the cheaper channel — real and valuable, but a different claim from operational efficiency, and phone's 7.7% unit-cost rise needs its own explanation.
- C) The figures are contradictory and the data team should re-extract them.
- D) The phone cost rise should be excluded as an outlier.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Verify the blend both years. Year one: 0.40 × 4.00 + 0.60 × 13.00 = 1.60 + 7.80 = £9.40 (close to the stated £9.20; treat the difference as chat/email simplification). Year two: 0.55 × 4.20 + 0.45 × 14.00 = 2.31 + 6.30 = £8.61 (≈ £8.40 stated). The direction and mechanism are unambiguous: the blended average fell while both unit costs rose — arithmetically possible only because the mix shifted toward the cheap channel. The distinction option B draws is the one boards need: channel migration is a genuine financial gain (the desk really does spend less per contact), but presenting it as "efficiency" implies the operation got leaner, when in fact both channels got unit-costlier — phone's £1.00 rise may be benign specialisation (simple calls leaving, hard calls remaining, per the selection dynamics of channel shift) or genuine cost drift, and only decomposition will say. Option A is how leadership teams get surprised later: the mix effect saturates (portal share cannot grow forever), at which point the "efficiency trend" reverses without anything operationally changing. Option C mistakes an explainable composition effect for a data error; option D would delete the most informative number in the pack. This is your holistic-reporting skill at its most concrete: headline metrics inherit the mix, and the accompanying sentence — "both components rose; the blend fell because of migration" — is the difference between a board that understands its desk and a board that merely feels good about it.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Capacity for an absorbed function**

A machinery-of-government change transfers a 6,000-user department to your desk in Q3. Their previous desk handled 1.4 contacts per user per month with 20 analysts. Your desk runs at 0.9 contacts per user per month, and your analysts each handle 700 contacts per month. Transition experience elsewhere suggests absorbed populations run 25% above their historical contact rate for the first two quarters. How many additional analysts do you need for the first two quarters, and at steady state if the population converges to your 0.9 rate?

- A) 15 for transition; 8 at steady state
- B) 15 for transition; 12 at steady state
- C) 12 for transition; 8 at steady state
- D) 20 for transition; 20 at steady state — match their previous staffing

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Transition demand: historical rate 1.4 × 1.25 uplift = 1.75 contacts per user per month; volume = 6,000 × 1.75 = 10,500 contacts per month; analysts = 10,500 ÷ 700 = 15. Steady state on convergence: 6,000 × 0.9 = 5,400 contacts per month; analysts = 5,400 ÷ 700 = 7.7 → 8 (round people up). Option A carries both. Option D — "match their previous 20" — imports the transferring desk's staffing without importing its arithmetic: their 20 analysts implies roughly 8,400 ÷ 20 = 420 contacts per analyst per month, a productivity 40% below yours, and replicating their ratio would embed that gap permanently. Option C understates transition (using 1.4 without the uplift); option B overstates steady state (using 1.4 as the converged rate). Two strategic notes make this a head-of-function answer rather than a spreadsheet's. The convergence assumption (1.4 → 0.9) is doing heavy work: it presumes the absorbed population's higher contact rate reflects their old desk's service model rather than their user base's nature — worth validating early, because if their users genuinely need 1.4, your steady state is 12, not 8, and the budget case changes by half a million pounds. And the transition surplus (15 falling to 8) is a workforce-planning gift if handled deliberately — fixed-term contracts, secondments, or scheduled attrition absorption — and a redundancy problem if handled by default. Absorbing functions is your role's literal remit ("managing a team who provides first- and second-line technical support" at whatever scale the machinery of government delivers); the arithmetic is the easy half, and stating its assumptions is the leadership half.

### Preparation tips

- Drill the weighted-versus-unweighted distinction until violations jump off the page — in your own board packs first. Portfolio rates, blended costs, and combined satisfaction are all weighted objects, and every unweighted "average of averages" in a paper you sign is yours.
- Practise forecast hygiene as a unit: measure the rate, verify its stability, compound correctly, and attach the assumption to the number in the same sentence. Separately, rehearse reading prediction intervals as staffing decisions — centre for core, upper bound for surge, documented residual beyond.
- For any benefits or savings case, verify the internal arithmetic in one pass, then spend your time on the flattering assumptions: day-one savings, full diversion rates, on-budget transitions. The arithmetic is rarely wrong; the assumptions usually are.
- Fix boundary and rounding conventions before computing exposure: "below 92%", "per full point", "pro-rated". Contract arithmetic turns on drafting, and so do the corresponding test questions.
- When comparing options, compute identically-structured nets for each, then sensitivity-test the softest input before trusting a close ranking.
- Keep unit discipline under time pressure: per user per month, per quarter, per analyst. Most senior-level errors are unit slips executed confidently.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Averaging averages at portfolio scale.** The error survives because it flatters someone; assemble every combined figure from counts.
- **Planning on central estimates.** A forecast's centre is where demand is likeliest, not where planning is safest — use the interval the methodology gave you.
- **Reading mix effects as efficiency.** A falling blend with rising components is migration, not leanness; say so before the mix saturates and the "trend" reverses.
- **Importing ratios with transferred functions.** Absorbed desks bring their staffing history; your model should price their demand at your productivity, with convergence assumptions stated and tested.
- **Sizing pipelines to annual flows.** In-flight stock equals one training-period's worth of leavers, not a year's — the flow/stock confusion misstaffs in both directions.
- **Letting close rankings decide.** When two options' computed nets sit within estimate error, the arithmetic has spoken — it said "strategic judgement required", not "option (b) by £700".

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment measures precision in reading, inference, and expression across the written material a head of service desk commands: strategy and policy papers, governance submissions, contract schedules, audit findings, research syntheses, and correspondence with directors, suppliers, and programme leadership. The core format presents a passage followed by statements to judge **True** (supported), **False** (contradicted), or **Cannot say** (undetermined by the passage); complementary formats test best-summary judgement, inference discipline, and the selection of wording that communicates exactly for a given audience.

The typical administration is an online, timed test of 15 to 25 minutes with 20 to 30 statements. The governing rule — answer from the passage alone — is at its most demanding for the most experienced candidates, because your accumulated knowledge of how contracts, audits, and strategies usually read is precisely what must not leak into judgements about how this one reads.

At head-of-function level the stakes of verbal precision are institutional. Your reading errors scale: a misconstrued audit finding shapes a remediation programme; an over-read research synthesis redirects channel investment; a misjudged clause concedes a commercial position. Your writing errors scale further, because your words are quoted — in board minutes, in supplier disputes, in the framing your managers repeat to their teams. And a distinctive senior burden appears: much of what you read is written to persuade you — business cases, supplier accounts, programme assurances — so the assessment's discipline of separating what a text establishes from what it merely suggests is, at your level, a daily defensive skill. Employers test it because the written channel is where organisational reality is negotiated.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Exact comprehension of governance and contract text** maps to your **Service management framework knowledge** and your SLA accountability: frameworks, schedules, and credit regimes are precision instruments, and you are their most senior reader.
- **True/False/Cannot-say discipline** maps to your **Ownership** and **IT service reporting** skills: accountable reporting rests on distinguishing what evidence establishes, contradicts, and leaves open — under pressure to blur all three.
- **Inference restraint with research and trend narratives** maps to your **User focus** skill: bringing "insight and expertise in how user needs have changed over time" requires handling longitudinal evidence at its supported strength, resisting the story that outruns the data.
- **Faithful synthesis for boards** maps to your holistic **IT service reporting** duty: compressing multiple sources into decision-grade summary without distortion is the writing half of the prediction-and-trends skill.
- **Audience-exact expression** maps to your leadership of managers and your advisory duty to project teams: instructions, standards, and advice are executed as written by people who cannot see your intent — only your words.

### Practice questions

**Passage A (for Questions 1-3):** From an internal audit report: "The service desk's asset register was sampled against physical devices at three sites. Of 150 sampled records, 132 matched fully; 11 showed incorrect locations; 7 could not be matched to a physical device. The desk's configuration management procedure is comprehensive and current, and staff interviewed demonstrated good awareness of it. However, the procedure's reconciliation step is specified as quarterly, and evidence showed the last three reconciliations were completed between five and seven weeks late. The unmatched records are concentrated at the site that absorbed a departmental merger last year. We assess the control design as adequate and the control operation as requiring improvement. We recommend the desk implement automated reconciliation alerts and complete a full register validation within three months."

**Question 1 (easy) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "The audit found the desk's configuration management procedure itself to be deficient."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage draws precisely the distinction the statement blurs: the procedure is "comprehensive and current", staff awareness is good, and the formal assessment is "control design adequate" — the deficiency lies in operation (late reconciliations, unmatched records), not in the procedure's design. The statement attributes the failure to the procedure itself, which the passage explicitly contradicts: False. The design/operation distinction is audit's most load-bearing vocabulary and the most commonly collapsed in onward reporting — a head who relays this as "audit found our procedure deficient" commissions a procedure rewrite nobody needs, while the actual finding (a scheduling and follow-through failure) goes unaddressed. Reading audit language in its own terms, then preserving those terms in your summary upward, is the difference between remediating the finding and remediating a paraphrase of it.

**Question 2 (easy) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "More than 90% of sampled records either matched fully or showed only location errors."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Compute from the passage's own figures: 132 full matches + 11 location errors = 143 of 150 sampled records = 95.3%, which exceeds 90%: True. The check takes fifteen seconds, and the habit it rehearses is consequential: quantitative claims in statements should be verified against source arithmetic, never against impression — the passage's tone (an audit citing failures) primes readers to underrate how strong the underlying match rate is, and conversely a reassuring tone often primes over-rating. Numbers in text are checkable facts; tone is not evidence. At your level this cuts both ways: when you quote an audit to your board, your sentences will be checked the same way, so build them from the arithmetic — "95% of sampled records were correct or nearly so; the 5% unmatched tail is concentrated at the merger site" is a sentence that survives scrutiny and locates the real issue simultaneously.

**Question 3 (easy) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "The departmental merger caused the unmatched records."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage establishes concentration — unmatched records cluster at the merger site — which is a correlation with an obvious candidate mechanism (absorbed assets arriving with poor records). But the audit asserts only location, not causation: alternative explanations (that site's reconciliation practice, a local tooling issue, coincidence in a sample of 7) are neither raised nor excluded, and "caused" is a claim the text never makes. Cannot say — and note the pull toward True is strongest for the most experienced readers, because you have seen mergers corrupt registers before. That experience makes the causal story plausible; it does not make this passage assert it. The working translation: "concentrated at the merger site" belongs in your report as stated, with the causal hypothesis labelled as hypothesis and a targeted check (sample more records at that site) as the action. Precision about what evidence establishes is not caution for its own sake — it is what keeps your remediation aimed at causes rather than narratives.

**Passage B (for Questions 4-6):** From a supplier's annual service review paper: "Our partnership has delivered consistently strong outcomes this year. Response targets were achieved in 11 of 12 months, and customer satisfaction with our second-line service averaged 4.1. The single missed month (February, 87% against a 90% target) reflected an unprecedented spike in escalations from the desk, 40% above forecast, which no reasonable capacity plan could have absorbed. We nonetheless credit the month in line with the contract. Looking forward, we propose evolving the response targets toward 'resolution-focused' metrics, which better reflect the value our engineers deliver, and note that per-incident pricing will require adjustment at renewal to reflect rising complexity in the work reaching us."

**Question 4 (moderate) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "The February escalation spike was caused by a failure in the desk's escalation processes."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Test what the passage establishes about February: escalations ran 40% above forecast — a volume fact — and the supplier characterises the spike as "unprecedented" and unabsorbable. Nothing in the passage attributes the spike to any cause: a desk process failure, a genuine incident surge, a change-driven fault wave, or forecast error are all consistent with the text, which notably does not even claim the desk was at fault — only that the volume exceeded forecast. Cannot say. The reading discipline has a commercial edge here: the supplier's framing ("no reasonable capacity plan could have absorbed") invites you to accept an implicit allocation of responsibility without ever stating one, so that by renewal the February narrative has hardened into "the desk's spike". The defensive habit for persuasive documents: inventory what is asserted versus what is implied, and accept only the former into your record — then commission your own analysis of February's escalations before any conversation that prices them.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Reading the proposal's structure**

Which statement best characterises the supplier's two forward-looking proposals?

- A) Routine contract housekeeping with no commercial significance.
- B) Two linked repositionings favourable to the supplier: replacing response metrics (which they occasionally miss) with "resolution-focused" ones whose definition is not yet stated, and preparing a price increase justified by a complexity claim for which the paper offers no evidence.
- C) Customer-focused improvements that the desk should accept as drafted.
- D) A threat to terminate the contract at renewal.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Read the proposals as positions, not descriptions. The metric proposal replaces a target the supplier concretely missed (response, 87% in February) with an undefined alternative — "resolution-focused metrics" sounds user-aligned, and may even be genuinely preferable, but as drafted it is an unmeasured promise replacing a measured obligation; its merit is undecidable until definitions, baselines, and targets are stated, which is exactly why they are not yet stated. The pricing note pre-frames renewal with a causal claim ("rising complexity in the work reaching us") offered without data — and note its quiet consistency with the February framing: both position the desk's demand as the driver of the supplier's costs. Option B reads both moves and their linkage. Option A misses that metric definitions and pricing bases are the commercial contract; option C concedes negotiating ground a paper has merely requested; option D invents an ultimatum nowhere present — over-reading is as much a failure as under-reading. The senior skill: supplier papers are opening positions in the review cycle, and reading them structurally — what obligation softens, what claim lacks evidence, what frame is being rehearsed for renewal — is how you arrive at the meeting with the counter-questions already drafted: define the resolution metric, evidence the complexity trend, and revisit February with your own escalation analysis in hand.

**Question 6 (moderate) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "The supplier paid a service credit for February."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The passage states: "We nonetheless credit the month in line with the contract." That is a direct assertion that the credit was applied — True — and the surrounding rhetoric is what makes the question worth asking. The sentence's architecture ("nonetheless... in line with the contract") wraps a contractual obligation in the language of magnanimity: the supplier was required to credit the month, and the word "nonetheless" repositions compliance as generosity, an impression that matters when the same paper later requests metric relief and price increases. The tested skill is separating the fact asserted (credit paid: True, and creditable) from the framing carried (voluntary graciousness: not asserted, merely perfumed). Heads of function meet this construction constantly — "we have nonetheless agreed to", "as a gesture we have" — and the disciplined reading is always the same: check the obligation first, then price the gesture at obligation-minus-requirement, which here is zero. Verbal reasoning tests compress this into three sentences; supplier relationship management extends it across years.

**Passage C (for Questions 7-9):** From a draft strategy paper your deputy has written for the departmental technology board: "User needs have evolved significantly over five years. Our research shows staff increasingly expect consumer-grade self-service, and our portal now resolves 34% of all contacts without analyst involvement, up from 9%. However, assisted channels remain critical: satisfaction among users with accessibility needs is 0.8 points lower on the portal than on the phone, and 62% of contacts from frontline operational staff — who work away from desks — arrive by phone or chat. Our strategy is therefore 'digital first, but never digital only': we will invest in portal capability while maintaining assisted channels at full quality, and we will measure success by resolution and satisfaction across every channel, not by channel shift alone. We request £1.2m over two years, of which £700k funds portal development and £500k funds assisted-channel modernisation, including accessibility improvements."

**Question 7 (moderate) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "The paper proposes reducing investment in assisted channels as users migrate to self-service."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The paper commits to the opposite in both principle and allocation: the strategy is "digital first, but never digital only", assisted channels are to be maintained "at full quality", and £500,000 of the £1.2m request — over 40% — funds assisted-channel modernisation. The statement asserts a reduction the paper explicitly contradicts: False. The question's realism lies in why the misreading occurs: "digital first" headlines are routinely compressed, by hurried readers and hostile questioners alike, into "digital only, eventually" — and at a board, that compression will arrive as a question ("so you're winding down the phone lines?"). The paper's own defence is its allocation table, which is why strategy documents that pair every principle with a number survive boards better than those that pair principles with adjectives. As the approving head, your pre-board read should confirm exactly this alignment — the stated strategy, the money, and the metrics all saying the same thing — because any daylight between them is where the paper will be attacked.

**Question 8 (challenging) — Inference from the evidence structure**

Which conclusion is best supported by the passage's evidence as presented?

- A) The accessibility satisfaction gap proves the portal fails accessibility standards.
- B) The paper's evidence supports differentiated channel investment: self-service resolution has grown strongly (9% to 34%), while two identified populations — users with accessibility needs and deskless frontline staff — currently depend disproportionately on assisted channels, which the funding split addresses.
- C) Frontline staff prefer phone and chat and will never adopt the portal.
- D) The portal will resolve a majority of contacts within two years if the trend continues.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Match each conclusion to its evidential warrant. Option B restates exactly what the cited evidence carries: a growth fact (9% → 34%), two dependency facts (the 0.8-point satisfaction gap; the 62% phone-and-chat share among deskless staff), and the strategy's responsive structure — nothing more. Option A converts a satisfaction gap into a standards verdict: a 0.8-point deficit evidences a worse experience, but "fails accessibility standards" is a compliance claim requiring an audit the passage does not mention. Option C converts a current channel share into a permanent preference — "62% arrive by phone or chat" describes behaviour under today's portal, and says nothing about adoption under an improved one; indeed the investment case implicitly assumes otherwise. Option D extrapolates 9% → 34% forward without warrant: the passage neither states the trajectory's shape nor claims its continuation, and self-service growth characteristically saturates as the automatable contact types are consumed — the remaining contacts are harder precisely because they remained. The senior discipline on show: strategy evidence supports the differentiated present-tense claim, and each wrong option commits a distinct inflation — gap-to-verdict, share-to-preference, trend-to-destiny. Boards are persuaded by all three inflations daily; heads of function are paid to decline them, including in their own papers.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Best summary for the board**

The board secretary asks you to compress the paper into two sentences for the agenda pack. Which version is best?

- A) "The desk requests £1.2m to continue its shift to digital self-service, which now resolves 34% of contacts. Assisted channels will be maintained during the transition."
- B) "Self-service resolution has grown from 9% to 34% in five years, but accessibility-need users and deskless frontline staff still depend on assisted channels, where satisfaction and volume data justify continued quality. The £1.2m request funds both: £700k portal development, £500k assisted-channel modernisation, with success measured by resolution and satisfaction across all channels rather than channel shift."
- C) "The desk proposes a digital-first strategy costing £1.2m. Users increasingly expect consumer-grade experiences and the investment reflects this."
- D) "Following user research, the desk will invest £1.2m in its award-winning portal while protecting traditional channels for users who struggle with technology."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Judge summaries by what the board can decide from them. Option B preserves the paper's complete decision structure: the evidence (growth plus two dependencies), the allocation (both figures), and the success measure (the guard against channel-shift being gamed as a target) — a board reading only these two sentences could interrogate the right things. Option A quietly re-frames: "continue its shift... maintained during the transition" recasts assisted channels as temporary scaffolding, contradicting the paper's "never digital only" commitment — a summary that reverses its source's strategic centre while sounding faithful, which is the most dangerous species of compression because nobody checks it. Option C keeps the price and discards every piece of evidence and allocation — decorative, not decisional. Option D fabricates ("award-winning"), patronises the identified populations ("struggle with technology" mischaracterises deskless operational staff whose constraint is their work pattern, and accessibility-need users whose constraint is the portal's current design), and drops the numbers. The craft being tested is the one your role exercises weekly: synthesis under a word limit is a chain of custody for meaning — the summary must transmit the source's structure, not a plausible neighbour of it, because downstream readers act on the summary alone.

**Passage D (for Questions 10-11):** An email from the department's chief operating officer: "I've seen the quarterly figures and I'm concerned about the desk's direction — costs per contact are flat while headcount has grown by six, and I'm told other departments are resolving more through automation. I'd like your view before Thursday's performance committee: specifically, whether the desk's operating model is still the right one, and what you would change with a free hand. I should say the committee has asked whether shared services options ought to be examined; no decision has been taken, and I'd rather hear your assessment than have the committee form one without it."

**Question 10 (challenging) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "The chief operating officer has decided to move the desk into a shared services arrangement."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage addresses this directly: "no decision has been taken", and the shared-services question is attributed to the committee, not the COO, who further signals preference for hearing your assessment before positions form. The statement asserts a decision the text explicitly negates: False. The precision matters because anxiety reads pressure correspondence badly: a head who receives this email as "the decision is made, I'm being managed out" responds defensively to Thursday's committee — and defensive responses to open questions are how open questions close unfavourably. The accurate reading is materially better news and materially more demanding: the decision space is genuinely open, the COO is offering you first authorship of the analysis, and the quality of your Thursday paper — honest about the cost-flat/headcount-up tension, evidenced on automation comparisons, clear-eyed on operating model options including the uncomfortable ones — will substantially shape the outcome. Reading the modality exactly ("asked whether... ought to be examined"; "no decision"; "rather hear your assessment") is what distinguishes an invitation from a verdict, and responding to the one actually sent is the whole game.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Choosing the reply's opening position**

Which opening approach for your written response to the COO is most effective?

- A) A defence of the desk: the headcount growth was approved, the flat costs reflect inflation absorption, and other departments' automation figures are not comparable.
- B) An acknowledgement that the questions are the right ones, followed by your own analysis at full candour: where the operating model is strong (with evidence), where it is genuinely behind (automation adoption, if true), what you would change with a free hand — including options that reduce your own empire — and a framework for how shared-services alternatives should be evaluated on user outcomes and total cost, which you are content to be tested against.
- C) A request to delay the committee discussion until a consultancy review can be commissioned, ensuring the committee has independent evidence.
- D) A concise factual pack — the figures, their definitions, and their context — with no assessment, since the committee should form its own view.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Map the reply to what the COO actually requested and what the situation rewards. The email asks explicitly for assessment ("your view", "what you would change with a free hand") ahead of a committee forming its own; the strategic reality is that first credible analysis anchors the discussion. Option B supplies exactly that, and its candour is not saintliness but positioning: a head who names the desk's genuine weaknesses, proposes changes that are not self-serving, and volunteers the evaluation framework for alternatives becomes the committee's most trusted input — and trusted inputs shape criteria, which shape outcomes far more durably than advocacy does. Note the defensible-points paradox: option A's arguments may each be true (approved growth, inflation absorption, incomparable figures), but deployed as an opening they convert an invited assessment into a rebuttal, confirming the very concern — a leadership unable to examine itself — that makes committees reach for external options. Option C reads as what it is: purchased delay, with your assessment conspicuously absent — and the email's closing sentence has already told you silence will be filled by others. Option D declines the question asked; neutral fact packs are appropriate when you are a witness, not when you are the accountable head whose judgement is the requested item — withholding assessment is not rigour but abdication, and your role summary's ownership language ("take accountability", "proactive in searching for potential problems") describes precisely the posture option B performs in writing. The generalisable craft: when scrutiny arrives as an invitation, the strongest response is fuller candour than the inviter expected, structured so that every alternative will be evaluated on criteria you helped set honestly.

### Preparation tips

- Practise on persuasive documents, not just neutral ones: supplier reviews, business cases, and vendor papers from your own archive. For each, write two columns — asserted versus implied — and admit only the first into your notes. This is the senior variant of Cannot-say discipline.
- Preserve source vocabulary in summaries: design versus operation, response versus resolution, concentration versus causation. Most institutional misreadings begin as vocabulary substitutions in onward reporting.
- Verify every quantitative claim against the passage's arithmetic, in both directions: statements about the text, and your own sentences about it. Tone primes misjudgement; numbers correct it.
- Read modality like a lawyer: who decided, who asked, what "no decision has been taken" actually establishes. Pressure correspondence rewards exact parsing over emotional parsing.
- For summary questions, extract the source's decision structure first — evidence, allocation, success measure — and check the candidate summaries against it. The wrong options will preserve the tone and reverse the structure.
- Draft replies to the hardest passage types (scrutiny invitations, supplier repositionings) in your own practice: choosing between written options sharpens reading; writing your own sharpens both.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Letting experience answer instead of the passage.** You have seen mergers corrupt registers and suppliers soften metrics; this passage may not say so. Text only.
- **Collapsing audit and contract vocabulary.** "Design adequate, operation requiring improvement" is not "procedure deficient"; response is not resolution. The distinctions are the content.
- **Accepting implied allocations of responsibility.** Framing that positions blame without asserting it hardens into fact by renewal unless caught at first reading.
- **Inflating gaps, shares, and trends.** A satisfaction gap is not a standards verdict; a channel share is not a preference; a growth trend is not a destiny. Each inflation misdirects real money.
- **Summarising the tone and reversing the structure.** The dangerous summary sounds faithful while relocating the strategic centre. Check candidate summaries against the source's evidence-allocation-measure skeleton.
- **Responding to imagined verdicts instead of actual questions.** Scrutiny read as condemnation produces defence; scrutiny read exactly produces the analysis that shapes the outcome.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement test (SJT) presents realistic workplace dilemmas and asks you to evaluate candidate responses — selecting the most effective, identifying most and least effective, ranking, or rating each option. It measures professional judgement: the weighing of users, staff, suppliers, governance, truth, and organisational interest when they conflict, which at head-of-function level is less an occasional event than the substance of the job.

The typical administration is an online test of 20 to 40 minutes with 10 to 20 scenarios, generously timed. Scoring compares responses against a key calibrated by experienced senior practitioners and organisational values — in UK government, honesty and integrity, user focus, taking accountability, developing capability, inclusion, and sound governance.

Three properties distinguish SJT scenarios at your level, and recognising them sharpens your answers. First, you act through others: most scenarios involve your managers' decisions, not your own first-hand work, and the strong responses develop and correct through the management line rather than around it — reaching past your managers to fix things personally is usually a scored error, however satisfying. Second, your choices are precedents: at head level, every visible decision becomes policy in the minds of observers, so the key prices the teaching effect of each option — what it trains managers, suppliers, and directors to expect. Third, the dilemmas are governance-shaped: risk is to be surfaced, priced, and owned at the right level rather than absorbed; bad news is to travel fast and accurately; and the interests of users — who are absent from every meeting — are yours to represent, per your role summary's "achieve excellent user outcomes" and "apply strategic thinking to provide the best service for the end user". Answer as your realistic best professional self, consistently; keys read consistency as signal and persona-switching as noise.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Accountability at organisational scale** maps to your **Ownership** skill: taking accountability publicly for the function's failures, correcting the record upwards, and refusing to relocate blame downwards.
- **Leading through managers** maps to your responsibility for the management team's development: scenarios test whether you build your managers' judgement and authority or quietly substitute your own.
- **Governance of risk and transition** maps to your duty to ensure support for **emerging ICT services** and advise project teams: holding readiness lines, pricing risk for the bodies entitled to accept it, and keeping advisory candour when programmes push.
- **Evidence over advocacy** maps to your **IT service reporting** and **Problem management** skills: choosing investigation, honest baselines, and predictive candour when comfortable numbers are on offer.
- **Strategic user focus** maps to your **User focus** and **Service focus** skills: representing users across channels and services, especially the minorities whose experience aggregate metrics hide.
- **Continual improvement culture** maps to your **Customer service management** skill: converting failure into system learning — and protecting the reporting climate that makes learning possible.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — The major incident post-mortem**

A six-hour outage of a critical service last week embarrassed the department; the desk's initial misdiagnosis added two hours. The review meeting you chair includes your managers and the supplier. Your service desk manager opens by presenting a timeline that soft-pedals the misdiagnosis, attributing the delay mainly to the supplier's slow escalation acceptance. The supplier's account manager visibly bristles. What is the most effective intervention?

- A) Let the presentation stand — your manager's credibility should be protected in front of the supplier.
- B) Correct the record in the meeting, factually and without heat: restate the timeline including the desk's two-hour misdiagnosis alongside the supplier's slow acceptance, and redirect the meeting from allocation of blame to the mechanisms that allowed both failures; afterwards, privately coach your manager on why the desk's account must be the most accurate in the room.
- C) Adjourn the meeting and reconvene once the timeline is agreed offline.
- D) Correct your manager firmly in the meeting so the supplier sees the desk holds itself to account.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The situation contains two failures — the outage's and the account's — and the second is the one unfolding live. Option B repairs it at the necessary moment (a soft-pedalled timeline left standing becomes the desk's official story, and the supplier, who holds their own logs, will disprove it later at maximum cost to your credibility), does so without theatre (factual restatement, then forward to mechanisms — which is where post-mortems earn their cost), and completes the leadership loop privately: the coaching conversation is where your manager learns that accuracy is the desk's negotiating asset, not a concession. Option A purchases a moment's comfort with the desk's truthfulness — and the supplier's bristling tells you the account is already contested; protection that requires falsehood protects nothing. Option D performs accountability at your manager's expense: "firmly" in front of a supplier converts a correction into a humiliation, teaching your management team that honesty arrives as public punishment — after which timelines reach you pre-sanitised. Option C treats a correctable fact as a negotiation: adjourning signals the record is a matter for drafting, and loses the meeting's momentum for the mechanism work. The head-level pattern: correct the record immediately, blame nothing but mechanisms in public, and develop the person in private.

**Question 2 (easy) — The green dashboard and the corridor conversation**

Your monthly dashboard shows all services green. In a corridor conversation, a frontline analyst you have known for years mentions, hesitantly, that "mornings have been chaos for weeks" on a service the dashboard rates healthy — long queues before 10:00 that vanish by the afternoon measurement snapshots. What is the most effective response?

- A) Thank them and explain the dashboard methodology so they understand why the service is genuinely green.
- B) Thank them seriously, have the intraday data pulled — queue lengths by hour, not daily averages — and if the morning pattern is real, treat it as two findings: a service problem to fix, and a measurement design that averages away the worst user experience; then close the loop with the analyst either way.
- C) Ask the analyst to raise it through their manager, since the management line exists for exactly this.
- D) Note it for the next quarterly measurement review.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The analyst has handed you the most valuable commodity a head of function receives: ground truth that contradicts the instruments. Option B takes it at its worth. The intraday pull is the correct diagnostic (daily averages are constitutionally blind to intraday concentration — a service can run four-hour morning queues and average green), and the two-findings framing is the head-level move: a confirmed pattern indicts not just the service but the measurement design, and measurement that hides the worst hour of the day will be hiding it on every service, not this one. Closing the loop with the analyst is not courtesy but infrastructure: corridor truth flows toward leaders who visibly act on it and dies around leaders who explain it away. Which is option A's failure — methodology explanations convert a report into a rebuttal, teaching the frontline that the dashboard outranks their eyes. Option C is proceduralism at the wrong moment: the management line matters, but a hesitant analyst who has finally spoken to the head directly, redirected to channels, hears "wrong door" and does not knock again — you can honour the line by involving their manager in the follow-up rather than by bouncing the report. Option D files a live user-experience failure for quarterly consideration. Your role's words are exact here: "be proactive in searching for potential problems" — and proactivity's rarest form is treating inconvenient anecdote as a hypothesis worth data, fast.

**Question 3 (easy) — The capability gap in your management team**

Preparing next year's structure, you conclude the desk needs serious data and automation capability at management level. One of your three service desk managers — solid operationally, twenty years' service, deeply loyal — has repeatedly declined data-skills development, saying they "manage people, not spreadsheets". The other two are developing well. What is the most effective approach?

- A) Design the structure around the gap: route data-dependent responsibilities to the other two managers permanently.
- B) Have the direct conversation you have been avoiding: the role is changing — prediction, automation, and analytics are now management-level requirements, not optional extras; offer serious, supported development with time and coaching, be honest that the requirement is not waivable, and give them a genuine period to step up before any structural conclusion.
- C) Include the requirement in next year's objectives and let the appraisal cycle surface the issue formally.
- D) Move them to a project-liaison role where the gap matters less, framing it as recognition of their operational strengths.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Your role summary makes this scenario near-literal: "ensure that the support team undertakes development so that they can advise on future projects and current issues" — and the team most needing that guarantee is your management team. Option B is the only option that treats the manager as capable of growth while treating the requirement as real. Its honesty is the kindness: twenty years of service has earned a direct conversation, not a quiet re-architecture; "not waivable" said plainly, with genuine support attached, gives the manager an actual choice with actual information — and many "people, not spreadsheets" positions soften when development is offered as investment rather than remediation. Option A embeds the gap structurally: it taxes two managers with a third's unmet requirement, signals to the whole function that development requirements are negotiable through refusal, and quietly writes off a person without telling them. Option C outsources a leadership conversation to a form: appraisal cycles document known problems months later, and the manager learns the stakes from paperwork rather than from you — a process correct in sequence and hollow in respect. Option D is the misdirection with the kindest face: an invented role, framed as recognition, that both parties will eventually decode as exile — and that spends a real headcount on avoiding one conversation. Heads of function shape capability through direct expectation plus real support; every other instrument is either its preparation or its substitute.

**Question 4 (moderate) — The programme that wants a number**

A major digital programme, politically visible and behind schedule, asks you to sign an assurance that the desk "will be ready" to support its national launch in ten weeks. Your honest position: readiness is achievable but not yet achieved — training is 60% complete, the knowledge base is drafted but unvalidated, and the programme has slipped twice before, which stopped your earlier preparation both times. The programme director needs the signed assurance for a ministerial submission this week. What is the most effective response?

- A) Sign — the gaps are closable in ten weeks, and refusing would make the desk the story.
- B) Refuse to sign until readiness is complete, whatever the submission timetable.
- C) Provide a conditional assurance in writing: state what is complete, what remains, the dates each remaining item closes, and the dependencies on programme stability — signing to the plan and its conditions rather than to an unconditional "ready", and offering to brief the submission's drafters on accurate wording.
- D) Sign, but keep your own file note recording that readiness was incomplete at signature.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The request conflates two different assurances — "we will be ready" (a forecast with conditions) and "we are ready" (a state) — and option C refuses the conflation rather than the programme. A conditional assurance is not a hedge; it is the accurate instrument: complete items evidenced, remaining items dated, and the dependency named that the programme itself controls (its own stability — which has already consumed your preparation twice, a fact that belongs in writing precisely because it is awkward). Offering to help draft accurate submission wording converts the desk from obstacle to collaborator while keeping the record true. Option A signs the desk's name to a state that does not exist, on the theory that the future will redeem it — and if the programme slips a third time or training stalls, the signed unconditional assurance becomes the document around which accountability organises, with your signature on it. Option B mistakes the strongest available position for refusal: an accurate conditional assurance protects users and the desk better than a standoff that invites the programme to route around you — and "whatever the submission timetable" abandons the advisory role your summary assigns ("provide technical advice to project teams"). Option D is the signature you know to be wrong plus evidence that you knew: file notes do not neutralise false assurances; they document bad faith. The governance principle the key rewards: sign what is true, condition what is contingent, and make the accurate version easy for power to use.

**Question 5 (moderate) — The automation programme and the workforce**

Your three-year strategy includes automation that will reduce first-line demand by an estimated 35%. Finance wants the benefit banked as headcount reduction in next year's budget. Your workforce analysis says natural attrition over three years covers most of the reduction, and the remaining roles can shift toward the more complex second-line and advisory work your strategy needs — but only if the reduction is phased with attrition rather than taken up front. What is the most effective position?

- A) Accept finance's timetable — automation benefits that are not banked promptly tend never to be banked.
- B) Contest the reduction entirely: the desk should retain all posts and redeploy everyone.
- C) Present the workforce arithmetic as the business case: commit to the full 35% benefit, but phased against attrition, showing that forced early reduction pays redundancy costs to remove people whose capacity the transition itself needs (training backfill, knowledge transfer, elevated transition contact rates) — and offer finance a tracked benefits schedule with dates, so the commitment is auditable rather than deferred.
- D) Agree the reduction in principle and quietly delay its implementation through vacancy management.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The genuine tension — finance's discipline against workforce reality — is resolved by neither capitulation nor resistance but by better arithmetic, which is option C's substance. The commitment is real (full 35%, dated, auditable), and the phasing is argued in finance's own currency: early forced reduction incurs redundancy cost, then rehires or backfills for transition work attrition would have covered free — a demonstrably worse cash position, not a softer one. The tracked schedule is what separates a credible phasing from an indefinite deferral, and offering it unprompted is what makes finance a partner in the phasing rather than its policeman. Option A trades the transition's success for this budget round: automation programmes fail operationally precisely when their savings are extracted before the work has moved. Option B discredits the desk's arithmetic entirely — a 35% demand reduction with zero workforce consequence is not a position finance can respect, and defending it spends the credibility the phasing argument needs. Option D is the finance-relations equivalent of the file note in Question 4: agreement performed, implementation withheld — and when discovered (vacancy management is visible in every workforce report), it converts a winnable argument about phasing into an unwinnable one about good faith. The role connection is direct: "investigate which processes can be automated" carries a workforce corollary at head level, and owning both halves — the saving and its safe extraction path — is what distinguishes strategy from a spreadsheet.

**Question 6 (moderate) — The user constituency nobody champions**

Satisfaction data across your channels is healthy — except among users with accessibility needs, where portal satisfaction runs 0.8 points below average, a gap unchanged for two years. Fixing it requires £150k of portal remediation. The affected population is roughly 4% of users; the same £150k spent on general portal features would improve satisfaction modestly for the other 96%, and utilitarian arithmetic favours the general spend. Your improvement budget round is now. What is the most effective position?

- A) Fund the general improvements — the greatest good for the greatest number is the defensible public-sector calculus.
- B) Fund the accessibility remediation, and frame it correctly upwards: public services carry obligations to accessible provision that are not subject to utilitarian netting; a two-year-old known gap for users who often have no alternative channel is a service failure and plausibly a legal exposure, not one bid among peers — while directing the general improvements into next year's case.
- C) Split the funding between the two, delivering partial progress on each.
- D) Defer both and commission research into whether the accessibility gap reflects genuine need.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The scenario's utilitarian framing is the trap, and option B names why it does not govern: accessible public services are an obligation — legally under equality duties, and ethically because the affected users frequently cannot channel-switch away from a portal that serves them worst — so the comparison "4% versus 96%" prices a duty as if it were a preference. The two-year persistence is the aggravating fact: a known, measured, unremediated gap is the profile of organisational neglect, and heads of function are precisely the altitude at which such gaps either get owned or get perpetually outranked by broader-shallower bids. B also does the strategic work: framing the remediation as obligation repositions it out of the improvement-bid beauty contest (where it will lose every year to arithmetic), which is both true and the only sustainable funding argument for it. Option A is the calculus that produced the two-year gap. Option C half-funds an obligation — remediation thresholds unmet are money spent without the gap closing. Option D commissions research into a question two years of measured data has answered, purchasing delay in rigour's clothing; "understand user needs across a variety of channels", your named skill, is here a completed task awaiting action. The generalisable principle the key rewards: distinguish obligations from optimisations before comparing them, and spend your seniority making that distinction stick in rooms where arithmetic is the only native language.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Most and least effective: the failing absorbed desk**

Three months after your function absorbed another department's desk, its former analysts' performance is well below your standards: longer calls, lower FCR, higher error rates. Your managers are frustrated; some absorbed staff are audibly demoralised; one of your original team leaders refers to them in a meeting as "the legacy lot". Four candidate responses:

1. Diagnose before judging: commission a proper look at whether the gap is skills, tooling familiarity, process differences, or morale — while publicly framing the absorbed team as colleagues whose integration is the function's shared responsibility, and correcting the "legacy lot" language on the spot.
2. Set the absorbed staff a three-month performance improvement window with individual targets.
3. Pair each absorbed analyst with an experienced mentor and fund conversion training, without further diagnosis.
4. Accept a transition dip as normal and let time integrate the teams.

Which is the MOST effective and which is the LEAST effective response?

- A) Most: 1; Least: 4
- B) Most: 3; Least: 2
- C) Most: 1; Least: 2
- D) Most: 3; Least: 4

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option 1 is most effective because it sequences correctly and leads culturally. The performance gap has at least four candidate mechanisms with entirely different remedies — skills (training), tooling unfamiliarity (time and support), process differences (harmonisation, possibly adopting their better practices), and morale (leadership behaviour, of which "the legacy lot" is a live specimen requiring correction in the moment, since language that others hear the head tolerate becomes licensed) — and acting before diagnosing risks funding the wrong remedy while the real mechanism compounds. Option 3 is attractive but premature: mentoring and training are the right response to a skills-and-familiarity diagnosis, which has not yet been made — if the gap is process mismatch or morale, option 3 spends the budget while insulting the absorbed staff's actual competence. For least effective, compare 2 and 4. Option 4 is passive, but transition dips genuinely are common, and time plus exposure does close familiarity gaps — it under-manages, yet does limited active harm. Option 2 is worse than passivity: individual performance management applied to a cohort three months after an organisational upheaval attributes a structural situation to personal failing — it will be experienced (and possibly formally contested) as targeting absorbed staff as a class, it converts demoralisation into grievance and attrition of exactly the people carrying the absorbed department's knowledge, and it teaches your original staff that the integration is a culling. The head-level principles: diagnose structure before managing individuals, and treat integration language as a leadership-owned artefact — both are what "resourcing and development of the team" means when the team has just doubled.

**Question 8 (challenging) — The prediction you don't want to present**

Your demand model — which you championed and the board funded — now forecasts that a flagship departmental app's next release will drive desk contact volumes 20% over budgeted capacity for a quarter. The release owner, a well-connected director, disputes the model informally: "Your model overreacts; the release is mostly cosmetic." Your data team stands by the forecast with moderate confidence. The board meets next week; presenting the forecast strains your relationship with the director and, if the model is wrong, damages its credibility and yours. What is the most effective course?

- A) Present the forecast with its confidence attached, the director's dissent fairly stated, the checkable assumptions listed, and a contingent mitigation plan (surge capacity triggers) that the board can fund cheaply against the risk — inviting the board to decide with the disagreement visible.
- B) Soften the forecast to a "possible moderate increase" to preserve relationships and revisit after the release.
- C) Withhold the forecast this cycle; if early post-release data confirms it, present with evidence next month.
- D) Present the forecast at full strength and characterise the director's objection as unevidenced.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** This is the ownership test in predictive form: the entire value of a funded forecasting capability is realised at exactly the moment its output is unwelcome — a model consulted only when convenient is decoration. Option A presents the instrument's reading honestly and does four senior things at once: confidence stated (moderate — the board deserves calibration, not theatre), dissent represented fairly (the director's "cosmetic" claim is a checkable assumption about the release, and stating it fairly both respects the relationship and puts the disagreement's substance, rather than its politics, before the board), assumptions listed (so the model's eventual vindication or failure is diagnosable either way), and — the decisive element — a contingent mitigation priced cheaply (surge triggers cost little if the director is right and save a quarter's service if the model is; asymmetric options like this are what forecasts are for). Option B is the characteristic senior failure this scenario exists to catch: softening language until a specific, actionable prediction becomes an unfalsifiable murmur — the board hears nothing decidable, the capacity goes unfunded, and if the surge arrives, the record shows you knew and blurred it. Option C converts foresight into hindsight voluntarily: post-release confirmation arrives after the lead time surge capacity requires, which is to say, uselessly. Option D wins the argument and burns the coalition: characterising a director's position as "unevidenced" in a board paper makes the disagreement about standing rather than assumptions, and forecasting functions live on the cooperation of the programmes they forecast. The pattern for every unwelcome-evidence scenario at your level: full strength on the finding, full fairness to the dissent, and a cheap contingent action that makes the board's decision easy under either truth.

**Question 9 (challenging) — The supplier failure with a personal dimension**

Your out-of-hours support supplier has missed its response targets for four consecutive months. The account director — who you know is managing a serious family illness, and who has been personally candid with you about it — keeps promising recovery that does not materialise. Escalating formally within the supplier will likely end their account leadership; not escalating leaves overnight users of critical services exposed, and your own governance now requires action. What is the most effective course?

- A) Give the account director one further quarter, given the circumstances and the relationship's long-term value.
- B) Escalate formally to the supplier's leadership about the service, as governance requires — framing the failure institutionally (four months of missed targets is an organisational resourcing failure, not one person's) — while separately and privately telling the account director what you are doing and why before the letter lands; how the supplier supports its own staff is then theirs to manage, and you can say honestly what you observed of the account director's professionalism.
- C) Escalate formally and include your knowledge of the account director's personal situation so the supplier's leadership has full context for their staffing decision.
- D) Bypass the account director informally: ask the supplier's delivery director, whom you know from a previous contract, to quietly fix the resourcing without a formal escalation on file.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Separate the strands the scenario deliberately tangles: a service accountability (four months of exposure for overnight users of critical services — and your role summary's SLA-management duty makes the escalation non-optional), a personal confidence (the illness, shared with you in trust), and a professional relationship (worth preserving, but not at users' expense). Option B keeps each strand in its lane. The escalation is institutional in framing because that is where four-month failures actually live — a supplier whose recovery depends on one individual's circumstances has an organisational resourcing failure by definition, and framing it so is both true and the account director's best protection. The private pre-notification honours the relationship without subordinating the service to it: they hear it from you, before, with reasons — which is how professional respect and formal accountability coexist. And the confidence stays kept: option C breaches it under the flag of "full context", disclosing health information shared personally into an employment decision — a violation that is unethical in itself and, once known, would teach every counterpart that candour with you is unsafe. Option A extends users' overnight exposure to a fifth, sixth, and seventh month as a private kindness funded from a public service — compassion exercised with other people's risk. Option D is the subtle trap for relationship-rich seniors: the back-channel "quiet fix" leaves no record for your governance (which now requires action — an off-books remedy is non-compliance wearing pragmatism), humiliates the account director more surely than formal process (everyone learns they were routed around), and converts your supplier management from institutional to personal — precisely the fragility that caused the problem. The key's underlying principle: at head level, kindness operates through how formal accountability is exercised — its framing, its notice, its fairness — never through whether.

**Question 10 (challenging) — The shared-services study**

The department commissions a formal study of moving your desk into a cross-government shared-services arrangement. You believe, on the evidence, that the move would degrade service for your department's specialised user groups — but you also recognise your judgement is conflicted: the move would shrink your role, and everyone in the room knows it. The study team asks you to nominate the desk's representative and to provide the desk's data. What is the most effective posture?

- A) Nominate your strongest advocate, provide the data with accompanying analysis highlighting the risks of the move, and work your departmental relationships to ensure the study hears the counter-case early and often.
- B) Provide complete, unspun data; nominate a credible expert rather than an advocate; put your service-degradation concerns into the study as specific, testable claims (which user groups, which requirements, what evidence) while explicitly acknowledging your structural conflict; and privately prepare your function to succeed under either outcome.
- C) Decline close involvement so the study is visibly independent of you, and let its conclusions arrive on their own merits.
- D) Cooperate formally while quietly briefing sympathetic stakeholders on the study's likely flaws.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The scenario's centre is the acknowledged conflict, and option B is what integrity under conflict actually looks like operationally. Complete unspun data is non-negotiable — a head who curates evidence toward their own preservation converts a legitimate professional judgement into exactly the self-interest everyone suspects, and study teams detect curation. The expert-not-advocate nomination serves the same logic: advocates are discounted; experts are consulted. The decisive move is converting your concern into testable form — "specialised user groups X and Y require A and B, which shared arrangements elsewhere have failed to provide; here is the evidence" — because testable claims survive their author's conflict: the study can verify them independently of your interest, which is the only way a conflicted party's judgement can legitimately influence an outcome. Explicitly naming your conflict is not confession but positioning: it pre-empts the discount others would apply anyway and marks you as the room's most trustworthy reader of your own evidence. And preparing the function for either outcome is the ownership duty running beneath: your obligation is to your users and staff under whatever structure wins, not to the structure. Option A is sophisticated advocacy — data plus spin plus relationship pressure — which stakes the desk's credibility on winning, and forfeits it on losing. Option C abdicates: your knowledge of the specialised user groups is evidence the study needs, and "visible independence" purchased by silence starves the analysis your users depend on — recusal is for judges, not witnesses. Option D is A's covert edition, with discovery risk attached; briefed flaws travel, and their provenance travels with them. The senior principle the key rewards: when structurally conflicted, maximise the testability of your claims and the completeness of your evidence, and let your influence flow entirely through what can be checked.

**Question 11 (challenging) — The culture signal**

Reviewing exit interviews across the function, you find a pattern: departing analysts describe your middle management layer as "results-focused but fear-based" — mistakes hidden, sickness worked through, overtime unclaimed and expected. Your dashboards show none of this: performance is strong, attrition is at benchmark, and your service desk managers — whom you developed, and who deliver reliably — are the layer being described. The account conflicts with your direct experience of them. What is the most effective response?

- A) Treat the pattern as serious signal despite its conflict with your experience: triangulate quietly (staff survey cuts, overtime and sickness data, skip-level conversations run as listening rather than investigation), examine your own contribution first — whether your results-focus and delivery pressure created the incentives the managers are transmitting — then address findings with the management team as a shared culture problem to fix, not an accusation to defend against.
- B) Dismiss it proportionately: exit interviews over-sample the disaffected, your dashboards are healthy, and managers who deliver reliably have earned the benefit of the doubt.
- C) Confront the management team with the exit-interview quotes and require their response.
- D) Commission an external culture review to establish the facts independently before you form any view.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Weigh the evidence structurally. Exit interviews are biased samples — but the bias is toward candour, not just disaffection: leavers are the only population with nothing to lose by describing fear, which is why fear-based cultures characteristically present exactly this signature — clean dashboards (fear suppresses the reporting that would dirty them: hidden mistakes are absent from error data by construction), benchmark attrition (fear delays leaving as well as speaking), and a head whose direct experience is warm (managers transmit pressure downward and manage upward; your experience of them is real and unrepresentative simultaneously). Option A reads this structure correctly and sequences correctly: triangulation first (unclaimed overtime against badge or system hours, sickness patterns, skip-level listening — data fear cannot easily fake), your own contribution examined before anyone is confronted — the head-level insight, since middle managers under delivery pressure are usually conducting a current that originates above them, and a culture fix that exempts its probable source fixes nothing — then the management team engaged as co-owners of a shared problem, which is the only framing under which managers disclose rather than defend. Option B is the trap built from true premises: each discount (sampling, dashboards, track record) is individually reasonable, and jointly they constitute the exact reasoning by which every fear-based culture stays invisible to its head — when three independent signals would each be explained away by the system they describe, their convergence is the finding. Option C converts unverified quotes into an indictment: it will be experienced as ambush, produce defence rather than truth, and — if the culture is as described — model precisely the fear it purports to address. Option D outsources what must begin as leadership: external reviews have their place later, but commissioning one before any internal listening tells your function that its head needs consultants to hear it, and delays months while the pattern continues. The deepest principle in the key: the signals most worth acting on at head level are the ones your own position makes hardest to see — and the leader's first investigative subject is their own transmission line.

### Preparation tips

- Before practising, reread your role summary as a set of dilemmas: accountability (when the record flatters you), development (when the person resists), prediction (when the forecast is unwelcome), user focus (when the users are few and the arithmetic is against them). The key lives where duties collide.
- Apply the through-the-line test: at head level, strong answers usually act through managers while developing them — options where you personally bypass, rescue, or perform are usually scored down even when operationally effective.
- Price the precedent in every option: what does this response teach managers, suppliers, and the frontline to do next time? Answers that solve today by corrupting tomorrow's incentives are the level's characteristic trap.
- Separate obligations from optimisations before weighing anything: accessibility, honest assurance, confidentiality, and governance compliance are not bids in a utilitarian auction.
- For most/least formats, hunt the option that is worse than passivity — usually the one that attributes structural problems to individuals, breaches a confidence for "context", or performs agreement while withholding implementation.
- Rehearse the conflicted-party posture: complete data, testable claims, named conflict. It generalises across supplier disputes, restructure studies, and your own model's unwelcome forecasts — and it is the posture selection panels most want to see evidenced.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Protecting people with falsehood.** Records softened for managers, assurances signed for programmes, and quarters extended for struggling suppliers all fail the same test: protection that requires untruth protects the protector last and the users never.
- **Performing accountability downward.** Public firmness with subordinates and private flexibility with power is the inversion the key detects fastest. Correct records in the room; develop people out of it.
- **Explaining away convergent weak signals.** Corridor anecdotes, exit interviews, and reopen rates are individually dismissible and jointly decisive — the dismissals are how invisible problems stay invisible.
- **Extracting benefits before the transition earns them.** Headcount banked ahead of attrition, workarounds closed as root causes, and forecasts softened into murmurs all mortgage next year to this quarter's comfort.
- **Substituting instruments for leadership.** Appraisal cycles, external reviews, and invented roles are legitimate tools that become avoidance when deployed instead of a direct conversation you owe someone.
- **Letting utilitarian arithmetic settle obligation questions.** The 4% with no alternative channel, the confidence shared in trust, the governance that requires action — these outrank the greater-number calculus, and the key is calibrated accordingly.
