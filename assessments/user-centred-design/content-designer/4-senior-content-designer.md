# Senior Content Designer - Psychometric Assessment Resources

## Introduction

Welcome! This guide has been created especially for you as a senior content designer working within the UK Government Digital and Data profession. Whether you are preparing for an internal assessment, applying for a new post, benchmarking your own capability, or simply curious about how psychometric assessments relate to your day-to-day work, this document will help you practise, reflect, and build confidence.

Psychometric assessments are structured, standardised exercises that measure the mental capabilities and behavioural tendencies that predict success in a role. For a senior content designer, that means far more than abstract puzzles or generic word games. The assessments described here are job-specific: they use the kinds of artefacts you genuinely handle — user stories, content patterns, style guides, research findings, analytics dashboards, prototypes, and stakeholder correspondence — and the kinds of decisions you genuinely make, such as developing content strategy for large-scale problems and high-profile events, assuring the quality of colleagues' work through review, leading cross-government content projects, and mentoring the content designers around you.

Why do these assessments matter for your role in particular? As a senior content designer, you are an expert practitioner who takes responsibility for content quality across a team, not just your own pages. You write and map user stories, you review the work of others, you negotiate with stakeholders who want the content to say something the evidence does not support, and you turn quantitative and qualitative research into confident content decisions. Assessments of cognitive ability, numerical reasoning, verbal reasoning, and situational judgement map directly onto those demands. Practising them sharpens exactly the skills your role depends on: spotting an inconsistency between a content pattern and its implementation, interpreting completion-rate data before deciding which journey fix to prioritise, extracting precise meaning from a dense policy instruction so your content reflects it accurately, and choosing the most effective course of action when a stakeholder, a deadline, and a user need all pull in different directions.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive ability assessment measures the exact mental processes your role demands, using practical workplace scenarios rather than abstract puzzles. Unlike a generic intelligence test, it presents you with the everyday materials of your job — in your case, content patterns, user story maps, style guide rules, journey diagrams, research findings, and review comments — and asks you to reason quickly and accurately about them.

Typical format: an online, timed test lasting 15 to 30 minutes, with questions covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective, comparing your responses against a norm group, and modern platforms often adapt question difficulty based on your previous answers — answer well and the questions get harder, which is a good sign, not a bad one. Employers usually receive a breakdown of speed versus accuracy rather than a single score, so working both quickly and carefully matters. You will normally be offered short, ungraded practice questions before the real test begins, so you can get comfortable with the interface without pressure.

For a senior content designer, cognitive ability assessment is particularly relevant because your professional value rests on structured thinking applied to language and journeys. You hold an end-to-end service in mind while editing a single sentence within it. You check that a content pattern established in one part of a service has been applied consistently everywhere else. You deduce what a change to eligibility rules means for every page that mentions eligibility. And because you review the work of others and assure quality across a team, your error-checking must be faster and more reliable than anyone else's in the room. A well-designed cognitive assessment simulates precisely those demands in miniature.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The assessment dimensions map directly to the skills in your capability framework:

- **Pattern recognition** maps to your **Content concepts and prototyping** skill: establishing content patterns, then testing and iterating them, means recognising recurring structures across pages and journeys — and instantly noticing when a page departs from the pattern.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **User-centred content design** and **Strategic thinking (content design)**: showing a deep understanding of end-to-end journeys means reasoning validly about what follows when one step, rule, or piece of content changes; leading strategic content improvement projects means reasoning from objectives to the work that genuinely serves them.
- **Error checking** maps to **User-centred content design**: taking responsibility for assuring the quality of content from more junior colleagues means spotting inconsistencies between a page and the style guide, between two pages in the same journey, or between the content and the underlying policy.
- **Prioritisation** maps to **Strategic thinking (content design)** and **Agile working (content design)**: focusing effort in the areas of greatest priority, and working in a fast-paced, evolving environment with an iterative approach, both depend on rapidly ranking what matters most against user needs and objectives.
- **Problem solving** maps to **User focus (content design)**: formulating hypotheses, gaining insights from research, and making decisions based on findings requires you to decompose a tangled journey problem, weigh evidence, and choose a next step you can explain clearly.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern recognition in a content pattern library**

Your team's pattern library specifies that every "check before you start" page follows this structure: (1) service name as the H1, (2) a lead paragraph stating what the user can do, (3) a bulleted list of what the user needs, (4) a green "Start now" button. Reviewing four draft pages from a junior colleague, you note their structures:

1. H1, lead paragraph, bulleted list, Start now button
2. H1, bulleted list, lead paragraph, Start now button
3. H1, lead paragraph, bulleted list, Start now button
4. H1, lead paragraph, bulleted list, Start now button

Which page breaks the pattern?

- A) Page 1
- B) Page 2
- C) Page 3
- D) Page 4

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The pattern fixes the order: lead paragraph before the bulleted list. Page 2 inverts elements (2) and (3), presenting the list of requirements before telling the user what the service lets them do. Pages 1, 3 and 4 follow the sequence exactly. This is the bread and butter of quality assurance at your level: patterns exist so users get a consistent experience across government, and a reviewer who cannot spot a transposed element quickly will miss it under deadline pressure. Notice also why the order matters for users — context ("what you can do") before requirements ("what you need") reduces cognitive load — so when you feed back to your colleague, you can explain the reason, not just the rule. That is the difference between correcting work and coaching, and your role asks for both.

**Question 2 (easy) — Logical deduction from a style rule**

Your style guide states: "Use 'sign in', not 'log in', in all service content unless the content refers to a third-party system that itself uses 'log in'." A page you are reviewing says "log in" in a sentence about your own department's tax account service. Which one of the following must be true?

- A) The page breaches the style guide.
- B) The junior designer who wrote it does not know the style guide.
- C) The page should use "login" as one word instead.
- D) The sentence refers to a third-party system.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The rule permits "log in" only for third-party systems that use that term. The sentence refers to your own department's service, so the exception cannot apply, and the usage breaches the guide — A must be true. B is tempting but goes beyond the evidence: the writer may know the rule and have slipped, or may have copied legacy text; assessments reward deducing only what the given information guarantees. C contradicts the guide entirely ("login" as a noun is not offered as an option here). D contradicts the stem. The habit of separating "this breaks the rule" from "here is my theory about why" also makes your 2i feedback kinder and more accurate — you comment on the content, never on assumed gaps in a colleague's knowledge.

**Question 3 (easy) — Prioritisation of review comments**

You are doing a quality review of a high-profile page due to publish in one hour, connected with a national event. You find four issues. Which should you fix first?

- A) A sentence says applicants "must apply by 31 June" — a date that does not exist.
- B) Two headings use title case instead of sentence case.
- C) A paragraph is 68 words long and would read better split in two.
- D) The page uses "utilise" where "use" would be plainer.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** All four are genuine issues, but only A is factually wrong in a way that could directly harm users: "31 June" does not exist, so users cannot know whether the deadline is 30 June or 1 July, and on a high-profile page during a national event that ambiguity generates failure demand — calls, complaints, missed deadlines. B, C and D are style and clarity improvements: worth fixing, but none misleads a user about what to do. Under a one-hour deadline, sequence by user harm: factual accuracy first, then clarity, then style polish. This mirrors your responsibility for content quality during high-profile events: perfection everywhere is the goal, but triage is the skill.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Error checking across a journey**

A service journey has four screens. Screen 1 says "You'll need your National Insurance number and a recent payslip." Screen 2 asks for the National Insurance number. Screen 3 asks the user to upload a P60. Screen 4 confirms "We've received your National Insurance number and payslip." Assuming the service genuinely requires a P60 and not a payslip, how many screens contain content that must change?

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Work through each screen against the true requirement (P60). Screen 1 promises a payslip will be needed — wrong, must change. Screen 2 asks for the National Insurance number — correct, no change. Screen 3 asks for the P60 — correct, no change. Screen 4 confirms receipt of a payslip — wrong, must change. Two screens must change. The tempting error is answering A after fixing only Screen 1, because the eye anchors on the first mention. This is exactly the deep understanding of end-to-end journeys your framework names: content errors rarely live on one page, because upstream promises and downstream confirmations must all agree. When you identify a journey fix, you trace every screen that touches the same fact.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Logical deduction from eligibility rules**

A policy instruction states: (i) applicants aged 66 or over are eligible; (ii) applicants under 66 are eligible only if they receive a qualifying benefit; (iii) nobody may apply on behalf of another person except an appointed representative. You are writing the eligibility page. A stakeholder asks you to add: "If you are 65 and do not receive a qualifying benefit, you may still be eligible — contact us to check." Is the proposed sentence consistent with the rules?

- A) Yes — contacting to check is always allowed.
- B) No — under the rules a 65-year-old without a qualifying benefit is not eligible, so "may still be eligible" is misleading.
- C) Yes — rules always have discretionary exceptions.
- D) Cannot say — the rules do not mention 65-year-olds.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Apply the rules to the case. A 65-year-old is under 66, so rule (i) does not apply. Rule (ii) then governs: under-66s are eligible only if they receive a qualifying benefit. This person does not, so they are not eligible. The proposed sentence says they "may still be eligible", which contradicts the rules and would generate false hope plus avoidable contact. D fails because the rules do cover 65-year-olds — through the "under 66" condition; "not mentioned by name" is not the same as "not covered". C invents discretion the instruction does not grant. At your level, this is a common and consequential moment: a stakeholder wants softer content, and your job is to show — gently, with the rules on the table — that soft content here is inaccurate content, and inaccurate content harms both users and the department.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Pattern recognition in user research findings**

Across five usability testing rounds of a benefit application, the researcher's notes record where each participant first hesitated: Round 1 — "evidence upload"; Round 2 — "evidence upload"; Round 3 — "declaration wording"; Round 4 — "evidence upload"; Round 5 — "evidence upload". The service team has budget to redesign one step before the next release. As the senior content designer, what is the most defensible reading of the pattern?

- A) The declaration wording is the priority — it is the most recent kind of problem to appear only once, so it is emerging.
- B) The evidence upload step is the priority — four of five rounds independently surfaced it as the first point of hesitation.
- C) Neither is a priority — five rounds is too small a sample to conclude anything.
- D) Both must be redesigned before release regardless of budget.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Four independent rounds converging on the same step is a strong qualitative signal — in usability research, recurrence across sessions matters more than raw sample size, because each round involves different participants and often iterated designs. A misreads a single occurrence as a trend. C over-applies quantitative instincts to qualitative evidence: small-sample usability testing is precisely designed to surface repeated blockers, and dismissing consistent findings would leave you unable to act on research at all. D ignores the stated constraint, and constraints are real. Your **User focus** skill asks you to understand the different types of user research and evidence, to gain insights from research and make decisions based on findings, clearly explaining how decisions have been made — B is that skill in one sentence: "four of five rounds hesitated first at evidence upload, so that is where our one redesign goes."

**Question 7 (moderate) — Prioritisation across a mixed workload**

It is Monday morning. Four items compete for your attention:

1. Content for a ministerial announcement publishes Wednesday; the fact-check from the policy team arrived overnight with three disputed sentences.
2. A junior content designer's first solo journey map needs your review before their show-and-tell on Friday.
3. The cross-government content community has asked you to confirm by tomorrow whether you will present next month.
4. Your team's content backlog refinement session is Thursday and needs user stories drafted for two new epics.

Which sequencing is most defensible?

- A) 1, 3, 2, 4 — publication deadline first, then the quick commitment, then the review, then refinement prep
- B) 3, 1, 2, 4 — clear the smallest task first
- C) 1, 2, 3, 4 — strict order of importance
- D) 2, 1, 4, 3 — developing others always comes first

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Item 1 is both urgent (Wednesday publication) and high impact (disputed sentences on a ministerial announcement — factual accuracy under national visibility). Item 3 is nearly effortless but has the earliest deadline (tomorrow) and blocks someone else's planning; a two-minute reply dispatches it — this is the "quick unblocking" move, and doing it second rather than first simply reflects that item 1 needs the morning's fresh attention. Item 2 has a Friday horizon and deserves unhurried attention — rushed feedback on a first solo piece of work is worse than slightly later feedback. Item 4 is Thursday and substantial, so it slots after the review is scheduled. B lets small-task comfort displace a publication deadline. C's "strict importance" leaves a colleague across government waiting for a two-minute answer. D is generous but mis-weights: mentoring matters, and Friday allows it to be done well. Notice the general technique: sequence by impact-weighted urgency, and dispatch genuinely tiny commitments early so they stop occupying mental space.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Applied problem solving with a content hypothesis**

Analytics show that 40% of users abandon a passport-renewal journey on the photo requirements page. You have three candidate explanations: (i) the requirements are genuinely hard to meet at that moment (users leave to take a photo and return later); (ii) the content is unclear so users give up; (iii) the page loads slowly. Which evidence, if observed, would most strongly support explanation (i) over the others?

- A) A high proportion of the abandoning users return and complete the journey within 48 hours.
- B) Session recordings show users scrolling up and down the page repeatedly before leaving.
- C) The page's average load time is 9 seconds.
- D) Users who reach the following page complete the journey at high rates.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Explanation (i) predicts a distinctive signature: abandonment that is really postponement, so the same users reappear and finish once they have a compliant photo. A observes exactly that signature and is hard to reconcile with (ii) or (iii) — confused or frustrated users are less likely to return in high proportions and sail through. B would support (ii): repeated scrolling suggests searching for understanding. C would support (iii). D is consistent with all three explanations, since it says nothing about the users who left. This is hypothesis-driven content design, straight from your **User focus** skill: formulate hypotheses, identify what evidence would discriminate between them, and only then decide whether the journey fix is a rewrite, a performance ticket, or a "save and return" feature — three completely different responses to the same 40% statistic.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Multi-constraint scheduling of a content sprint**

You lead a cross-government content project with four tasks in a two-week sprint (days 1–10): audit (A), pattern proposal (P), stakeholder walkthrough (W), and usability testing (T). Constraints: (i) A must finish before P starts; (ii) A takes 3 working days and starts day 1; (iii) W must happen on day 6 or day 7, when the stakeholders are available, and requires P to be complete; (iv) T requires two full days after W for the researcher to recruit participants, then occupies one day. What is the earliest day T can take place?

- A) Day 8
- B) Day 9
- C) Day 10
- D) T cannot fit in this sprint

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Work forwards. A occupies days 1–3. P starts day 4 at the earliest; suppose it completes on day 4 or 5 — either way it can be complete before day 6. W can then happen on day 6 (constraint iii is satisfiable at its earliest slot). T requires two full days after W — days 7 and 8 — and then occupies one day, so the earliest day for T is day 9. The tempting error is day 8, forgetting that "two full days after W" means days 7 and 8 must elapse before T runs. Answer C arises from placing W on day 7 unnecessarily, and D from misreading recruitment as one week. Dependency chains like this sit underneath every sprint you plan with researchers and stakeholders — your **Agile working** skill's fast-paced, iterative delivery only works when someone on the team can hold the constraint chain in their head and spot that moving W by one day silently moves testing out of the sprint.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Error checking a user story map**

Your user story map for "apply for a fishing licence" contains these stories: (1) "As an angler, I can check which licence I need, so that I buy the right one." (2) "As an angler, I can pay by card, so that I complete my purchase online." (3) "As the Environment Agency, we can collect payment data, so that we meet reporting duties." (4) "As an angler, I can receive my licence by email, so that I can prove I hold one." A junior colleague says story 3 is wrongly formed. Is the colleague right, and why?

- A) Yes — user stories must never mention organisations.
- B) Partly — the format is arguable, but the real problem is that story 3 describes an organisational need, which is valid to capture, though many teams would express it as a separate business or compliance story rather than mixing it into the user's journey narrative.
- C) No — all four stories are equally well formed.
- D) Yes — story 3 should be deleted because reporting duties are not user needs.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This question tests calibrated judgement, not rule-recitation. Story 3 follows the syntactic "As a… I can… so that…" template, so it is not malformed in a mechanical sense — which rules out a simple "yes". But it changes actor mid-map: stories 1, 2 and 4 narrate the angler's journey, while 3 states an organisational obligation. Government services must deliver policy intent as well as user goals, so the need is real and must be captured — deleting it (D) would lose a genuine constraint, and banning organisations from stories outright (A) is dogma, not craft. The mature move, reflected in B, is to keep the need but house it correctly, so the journey map stays a journey and compliance needs stay visible as acceptance criteria or parallel stories. As someone who writes and maps user stories and reviews others' work, your feedback to the junior colleague should model exactly this: affirm the instinct (something is off), correct the diagnosis, and show the fix.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Deduction under conflicting instructions**

Three sources conflict. The style guide says FAQs must not be used. A director has emailed: "The public keep asking the same six questions — put up an FAQ page this week." User research shows users are asking those six questions because the guidance page buries the answers below unrelated content. Which conclusion follows most strongly from the three sources taken together?

- A) The director's instruction overrides the style guide, so publish the FAQ page.
- B) The style guide overrides the director, so refuse and do nothing else.
- C) The research explains the questions: the guidance page fails to surface answers users need. Restructuring that page addresses the director's underlying goal without breaching the style guide.
- D) The research is irrelevant because a decision-maker has already decided.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Treat this as a logic problem before a politics problem. The director's goal is not literally "an FAQ page exists"; it is "the public stop asking us these six questions". The research identifies the mechanism generating the questions — answers buried below unrelated content — and therefore identifies the intervention that removes the cause rather than adding a workaround. C is the only option that satisfies all three sources: the questions get answered (director), the style guide holds (standards), and the fix follows the evidence (research). A satisfies the instruction but not the goal, and creates a second place for answers to go stale. B treats compliance as the end rather than the means and leaves the users' problem intact. D abandons evidence-based practice. In your role this pattern recurs constantly, and the deduction is the easy half — the influencing conversation it sets up appears again in the situational judgement section.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Applied problem solving at strategy level**

You are leading a strategic content improvement project across 400 pages of business guidance. Baseline data: 60 pages generate 80% of all traffic; 200 pages have not been updated in over 3 years; 25 pages have failed their accessibility audit; the 60 high-traffic pages include 10 of the 25 accessibility failures. Your sponsor funds a first phase covering at most 70 pages. Which scoping is most defensible?

- A) The 200 outdated pages — age is the biggest category of problem.
- B) All 25 accessibility failures plus the 45 highest-traffic remaining pages.
- C) The 60 high-traffic pages only — maximum reach per page improved.
- D) A random sample of 70 pages to avoid bias.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Rank the harms. Accessibility failures are legal and ethical obligations affecting real users now, so all 25 belong in phase one — including the 15 that sit outside the high-traffic set, because a user's right to accessible content does not depend on how popular the page is. That leaves 45 slots, best spent where improvements reach the most users: the highest-traffic pages not already covered. Option C fixes only 10 of 25 accessibility failures, leaving known exclusion in place. Option A treats age as harm, but an old page can be accurate and clear — staleness is a risk indicator, not an impact measure. Option D confuses statistical sampling (a research technique) with prioritisation (a delivery decision). The arithmetic of focus — obligations first, then reach — is your **Strategic thinking** skill made concrete: focusing effort in the areas of greatest priority, and being able to show your sponsor exactly why these 70 pages and not another 70.

### Preparation tips

- **Practise with your own artefacts.** Take a real journey you have worked on and quiz yourself: which screens repeat which facts? What breaks if the eligibility rule changes? Cognitive assessments reward the mental moves you already make daily — rehearse them consciously.
- **Do a timed 2i review.** Set 15 minutes, take an unfamiliar page, and list every issue in priority order. This trains error checking and prioritisation simultaneously, under the same time pressure the assessment applies.
- **Read the question stem twice, options once.** Most cognitive errors come from answering the question you expected rather than the question asked — the same skimming error you coach junior designers out of.
- **Deduce only what is guaranteed.** When a question asks what "must be true", strike out every option that is merely plausible. Your fact-checking discipline is precisely this skill.
- **Work forwards through dependencies.** For sequencing questions, build the timeline step by step from the earliest fixed point, as you would when planning a sprint with a researcher's recruitment lead times.
- **Keep moving.** Adaptive tests get harder as you succeed. If a question feels unusually difficult, that is often evidence you are doing well, not badly — answer your best and continue.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Anchoring on the first error found.** In journey-checking questions (and real content audits), people find one problem and stop looking. Train yourself to complete the sweep: every screen, every repetition of the fact.
- **Importing knowledge the question did not give you.** You know a great deal about government content standards; assessments test reasoning from the stated rules only. If the stem's style guide differs from GOV.UK's, the stem wins.
- **Confusing plausibility with necessity.** "The junior designer doesn't know the style guide" is plausible; it is not deducible. Options that explain rather than follow are the classic trap.
- **Over-weighting seniority in prioritisation items.** A director's undated request does not automatically outrank a publication deadline. Rank by impact and urgency, not hierarchy.
- **Dismissing qualitative signals for being small.** Five usability sessions converging on one blocker is strong evidence. Applying survey-scale sample-size instincts to usability data is a recognisable senior-level error.
- **Perfectionism under the clock.** You are trained to polish. In a timed assessment, a sure answer delivered beats an elegant answer half-finished — triage, as on publication day.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures your ability to interpret, manipulate, and draw sound conclusions from numerical information of the kind your role actually generates. It is not a mathematics exam: the arithmetic rarely goes beyond percentages, ratios, averages, and rates. The challenge is applying that arithmetic accurately, quickly, and sceptically to workplace data — in your case, page analytics, completion and drop-off rates, usability testing results, search-term volumes, readability measures, support contact statistics, and content audit figures.

Typical format: an online, timed test of 20 to 30 minutes with 15 to 25 questions, each built around a table, chart, or short data description. Calculators are usually permitted — the test measures reasoning, not mental arithmetic. Questions typically ask you to extract the right figures, perform one or two operations, and select the correct conclusion from close-looking options. Distractors are engineered from predictable mistakes: using the wrong denominator, confusing percentage points with percentages, averaging averages, or reading the wrong row. Adaptive scoring is common, and employers typically see accuracy, speed, and sometimes a comparison against a professional norm group.

For a senior content designer, numeric reasoning is core professional equipment, not an add-on. Your capability framework expects you to evaluate quantitative and qualitative data, collaborate with performance analysts, and make decisions based on findings while clearly explaining how those decisions were made. In practice that means reading a dashboard and knowing whether a change is signal or noise, converting a stakeholder's "engagement is up" into a precise claim you can verify, sizing a content problem before committing team effort to it, and presenting numbers honestly when you lead cross-government projects and report progress. Content designers who cannot interrogate numbers end up outsourcing their decisions to whoever can; this assessment measures your independence.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Percentage and rate calculations** map to **User focus (content design)**: evaluating quantitative data means fluently handling completion rates, drop-off rates, task success rates in usability testing, and changes in each over time.
- **Interpreting tables and charts** maps to **User focus (content design)** and your collaboration with performance analysts: dashboards, analytics extracts, and research summaries arrive as tables, and advocating effectively for users means reading them accurately before you argue from them.
- **Trend and comparison reasoning** maps to **Strategic thinking (content design)**: leading strategic content improvement projects and evaluating their impact and progress requires before-and-after comparisons, baselines, and honest attribution.
- **Proportional reasoning about effort and scope** maps to **Agile working (content design)** and **User-centred content design**: planning iterative delivery across a large content estate — how many pages, how many days, what share of the problem each phase addresses — is ratio work.
- **Sceptical checking of others' figures** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management (content design)**: when a stakeholder's slide claims a number that does not withstand arithmetic, you need the calculation at your fingertips to challenge constructively and keep the discussion evidence-based.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Completion rate**

Last month, 12,400 users started an online application journey you own, and 9,300 completed it. What was the completion rate?

- A) 70%
- B) 75%
- C) 80%
- D) 93%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Completion rate = completions ÷ starts × 100. Step by step: 9,300 ÷ 12,400 = 0.75. Multiply by 100: 75%. A quick sanity check: 75% of 12,400 = 12,400 × 0.75 = 9,300 ✓. Distractor D preys on digit-matching ("9,300 looks like 93%") — the classic wrong-denominator reflex. Completion rate is probably the single number you quote most often when arguing for or reporting on journey fixes, so this calculation should be automatic.

**Question 2 (easy) — Percentage change in support contacts**

After you rewrote a guidance page, related calls to the contact centre fell from 1,150 per week to 920 per week. What was the percentage reduction?

- A) 15%
- B) 20%
- C) 23%
- D) 25%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Reduction = 1,150 − 920 = 230 calls. Percentage reduction = change ÷ original × 100 = 230 ÷ 1,150 = 0.20 → 20%. The critical discipline is dividing by the original figure (1,150), not the new one: 230 ÷ 920 = 25%, which is distractor D. When you report the impact of a strategic content improvement, this is exactly the number a sponsor will quote onwards — getting it right protects your credibility, and knowing the two candidate denominators lets you spot when someone else has inflated their own result.

**Question 3 (easy) — Reading a usability testing table**

A usability round produced these task results:

| Task | Participants attempting | Participants succeeding |
|---|---|---|
| Find eligibility rules | 8 | 6 |
| Upload evidence | 8 | 4 |
| Check application status | 6 | 6 |

Which task had the lowest success rate?

- A) Find eligibility rules
- B) Upload evidence
- C) Check application status
- D) They cannot be compared because attempt numbers differ

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Convert each to a rate: eligibility 6 ÷ 8 = 75%; upload 4 ÷ 8 = 50%; status 6 ÷ 6 = 100%. Upload evidence is lowest at 50%. Option D is a half-remembered statistical scruple: differing attempt numbers are exactly why you convert to rates before comparing — that conversion is the comparison method, not a barrier to it. Reading small research tables like this accurately, and in seconds, is what lets you walk out of a research debrief already knowing where the journey fix belongs.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Percentage points versus percentages**

Task success on a journey rose from 60% to 69% after your content changes. A colleague drafts the show-and-tell slide: "Success improved by 9%." Is the slide accurate?

- A) Yes — 69 minus 60 is 9, so 9%.
- B) No — success improved by 9 percentage points, which is a 15% relative improvement.
- C) No — success improved by 6 percentage points.
- D) Yes — percentages and percentage points are interchangeable.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The absolute change is 69 − 60 = 9 percentage points. The relative change is 9 ÷ 60 = 0.15 → 15%. So "improved by 9%" understates the relative improvement and mislabels the absolute one. The precise sentence is: "Success rose 9 percentage points, from 60% to 69% — a 15% improvement." This distinction is a favourite of numeric assessments because professionals genuinely confuse it, and it matters doubly for you: your role makes you responsible for clear, accurate communication, so a mislabelled statistic on your team's slide is both a numeracy error and a content quality error. Catching it kindly in review is your job on two counts.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Weighted average across content types**

Your content audit covers 300 pages: 200 transactional pages with an average quality score of 82, and 100 guidance pages with an average quality score of 64. What is the average quality score across all 300 pages?

- A) 73
- B) 76
- C) 78
- D) 80

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** You cannot simply average 82 and 64 (which gives 73, distractor A) because the groups differ in size. Weight by page count: (200 × 82) + (100 × 64) = 16,400 + 6,400 = 22,800. Divide by total pages: 22,800 ÷ 300 = 76. The unweighted average understates the estate's quality because it over-represents the smaller, weaker group. Averaging averages without weighting is among the most common dashboard errors you will encounter in stakeholder decks, and audit-wide summary scores are exactly what you report when leading a strategic content improvement project — so this pattern is worth over-learning.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Search-term analysis**

Internal site search logs show 45,000 searches last quarter. The top terms: "renew licence" 9,000; "contact" 6,750; "forms" 4,500; all other terms 24,750. What percentage of all searches were for the top three terms combined?

- A) 40%
- B) 45%
- C) 50%
- D) 55%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Top three combined = 9,000 + 6,750 + 4,500 = 20,250. Percentage = 20,250 ÷ 45,000 = 0.45 → 45%. Cross-check: the remainder is 24,750, and 24,750 ÷ 45,000 = 55%, and 45% + 55% = 100% ✓. Search-log analysis is a standard input when you decide where content is failing to surface: nearly half of all searches concentrating on three needs tells you those needs are prominent in users' minds — and possibly insufficiently prominent in your navigation. The arithmetic is simple; the professional skill is doing it before deciding what the numbers license you to conclude.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Reading-time budget for a review round**

You must 2i-review 18 pieces of content before Friday. A short piece takes you 20 minutes to review and a long piece takes 50 minutes. Of the 18 pieces, 10 are short and 8 are long. You can dedicate 2 hours per day to reviewing. How many working days do you need?

- A) 4 days
- B) 5 days
- C) 6 days
- D) 7 days

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Total review time = (10 × 20) + (8 × 50) = 200 + 400 = 600 minutes. Convert your daily capacity: 2 hours = 120 minutes. Days needed = 600 ÷ 120 = 5 days exactly. The common error is estimating from the average piece (600 ÷ 18 ≈ 33 minutes) and then mis-multiplying, or forgetting to convert hours to minutes and dividing 600 by 2. Capacity arithmetic like this underpins honest sprint commitments: when you tell a delivery manager the review workload fits — or does not fit — before Friday, this five-line calculation is what makes your answer trustworthy rather than hopeful.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Ratio reasoning in research recruitment**

For an accessibility-focused research round you need participants in the ratio 2 screen-reader users : 3 screen-magnifier users : 5 participants with no assistive technology. The researcher can recruit 30 participants in total. How many screen-magnifier users should be recruited?

- A) 6
- B) 9
- C) 12
- D) 15

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The ratio 2:3:5 has 2 + 3 + 5 = 10 parts. Each part = 30 ÷ 10 = 3 participants. Screen-magnifier users = 3 parts × 3 = 9. (Check: screen-reader 2 × 3 = 6, no-AT 5 × 3 = 15; 6 + 9 + 15 = 30 ✓.) Distractors are the other groups' counts, punishing a right method attached to the wrong row — so re-read which group the question asks about before answering. Putting accessibility requirements at the heart of your approach includes the unglamorous arithmetic of making sure assistive-technology users are properly represented in the sample your decisions will rest on.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Baseline comparison with seasonality**

Page views for a tax-credits guidance page: this January 80,000; this February 60,000. Last year: January 82,000; February 61,500. A stakeholder says: "Views dropped 25% from January to February — your rewrite in late January damaged the page." What does the data actually support?

- A) The stakeholder is right — a 25% drop is a 25% drop.
- B) The January-to-February drop almost exactly matches last year's seasonal pattern (a fall of about 25%), so the data shows seasonality, not damage from the rewrite.
- C) The rewrite improved the page, because February views are only slightly below last February.
- D) The data proves nothing about anything.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Compute both years' seasonal drops. This year: (80,000 − 60,000) ÷ 80,000 = 20,000 ÷ 80,000 = 25%. Last year: (82,000 − 61,500) ÷ 82,000 = 20,500 ÷ 82,000 = 25%. The drop is identical in percentage terms across both years — a seasonal pattern (tax-credit interest falling after January) that occurred before the rewrite existed. So the stakeholder's causal claim collapses: the rewrite coincided with, rather than caused, the fall. C overreaches in the opposite direction — matching the seasonal baseline is evidence of no damage, not proof of improvement. D is lazy scepticism: comparative baselines are exactly how page data does support conclusions. Year-on-year comparison is the single most useful defensive move when a stakeholder reads a seasonal dip as your fault — and delivering it calmly, with the arithmetic shown, is stakeholder management through numeracy.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Cost per contact avoided**

A strategic content project costing £24,000 in team time rewrote a cluster of pages. In the six months after launch, related contact-centre calls fell from 5,200 to 3,700 per month, and the analysts attribute 80% of the reduction to the content changes. Each call costs the department £6. What is the approximate payback period for the project?

- A) About 1.4 months
- B) About 2.8 months
- C) About 3.3 months
- D) About 6 months

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Step 1: monthly call reduction = 5,200 − 3,700 = 1,500 calls. Step 2: attribute 80% to content: 1,500 × 0.8 = 1,200 calls per month. Step 3: monthly saving = 1,200 × £6 = £7,200. Step 4: payback = £24,000 ÷ £7,200 = 3.33 months. Distractor B comes from skipping the attribution step (1,500 × £6 = £9,000; 24,000 ÷ 9,000 ≈ 2.7); distractor A from also misreading the cost. The attribution step is the one senior people are expected not to skip: claiming the whole reduction when analysts attribute 80% is precisely the over-claim that gets challenged in a benefits review, and conceding it in advance makes your business case stronger, not weaker. This is the arithmetic that turns content design from a cost into an investment in your sponsor's eyes.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Sample size and observed difference**

An A/B test of two versions of a start-page shows: version A, 4,000 users, 62.0% continued; version B, 4,000 users, 62.4% continued. Your performance analyst says the difference is not statistically significant. The product manager wants to declare B the winner and move on. What is the numerically sound position?

- A) B won — 62.4% is higher than 62.0%, and numbers do not lie.
- B) The observed difference is 0.4 percentage points and the analyst has assessed it as within the range expected by chance; the test has not demonstrated a winner, so decide on other grounds or test a bolder variation.
- C) A won — the older version deserves the benefit of the doubt.
- D) Run the same test repeatedly until it reaches significance.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The raw difference is 62.4 − 62.0 = 0.4 percentage points — a relative difference of well under 1% — and the analyst, whose job this judgement is, has assessed it as consistent with chance variation. Declaring a winner (A or C) asserts a conclusion the evidence does not support in either direction. D is a known malpractice: repeating a test until noise crosses a threshold manufactures false positives. B reflects both sound numeracy and your framework's expectation that you understand how different types of evidence are used and collaborate with performance analysts rather than overruling them. The senior-level insight in the final clause matters too: a test that cannot separate two variants often means the variants were too similar — the craft response is a bolder content hypothesis, not a louder claim.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Multi-step audit planning**

Your content estate has 480 pages. An audit finds 35% need minor edits (average 1 hour each), 25% need substantial rewrites (average 4 hours each), 10% should be removed (average 30 minutes each including redirects), and the remainder need no action. Your team can commit 60 hours per week to this work. Approximately how many weeks will the programme take?

- A) About 8 weeks
- B) About 11 weeks
- C) About 13 weeks
- D) About 16 weeks

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Compute each workstream. Minor edits: 480 × 0.35 = 168 pages × 1 hour = 168 hours. Rewrites: 480 × 0.25 = 120 pages × 4 hours = 480 hours. Removals: 480 × 0.10 = 48 pages × 0.5 hours = 24 hours. (No-action pages: 30%, zero hours.) Total = 168 + 480 + 24 = 672 hours. Weeks = 672 ÷ 60 = 11.2 → about 11 weeks. The rewrites dominate — 480 of 672 hours, or 71% of the effort from only 25% of the pages — which is itself the strategic insight: if the sponsor needs the programme shorter, the negotiation is about the rewrite list, not the minor edits. Presenting a plan this way — total, breakdown, and the lever that changes it — is what your framework means by leading strategic improvement projects and clearly explaining how decisions have been made.

### Preparation tips

- **Rebuild your own dashboard numbers by hand.** Take last month's figures for a journey you own and recompute the completion rate, the month-on-month change, and the year-on-year change. Practising on familiar data builds speed without the anxiety of novelty.
- **Write the formula before the numbers.** For each question, jot the relationship first (change ÷ original; parts of ratio; weighted sum ÷ total). Most errors are structural, not arithmetical — the calculator cannot save you from the wrong denominator.
- **Master the percentage-point distinction.** Rehearse saying "from 60% to 69% is 9 percentage points and a 15% improvement" until it is reflexive. It appears in almost every numeric test and in almost every performance report you will ever write.
- **Sanity-check against round numbers.** Before selecting an answer, ask whether it is plausible: a 75% completion rate from 12,400 starts must be near 9,300. Estimation catches keying errors that exactness misses.
- **Read the question's last line first.** Tables contain more numbers than you need. Knowing what is being asked — which group, which period, which rate — tells you which cells matter and which are decoration.
- **Time-box each question.** Roughly a minute each: if a question resists after ninety seconds, select your best estimate, flag it if the platform allows, and bank the remaining questions. Accuracy on twenty questions beats perfection on twelve.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **The wrong denominator.** Percentage change divides by the original value; completion divides by starts, not visits; success rates divide by attempts. Denominator discipline is the single highest-value habit in numeric testing.
- **Averaging averages.** Whenever groups differ in size, weight before averaging. Unweighted averages of page scores, task rates, or team figures are the most common error in real content reporting too.
- **Digit-matching.** Options are engineered so that a number visible in the table (like 9,300 → 93%) appears among the answers. If you did no arithmetic, you have not answered the question.
- **Accepting causal claims from raw drops.** "Views fell after your rewrite" needs a baseline comparison before it needs a defence. In tests and in meetings, compute the counterfactual first.
- **Skipping the attribution step.** When only part of an improvement is attributable to your work, claiming all of it is an error in tests and a credibility risk in benefit reports.
- **Calculator overconfidence.** The calculator executes your plan; it does not check it. Keying 230 ÷ 920 instead of 230 ÷ 1,150 produces a confident, precise, wrong answer.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately you understand, analyse, and draw conclusions from written material of the kind your role handles daily. The standard format presents a short passage — a policy extract, a standard, an email, a research summary, a set of guidelines — followed by statements you must classify as True (the passage supports it), False (the passage contradicts it), or Cannot Say (the passage provides insufficient information either way). Variants ask you to identify the best summary, the valid inference, the author's purpose, or the clearest rewrite of a sentence.

Typical format: online and timed, usually 15 to 25 minutes for 20 to 30 items, with the time pressure deliberately preventing leisurely re-reading. Scoring is against a norm group, and adaptive difficulty is common. The discipline being measured is precision: reasoning strictly from the passage in front of you, not from background knowledge, however expert.

There is a pleasing irony in a content designer sitting a verbal reasoning test, and it is worth taking seriously rather than smugly: you are a professional reader and writer, which gives you real advantages — you parse sentence structure fast, you notice qualifiers, you know how meaning shifts with word order — but it also gives you a specific vulnerability. You know so much about government content, standards, and users that your expertise volunteers answers the passage never gave. Senior reviewers fail "Cannot Say" items more than junior ones for exactly this reason. For your role, the assessment simulates the moments that matter most: reading a policy instruction precisely enough that your content neither overstates nor understates the rule; reading a research report precisely enough to know what it found versus what it suggests; and reading a stakeholder's email precisely enough to respond to what was actually said.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Reading comprehension of dense source material** maps to **User-centred content design**: making things easier for people to understand starts with understanding the source perfectly yourself — you cannot simplify what you have not precisely grasped, and a misreading at your desk becomes a misleading page for millions.
- **True/False/Cannot Say discipline** maps to **User focus (content design)**: gaining insights from research and making decisions based on findings requires distinguishing what the evidence establishes from what it merely suggests — the exact distinction the format tests.
- **Inference and argument evaluation** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management (content design)**: influencing decisions and dealing with challenging situations means hearing the argument a stakeholder is actually making, locating its load-bearing claim, and responding to that rather than to a caricature.
- **Clear-writing judgement** maps to **Content concepts and prototyping** and your responsibility for reviewing the work of others: choosing the clearest formulation among close alternatives is a 2i review in miniature.
- **Summarising accurately** maps to **Agile working (content design)**: keeping a team situationally aware of one another's work and how it relates to objectives depends on faithful compression — user stories, show-and-tell narratives, and sprint summaries are all summaries with consequences.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — True/False/Cannot Say on a standard**

Passage: "The service standard requires teams to make sure everyone can use the service, including people with disabilities and those who lack access to the internet or the skills or confidence to use it. Teams must provide appropriate assisted digital support that is free to use."

Statement: "The standard requires assisted digital support to be free to use."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The final sentence states directly that teams "must provide appropriate assisted digital support that is free to use." The statement restates this without addition or distortion, so it is True. Easy items like this are calibration: they teach you what full support looks like — an explicit sentence in the passage — so that later, when a statement is merely consistent with the passage rather than stated by it, you feel the difference. That felt difference is the core skill of the entire test format.

**Question 2 (easy) — True/False/Cannot Say on a policy extract**

Passage: "From April, applicants must provide two proofs of address. Applications submitted without the required documents will be placed on hold until the documents are received. Applications on hold for more than 60 days will be closed."

Statement: "Applications submitted without two proofs of address are immediately closed."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage describes a two-stage process: incomplete applications are first placed on hold, and closure happens only after more than 60 days on hold. "Immediately closed" contradicts the stated sequence, so the statement is False. Notice that the statement is False rather than Cannot Say because the passage directly supplies the contrary fact — the hold stage. Reading procedural sequences precisely is everyday work when you turn policy instructions into user-facing content: the difference between "your application will be closed" and "your application will be put on hold for up to 60 days" is the difference between panic and a to-do list for the user.

**Question 3 (easy) — Identifying the clearest sentence**

Which sentence most clearly tells users what to do?

- A) "It is a requirement that the completion of the declaration form be undertaken by applicants prior to submission."
- B) "Applicants are advised that declarations, once completed, facilitate submission."
- C) "You must complete the declaration form before you submit your application."
- D) "Prior to the submission process, the aforementioned declaration documentation should be completed."

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** C addresses the user directly ("you"), uses an active verb ("complete"), states the obligation plainly ("must"), and puts the actions in chronological order. A buries the action in nominalisations ("the completion… be undertaken"); B is vague about whether the declaration is required at all ("advised", "facilitate"); D combines passive voice, "aforementioned", and "should" — which weakens a mandatory step into a suggestion. In a verbal test this item type is quick marks for you; in your role it is the judgement you exercise dozens of times a day and teach through every review comment. The transferable checklist: direct address, active voice, accurate modal verb, chronological order.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Cannot Say discipline against expertise**

Passage: "The department's style guidance recommends sentences of no more than 25 words for body text. Guidance pages reviewed this quarter averaged 21 words per sentence."

Statement: "The department's guidance pages this quarter met the accessibility requirements of WCAG."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage discusses sentence-length guidance and an average measurement. It says nothing about WCAG or accessibility requirements — which cover far more than sentence length. You know, as an expert, that short sentences support accessibility; the passage does not say so, and expertise is exactly what this item is designed to exploit. The statement is neither supported nor contradicted: Cannot Say. This is the single most instructive item type for a professional reader. The rule is austere: if the passage does not assert it and does not entail it, you cannot say it — no matter how true it happens to be in the world. The same austerity is what makes your evidence summaries trustworthy: "the research showed X" must never quietly become "the research showed X, which means Y".

**Question 5 (moderate) — Inference from a research summary**

Passage: "In moderated testing, 7 of 9 participants completed the renewal journey. Both participants who failed did so at the identity verification step. Four participants described the confirmation email as 'reassuring'. No participant used the help link."

Which inference is best supported?

- A) The identity verification step caused the most severe completion failures observed in this round.
- B) The help link is unnecessary and should be removed.
- C) The confirmation email is the journey's greatest strength.
- D) 78% of all users will complete the journey.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Both observed failures occurred at identity verification, so within this round it was the site of the only — and therefore the most severe — completion failures: A stays inside the data. B overreaches: non-use in nine sessions does not establish that no user ever needs the link, and removal is a design decision requiring more evidence. C converts four positive comments into a superlative the passage cannot support ("greatest strength" compared to what?). D commits the classic generalisation error: 7 of 9 is 78% of this sample, not a population prediction — small-sample usability testing identifies problems; it does not estimate rates. Your framework asks you to understand what different types of research can and cannot tell you at each stage; this item is that understanding in test form.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Author's purpose in a stakeholder email**

Email extract: "Thanks for the proposed rewrite. I recognise the current page is long, and your user research is compelling. However, the legal position on the discretionary payments is genuinely finely balanced, and the two paragraphs you propose removing are the ones our lawyers drafted after the tribunal ruling last year. Could we meet before anything is changed?"

What is the writer primarily doing?

- A) Rejecting the rewrite outright.
- B) Accepting the rewrite subject to minor edits.
- C) Flagging a specific legal risk in the proposed cuts and asking to discuss before publication.
- D) Questioning the validity of the user research.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Map the email's moves: it concedes the page's length, praises the research ("compelling" — which eliminates D), then pivots on "however" to one specific concern — the two legally drafted paragraphs — and closes with a request to meet "before anything is changed". That is neither rejection (A: the writer asks to meet, not to stop) nor acceptance (B: nothing is agreed). Reading stakeholder correspondence at this resolution is the foundation of your **Stakeholder relationship management** skill: the designer who hears "rejection" becomes defensive; the designer who hears "specific risk, open door" arrives at the meeting with a proposal that preserves the legal substance in plainer language — and usually wins.

**Question 7 (moderate) — True/False/Cannot Say on data ethics guidance**

Passage: "Research participants must give informed consent before sessions are recorded. Consent may be withdrawn at any time, including after the session has taken place. Where consent is withdrawn, recordings must be deleted within 30 days."

Statement: "A participant who gave informed consent before a recorded session cannot later require the recording to be deleted."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage states that consent may be withdrawn at any time, "including after the session has taken place", and that withdrawal triggers deletion within 30 days. So a participant can later require deletion (by withdrawing consent) — the statement asserts the opposite and is False. The trap is the statement's confident legalistic tone, which invites agreement by sounding like a rule. Assess the proposition, not its register. Content designers meet this trap constantly in the wild: confident phrasing in a stakeholder's draft is not evidence of accuracy, and your quality assurance role means being the person who checks the claim beneath the cadence.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Best summary of a passage**

Passage: "The channel-shift programme aimed to move 60% of licence renewals online within two years. Eighteen months in, 52% of renewals are online. Telephone renewals have halved, but paper renewals have barely moved, remaining near 20%. Analysis suggests paper users are disproportionately older and more likely to live in areas with poor connectivity. The programme board has asked for options rather than an extension of the current approach."

Which is the best summary?

- A) The programme has failed and will be cancelled.
- B) Online renewals are ahead of schedule, so the target will certainly be met.
- C) Progress is substantial but has come mainly from telephone users; paper use persists among older and poorly connected users, and the board wants a changed approach.
- D) Older people cannot use online services.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A good summary preserves the passage's structure: overall progress (52% against 60% with six months left), its composition (telephone halved, paper static), the explanatory analysis (age and connectivity), and the board's request (options, not more of the same). C captures all four. A invents failure and cancellation. B converts progress into certainty the passage does not offer — 52% at month eighteen does not guarantee 60% at month twenty-four, especially when the remaining users are the hardest to shift. D turns a statistical association into a capability claim about a whole group — a distortion with real inclusion consequences. Faithful compression is a daily senior-level act: your board papers, user stories, and community talks all summarise, and every distortion you introduce propagates downstream with your name on it.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Argument evaluation**

A stakeholder argues: "We should publish the full 40-page policy document as one web page. Users complain they cannot find answers, and search engines index long pages well. Splitting content into many short pages just means more clicks. Therefore one long page will solve the findability problem."

Which is the strongest criticism of this argument?

- A) Policy documents should never be published online.
- B) The argument assumes findability fails because content is split across pages, but the complaints are equally consistent with poor structure, headings, or titling — and one 40-page page could worsen within-page findability even if the page itself is easy to find.
- C) Search engines are not important for government content.
- D) Users never complain about findability.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The argument's load-bearing assumption is a diagnosis: that page-splitting causes the findability failure. B attacks exactly that joint — the evidence offered ("users cannot find answers") does not discriminate between the stakeholder's diagnosis and alternatives (bad titles, weak headings, missing internal search terms), and the proposed cure could relocate the problem from "finding the page" to "finding the answer within 40 pages of scrolling". A, C and D each deny something either absurd or not required by the argument. The technique — find the inference the conclusion cannot survive without, and test whether the evidence really supports it — is how you evaluate stakeholder proposals respectfully: you are not dismissing the goal (findability), you are showing the evidence has not yet chosen a solution. That framing keeps influence and relationship intact simultaneously.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Precision with qualifiers**

Passage: "Most departments that piloted the new consent pattern reported fewer user errors. No department reported an increase in errors. Two departments have not yet reported."

Statement: "Every department that piloted the pattern reported fewer user errors."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Track the quantifiers carefully. "Most… reported fewer errors" plus "no department reported an increase" still leaves two possibilities for the remainder: departments reporting no change, and the two departments that have not reported at all. Either possibility falsifies "every department… reported fewer errors" — and the passage guarantees the second one exists: two departments have not yet reported, so it is false that every department reported fewer errors (they have not reported anything). The subtle point is that the statement fails on the reporting, not the errors. This is qualifier-tracking at the resolution assessments demand and at the resolution your fact-checking demands: "most", "no", "not yet" each carry precise load, and content that upgrades "most" to "all" — in either direction, test or web page — is misinformation with good intentions.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Inference across two sources**

Source 1 (policy team): "The discretionary fund is available to applicants who meet the hardship criteria. Decisions are made case by case; meeting the criteria does not guarantee an award."

Source 2 (current web page): "If you meet the hardship criteria, you will receive a payment from the discretionary fund."

Which statement about the web page is best supported?

- A) The web page is accurate as written.
- B) The web page overstates the entitlement: it converts case-by-case eligibility into a guarantee, and should say that those who meet the criteria can apply and may receive a payment.
- C) The web page understates the entitlement.
- D) The two sources do not address the same fund.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Align the claims. Source 1: meeting criteria → may receive (explicitly "does not guarantee"). Web page: meeting criteria → "you will receive" — a guarantee. The page therefore asserts more than the policy licenses: an overstatement, precisely diagnosed and corrected by B. C reverses the direction of the error. D manufactures doubt the sources do not support — both discuss "the discretionary fund" and "the hardship criteria". This two-source alignment is arguably the most consequential reading task in your entire role: user-facing content that overpromises discretionary payments creates appealable expectations, complaints, and genuine hurt for users who budget around a "will" that was really a "may". Verbal reasoning tests compress that professional stake into one item; your daily fact-checking is the extended version.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Clear-writing judgement with accuracy constraints**

The policy rule: applicants must report a change in circumstances within 30 days of the change, and late reporting can lead to a penalty unless there is good reason. Which rewrite is both clearest and accurate?

- A) "Report any change in your circumstances within 30 days of the change. If you report late without good reason, you may have to pay a penalty."
- B) "Report changes promptly. Late reporting leads to a penalty."
- C) "Failure to report changes of circumstances within the prescribed 30-day period may render applicants liable to penalties, absent reasonable justification."
- D) "You might want to report changes within about a month, or there could possibly be consequences in some cases."

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Test each against both clarity and the rule. A: direct address, active verbs, the concrete deadline (30 days, anchored to the change), the penalty as a possibility ("may"), and the good-reason exception preserved — accurate and clear. B is clear but inaccurate twice: "promptly" deletes the 30-day rule, and "leads to" converts a conditional penalty into an automatic one, erasing the good-reason safeguard. C is accurate but written in officialese — "render applicants liable", "absent reasonable justification" — that shifts all the work onto the reader. D is so hedged ("might", "about", "possibly", "in some cases") that it fails users who need to know the deadline is real. The senior-level lesson: clarity and accuracy are not rivals to be traded off; the professional standard is the sentence that achieves both, and in reviews you should decline drafts that sacrifice either.

### Preparation tips

- **Practise the three-way verdict daily.** Take any policy paragraph and write one statement it makes True, one it makes False, and one it leaves Cannot Say. Composing items teaches the boundaries faster than answering them.
- **Quarantine your expertise.** Before each passage, tell yourself: "Only this text exists." Your knowledge of GOV.UK standards is a liability in Cannot Say items — the test is of reading, not of memory.
- **Underline qualifiers and modals.** "Most", "may", "must", "unless", "not yet" carry the meaning that statements will probe. You already read this way when fact-checking; do it deliberately under time pressure.
- **For summary items, list the passage's moves.** Concession, pivot, evidence, request — a passage's best summary preserves its skeleton. This is your show-and-tell craft applied in reverse.
- **For argument items, find the load-bearing assumption.** Ask: what must be true for the conclusion to follow? The strongest criticism targets that, not a side detail.
- **Simulate real timing.** Do practice sets at one minute per item. Your professional reading is usually unhurried; the test's constraint is speed with precision, and that mix needs specific rehearsal.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Answering from the world instead of the passage.** The most common senior-level failure. If the passage does not say it or entail it, the answer is Cannot Say — even when you know it is true.
- **Confusing False with Cannot Say.** False requires the passage to contradict the statement; absence of support is not contradiction. Ask: does the text assert the opposite?
- **Trusting confident registers.** Legalistic or authoritative phrasing in a statement is rhetoric, not evidence. Assess the proposition as if it were written in crayon.
- **Upgrading quantifiers.** "Most" is not "all"; "may" is not "will"; "no increase" is not "a decrease". Both tests and user-facing content punish silent upgrades.
- **Summaries that improve on the passage.** Choosing the summary you wish the author had written — more balanced, more sensible — instead of the one they did write. Fidelity first.
- **Speed-reading the statement instead of the passage.** Under pressure, people skim the long text and study the short statement. Reverse it: most verdicts are decided by one clause in the passage, and only careful reading finds it.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment presents realistic workplace scenarios and asks you to evaluate possible responses — typically choosing the most effective and least effective actions, or ranking all options. There are no trick calculations here; the test measures professional judgement: how you balance user needs, stakeholder relationships, quality standards, team development, and delivery pressure when they conflict, which in a senior role is most weeks.

Typical format: 12 to 25 scenarios, often untimed or generously timed, scored against a key agreed by subject-matter experts and experienced practitioners. Some versions ask "what would you do?" (behavioural tendency); others ask "what is the best action?" (judgement). Read the instruction carefully — they are different questions. Scoring usually rewards responses that solve the immediate problem, address the underlying cause, and preserve or strengthen relationships, while penalising passivity, avoidance, unilateral aggression, and rule-breaking however well-intentioned.

For a senior content designer, situational judgement is arguably the most job-relevant assessment of the four. Your framework is dense with situational verbs: negotiate, influence, remove blockers, deal with challenging situations, mentor, coach, critique, champion, take responsibility. The scenarios below are drawn from the genuine pressure points of your level: a stakeholder demanding inaccurate content, a junior colleague's work needing honest critique, a cross-government project stalling, research evidence arriving at an inconvenient moment, accessibility being traded against a deadline, and the recurring senior dilemma of when to do the work yourself versus develop someone else by letting them do it. Each question names a most-effective and least-effective response, because real judgement involves recognising both what excellence looks like and what damage looks like.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Stakeholder scenarios** map to **Stakeholder relationship management (content design)**: negotiating, influencing decisions, dealing with challenging situations, and removing blockers while supporting mutual needs and keeping user needs central.
- **Quality and review scenarios** map to **User-centred content design**: taking responsibility for assuring the quality of junior colleagues' content while coaching and guiding them to improve — critique that raises both the content and the person.
- **Evidence scenarios** map to **User focus (content design)**: advocating effectively for users, championing research, making decisions from findings, and putting accessibility requirements at the heart of your approach even when it is inconvenient.
- **Delivery and team scenarios** map to **Agile working (content design)**: iterating under pressure, keeping the team situationally aware, taking sensible risks, and learning openly from mistakes.
- **Leadership and community scenarios** map to **Strategic thinking (content design)** and your duties to lead cross-government projects and contribute to the content community: focusing effort where priority is greatest and representing the discipline beyond your own team.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — A stakeholder wants promotional language**

A policy stakeholder asks you to open a guidance page with: "This world-leading, award-winning scheme demonstrates the department's commitment to excellence." User research shows users come to the page anxious, wanting to know if they qualify and what to do.

Which response is MOST effective, and which is LEAST effective?

- A) Add the sentence — the stakeholder owns the policy.
- B) Refuse by email, citing the style guide, and publish without the sentence.
- C) Share the research showing what users need first, propose an opening that answers eligibility immediately, and suggest where departmental achievements could live if needed elsewhere.
- D) Add the sentence now and quietly remove it in a future iteration.

**Correct answer: MOST effective: C. LEAST effective: D.**

**Explanation:** C does the three things strong situational answers do: it solves the content problem (an opening that serves anxious users), addresses the underlying cause (the stakeholder has a need for recognition that can be met without harming the page), and preserves the relationship (evidence and alternatives rather than refusal). This is textbook **Stakeholder relationship management** — influencing with research while supporting mutual needs. A capitulates and harms users. B may win the battle but spends relationship capital unnecessarily and teaches the stakeholder that content designers are blockers with rulebooks. D is least effective because it is dishonest in both directions: it publishes content you believe is wrong, then removes agreed content without consent — when discovered (it will be), it poisons trust in you and in the discipline you represent.

**Question 2 (easy) — A junior colleague's weak first draft**

A content designer you mentor shares their first solo attempt at a complex eligibility page. It has structural problems throughout, though the plain-language instincts are good. Their confidence is fragile, and the page is not due for two weeks.

Which response is MOST effective, and which is LEAST effective?

- A) Rewrite it yourself tonight so the team is not embarrassed, and send it back with "made a few tweaks".
- B) Book a session: start with what genuinely works (the plain-language instincts), then work through the structural issues together on a shared screen, and agree what they will revise before you review again.
- C) Return it with "needs restructuring — see the style guide" and let them work it out.
- D) Tell their line manager the work is below standard.

**Correct answer: MOST effective: B. LEAST effective: A.**

**Explanation:** B is coaching as your framework defines it: assuring quality while guiding a colleague to improve, with specific praise that is true, joint working that shows rather than tells, and a clear next step that keeps ownership with them. The two-week runway makes this the ideal development opportunity. C abdicates the coaching half of your responsibility — a pointer to the style guide is not guidance for someone who cannot yet see the structural problem. D escalates a routine development need into a performance conversation, damaging trust twice over. A is least effective despite feeling kind and efficient: it deceives the colleague about the state of their work ("a few tweaks"), teaches them nothing, ensures the next draft has the same problems, and quietly converts you from a mentor into a bottleneck. The senior designer's product is capable colleagues, not corrected pages.

**Question 3 (moderate) — Research contradicts your own design**

Usability testing results arrive for a content pattern you personally designed and championed across the programme. Four of six participants misunderstood the pattern's key interaction. The researcher is presenting the findings at tomorrow's show-and-tell, and a partner team is about to adopt the pattern.

Which response is MOST effective, and which is LEAST effective?

- A) Ask the researcher to soften the findings, since six participants is a small sample.
- B) Attend, thank the researcher publicly, flag the finding to the adopting team yourself before they hear it second-hand, and schedule an iteration of the pattern with follow-up testing.
- C) Say nothing and let the show-and-tell take its course; the pattern may still be adopted.
- D) Withdraw the pattern entirely and apologise to the programme.

**Correct answer: MOST effective: B. LEAST effective: A.**

**Explanation:** B models everything your framework asks: championing user research even when it bites you, being unafraid to learn from mistakes in public, keeping teams situationally aware (proactively telling the adopting team), and treating a pattern as your framework treats all patterns — something you establish, test, and iterate, not something you defend. Your personal authorship is precisely why your public response matters: junior designers will calibrate their own honesty on yours. C is passive and lets a known problem propagate to another team. D over-corrects — one round showing a fixable misunderstanding warrants iteration, not withdrawal, and dramatising the failure discourages future risk-taking. A is least effective and crosses an integrity line: pressuring a researcher to soften findings corrupts the evidence base every team relies on, misuses your seniority, and — once visible — destroys the thing your influence rests on, which is that your advocacy follows the evidence.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Accessibility versus deadline**

Two days before a high-profile service launches, an accessibility audit finds that error messages fail WCAG: they rely on colour alone and do not describe how to fix the problem. The service manager says: "Launch anyway — we'll fix it in the first sprint after. Delay is not an option; the minister has announced the date."

Which response is MOST effective, and which is LEAST effective?

- A) Accept the decision — the service manager owns the launch call.
- B) Size the fix first: error-message content is your craft, so establish with the developers today whether corrected messages can ship within the two days. Present the service manager with that option, and if it truly cannot be done, set out the user impact and legal risk in writing and ask for the deferred fix to be a committed, dated item, escalating if the risk is brushed aside.
- C) Refuse to be associated with the launch and copy the programme director.
- D) Post your concerns in the team channel and leave the decision to others.

**Correct answer: MOST effective: B. LEAST effective: A.**

**Explanation:** The senior move is to shrink the dilemma before contesting it: many "accessibility versus deadline" standoffs dissolve when someone who can actually write the corrected error messages — you — establishes the real cost of fixing them, which for message content is often hours, not weeks. B does that, and only then handles the residual case with proportionate escalation: impact and risk in writing, a committed remediation date, and upward escalation if dismissed — which honours your framework's demand to put accessibility requirements at the heart of your approach and encourage others to do the same. C escalates before attempting to solve, spending maximum capital on a problem not yet sized. D is visible passivity — a concern logged is not a user protected. A is least effective: quiet acceptance makes you complicit in launching a service that tells disabled users, at their moment of error, nothing they can perceive or act on — and "fix it next sprint" without your challenge routinely becomes "fix it never".

**Question 5 (moderate) — Cross-government project stalling**

You lead a cross-government project to align content patterns for a shared user journey spanning three departments. One department's representative has missed three consecutive working sessions, and their department's pages are drifting further from the agreed patterns. The project's success depends on all three departments.

Which response is MOST effective, and which is LEAST effective?

- A) Proceed with two departments and publish the patterns as "cross-government" anyway.
- B) Contact the representative directly and with curiosity: find out what is blocking their participation (capacity, mandate, disagreement), adjust the project's demands if possible, and if the blocker is above their level, agree together who in their department to approach.
- C) Email the representative's director to report the missed sessions.
- D) Wait — they will probably return when their workload eases.

**Correct answer: MOST effective: B. LEAST effective: C.**

**Explanation:** B diagnoses before acting, which is the heart of removing blockers: three misses is a signal of something — often a capacity or mandate problem the representative is embarrassed about — and the response that starts with curiosity usually recovers the relationship and the participation together. It also keeps escalation available but consensual ("agree together who to approach"), which preserves the representative's standing in their own department. A produces a claim ("cross-government") that the facts no longer support and plants a defection incentive in the alliance. D hopes, and drift compounds. C is least effective because it is escalation as a first resort, over the representative's head, without diagnosis: it converts a probably-recoverable colleague into a defensive one, teaches the community that your projects generate complaints to directors, and — since you lead this project and engage with the cross-government community — the reputational cost lands on the discipline, not just on you.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Conflicting feedback from two seniors**

On a high-profile page, the deputy director wants the key figures in a prominent table; the legal adviser wants the same figures embedded in careful prose with caveats attached to each number. Both have emailed you, copying each other, with incompatible instructions and a publication deadline on Friday.

Which response is MOST effective, and which is LEAST effective?

- A) Implement the deputy director's version — seniority decides.
- B) Implement the legal adviser's version — legal risk trumps presentation.
- C) Produce a quick prototype showing caveated figures in an accessible table — captions and footnotes carrying the legal qualifications — and convene both stakeholders briefly to agree it against user needs before Friday.
- D) Split the difference by publishing the table on one page and the prose on another, satisfying each email separately.

**Correct answer: MOST effective: C. LEAST effective: D.**

**Explanation:** The two instructions conflict as stated but their underlying needs — findable figures, legally accurate figures — are compatible, and C uses your **Content concepts and prototyping** skill to prove it: a concrete artefact turns an abstract standoff into a joint review, which is how senior designers arbitrate between stakeholders without picking a loser. It also keeps the user's need (scannable, accurate information) in the middle of the conversation. A and B each resolve the conflict by ignoring one legitimate need, and both make you the executor of a decision that was yours to design a way out of. D is least effective because it looks diplomatic but serves no one: users find either the caveat-free table (legal's risk realised) or the buried prose (the deputy director's concern realised), the two pages will drift out of sync, and neither stakeholder actually agreed to the arrangement — you have created future conflict and doubled the maintenance burden to avoid one meeting.

**Question 7 (moderate) — A pattern of missed accessibility in a team you influence**

Reviewing your team's recent work, you notice the same accessibility issues recurring across several designers' pages: undescriptive link text and images without meaningful alternative text. Each instance is small; the pattern is not.

Which response is MOST effective, and which is LEAST effective?

- A) Fix each instance yourself during 2i reviews — it is quicker than raising it.
- B) Raise the pattern at the team's next retrospective or crit: show the recurring issues without naming culprits, refresh the "why" with a short demonstration (for example, how a screen reader announces bad link text), agree a team checklist, and follow up in future reviews.
- C) Email the team a link to the accessibility guidance.
- D) Add "accessibility" as a standing agenda item and consider the matter handled.

**Correct answer: MOST effective: B. LEAST effective: A.**

**Explanation:** A recurring pattern across multiple designers is a capability gap, not a set of typos, and B treats it at the right level: your framework asks you to encourage a continuous improvement mindset in teams, encourage others to put accessibility at the heart of their approach, and coach rather than merely correct. The demonstration matters — designers who have heard a screen reader announce "click here, link" forty times rarely write it again — and the follow-up converts a good meeting into changed behaviour. C transmits information without attention and predictably changes nothing. D creates the appearance of action; agenda items do not teach. A is least effective even though every individual fix is correct: silent correction hides the feedback loop, guarantees the errors keep arriving, makes quality depend permanently on your personal vigilance, and deprives colleagues of learning they would want. At your level, fixing everything yourself is not diligence; it is a scaling failure.

**Question 8 (challenging) — Ministerial pressure to publish early**

A minister's office asks for guidance content on a new scheme to publish Monday, three days before the scheme's rules are finalised. The policy team says the rules are "95% settled" but two eligibility details could still change. Publishing wrong eligibility rules would cause users to make plans they may have to unmake; publishing nothing while the announcement lands leaves users searching and calling.

Which response is MOST effective, and which is LEAST effective?

- A) Publish full guidance Monday using the 95%-settled rules; update later if details change.
- B) Publish nothing until the rules are final — accuracy is absolute.
- C) Publish Monday, scoped to what is certain: what the scheme is, who it is broadly for, when full details and applications open, and what users can do now — explicitly excluding the two unsettled eligibility details, with a commitment date for the full guidance, agreed with the minister's office and policy team.
- D) Publish the full guidance with a disclaimer that details may change.

**Correct answer: MOST effective: C. LEAST effective: A.**

**Explanation:** This is the high-profile-events judgement your role level exists for, and C is the craft answer: scope the content to the certainty. Users searching after the announcement get real answers to their first questions (what is this, am I broadly in scope, when can I act), the department meets the moment, and nobody is told an eligibility rule that later reverses — the specific harm that matters most, because eligibility drives users' decisions. Note that C also manages upwards: it gives the minister's office a Monday publication, which is what they actually need. B protects accuracy by abandoning users to speculation and call centres — and positions content design as the discipline that says no. D publishes the harmful details anyway and hopes a disclaimer immunises them; users act on stated rules, not caveats. A is least effective because the 5% uncertainty sits exactly on the highest-consequence content: if either detail changes, users who acted on the guidance are harmed, corrections must chase the original through the press cycle, and the department's guidance loses the trust that took years to earn.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Your improvement, their credit**

At a programme board, a senior stakeholder presents the contact-reduction results of a content overhaul your team delivered, describing it as "the policy team's simplification work" without mentioning content design. Your team watched the presentation. The stakeholder is influential in next year's funding decisions, including the content team's headcount.

Which response is MOST effective, and which is LEAST effective?

- A) Interrupt during the meeting to correct the record.
- B) Say nothing — credit is vanity, and the users benefited either way.
- C) Afterwards, speak with the stakeholder privately and without accusation: welcome the shared success, note that the results came from the joint policy–content work, and agree how the collaboration is described in future — then make sure your team hears you name their work, and ensure the content contribution is visible in the programme's own reporting.
- D) Email the board members afterwards with evidence that your team did the work.

**Correct answer: MOST effective: C. LEAST effective: A.**

**Explanation:** Separate the three real stakes: your team's morale (they watched), the discipline's visibility in funding decisions (your framework asks you to build long-term strategic relationships and lead the discipline's case), and the stakeholder relationship itself. C addresses all three without manufacturing a conflict: private, generous framing ("joint work") lets the stakeholder adjust without losing face — which they usually will — while the team hears their work named and the record is corrected where it counts, in reporting that outlives any meeting. B mistakes strategic visibility for vanity: unattributed results become someone else's headcount case, and your silence teaches your team that their leader will not advocate for them. D corrects the record at maximum relational cost — a reply-all evidence dossier reads as an accusation and makes the stakeholder an adversary in the very funding conversations you need them for. A is least effective: a public interruption forces a senior stakeholder to choose between losing face and defending their version, in front of the board, with your team watching — the outcome is almost always worse standing for you, embarrassment for them, and a relationship problem that outlasts the credit question entirely.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Coaching versus delivery under pressure**

A critical page must be finished by tomorrow. The junior designer who owns it has produced a draft that needs significant rework; they are keen to finish it themselves and would learn a great deal from doing so. Realistically, their rework would take two days with your feedback; you could rewrite it yourself in three hours. There is no possibility of moving the deadline.

Which response is MOST effective, and which is LEAST effective?

- A) Let them finish it — development comes first, and the deadline will just have to absorb the delay.
- B) Rewrite it yourself tonight without discussion; explain afterwards if they ask.
- C) Pair with them for the three hours: you drive the rework at the pace the deadline requires, thinking aloud so the reasoning is visible; they contribute, question, and afterwards take ownership of the follow-up improvements — with a debrief on what changed and why.
- D) Give them written feedback tonight and let them attempt the rework overnight alone.

**Correct answer: MOST effective: C. LEAST effective: A.**

**Explanation:** This scenario tests whether you treat "deliver" and "develop" as a dilemma or a design problem. C refuses the false choice: pairing meets the immovable deadline (your pace), converts the rework into the richest available learning (visible expert reasoning on their own draft — better than any style-guide pointer), and protects their ownership going forward. This is your framework's "deliver through others, and direct and critique their work" under real constraint. D looks developmental but sets them up to fail alone overnight on work already assessed as two days deep — the deadline then fails and the learning is mostly distress. B hits the deadline at the cost of the person: excluded from their own page's rescue, they learn only that pressure means being displaced. A is least effective because it converts a leadership resource decision into a delivery failure: the deadline was stated as immovable, and a senior designer who lets a critical page miss its date to honour a development preference has prioritised one colleague's growth over users and the team's credibility — and taught the junior designer, unhelpfully, that deadlines bend to comfort. Development is the second-best thing you owe them; honest modelling of professional judgement is the first.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Community contribution versus team demands**

You have been asked to co-lead a three-month cross-government working group producing shared guidance patterns — significant, discipline-advancing work aligned with your role's community duties. The same week, your delivery manager warns that the team's next quarter is the heaviest yet and asks everyone to minimise external commitments.

Which response is MOST effective, and which is LEAST effective?

- A) Decline the working group — the team comes first, always.
- B) Accept without telling the delivery manager; you will manage your own diary.
- C) Discuss it openly with the delivery manager: quantify the real commitment, propose how to bound it (for example, a defined day per fortnight, or co-leading with a deputy who gains development from it), name the benefits flowing back to the team, and make the accept/decline decision together with eyes open.
- D) Accept, then quietly delegate all of the working-group work to a junior colleague.

**Correct answer: MOST effective: C. LEAST effective: B.**

**Explanation:** Your framework contains both duties — engage with and contribute to the cross-government content community, and work reliably within your team's Agile rhythm — so the judgement being tested is how you reconcile visible commitments, not which one you abandon. C treats capacity as a shared planning fact: quantified, bounded, negotiated, with the return benefits (patterns the team will reuse, discipline standing that protects future funding) made explicit, and the outcome co-owned. Note the deputy option inside C — turning your commitment into someone's development opportunity is a characteristically senior move. A is defensible but weaker than it looks: reflexively sacrificing community work keeps the discipline fragmented and is exactly the pattern that leaves content design under-represented across government. D takes credit for a commitment while transferring its load, unannounced, onto someone junior — a fairness failure and a quality risk. B is least effective because it breaks the specific thing Agile teams run on: situational awareness of one another's work and capacity. A hidden commitment surfaces as unexplained unavailability at the worst moment, and when discovered, converts a reasonable negotiation you could have won into a trust problem you cannot easily repair.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Discovering a factual error after publication**

Reviewing analytics on a busy page your team published three weeks ago, you notice the page states a fee as £115 when the correct figure is £151 — a transposition that survived 2i review. Roughly 40,000 users have viewed the page. Some may have budgeted on the wrong figure. The error is yours collectively; the reviewer was a designer you mentor.

Which response is MOST effective, and which is LEAST effective?

- A) Correct the page immediately and quietly; with luck, nobody noticed.
- B) Correct the page immediately; tell the service and policy teams the same day so contact-centre staff and caseworkers can handle affected users; assess with them whether users who acted on the wrong figure need proactive communication; then run a blameless review of how the error passed 2i and strengthen the check — treating the reviewer's involvement as a team process lesson, not an individual failing.
- C) Ask the junior reviewer to correct it and write up what they missed, as a learning exercise.
- D) Report the error and wait for the policy team to decide whether the page changes.

**Correct answer: MOST effective: B. LEAST effective: C.**

**Explanation:** B follows the full arc that ownership at your level requires: stop the harm (immediate correction — the page misleads someone every minute), manage the consequences (the teams facing affected users need to know today; whether 40,000 viewers warrant proactive correction is a judgement to make deliberately with policy colleagues, not by default), and fix the system (a blameless review that asks why the transposition survived, and adjusts the check — perhaps requiring figures to be verified against source documents rather than proof-read). The blameless framing is not softness; process errors recur until processes change, and a mentee who is made safe to examine their miss will become your most careful reviewer. A stops the harm but conceals it from the very teams currently fielding confused users — and quiet corrections on government pages, once noticed, read as cover-ups. D inverts accountability: the figure is wrong against the policy source that already exists; correcting it needs no permission, and waiting extends the harm. C is least effective because it converts a collective quality failure — writer, reviewer, and process all missed it — into an individual punishment dressed as development: the correction is delayed by handing it away, the systemic fix never happens, and the designer learns that errors near you end in public penance, which is precisely how teams learn to hide mistakes. Being unafraid to learn from mistakes starts with how the senior person behaves on the bad day.

### Preparation tips

- **Answer as your best professional self, consistently.** Situational tests measure judgement, not mind-reading. The version of you who has had enough sleep, holds the user's interest steady, and coaches generously is the one the scoring key rewards — and the one worth rehearsing into habit.
- **Look for the response that solves, addresses, and preserves.** The strongest options tend to solve the immediate problem, address its underlying cause, and preserve the relationships involved. Options achieving only one of the three are rarely "most effective".
- **Diagnose before you escalate; size before you fight.** Across scenarios, first-resort escalation and unexamined standoffs score badly. The senior pattern is: understand the blocker, shrink the dilemma with craft, then escalate proportionately with options in hand.
- **Rehearse your real repertoire.** Before the assessment, recall specific occasions when you influenced a stakeholder with evidence, coached someone through weak work, held an accessibility line, or owned a public mistake. Fresh memories calibrate judgement better than principles do.
- **Mind the altitude.** Senior candidates fail in two symmetrical ways: doing the junior's job (rewriting everything yourself) and over-escalating (sending upward what you are paid to resolve). Check each option against what your level owns.
- **Read whether the item asks "would" or "should".** "What would you do?" wants your honest tendency; "what is the best action?" wants your judgement of effectiveness. Answering one as the other costs marks.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Choosing the passive option.** Waiting, hoping, noting concerns, and posting in channels all delay action while harm continues. Even where escalation is right, active escalation with a proposal beats passive referral.
- **Choosing the heroic option.** Quietly rewriting, silently fixing, absorbing everything yourself — these feel virtuous and score poorly, because they hide problems, block learning, and do not scale. Your role is to deliver through others.
- **Treating honesty and diplomacy as alternatives.** The scoring key almost always rewards the option that is both candid and kind — private, specific, evidence-based, face-saving. Bluntness is not integrity; concealment is not tact.
- **Sacrificing accessibility or accuracy to be agreeable.** In UK public sector scenarios, options that trade user safety, inclusion, or factual accuracy for smooth relations are close to automatic "least effective" candidates — and correctly so.
- **Punishing individuals for system failures.** When an error passed a process, the strong option fixes the process blamelessly. Options that single out the junior person read as leadership failures to assessors, as they do to teams.
- **Optimising for blamelessness.** Covering emails, documented dissent, and decisions deferred upward protect you rather than users. Assessors — like the colleagues who will one day describe your leadership — can tell the difference.
