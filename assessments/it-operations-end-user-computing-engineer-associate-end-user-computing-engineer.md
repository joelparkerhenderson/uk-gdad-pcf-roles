# Associate End User Computing Engineer - Psychometric Assessment Resources

## Introduction

Welcome! This guide has been written especially for you as an associate end user computing engineer working within the UK Government Digital and Data profession. You are at the start of an exciting career in IT operations, and this document is here to help you practise, build confidence, and get comfortable with a type of exercise you may meet during your career: the psychometric assessment.

First, let's take the mystery out of the name. A psychometric assessment is simply a structured set of questions that measures the thinking skills and workplace judgement that help people succeed in a role. There are no trick questions designed to catch you out, and you do not need any special preparation beyond practice and a calm approach. The assessments in this guide are job-specific, which means they use the kinds of things you genuinely see every day: incident tickets, service requests, knowledge base articles, simple reports with Key Performance Indicators (KPIs), and conversations with the users you support. If you have ever registered an incident, gathered information from a user, passed a tricky problem to your team, or helped keep a laptop or printer working, you already have real experience to draw on.

Why do these assessments matter for your role in particular? As a trainee working in an established team, you support end user computing engineers in their daily work, under their direction. That work depends on a handful of core thinking skills: noticing patterns (is this the third user today reporting the same error?), following logical rules (which queue should this ticket go to?), checking details carefully (is this asset record correct?), working with simple numbers (how many tickets did we close this week, and is that better than last week?), reading instructions and messages accurately, and making sensible judgement calls (should I try this myself, or ask a colleague?). Psychometric assessments measure exactly these skills, so practising them helps you both in assessments and in your day-to-day work.

This document is organised into four main sections, one for each assessment type you are most likely to meet:

1. A **cognitive assessment**, which covers pattern spotting, logical thinking, error checking, prioritisation, and problem solving.
2. A **numeric reasoning assessment**, which covers percentages, averages, and reading simple tables and charts — using ticket volumes, KPIs, and service data.
3. A **verbal reasoning assessment**, which covers reading and understanding passages such as process guides, emails, and knowledge articles.
4. A **situational judgement assessment**, which presents realistic workplace situations and asks which responses are most and least effective.

Each section follows the same friendly pattern: a short explanation of what the assessment measures, a mapping to the specific skills in your role, a set of practice questions with full worked explanations, practical preparation tips, and common pitfalls to avoid.

Here is how to get the most from this guide. Take it one section at a time — there is no rush. For each practice question, write down your answer before you read the explanation; honest attempts teach you far more than skimming. Read every explanation, even for questions you answered correctly, because the walkthroughs show you a reliable technique you can reuse. And be kind to yourself: getting a question wrong during practice is not failure, it is exactly what practice is for.

You already do more of this thinking than you may realise. Let's begin.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the everyday thinking skills your role depends on, using practical workplace scenarios rather than abstract puzzles. Instead of asking you to rotate shapes or complete number grids, it presents you with the materials of your actual job — incident tickets, ticket queues, knowledge base articles, escalation rules, and simple checklists — and asks you to reason about them quickly and accurately.

The typical format is an online, timed test lasting around 15 to 30 minutes, usually with somewhere between 15 and 30 questions. The questions cover pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective: your answers are compared against a large group of other people who took the same test, so there is no examiner opinion involved. Some modern platforms are adaptive, which means the questions get slightly harder when you answer correctly and slightly easier when you do not — so do not panic if the questions seem to be getting tougher; that usually means you are doing well. Most platforms offer a few ungraded practice questions before the real test starts, so you can get used to the buttons and layout without any pressure.

Why do employers use this kind of assessment for an associate end user computing engineer? Because your role is built on careful, structured thinking done at pace. When a user reports a fault, you gather the required information, decide what kind of incident it is, and allocate it to the appropriate channel. When something looks odd — three similar tickets in one morning, a record that does not match reality — noticing the pattern quickly can save the whole team hours. A cognitive assessment samples these exact skills in miniature, which is why it predicts success in support roles so well.

One reassuring note before you begin: at your level, employers are not expecting deep technical expertise. They are looking for careful reading, sensible logic, and good attention to detail — all things you can strengthen through practice.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The assessment dimensions map directly to the named skills in your role summary:

- **Pattern recognition** maps to your **Problem management** skill: investigating problems in systems, processes and services starts with noticing that several separate incidents share a common cause — for example, spotting that a cluster of "cannot print" tickets all come from the same building.
- **Logical deduction** maps to your **Incident management** skill: identifying and registering incidents, gathering the required information and allocating each one to the appropriate channel means applying routing rules correctly — "if the incident affects more than one user, then..." — every single time.
- **Error checking** maps to your **IT service reporting** skill: when you collate data from data repositories and provide basic reporting, a single wrong figure or mistyped category can make a whole report misleading, so spotting small errors matters a great deal.
- **Prioritisation** maps to your **Ownership and topic** skill: showing an awareness of problem resolution processes and knowing when to pass problems on to your team requires you to judge which task to do first and which issue is beyond your remit.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to your **User focus** skill and your **Problem management** skill together: understanding user stories and the focus on user needs helps you work out what a user is really trying to achieve, which is often the key step in solving their problem.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern recognition in a ticket queue**

Your team's ticketing system gives every incident a reference in the format INC, then a hyphen, then six digits — for example INC-104276. Service requests use REQ, then a hyphen, then six digits — for example REQ-104277. While tidying the queue under your mentor's direction, you see these references:

1. INC-104301
2. REQ-104302
3. INC-10430
4. REQ-104304
5. INC-104305

Which reference breaks the format?

- A) Reference 1
- B) Reference 3
- C) Reference 4
- D) Reference 5

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Work through the rule step by step. The format needs three things: the right prefix (INC or REQ), a hyphen, and exactly six digits. References 1, 2, 4 and 5 all have a valid prefix, a hyphen, and six digits. Reference 3 has only five digits after the hyphen (10430), so it breaks the format. Checking formats like this is a real part of your job: when you register incidents and collate data for basic reporting, records with broken references can get lost, miscounted, or sent to the wrong queue. A good habit is to check each part of a format rule one at a time — prefix, separator, digits — rather than trying to judge the whole reference at a glance.

**Question 2 (easy) — Logical deduction from an escalation rule**

Your team's guide says: "If an incident affects more than five users, it must be escalated to the senior engineer immediately." A colleague tells you: "Ticket INC-221040 was escalated to the senior engineer immediately." Which one of the following must be true?

- A) INC-221040 affected more than five users.
- B) INC-221040 affected five users or fewer.
- C) The rule was followed correctly.
- D) None of the above must be true.

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** This question tests careful logic. The rule says that affecting more than five users leads to escalation — but it does not say escalation only happens for that reason. An engineer might escalate a ticket for a different reason entirely, such as a security concern, so option A does not have to be true. Option B does not have to be true either, for the same reason — we simply do not know the user count. Option C sounds safe, but we cannot confirm the rule was followed without knowing how many users were affected. The only safe conclusion is D. In assessments, and in real support work, the discipline is the same: only conclude what the information guarantees. When you register incidents, recording facts (what you know) separately from guesses (what you assume) keeps your tickets accurate and trustworthy.

**Question 3 (easy) — Error checking a hardware request list**

A user emails a list of equipment for three new starters, and your mentor asks you to check it against the standard new starter kit, which is: one laptop, one headset, one mouse, and one security pass holder per person. The email lists: 3 laptops, 3 headsets, 2 mice, 3 security pass holders, and 1 docking station. Which two statements are correct?

- A) The list is one mouse short, and the docking station is not part of the standard kit.
- B) The list is one headset short, and the docking station is not part of the standard kit.
- C) The list is one mouse short, and one laptop too many.
- D) The list matches the standard kit exactly.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Compare the list against the standard kit item by item. Laptops: 3 needed, 3 listed — correct. Headsets: 3 needed, 3 listed — correct. Mice: 3 needed, only 2 listed — one short. Security pass holders: 3 needed, 3 listed — correct. Finally, the docking station appears on the list but is not part of the standard kit, so it needs to be queried — perhaps the user wants it, but it is not standard, and your team may need approval to supply it. Option A captures both findings. Careful line-by-line checking like this supports your request control duties: catching a shortage before the starters arrive avoids a bad first day for a new user, and querying the extra item avoids supplying kit without approval.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Prioritisation of a morning queue**

You arrive at 09:00 and, under your team's guidance, you may pick the order in which you handle four items:

1. A ticket from a user whose laptop will not switch on; the user has a presentation to a director at 10:00.
2. A routine request to install approved software for a user who says "no rush, any time this week."
3. A note from your mentor asking you to update the asset log with three laptop serial numbers "by end of day."
4. A ticket reporting that the printer on the fourth floor is showing a low toner warning but is still printing.

Which order is most sensible?

- A) 3, 1, 2, 4 — do your mentor's task first because they are senior
- B) 1, 3, 4, 2 — urgent user-blocking issue first, then the same-day task, then the warning, then the no-rush request
- C) 2, 4, 3, 1 — do the quick and easy items first
- D) 4, 1, 3, 2 — printers affect many users, so always start with printers

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Prioritisation means weighing urgency (when it is needed) and impact (how much harm delay causes) together. Item 1 is both urgent and high impact: the user is completely blocked and has a deadline at 10:00, so it comes first. Item 3 is due today, so it comes next — well before end of day. Item 4 is a warning, not a failure: the printer still works, so it can wait, though it should not be forgotten because low toner will eventually become "cannot print." Item 2 has an explicit week-long window, so it goes last. Option A confuses seniority with urgency — your mentor said "end of day," not "now." Option C picks tasks for your own convenience rather than user impact. Option D applies a rigid rule ("always printers first") instead of judging the actual situation. In your role, showing awareness of what to do first — and checking with your team when unsure — is part of good ownership.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Pattern recognition across incidents**

During one morning, the following incidents are registered:

- 09:12 — User in Building A, floor 2: "Wi-Fi keeps dropping."
- 09:31 — User in Building A, floor 2: "Laptop loses network every few minutes."
- 09:48 — User in Building B, floor 1: "Cannot open a spreadsheet attachment."
- 10:02 — User in Building A, floor 2: "Video calls keep freezing."
- 10:15 — User in Building A, floor 3: "Forgotten password."

You are asked what you notice. What is the most useful observation to share with your team?

- A) Five separate users have five separate problems.
- B) Three incidents from Building A floor 2 describe network-related symptoms in about an hour — they may share one cause worth investigating.
- C) Building A has more incidents than Building B, so Building A users need training.
- D) The spreadsheet issue is the most serious because attachments involve security.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Look for what the incidents have in common. Three of the five tickets come from the same location (Building A, floor 2) and all three symptoms — dropping Wi-Fi, losing network, freezing video calls — are consistent with one underlying network problem, perhaps a faulty wireless access point. Spotting this pattern and telling your team is exactly what your problem management skill asks of you: investigating problems starts with noticing that separate incidents may be one problem in disguise. Option A treats the tickets as unrelated and misses the pattern. Option C jumps from a count to a conclusion about users that the evidence does not support. Option D invents a security concern the ticket does not mention. Notice also the level of the problem: a faulty access point is an operational problem (day-to-day running), rather than tactical or strategic — naming the level helps your team route it correctly.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Logical deduction about allocation rules**

Your allocation guide says: (i) hardware faults go to the desk-side team; (ii) software faults go to the applications team; (iii) if a ticket involves both hardware and software, it goes to the desk-side team first; (iv) anything mentioning suspicious emails goes to the security team regardless of other rules. A user reports: "My laptop screen flickers, and since yesterday my email app also crashes when I open it." Where should the ticket go first?

- A) The applications team, because the email app is crashing
- B) The desk-side team, because the ticket involves both hardware and software
- C) The security team, because email is mentioned
- D) It should be split into two tickets and sent to two teams at once

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Apply the rules in order and read them precisely. The flickering screen is a hardware fault; the crashing email app is a software fault; so rule (iii) applies: tickets involving both go to the desk-side team first — answer B. Option C is a tempting trap: rule (iv) is about suspicious emails (a security signal, such as a phishing message), not about email software crashing, so it does not apply. Option A ignores rule (iii). Option D might sometimes be sensible in real life, but it is not what the allocation guide says, and this question — like your incident registration duty — is about applying the agreed process accurately. When rules seem to overlap, check the exact wording: "email app crashes" and "suspicious emails" look similar at a glance but mean very different things.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Error checking an asset log update**

Your mentor asks you to record three laptop swaps in the asset log. The rule is: for each swap, mark the old laptop "Returned to store" and the new laptop "In use," and record the user's name against the new laptop only. Afterwards, the log shows:

| Asset | Status | User |
|---|---|---|
| LT-0451 (old) | Returned to store | — |
| LT-0892 (new) | In use | J. Adeyemi |
| LT-0463 (old) | Returned to store | M. Kaur |
| LT-0899 (new) | In use | M. Kaur |
| LT-0470 (old) | Returned to store | — |
| LT-0901 (new) | Returned to store | P. O'Brien |

How many rows contain an error?

- A) 0
- B) 1
- C) 2
- D) 3

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Check each row against the two rules: old laptops should be "Returned to store" with no user; new laptops should be "In use" with the user's name. Row 1: old, returned, no user — correct. Row 2: new, in use, named user — correct. Row 3: old and returned, but still has a user (M. Kaur) recorded — error, because names go against the new laptop only. Row 4: new, in use, named — correct. Row 5: old, returned, no user — correct. Row 6: new laptop but marked "Returned to store" — error; it should be "In use." That makes two erroneous rows, so the answer is C. Accurate records like this matter because other people rely on them: if the log says P. O'Brien's new laptop is in store, someone may reissue it to another user. Careful, rule-by-rule checking is the foundation of your basic reporting skill.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Applied problem solving with a user story**

Your team writes user stories in the format: "As a [type of user], I want [something], so that [benefit]." A user rings and says: "I need admin rights on my laptop." Following your team's user-focus approach, what is the most useful first question to ask?

- A) "Which laptop model do you have?"
- B) "What are you trying to do that currently isn't working?"
- C) "Have you tried turning it off and on again?"
- D) "Who is your line manager, so I can request approval?"

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A user story separates what the user asks for from what the user needs. "I need admin rights" is a proposed solution, not a need. The most useful first question uncovers the underlying need: perhaps the user wants to install a specific application, which your team can provide through the approved software catalogue without granting admin rights at all — safer for the organisation and often quicker for the user. Option A gathers a detail that may matter later but does not clarify the need. Option C is a generic script that does not fit the request. Option D jumps to processing the request as stated before understanding it, which could waste an approval cycle on the wrong solution. This question reflects your user focus skill directly: understanding the purpose of user stories means focusing on user needs, not just user requests.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Multi-step deduction about a recurring fault**

A meeting room laptop fails every Monday morning but works for the rest of the week. You learn the following facts: (i) the laptop is left plugged in over the weekend; (ii) building power is shut down every Sunday from 02:00 to 04:00 for maintenance; (iii) the laptop's battery is known to be faulty and holds no charge; (iv) when the laptop loses all power completely, it needs a manual restart before it will boot. What is the most likely explanation for the Monday failures?

- A) Users are mistreating the laptop on Fridays.
- B) The Sunday power shutdown cuts mains power; with a faulty battery the laptop fully powers off and then waits for the manual restart nobody performs until Monday.
- C) The laptop has a software fault that only activates on Mondays.
- D) The building's Wi-Fi is switched off at weekends.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Chain the facts together step by step. Fact (ii): every Sunday, mains power goes off for two hours. Fact (iii): the battery holds no charge, so during those two hours the laptop has no power source at all. Fact (iv): a complete power loss means the laptop needs a manual restart before it will boot. Nobody is in the meeting room overnight, so the first person to touch the laptop on Monday morning finds it apparently dead. Every fact is used, and the weekly timing is explained by the weekly shutdown. Option A introduces a cause with no supporting facts. Option C ignores the power facts and invents a strange software behaviour. Option D is irrelevant — a Wi-Fi outage would not stop a laptop booting. This is problem investigation in miniature: a recurring pattern (every Monday) usually points to a recurring cause (every Sunday), and your role includes contributing to remedies — here, perhaps replacing the battery or adding a Monday restart to a checklist.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Prioritisation with incomplete information**

At 14:00 you have two open items: (1) a ticket from one user whose second monitor shows no picture, and (2) a ticket saying "the shared drive is slow," raised by one user but mentioning "others are complaining too." Your mentor is in a meeting until 15:00. What is the best course of action?

- A) Work on the monitor ticket first because it was raised first, and leave the shared drive ticket until your mentor returns.
- B) Spend a few minutes checking whether the shared drive issue really affects multiple users — for example, by checking for related tickets or asking in the team channel — because if it does, it is higher impact and may need escalating; meanwhile keep the monitor user informed.
- C) Escalate both tickets immediately to the senior engineer to be safe.
- D) Interrupt your mentor's meeting to ask which ticket to do first.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The key uncertainty is the scale of the shared drive issue. If it genuinely affects many users, it has far higher impact than one monitor, and your team's processes probably require it to be handled or escalated as a priority. A few minutes of checking — looking for similar tickets, asking colleagues — resolves the uncertainty without needing your mentor. Informing the monitor user of progress reflects your duty to keep customers informed. Option A uses arrival order instead of impact, which is rarely the right rule. Option C over-escalates: passing everything upward without any checking does not show the awareness of problem resolution processes your role asks for. Option D interrupts unnecessarily for something you can investigate yourself; save interruptions for genuine emergencies. The pattern to remember: when impact is uncertain, a small, quick investigation to size the issue is almost always the best first move.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Error checking against two sources**

Your team's KPI report says: "We closed 120 incidents this week, of which 96 were closed within the target time, giving 80% within target." The ticket system export shows 120 incidents closed, 90 within target. The knowledge article about the report says the target percentage is calculated as incidents closed within target divided by total incidents closed. Which statement best describes the situation?

- A) The report is correct and the export must be wrong.
- B) The export is correct and the report's 80% figure should actually be 75%.
- C) Both are correct because 96 out of 120 is 75%.
- D) The percentage cannot be checked without more information.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Check the arithmetic both ways. Using the report's own numbers: 96 divided by 120 = 0.80, which is 80% — so the report is internally consistent. Using the export's numbers: 90 divided by 120 = 0.75, which is 75%. The two sources disagree about the count of incidents closed within target (96 versus 90). The ticket system export is the original data source — the repository you collate from — while the report is derived from it, so when they disagree, the export is the more trustworthy source, and the report's percentage should be 75%. Option A gets the trust relationship backwards. Option C is arithmetically wrong: 96 out of 120 is 80%, not 75%. Option D gives up too early — you have everything you need. In practice you would flag the discrepancy to your team rather than silently correcting it, because the difference might reveal a filtering error worth understanding. This is your IT service reporting skill in action: reports are only useful when the numbers can be traced back to the data.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Applied problem solving under a process constraint**

A user's laptop fails completely at 16:30 on a Friday. The user is a caseworker who must submit statutory case decisions by a legal deadline on Monday. Your loan laptop cupboard is empty, and the process says replacement laptops are issued only after a fault is confirmed by a senior engineer, all of whom have left for the day. What is the most constructive action you can take before the end of the day?

- A) Tell the user nothing can be done until Monday and log the ticket for the morning.
- B) Give the user your own admin account details so they can use any spare machine.
- C) Register the incident with full details, flag the Monday statutory deadline clearly, check whether the user can access their casework through another approved route (such as a browser session on a shared or colleague device), and alert the on-call or duty contact if your team has one.
- D) Take a laptop from an empty desk and give it to the user, sorting the paperwork out next week.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The best answer works within the process while doing everything possible for the user. Registering the incident with complete information and a clearly flagged deadline means whoever picks it up — the duty contact on Friday evening or the senior engineer first thing Monday — immediately understands the urgency. Checking for an approved alternative route is advising on workarounds, which is a core part of your role's description, and it may solve the user's real need (submitting decisions) even without fixing the laptop today. Alerting a duty contact respects the escalation path rather than bypassing it. Option A follows the letter of the process but abandons the user's need. Option B is a serious security breach — never share credentials, whatever the pressure. Option D bypasses asset control and could take equipment someone else depends on. The lesson: when process and urgency collide, the strong move is maximum effort within the rules plus clear, loud communication — not rule-breaking and not helplessness.

### Preparation tips

- **Practise with your real materials.** Spend ten minutes looking at your team's ticket queue, asset log, or a KPI report and ask yourself: what patterns do I see? What would I check for errors? This turns daily work into free assessment practice.
- **Read the whole question before answering.** Many cognitive questions hide the key detail in the last line. Train yourself to read everything once, carefully, before looking at the options.
- **Check rules one part at a time.** Whether it is a ticket format, an escalation rule, or a kit list, break the rule into pieces and check each piece. This is slower for the first question and faster for every question after, because it prevents rework.
- **Learn the difference between "must be true" and "could be true."** Deduction questions reward conclusions the information guarantees. If you find yourself thinking "well, probably...", the answer is usually "cannot say."
- **Time yourself gently.** Try a few questions with a timer to get used to working at pace, but during early practice, accuracy matters more than speed. Speed grows naturally from good technique.
- **Ask your mentor about real examples.** Your team's experienced engineers spot patterns and errors all day. Asking "how did you know those tickets were related?" builds exactly the instincts these assessments measure.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Rushing the easy questions.** Early questions are meant to be gentle, and careless slips on them are the most common way to lose marks. Bank the easy marks with careful reading.
- **Assuming instead of checking.** Options that "sound right" often add details the question never gave you. If the question did not say it, do not assume it.
- **Letting one hard question eat your time.** If a question is taking too long, choose your best answer, flag it if the platform allows, and move on. Ten questions answered well beat three questions answered perfectly.
- **Choosing answers that break rules to be helpful.** In support scenarios, options that share passwords, skip approvals, or bypass asset control are always wrong, no matter how helpful they feel. The strong answers combine maximum help with full compliance.
- **Panicking when questions get harder.** On adaptive tests, harder questions usually mean you are performing well. Treat rising difficulty as a good sign, not a warning.
- **Skipping the explanations during practice.** The worked reasoning is where the learning lives. Reading explanations for questions you got right confirms your technique; reading them for questions you got wrong upgrades it.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures how confidently and accurately you work with the kinds of numbers your role genuinely involves. This is not a maths exam full of algebra: it is about practical, everyday arithmetic — percentages, averages, simple ratios, and reading tables and charts — applied to service data such as ticket volumes, closure rates, KPI figures, and stock counts.

The typical format is an online, timed test lasting 20 to 30 minutes, with around 15 to 20 questions. Each question usually shows you a small table, chart, or short description containing numbers, and asks you to calculate or interpret something. You can normally use a calculator and rough paper, and the test measures whether you choose the right calculation and carry it out carefully — not whether you can do long division in your head. As with cognitive tests, scoring is objective and often compares both your accuracy and your speed against a norm group.

Why does this matter for an associate end user computing engineer? Numbers run quietly through your whole role. When you collate data from data repositories and provide basic reporting, you are counting, summing, and calculating percentages. When your team talks about Key Performance Indicators — like the percentage of incidents resolved within target, or average time to respond — you need to understand what those figures mean and why they matter, which is an explicit part of your IT service reporting skill. Even simple daily tasks involve numbers: how many loan laptops are left, how many tickets came in versus how many were closed, whether this week's queue is growing or shrinking. An engineer who is comfortable with these small calculations spots mistakes before they spread and explains service performance clearly.

Be reassured: at your level, the arithmetic itself is deliberately simple. The skill being tested is care — choosing the right numbers from the table, using the right operation, and sense-checking the answer.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Percentages and KPIs** map directly to your **IT service reporting** skill: explaining what a Key Performance Indicator is and why it is important means being able to calculate one — such as "percentage of incidents closed within target" — and to say what a change in the figure means for users.
- **Reading tables and charts** maps to your **IT service reporting** skill as well: collating data from data repositories means pulling the right rows and columns out of larger datasets without mixing them up.
- **Counting and categorising** maps to your **Incident management** skill: gathering the required information and allocating incidents to the appropriate channel produces the very counts that reports are built from — miscounted categories mean misleading reports.
- **Trends and comparisons** map to your **Problem management** skill: investigating problems often starts with a number moving in the wrong direction — a rising count of similar incidents is frequently the first visible sign of an underlying problem.
- **Simple rates and averages** map to your **User focus** skill: quantitative data about users — how many are affected, how long they wait — helps your team turn user focus into decisions about what to fix first.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Percentage of tickets closed**

This week your team received 150 incident tickets and closed 120 of them. What percentage of the received tickets were closed?

- A) 70%
- B) 75%
- C) 80%
- D) 85%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A percentage is the part divided by the whole, multiplied by 100. Step 1: identify the part (tickets closed = 120) and the whole (tickets received = 150). Step 2: divide — 120 ÷ 150 = 0.8. Step 3: multiply by 100 — 0.8 × 100 = 80%. So the answer is C. A quick sense-check: 75% of 150 would be 112.5 and 80% of 150 is exactly 120, which matches. This is the simplest and most common KPI-style calculation in service reporting, and being able to do it quickly means you can check your team's weekly figures rather than just copying them.

**Question 2 (easy) — Reading a simple table**

The table shows tickets registered by category on Monday:

| Category | Tickets |
|---|---|
| Hardware | 14 |
| Software | 22 |
| Access requests | 9 |
| Network | 5 |

How many tickets were registered in total, and which category had the most?

- A) 50 total; Software most
- B) 48 total; Software most
- C) 50 total; Hardware most
- D) 45 total; Access requests most

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Add the categories step by step: 14 + 22 = 36; 36 + 9 = 45; 45 + 5 = 50. Total is 50. The largest single figure in the Tickets column is 22, which belongs to Software. So the answer is A: 50 total, Software most. When adding a column, run the addition twice — once top to bottom, once bottom to top (5 + 9 = 14; 14 + 22 = 36; 36 + 14 = 50) — because getting the same answer both ways almost always means it is right. Totalling and ranking categories like this is exactly what you do when you collate data for a basic daily or weekly report.

**Question 3 (easy) — Average tickets per day**

Over five working days, you registered the following numbers of incidents: 12, 8, 15, 10, and 5. What was the average (mean) number of incidents registered per day?

- A) 9
- B) 10
- C) 11
- D) 12

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The mean is the total divided by the number of days. Step 1: total the values — 12 + 8 = 20; 20 + 15 = 35; 35 + 10 = 45; 45 + 5 = 50. Step 2: divide by the number of days — 50 ÷ 5 = 10. So the average is 10 incidents per day, answer B. Sense-check: the values range from 5 to 15, and 10 sits comfortably in the middle, which is what you expect from a mean. Averages like this feed KPIs such as "average daily incident volume," which help your team plan how many engineers are needed on the desk each day.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Percentage change in ticket volume**

Last week your team received 120 incidents. This week it received 138. By what percentage did incident volume increase?

- A) 12%
- B) 15%
- C) 18%
- D) 20%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Percentage change is the difference divided by the original value, multiplied by 100. Step 1: find the difference — 138 − 120 = 18. Step 2: divide by the original (last week's) figure — 18 ÷ 120 = 0.15. Step 3: multiply by 100 — 15%. The answer is B. The most common error is dividing by the new figure instead of the original: 18 ÷ 138 ≈ 0.13, which would wrongly suggest 13%. Always divide by the starting value. Why this matters in your role: a 15% week-on-week rise in incidents is the kind of trend your problem management skill should notice — it might mean a new fault has appeared, and flagging the rise to your team could trigger a useful investigation.

**Question 5 (moderate) — KPI target check**

Your team's KPI target is: "At least 90% of password reset requests completed within 4 hours." This week there were 60 password reset requests, and 52 were completed within 4 hours. Did the team meet the target, and what was the actual percentage (to the nearest whole number)?

- A) Yes — 90%
- B) No — 87%
- C) Yes — 92%
- D) No — 82%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Step 1: calculate the actual percentage — 52 ÷ 60 = 0.8667, which is 86.67%, rounding to 87%. Step 2: compare with the target — 87% is below 90%, so the target was missed. The answer is B. A useful extra insight: to meet 90% of 60 requests, the team needed 0.9 × 60 = 54 completions within 4 hours, so it fell 2 requests short. That "how many more did we need?" calculation is often more useful in team discussions than the percentage itself, because it turns an abstract KPI miss into a concrete, fixable number. Understanding what a KPI is and why it matters — the heart of your IT service reporting skill — includes being able to check one honestly like this.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Ratio of stock**

The loan equipment cupboard holds laptops and tablets in the ratio 3:1. There are 24 devices in total. After a busy morning, 6 laptops are loaned out. How many laptops remain in the cupboard?

- A) 12
- B) 14
- C) 16
- D) 18

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Step 1: use the ratio to split the total. A ratio of 3:1 means 3 + 1 = 4 equal parts. Each part is 24 ÷ 4 = 6 devices. Laptops are 3 parts: 3 × 6 = 18 laptops (and 6 tablets — check: 18 + 6 = 24, correct). Step 2: subtract the loans — 18 − 6 = 12 laptops remain. The answer is A. The common slip is stopping after the first step and answering 18, forgetting the loans, so always re-read the question to check what is actually being asked. Stock arithmetic like this supports the support-and-maintenance side of your role: knowing what is genuinely left in the cupboard keeps your records honest and prevents promising equipment you do not have.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Reading a chart described in words**

A bar chart of calls to the service desk shows: Monday 80 calls, Tuesday 65, Wednesday 70, Thursday 60, Friday 45. Your mentor asks: "What share of the week's calls came on Monday?" (to the nearest whole percent)

- A) 20%
- B) 25%
- C) 30%
- D) 33%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Step 1: total the week — 80 + 65 = 145; 145 + 70 = 215; 215 + 60 = 275; 275 + 45 = 320 calls. Step 2: divide Monday by the total — 80 ÷ 320 = 0.25. Step 3: convert to a percentage — 25%. The answer is B. Sense-check: if calls were spread evenly, each of the five days would carry 20%, and Monday is clearly the busiest day, so an answer a little above 20% is plausible — 25% fits; 33% would mean a third of all weekly calls on one day, which the numbers do not support. Recognising that Mondays are consistently the heaviest day is genuinely useful operational knowledge: it explains why teams often schedule more desk cover at the start of the week.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Time calculations for response targets**

Your team's target is to respond to new incidents within 2 hours during working hours (09:00–17:00). A ticket arrives at 16:15 on Tuesday. Working hours pause overnight and resume at 09:00 on Wednesday. By what time must the team respond?

- A) 18:15 Tuesday
- B) 09:45 Wednesday
- C) 10:15 Wednesday
- D) 11:00 Wednesday

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The 2-hour clock only runs during working hours. Step 1: from 16:15 to 17:00 on Tuesday is 45 minutes of working time used. Step 2: the remaining time is 2 hours − 45 minutes = 1 hour 15 minutes. Step 3: the clock restarts at 09:00 Wednesday; adding 1 hour 15 minutes gives 10:15 Wednesday. The answer is C. Option A ignores the end of the working day; option B subtracts wrongly (45 minutes after 09:00 would mean only 1 hour 30 minutes total); option D adds a full 2 hours from 09:00, forgetting the 45 minutes already used on Tuesday. Understanding how SLA clocks pause and resume helps you register incidents with realistic expectations and keep users accurately informed about when they will hear back — a core part of informing customers on progress.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Comparing two weeks of KPI data**

The table shows two weeks of incident data:

| Measure | Week 1 | Week 2 |
|---|---|---|
| Incidents received | 200 | 250 |
| Incidents closed within target | 160 | 190 |

A colleague says: "Week 2 was better — we closed 30 more incidents within target." Is the colleague's conclusion sound as a KPI comparison?

- A) Yes — 190 is more than 160, so performance improved.
- B) No — as a percentage, Week 1 was 80% within target and Week 2 was 76%, so the rate actually fell.
- C) No — Week 2 was worse because more incidents were received.
- D) Cannot say — the table does not contain enough information.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** KPIs are usually rates, not raw counts, because the workload changes from week to week. Step 1: Week 1 rate — 160 ÷ 200 = 0.80 = 80%. Step 2: Week 2 rate — 190 ÷ 250 = 0.76 = 76%. Step 3: compare — the rate fell by 4 percentage points even though the count rose. The answer is B. The colleague's raw-count comparison hides the fact that the team handled 25% more incidents and kept a slightly smaller share of them within target. Option C is wrong for the opposite reason — receiving more incidents is not itself "worse performance." Option D underestimates the table, which contains everything needed. This is precisely why your role summary asks you to explain what a KPI is and why it is important: the percentage tells a truer story than the count, and being able to say so — politely — makes your reporting genuinely useful.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Stock forecasting**

Your team issues an average of 4 replacement headsets per week. The store currently holds 22 headsets. A delivery of new stock takes 3 weeks to arrive after being ordered. If your team wants never to run out, by the end of which week should an order be placed at the latest?

- A) End of week 1
- B) End of week 2
- C) End of week 3
- D) End of week 4

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Work out when the stock would run out, then step back by the delivery time. Step 1: at 4 headsets per week, 22 headsets last 22 ÷ 4 = 5.5 weeks — so stock runs out during week 6. Step 2: a delivery ordered at the end of week W arrives at the end of week W + 3. To have new stock before running out during week 6, the delivery must arrive by the end of week 5 at the latest, which means ordering by the end of week 2 (2 + 3 = 5). The answer is B. Check it: order at end of week 2; by end of week 5, 5 × 4 = 20 headsets have been issued, leaving 2 — tight but not empty — and the delivery then lands. Ordering at the end of week 3 would mean arrival at the end of week 6, after the stock ran out mid-week. Simple forecasting like this is part of supporting and maintaining end user services: running out of basic kit turns routine requests into complaints.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Weighted workload calculation**

Your mentor explains that different tasks take different average times: a password reset takes 10 minutes, a software install takes 30 minutes, and a laptop build takes 90 minutes. Today's queue contains 6 password resets, 4 software installs, and 2 laptop builds. Roughly how many hours of work is in the queue?

- A) 5 hours
- B) 6 hours
- C) 7 hours
- D) 8 hours

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Multiply each task type by its average time, then add. Step 1: password resets — 6 × 10 = 60 minutes. Step 2: software installs — 4 × 30 = 120 minutes. Step 3: laptop builds — 2 × 90 = 180 minutes. Step 4: total — 60 + 120 = 180, and 180 + 180 = 360 minutes. Step 5: convert to hours — 360 ÷ 60 = 6 hours. The answer is B. A good checking habit for multi-part sums is to re-add the parts in a different order (180 + 120 + 60 gives 360 again), because agreeing with yourself twice by two routes is strong evidence the total is right. The common errors here are unit slips — mixing minutes and hours mid-calculation — and miscounting one of the task types, so keep everything in minutes until the final step. Queue-time estimates like this help teams decide whether today's work fits today's staff, and are a simple example of the data-to-decision thinking your reporting skill builds towards.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Spotting a misleading average**

Your team reports "average time to resolve hardware incidents: 3.6 hours" for last week. The individual resolution times were: 1 hour, 1 hour, 1.5 hours, 1.5 hours, and 13 hours (one incident needed a part ordered). A user asks: "So hardware faults typically take about 3 or 4 hours to fix?" What is the most accurate and helpful reply?

- A) "Yes — the average is 3.6 hours, so that is the typical time."
- B) "Actually, most faults were fixed in 1 to 1.5 hours; one unusual incident needing a spare part pulled the average up. The typical time is closer to 1.5 hours."
- C) "No, faults take 13 hours if parts are needed, so expect 13 hours."
- D) "The average is meaningless and should be ignored."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** First verify the average step by step: 1 + 1 = 2; 2 + 1.5 = 3.5; 3.5 + 1.5 = 5; 5 + 13 = 18 hours total; 18 ÷ 5 = 3.6 hours — so the reported figure is arithmetically correct. But correct is not the same as representative: four of the five incidents took 1 to 1.5 hours, and one extreme value (13 hours) drags the mean far above the typical experience. Option B gives the user an honest, useful picture: the typical time plus the reason the average looks higher. Option A repeats a technically true figure in a way that misleads the user about what to expect. Option C treats the rare worst case as the norm. Option D throws away information instead of explaining it. When you provide basic reporting, remember that a mean can be pulled around by one extreme value; describing what usually happens, and what rarely happens, keeps users properly informed — which is exactly your duty of informing customers on progress with honest expectations.

### Preparation tips

- **Refresh the three core moves.** Almost every question at your level uses one of three calculations: percentage (part ÷ whole × 100), percentage change (difference ÷ original × 100), or average (total ÷ count). Practise each until it feels automatic.
- **Always sense-check.** After every calculation, pause and ask: is this answer plausible? If 52 out of 60 requests met target, an answer of 20% is obviously wrong before you re-check a single digit.
- **Write down the numbers you select.** Most table-question errors come from picking the wrong row or column, not from bad arithmetic. Jot the numbers on rough paper before calculating so you can see exactly what you used.
- **Divide by the original in change questions.** Percentage increase and decrease always divide by the starting value. Say it to yourself before every change question.
- **Practise with your team's real reports.** Take last week's ticket figures and calculate the closure rate, the busiest day's share, and the week-on-week change yourself. Real data makes practice meaningful and helps you contribute in team meetings.
- **Use the calculator, but own the method.** The calculator does the arithmetic; you choose the operations. Most wrong answers come from a wrong method carried out perfectly, so slow down at the "what do I divide by what?" step.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Confusing counts with rates.** "We closed more tickets" is not the same as "we performed better" if the workload also grew. Check whether the question wants a number or a percentage.
- **Forgetting the second step.** Many questions have two stages (split by ratio, then subtract; calculate, then compare with a target). Re-read the question after calculating to confirm you have answered what was actually asked.
- **Rounding too early.** Keep full precision until the final step. Rounding 0.8667 to 0.87 is fine at the end, but rounding intermediate values can push you to the wrong option.
- **Misreading time windows.** SLA clocks that pause outside working hours are a classic trap. Track "time used" and "time remaining" separately and the calculation stays simple.
- **Trusting a printed figure over your own check.** If a report says 80% and the raw numbers say 75%, the raw numbers win. Assessments — and good engineers — reward the person who checks.
- **Freezing on charts.** A chart is just a table wearing different clothes. Read off the values, write them down, and treat it like any other table question.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately you read, understand, and draw conclusions from written material — using the kinds of documents your role genuinely involves. For an associate end user computing engineer, that means knowledge base articles, process guides, emails from users and colleagues, service announcements, and short policy extracts.

The typical format is an online, timed test lasting 15 to 25 minutes. You are shown a short passage and asked questions about it. The most common question style is **true / false / cannot say**: a statement is presented, and you must decide whether the passage makes it definitely true, definitely false, or whether the passage simply does not say. Other question styles ask you to pick the best summary of a passage, identify what a user's email is actually asking for, or choose the clearest way to phrase a message. The golden rule throughout is: answer using only the passage. Your own background knowledge — even when correct — must not be added to what the text says.

Why do employers test this for your role? Because reading accurately is a support skill in disguise. Every day you read knowledge articles and must follow them exactly — a skipped step in a fix procedure can make a fault worse. You read user emails and must work out what is really being reported, which is often different from the subject line. You read process guides that tell you what to do and, just as importantly, what needs someone else's approval. And when you write updates for users — part of your duty of informing customers on progress — clear, accurate wording prevents confusion and repeat contact. Verbal reasoning practice sharpens all of this.

A reassuring note: this is not a vocabulary test, and it does not reward fancy language. Plain, careful reading wins. At your level the passages are short and practical, and the skill being measured is discipline — keeping what the text says separate from what you assume.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Comprehension of procedures** maps to your **Incident management** skill: identifying and registering incidents means following the registration procedure exactly as written — which fields are required, which channel each category goes to.
- **True/false/cannot-say discipline** maps to your **Problem management** skill: investigating problems fairly means separating what the evidence states from what you suspect; jumping to conclusions leads investigations astray.
- **Identifying what a message really asks** maps to your **User focus** skill: understanding the purpose of user stories and the focus on user needs starts with reading a user's words carefully enough to find the need behind the request.
- **Clear-writing judgement** maps to your role's duty of **informing customers on progress and advising on workarounds**: users are often stressed and non-technical, so choosing wording that is accurate, plain, and kind is a genuine job skill.
- **Reading reports and definitions** maps to your **IT service reporting** skill: explaining what a KPI is and why it matters requires reading definitions precisely — "within 4 working hours" and "within 4 hours" are different promises.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Comprehension of a knowledge article**

A knowledge article reads: "Before reimaging any laptop, confirm that the user's files are synchronised to cloud storage. If synchronisation cannot be confirmed, do not reimage; instead, escalate to a senior engineer. Reimaging deletes all local data."

Statement: "You may reimage a laptop once the user's files are confirmed as synchronised to cloud storage."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The article sets one condition for reimaging: confirming the files are synchronised. The statement matches this directly — once synchronisation is confirmed, the stated barrier to reimaging is removed, so the statement is true according to the passage. Notice how the article's second sentence supports this reading: escalation is required only "if synchronisation cannot be confirmed." When reading procedures, it helps to underline the condition words — "before," "if," "do not... instead" — because they carry the logic. In real work, this is exactly the kind of instruction where careful reading protects users: reimaging without confirming synchronisation would delete someone's local files permanently.

**Question 2 (easy) — What is the user actually asking?**

A user emails: "Subject: Broken laptop. Hi — my laptop is fine actually, but ever since the update last week, when I plug into the meeting room screen nothing appears on it. The laptop screen still works. I have an important session with external visitors on Thursday. Can you help before then?"

What is the user actually reporting, and what is the deadline?

- A) A broken laptop, needed by Thursday
- B) A problem displaying to an external screen since an update, needed before Thursday
- C) A faulty meeting room screen, no deadline
- D) A request for a new laptop before Thursday

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The subject line says "Broken laptop," but the body corrects it immediately: "my laptop is fine actually." The real report is that output to the meeting room screen stopped working after last week's update — and the user needs it fixed before Thursday. Option B captures the fault, the likely trigger (the update), and the deadline. Option A trusts the subject line over the body. Option C is possible in reality but the passage points to the update as the change — and it drops the deadline the user clearly stated. Option D invents a request the user never made. This mirrors daily incident registration: users often mislabel their issues, and gathering the required information means reading the whole message, recording the real symptom, the recent change, and the user's time constraint.

**Question 3 (easy) — True/false/cannot say on a service notice**

A service notice reads: "The printing service will be unavailable on Saturday from 08:00 to 12:00 for planned maintenance. Email and shared drives will not be affected."

Statement: "The printing service will be available on Sunday."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The notice only tells us about Saturday morning. It says nothing about Sunday at all. It might be natural to assume the service will be back — maintenance is planned to finish at 12:00 on Saturday — but "cannot say" questions test exactly this discipline: the passage does not state anything about Sunday, so we cannot say. Choosing "true" here means adding an assumption to the text. This matters in practice: if a user asked you "so printing will definitely be fine on Sunday?", the accurate answer is "that's the plan — maintenance is due to finish Saturday at midday," not an unconditional promise. Accurate reading leads to accurate promises, and accurate promises are the heart of informing customers well.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Following a procedure with conditions**

A process guide states: "New software requests: (1) Check the application is in the approved catalogue. (2) If it is in the catalogue, install it and close the request. (3) If it is not in the catalogue, do not install it; forward the request to the software assurance team and inform the user of the expected 10 working day review. (4) Requests from the finance department during month-end week must additionally be scheduled outside working hours."

A finance user requests a catalogue-approved application during month-end week. What does the guide require?

- A) Install it immediately and close the request.
- B) Forward the request to the software assurance team.
- C) Install it, but schedule the installation outside working hours.
- D) Refuse the request until month-end week is over.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Walk the conditions in order. Step 1: is the application in the approved catalogue? Yes — so rule (2) applies: install and close, and rule (3) does not apply. But rule (4) adds an extra condition for this particular user: finance department, month-end week — so the installation must be scheduled outside working hours. Combining the applicable rules gives option C. Option A misses rule (4); option B wrongly applies the not-in-catalogue path; option D invents a refusal the guide never mentions. The reading skill here is recognising that "additionally" means rule (4) stacks on top of the others rather than replacing them. Procedures with stacking conditions are everywhere in service work — and reading them precisely is what makes your request control reliable.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Inference from a colleague's handover note**

A handover note reads: "INC-30877 — user's laptop overheats and shuts down. Cleaned the fan vents, updated firmware, ran hardware diagnostics twice: all passed. Shutdowns continued during my tests. I've requested a like-for-like replacement device; awaiting stock. User has been told and is using a hot-desk PC meanwhile."

Statement: "The engineer believes the fault has been fixed by the firmware update."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The note explicitly says "Shutdowns continued during my tests" — after the cleaning and the firmware update — and the engineer went on to request a replacement device. Both facts show the engineer does not believe the fault is fixed; a replacement would be pointless if the update had solved it. So the statement is false according to the passage — not "cannot say," because the passage gives clear evidence against it. Distinguishing "false" from "cannot say" is a classic verbal reasoning skill: choose "false" when the passage contradicts the statement, and "cannot say" only when the passage is silent. Reading handovers this precisely matters because you may be the next person to touch the ticket — misreading the state of an investigation wastes effort repeating steps a colleague already ruled out.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Choosing the clearest user update**

You need to update a non-technical user whose incident (a failed email sync on their phone) is waiting on another team. Which message is best?

- A) "Your ticket has been escalated to Tier 3 pending MDM policy remediation; ETA unknown at this juncture."
- B) "Hi — an update on your phone email issue: we've found the cause and passed it to our specialist team to fix. We expect news by Thursday, and I'll update you then even if it isn't finished. Meanwhile, your email still works on your laptop."
- C) "We are working on it."
- D) "Hi — unfortunately the MDM enrolment token expired, which invalidated the ActiveSync certificate chain. Reprovisioning requires a policy push from the EMM console."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A good status update tells the user four things in plain language: what is happening, who is doing it, when they will next hear from you, and what they can do meanwhile. Option B delivers all four: cause found, specialist team engaged, an update promised by Thursday (with the important reassurance that you will write even without news), and a workaround — email still works on the laptop. Option A is jargon-heavy and offers no commitment. Option C is plain but empty: no cause, no timescale, no workaround. Option D is technically detailed but meaningless to a non-technical user and, notably, contains no next step for them at all. Advising on workarounds and informing customers on progress are named parts of your role; this question shows what doing them well looks like in writing.

**Question 7 (moderate) — True/false/cannot say on a policy extract**

A policy extract reads: "Portable devices must be encrypted before being issued to users. Devices returned from users must be securely wiped within five working days of return. Devices awaiting wiping must be stored in the locked cabinet in the IT store."

Statement: "A returned device may be reissued to a new user four days after its return, provided it has been securely wiped and encrypted."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Test the statement against each rule. Wiping within five working days: a wipe completed by day four is within the limit — allowed. Encryption before issue: the statement says the device has been encrypted before reissue — satisfied. Storage in the locked cabinet applies while awaiting wiping, and this device has already been wiped. No rule sets a minimum waiting period before reissue, and every stated condition is met, so the passage makes the statement true. If you chose "cannot say," you may have felt the policy should say more about reissue — but the question asks what these rules permit, and they permit it. The skill is exact: policies constrain what they constrain, and no more. In asset handling, knowing precisely what the policy requires — and what it leaves to judgement — keeps you compliant without inventing extra rules that slow users down.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Identifying the main point of a passage**

A team announcement reads: "From next month, all incidents will be registered through the new service portal rather than by email. Emailed reports will receive an automatic reply directing users to the portal. During the first four weeks, the service desk will register portal-shy users' incidents by phone as a transition measure. The change aims to capture complete information at first contact, reducing the back-and-forth that currently delays resolution."

Which sentence best summarises the announcement?

- A) The service desk will stop helping users who email.
- B) Incident registration is moving to a portal to capture better information up front, with phone support easing the transition.
- C) Email is being switched off across the organisation.
- D) Resolution times are currently too slow because users provide poor information.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A good summary captures the main change, its purpose, and any significant qualifier. Option B does all three: the move to the portal (change), capturing complete information at first contact (purpose), and phone registration during the transition (qualifier). Option A distorts the passage — emailers are redirected, and phone help exists, so nobody is abandoned. Option C wildly overstates: email as a whole is not being switched off; only incident registration is moving. Option D turns the diplomatic purpose statement into blame, which the passage does not do — it blames the back-and-forth process, not the users. Summarising fairly is a real workplace skill: when you explain a change like this to a frustrated user at a desk, your one-sentence version needs to be accurate, complete, and blame-free.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Inference across two passages**

Passage 1 (knowledge article): "Model X laptops with the July firmware are affected by a known battery drain fault. The August firmware resolves it. Affected users should be offered the firmware update at first contact."
Passage 2 (email from a user): "My Model X laptop's battery has been draining fast for weeks. Your colleague updated my firmware in September and it made no difference. Can someone help?"

Statement: "The user's battery drain is caused by the known July firmware fault."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Combine the passages carefully. The knowledge article says the August firmware resolves the July firmware fault. The user received a firmware update in September — after the fix was available — and the drain "made no difference." If the known fault were the cause, the update should have resolved it. The passages together therefore contradict the statement: this user's drain is evidently not (or at least not only) the known firmware fault, so the statement is false on the evidence given. Choosing "true" here is the tempting error — the symptoms match the known fault, so pattern matching pulls you towards it. But the failed fix is decisive counter-evidence. This is investigation discipline in miniature: a known problem is a hypothesis to test, not an automatic diagnosis, and evidence that a remedy failed must update your conclusion. That is exactly the mindset your problem management skill asks you to develop.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Precise reading of a KPI definition**

A reporting guide defines: "First-time fix rate: the percentage of incidents resolved by the first engineer who handles them, without escalation or reassignment, measured across all incidents closed in the calendar month. Incidents reopened within five working days of closure are excluded from the numerator but remain in the denominator."

Statement: "An incident resolved by the first engineer, but reopened three working days later, increases the first-time fix rate."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Parse the definition slowly. The rate is a fraction: numerator = incidents resolved first-time; denominator = all incidents closed in the month. The exclusion rule says a quickly-reopened incident is removed from the numerator but stays in the denominator. So the incident in the statement adds one to the bottom of the fraction and nothing to the top — which pulls the rate down, not up. The statement claims it increases the rate, so the statement is false. This kind of reading feels fussy, but it is precisely the skill your IT service reporting duties require: KPI definitions are full of carefully-worded inclusion and exclusion rules, and misreading one produces a report that quietly flatters or punishes the team unfairly. When you meet a definition in an assessment, sketch the fraction on rough paper and place the scenario's incident into it — the arithmetic picture makes the verbal logic easy.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Judging tone and accuracy together**

A user has waited two days for a laptop repair that your team promised in one day, because a part arrived late. Your mentor asks you to draft the apology and update. Which draft is best?

- A) "We apologise for any inconvenience caused. Your ticket remains in progress."
- B) "Hi — I'm sorry your repair has taken longer than the one day we promised. The replacement part arrived late from our supplier; it's now here, and your laptop will be ready by 15:00 today. If it would help sooner, we can set you up on a loan laptop this morning — just reply and I'll arrange it."
- C) "Hi — sorry for the delay, but this was the supplier's fault, not ours. These things happen and are outside our control. It'll be done at some point today hopefully."
- D) "Hi — great news, your laptop is nearly ready! Thanks so much for your amazing patience, you're a star!"

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The strongest message does four things: owns the missed promise ("longer than the one day we promised"), explains the cause honestly without dodging responsibility, gives a specific new commitment (15:00 today), and offers a workaround (a loan laptop) that addresses the user's real need — working today. Option A is vague boilerplate: no cause, no new time, no workaround, and "any inconvenience" reads as unfelt. Option C shifts blame to the supplier and hedges the new promise ("at some point... hopefully"), which damages trust twice. Option D is warm but content-free — enthusiasm is not a substitute for a time and a plan, and over-familiar tone can grate on a user who has been let down. Notice that B is honest about the failure; assessments and real users alike reward accuracy plus accountability plus a concrete next step. That combination is your role's communication duty done properly.

### Preparation tips

- **Use only what the passage says.** Before answering, ask: "where in the text is my evidence?" If you cannot point to a sentence, your answer is probably an assumption — and the answer to the question may be "cannot say."
- **Learn the false/cannot-say boundary.** "False" needs the passage to contradict the statement; "cannot say" means the passage is silent. Practise sorting statements into these two bins until the distinction feels natural.
- **Underline condition words.** Words like "if," "unless," "before," "only," "additionally," and "excluding" carry the logic of procedures and policies. Spotting them first makes condition-stacking questions much easier.
- **Read user messages twice.** Once for the facts (symptom, change, deadline), once for the need behind the words. Subject lines and first sentences often mislead; the real request is frequently in the middle.
- **Practise on real documents.** Knowledge articles, service notices, and policy pages from your own workplace are ideal practice material. Try writing one true, one false, and one cannot-say statement about each — setting questions teaches the skill even faster than answering them.
- **Draft user updates deliberately.** When you next write a status update, check it for the four elements: what is happening, who is acting, when the user will next hear, and what they can do meanwhile. This habit doubles as verbal reasoning practice and better daily work.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Adding your own technical knowledge.** You may know that a service usually restores after maintenance — but if the passage does not say so, the answer is "cannot say." The test measures reading, not expertise.
- **Trusting the subject line.** In message-comprehension questions (and real tickets), the stated topic is often wrong. Always let the body of the message overrule the label.
- **Missing stacking conditions.** Words like "additionally" and "must also" add rules on top of others rather than replacing them. Answers that satisfy only one rule of several are the most common trap.
- **Confusing plausible with stated.** An option can be sensible, likely, and still wrong — because the question asks what the passage says, not what would probably happen.
- **Over-hedging on clear evidence.** Some candidates, once taught "cannot say," start choosing it everywhere. If the passage clearly contradicts a statement, the answer is "false" — do not let caution blur into indecision.
- **Rushing long options.** In best-summary and best-message questions, wrong options often differ from the right one by a single distortion — an exaggeration, a dropped qualifier, a shifted blame. Read every option fully before choosing.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment (often called an SJT) presents realistic workplace situations and asks you to judge how effective different responses would be. Unlike the other assessments in this guide, there is no arithmetic and no passage to parse — the material is human: a frustrated user, a mistake you have made, a queue that is too long, a colleague who needs help. For each scenario you are typically asked to pick the **most effective** response, the **least effective** response, or to rate every option on an effectiveness scale.

The typical format is an online test of 10 to 20 scenarios, usually untimed or generously timed — the emphasis is on judgement, not speed. There are no formally "right" answers in the way arithmetic has right answers; instead, your choices are scored against the judgement of experienced professionals and against the values of the organisation. For UK government digital roles, that means responses are judged against professional behaviours: user focus, collaboration, ownership, honesty, appropriate escalation, and inclusion.

Why do employers use SJTs for associate end user computing engineers? Because at your level, judgement calls are daily events, and most of them are about knowing your boundaries as a trainee: when to try something yourself, when to ask, when to pass a problem to your team, and how to treat users well while you learn. Your role summary says it directly — you work in an established team, supporting end user computing engineers under their direction, showing awareness of problem resolution processes and passing problems on to your team. An SJT measures whether you can do that gracefully: neither freezing and asking about everything, nor over-reaching and hiding mistakes.

Here is the reassuring truth about SJTs at your level: the scoring rarely expects heroics. The best answers are usually honest, prompt, user-focused, and appropriately humble. If you remember to protect the user, tell the truth, and use your team, you will rarely go far wrong.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Knowing when to escalate** maps to your **Ownership and topic** skill: showing an awareness of problem resolution processes and passing problems on to your team is the defining judgement of the associate level — the assessment tests whether you escalate the right things at the right time, with the right information.
- **Handling users with care** maps to your **User focus** skill and your role's duty of informing customers on progress: scenarios test whether you keep users informed, honest, and supported even when you personally cannot fix their issue.
- **Owning mistakes** maps to your **Incident management** skill: gathering and recording accurate information includes being accurate about your own errors — SJTs frequently test whether you report a mistake promptly or quietly hope it goes unnoticed.
- **Contributing to the team** maps to your **Problem management** skill: contributing to the implementation of remedies and preventative measures means speaking up when you notice patterns, even as the most junior person in the room.
- **Working under direction** maps to your role level itself: supporting engineers under their direction means following guidance faithfully while still using your own eyes and voice — the assessment probes both halves of that balance.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — A task beyond your training**

A user asks you to fix an error on their laptop. You recognise the error from a knowledge article, but the fix involves changing system settings you have not been trained to change, and your mentor is at lunch. The user says: "Go on, just try it — how hard can it be?"

Which response is most effective?

- A) Attempt the fix — the knowledge article describes it, and users come first.
- B) Tell the user you cannot help and ask them to raise another ticket later.
- C) Explain that this fix needs a trained colleague, register the incident fully with the article reference attached, and let the user know when your mentor will be back and that you will hand it straight over.
- D) Ask the user to try changing the settings themselves while you read the article aloud.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** This scenario tests the core associate judgement: helpfulness within your boundaries. Option C does everything available to you at your level — it is honest with the user, registers the incident properly with the useful reference you found, and sets a clear expectation for handover. That is ownership done correctly: you do not personally fix it, but you make sure it will be fixed. Option A risks damaging a user's machine with an untrained change; user focus never means exceeding your competence on someone's working equipment. Option B abandons the user and creates pointless extra work for them — passing a problem to your team is your job, not the user's. Option D outsources the risk to the least qualified person present. Notice that the strong answer is not the fastest fix; it is the reliable path to a fix, with the user informed throughout.

**Question 2 (easy) — You make a mistake**

While updating the asset log, you realise you recorded yesterday's laptop issue against the wrong user — the laptop given to A. Begum is logged against a different person entirely. Nobody has noticed.

Which response is most effective, and which is least effective?

- A) Correct the record quietly — no harm done, no need to mention it.
- B) Correct the record, tell your mentor what happened and what you fixed, and double-check the other entries you made yesterday.
- C) Leave it — you are not sure how to edit records, and it might fix itself.
- D) Report it to your mentor but leave the correcting to someone more senior.

**Correct answer: most effective B; least effective C**

**Explanation:** Option B combines three good behaviours: fixing the error, being transparent about it, and — the mark of a genuinely careful engineer — checking whether the same slip happened elsewhere. Telling your mentor is not self-punishment; it lets the team judge whether the error had knock-on effects (for example, if the wrongly-logged laptop was reported lost) and it builds the trust that makes people happy to give you more responsibility. Option A fixes the data but hides the event, which means nobody checks for consequences and you learn to conceal rather than correct. Option D is honest but passive — correcting a record you created is within your remit, and bringing your mentor a fixed problem plus a report beats bringing an unfixed one. Option C is least effective: it leaves wrong data in a system others rely on and hopes, against all experience, that records repair themselves. In asset and incident data, silent errors compound; honest, prompt correction is the professional standard.

**Question 3 (easy) — A frustrated user at the desk**

A user arrives at the IT desk visibly angry: "This is the third time my laptop has crashed this week and nobody has done anything!" You check the system and see two previous tickets, both closed as "resolved" by different engineers.

Which response is most effective?

- A) Point out that both previous tickets were marked resolved, so the earlier fixes must have worked at the time.
- B) Listen to the user's account without interrupting, acknowledge the frustration of a recurring fault, register a new incident linking the two previous tickets, and flag the recurrence to your team so it can be investigated as a possible underlying problem.
- C) Apologise repeatedly and promise the laptop will be fixed today, whatever it takes.
- D) Explain that crashes are usually caused by users running too many programs at once.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B does justice to both the person and the problem. Listening first and acknowledging the frustration de-escalates — the user's anger is reasonable: three failures in a week is a poor experience regardless of what the ticket statuses say. Linking the previous tickets when you register the new one is exactly the recurring-pattern awareness your problem management skill asks for: three "resolved" crashes are one unresolved problem wearing three disguises. Flagging it to the team routes it to people who can find the underlying cause. Option A wins the argument and loses the user — quoting ticket statuses at an angry person is technically true and completely unhelpful. Option C soothes with a promise you cannot guarantee, storing up worse anger for later; never promise outcomes you do not control. Option D blames the user with a guess, which is both rude and evidence-free. The pattern to remember: acknowledge the person, capture the history, escalate the pattern.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Conflicting instructions**

Your mentor asked you this morning to spend the afternoon updating asset records "without fail — the audit is Friday." At 14:00, a different senior engineer tells you to drop everything and help them move equipment to another building, saying "the records can wait."

Which response is most effective?

- A) Follow the most recent instruction — the senior engineer outranks you.
- B) Refuse the senior engineer because your mentor's instruction came first.
- C) Briefly explain the conflict to the senior engineer — "my mentor asked me to finish the audit records today" — and if they still need you, quickly confirm the change of plan with your mentor before switching.
- D) Try to do both by moving equipment now and updating records after hours without telling anyone.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The trap in this scenario is treating it as a question about rank when it is really a question about communication. You have two legitimate instructions in conflict, and the only person who cannot correctly resolve that conflict alone is you — because you may not know what the audit deadline really allows or how urgent the equipment move truly is. Option C surfaces the conflict immediately and cheaply: one sentence to the senior engineer, and if needed one quick message to your mentor. Ninety seconds of communication protects both tasks and both relationships. Option A silently drops a deadline your mentor called "without fail" — the senior engineer probably does not know that, and you have not told them. Option B refuses help based on arrival order, which may get the priorities exactly backwards. Option D hides the conflict, commits you to unapproved extra hours, and means nobody with actual authority ever made the decision. Working under direction — your role level's defining feature — includes telling your directors when their directions collide.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Noticing a pattern nobody asked about**

While registering tickets, you notice that four incidents this week mention the same application freezing after a recent update — but each was logged by a different engineer, resolved individually with a restart, and closed. Nobody has connected them. You are the newest member of the team.

Which response is most effective, and which is least effective?

- A) Say nothing — the tickets are closed, the engineers are experienced, and it is not your place.
- B) Reopen all four tickets yourself and merge them into a problem record.
- C) Mention what you noticed to your mentor or in the team channel, with the four ticket references, and ask whether it is worth raising as a possible problem.
- D) Email the application's vendor directly to report a fault in their update.

**Correct answer: most effective C; least effective A**

**Explanation:** Option C is precisely what your role summary means by contributing to problem management: you investigated (noticed the pattern), you gathered evidence (four ticket references), and you passed the problem to your team through the right channel, with appropriate humility ("is it worth raising?"). Junior team members are often best placed to spot patterns because registration passes through their hands — the team benefits only if you speak. Option B has the right instinct but the wrong authority: reopening and merging records changes the team's data and is a decision for the problem process, not a trainee acting alone. Option D leaps over your entire team and organisation to an external party — vendor contact is a managed relationship, not a personal email. Option A is least effective: silence guarantees the restarts continue forever, and "not my place" is exactly wrong — noticing is everyone's place; deciding is the team's. The professional behaviour being scored here is ownership with humility: raise it, evidence it, and let the process decide.

**Question 6 (moderate) — A colleague's shortcut**

You are shadowing an engineer who resolves a user's issue by logging in with the user's password, which the user wrote on a sticky note "to save time." Afterwards, the engineer says: "I know we're not supposed to, but it's quicker, and everyone does it."

Which response is most effective?

- A) Say nothing — you are the trainee and they are teaching you.
- B) Publicly report the engineer to the security team immediately.
- C) Tell the engineer, privately and without accusation, that you are not comfortable working that way, and if the practice seems routine, raise the pattern with your mentor or team leader so it can be addressed properly.
- D) Adopt the shortcut yourself — consistency with the team matters most.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Password sharing breaches security policy in a way that puts users and the organisation at real risk — accounts stop being personal, audit trails stop meaning anything, and users learn terrible habits. But the scenario also involves relationships: this is a colleague who is otherwise helping you learn. Option C handles both truths. Speaking privately and without accusation preserves the relationship while making clear you will not copy the practice; raising a routine pattern ("everyone does it") with your mentor addresses the systemic issue through the proper channel rather than turning it into a personal report about one person. Option A makes you complicit through silence — and trainees who stay silent about small breaches find it ever harder to speak about big ones. Option B escalates to the most severe channel first, skipping every proportionate step, and will likely poison your standing in the team over something better fixed by a team-level reset. Option D is the worst learning outcome available: adopting a breach as a habit in your first months. The scored behaviours here are integrity and proportionate escalation — hold the line personally, and route the pattern upward calmly.

**Question 7 (moderate) — The queue and the walk-up**

You are alone at the walk-up desk for twenty minutes while your colleagues attend a stand-up. Three things happen at once: the phone rings; a user arrives at the desk holding a laptop; and a chat message arrives from a user saying "my screen froze mid-presentation, audience waiting!!".

Which response is most effective?

- A) Handle them strictly in arrival order: phone, then desk, then chat.
- B) Quickly triage all three: acknowledge the desk user ("with you in one moment"), check the chat — a live presentation failure is likely the most urgent — and let the phone go to voicemail or ask the caller to hold while you deal with the most time-critical issue first.
- C) Deal with the person physically present first — face-to-face always takes priority.
- D) Send the chat user a link to the self-service portal so you can focus on the phone and the desk.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This is prioritisation under pressure, and the key move in option B is triage before commitment: spend the first thirty seconds finding out what each contact needs, because urgency lives in the content, not the channel. The chat user has an audience waiting on a frozen screen — a live, minute-by-minute impact that outranks a laptop that can sit on the desk and a phone call of unknown content. Acknowledging the desk user costs five seconds and buys enormous goodwill; people wait far more happily once they have been seen. Option A and option C both apply channel rules ("arrival order," "face-to-face first") instead of judging impact — rules like that feel fair but ignore what is actually at stake. Option D deflects the most urgent user to self-service at their worst moment. Note also what B does not do: it does not attempt all three at once, which serves everyone badly. Judged triage, brief acknowledgements, and one issue at a time is how experienced desk engineers survive busy spells — and the behaviour SJTs reward.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Asked to skip the process**

A manager from another team approaches you directly: "My new starter begins Monday and has no laptop — the request got lost somewhere. Just grab one from the store for me now and we'll sort the paperwork next week. I'll take responsibility."

Which response is most effective?

- A) Hand over the laptop — a manager has accepted responsibility, and the new starter's first day matters.
- B) Refuse and walk away — process is process.
- C) Explain that laptops can only be issued through the asset process, then actively help: register the request yourself immediately, flag it urgent with the Monday start date, and alert your mentor or team lead who can expedite it properly the same day.
- D) Tell the manager to email the service desk and hope for the best.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The manager has a genuine problem and a bad solution. Issuing untracked equipment breaks asset control — the very skill of verifying the location and state of IT assets exists because "we'll sort the paperwork later" is how devices vanish, and "I'll take responsibility" does not transfer your accountability for following the process. But pure refusal (option B) fails the user too: somewhere behind this manager is a real new starter facing a first day without equipment. Option C is the professional middle path: hold the process line and make the process fast. Registering the request yourself, marking the urgency, and engaging your team lead converts a corridor favour into a legitimate expedited request — which most teams can genuinely fulfil by Monday. Option A trades a rule that protects the organisation for one person's convenience. Option D is technically compliant and practically useless — it hands the work back to the requester and adds a delay you could have removed. The scored behaviour: compliance with energy. Say no to the shortcut and yes to the outcome.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Honest uncertainty with a user**

You tell a user their software installation is complete. As they walk away, you realise you may have installed the wrong version — you are not certain, and checking will take about ten minutes. The user has already thanked you and left, and your shift ends in fifteen minutes.

Which response is most effective, and which is least effective?

- A) Say nothing — if it is wrong, they will raise another ticket, and it will be handled tomorrow.
- B) Check immediately; if the version is wrong, contact the user straight away, apologise, and either fix it now or arrange the earliest fix — recording the true status on the ticket before you leave.
- C) Leave a note for tomorrow's shift asking someone to check the version "when they get a chance."
- D) Message the user: "Please double-check the version yourself and let me know if there's a problem."

**Correct answer: most effective B; least effective A**

**Explanation:** The decisive fact is that you have specific reason to doubt your own work, and only you currently know it. Option B closes the loop while the knowledge is fresh and the cost is smallest: ten minutes of checking against a possible day of a user working with wrong software — or worse, the user discovering the error mid-task tomorrow and losing trust in the desk. Recording the true status matters just as much: tickets closed as "resolved" when you doubt the resolution corrupt the team's data and its first-time-fix figures. Option A is least effective because it knowingly converts your mistake into the user's problem and gambles their working day to protect fifteen minutes of yours. Option C dilutes ownership — "when they get a chance" carries no urgency and no accountability, and the note may never be actioned. Option D transfers your quality-checking duty to the person least equipped to perform it. The behaviour under test is ownership at the small scale: your role summary says you own an issue's honest state until it is genuinely resolved or handed over properly — and that includes the last fifteen minutes of a shift.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Inclusion at the desk**

A user with a visual impairment asks for help configuring their new laptop's screen reader and magnification settings. You have never configured assistive technology before. The user mentions, tiredly, that "IT always makes this take weeks."

Which response is most effective?

- A) Configure the settings by trial and error while the user waits — refusing would be discriminatory.
- B) Tell the user assistive technology is a specialist matter and they should contact the accessibility team themselves.
- C) Be honest that you have not configured these tools before, register the request with clear detail, find out whether your organisation has an assistive technology specialist or guide, and personally track the ticket so the user gets a named contact and does not have to chase — asking the user what setup has worked for them before.
- D) Give the user a loan laptop with default settings and suggest they manage without the tools for now.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Accessibility requests deserve the same energy as any urgent fault — and this user's weary comment tells you their past experience has been poor, which makes reliable follow-through the most valuable thing you can offer. Option C is honest about your limits (no experimenting on someone's essential tools), engages the right expertise, and — crucially — keeps you as the owner who tracks progress so the user is not bounced between teams yet again. Asking what has worked before treats the user as the expert in their own needs, which they usually are; experienced assistive technology users often know their exact configuration. Option A means well but gambles with tools the user depends on to work at all — trial and error on assistive technology can leave someone unable to use their machine. Option B hands the coordination burden to the user, repeating exactly the experience that exhausted them. Option D asks a disabled user to simply manage without their equipment, which no deadline pressure ever justifies. The behaviours scored: inclusion, honesty about competence, and ownership that survives handover — a combination your user focus skill will keep demanding throughout your career.

**Question 11 (challenging) — When help does not arrive**

You escalated a network fault affecting one meeting room to the senior engineers two days ago, following the process exactly. The ticket has not been picked up. Today a user books that room for an important external meeting tomorrow morning and asks you whether it will be working. Your mentor is on leave.

Which response is most effective?

- A) Assure the user it will probably be fine by tomorrow — escalated tickets usually get resolved quickly.
- B) Tell the user the room has a fault and there is nothing more you can do — the ticket is with another team.
- C) Chase the escalation actively today: speak to the senior team, explain the new deadline (external meeting, tomorrow morning), update the ticket with this urgency, and give the user an honest status plus a practical fallback — help them book an alternative room now in case the fix does not land in time.
- D) Attempt to fix the network fault yourself this evening when the office is quiet.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Escalating a ticket is not the end of ownership — your role summary's ownership skill says you show awareness of resolution processes and pass problems to your team, but passing on a problem still leaves you as the person who knows the user's situation. Option C re-energises the escalation with new information (the deadline did not exist two days ago; the senior team cannot prioritise urgency nobody told them about), keeps the record accurate, and — the part users remember — pairs an honest status with a concrete fallback. Booking an alternative room converts tomorrow from a gamble into a plan. Option A is a comfortable guess dressed as reassurance; if the room fails during an external meeting, your assurance becomes the story. Option B is technically true and a complete abdication — "it's with another team" helps nobody standing in front of you. Option D exceeds your training on infrastructure you do not own, alone, out of hours: three risks stacked on top of each other. The scored behaviours: persistent ownership, honest communication, and contingency thinking — the trio that turns a junior engineer into a trusted one.

### Preparation tips

- **Anchor every answer on the user and the truth.** Before choosing, ask two questions: "which option best protects the user's ability to work?" and "which option is fully honest?" The strongest response almost always scores well on both.
- **Know your level's boundaries.** At associate level, the scored judgement is usually escalate well, not solve alone. Escalating with evidence, urgency, and follow-through is a strong answer; heroic solo fixes beyond your training are weak ones, however brave they feel.
- **Look for the option that acts and communicates.** Weak options tend to do one without the other — silent action or talk without action. Strong options fix (or route) the problem and keep people informed.
- **Rehearse with real memories.** Recall situations from your own placements or shifts: a mistake you made, a conflict of instructions, an angry user. Ask yourself what the best version of your response would have been. Fresh personal examples calibrate judgement better than abstract rules.
- **Read scenarios slowly.** SJTs are rarely time-pressured, so use the time: small details (a deadline, a user's past experience, who is on leave) usually determine which option is best.
- **Talk options through with your mentor.** Discussing "what would you do if..." scenarios with experienced colleagues is the fastest way to absorb your team's real standards — and mentors generally enjoy being asked.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Choosing the heroic answer.** Options where you single-handedly fix something beyond your training feel impressive and score badly. Assessors read them as risk, not initiative.
- **Choosing the invisible answer.** Saying nothing, waiting, hoping, and "not my place" are the quiet failures. Noticing without raising helps nobody; at your level, your voice plus your team's authority is the winning combination.
- **Confusing honesty with harshness.** Being truthful with users does not mean quoting ticket statuses at angry people or listing everything that went wrong. The scored skill is honest and kind.
- **Over-escalating everything.** Passing problems to your team is right; passing every minor decision upward, or jumping to the most severe channel first, reads as avoidance. Match the escalation to the size of the issue.
- **Letting promises outrun control.** "It will definitely be fixed today" feels supportive and creates tomorrow's angrier user. Promise what you control: your actions, your updates, your follow-through.
- **Forgetting the least-effective half.** When a question asks for most and least effective, give the second choice real thought. Least-effective answers cluster around silence, blame, credential-sharing, abandoned users, and hidden mistakes — learn the clusters and you will spot them quickly.
