## Introduction

Welcome, and congratulations on beginning your career as an associate graphic designer in the UK Government Digital and Data profession. Let us take the fear out of these assessments first. Psychometric assessments are not intelligence tests. They are not designed to catch you out, and they are not a verdict on your talent. They are structured, standardised exercises that measure the everyday thinking a particular job actually uses. For you that means checking a layout against a specification and spotting where it diverges, noticing that a colour pairing will fail for someone with low vision, working out what a set of numbers about a page is telling you, reading a standard carefully and applying it, and making sensible decisions when a stakeholder asks for something that would make a design harder to read. None of that is exotic. It is the ordinary, careful thinking that good graphic design rests on.

Your role level shapes this whole guide. As an associate graphic designer you are a trainee in an entry-level position, working under supervision, expected to show aptitude, potential and an understanding of the role. Nobody assessing you at this level expects you to have built a design system or negotiated with a communications directorate over a brand. What they are looking for is exactly what your role description names: aptitude, potential, understanding. Every rule, value and figure you need appears inside the question — you never have to have memorised a contrast ratio or a type scale.

There is one thing worth saying about graphic design in government specifically. Your role description defines it as creating the graphic elements that underpin interaction and service design — using layout, spacing, colour, type and iconography to ensure content is legible and readable and that users see and understand interactions. That is a functional definition, and it is deliberate. In this context a design decision is not a matter of preference: a type size, a contrast ratio, a spacing value or an icon either lets somebody complete a task or prevents them, and the difference is measurable. A surprising amount of what these assessments measure is the habit of treating visual decisions as decisions with consequences rather than as taste.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the thinking your role genuinely demands, using practical workplace materials rather than abstract shapes and sequences. Instead of rotating cubes, you will work with what an associate graphic designer actually handles — a layout against a grid specification, a type scale, a set of colour values, an icon set, an extract from an accessibility standard, a list of research observations.

The typical format is an online test lasting fifteen to thirty minutes. Questions cluster into five families: pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective, comparing your answers against a norm group at a similar level. Many platforms are adaptive, so questions get harder after correct answers — if the test suddenly feels difficult, that usually means you are doing well. And most employers receive an acuity report showing speed against accuracy, which means careful work is rewarded.

For an associate graphic designer this section matters because so much of your early value comes from precision. You notice that a spacing value is 14 pixels where the system uses multiples of 8. You notice that a grey passes contrast against white but fails against the light grey panel it sits on. You notice that two icons in the same set are drawn at different optical weights. You notice that a heading is smaller than the body text below it. Each is a small act of comparison, and each protects whether somebody can read the thing.

One reassurance. At your level, employers are looking for aptitude, not memorised values. You will never be asked to recall a contrast ratio threshold from memory. Every value you need is stated in the question. Your job is to apply stated rules carefully — exactly what you do at work when a senior designer gives you a specification and you work to it.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The cognitive dimensions map onto the skills named in your role summary:

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **Designing strategically**, where you explain the value of using patterns and components in design. Recognising when something follows a system — and when it quietly departs from one — is where that starts.
- **Error checking** maps to **Designing for everyone**, where you explain the importance of inclusive and accessible design and describe different access needs. A contrast failure or an undersized touch target is an error with a human consequence.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Evidence-based design**, where you frame ideas as design hypotheses to be tested.
- **Prioritisation** maps to **Iterative design**, where you describe agile methodologies and how iteration supports user-centred design.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Iterative design** again, where you describe prototyping at different fidelities, and to **Designing together**.
- **Explaining a visual decision** maps to **Design communication**, where you explain the importance of articulating design decisions and describe methods for doing so.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Error checking against a spacing system**

Your design system uses a spacing scale of multiples of 8 pixels only. You review a layout with these gaps: 16, 24, 20, 32, 8. How many values break the rule?

- A) 0
- B) 1
- C) 2
- D) 3

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Check each against the rule. 16 is 8 × 2, 24 is 8 × 3, 32 is 8 × 4 and 8 is 8 × 1 — all compliant. 20 is not a multiple of 8, since 8 × 2 is 16 and 8 × 3 is 24, so it is the only breach. The answer is B. This is a straightforward check and it is worth noticing why the rule exists: a spacing scale is what makes a set of pages look like one service rather than several, and a single off-scale value is usually a sign that somebody nudged something by eye to fix a local problem. That is why the useful follow-up question is not "which value is wrong" but "what was that 20 trying to solve", because the answer is often a genuine layout problem that needs a proper solution rather than a nudge.

**Question 2 (easy) — Logical deduction from a design rule**

Your team's rule states: "A custom icon may be used only if no suitable icon exists in the design system, unless the icon has been approved by the lead graphic designer." A colleague says: "We used a custom icon last week, and there is a suitable one in the design system." Which one must be true?

- A) The rule was broken.
- B) The icon was approved by the lead graphic designer.
- C) Either the icon was approved by the lead, or the rule was broken.
- D) The custom icon is worse than the system icon.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The rule is a conditional with one permitted exception, and the facts do not tell you whether the exception applies. Option A assumes it does not; option B assumes it does. Option D introduces a quality judgement nothing supports — a custom icon may be better than the system one, which is sometimes exactly why an exception is approved. Only C is guaranteed. This structure recurs constantly in assessments, and the workplace parallel is worth keeping: before suggesting a colleague broke a rule, ask which route was used, because if the lead approved it you will be glad you asked rather than told.

**Question 3 (easy) — Pattern recognition in a type scale**

A type scale runs: 14, 16, 19, 24, 30, 36. A designer proposes adding a size between 19 and 24. Which value fits the pattern of the existing scale most closely?

- A) 20
- B) 21
- C) 22
- D) The scale has no consistent pattern

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Look at the ratios between consecutive steps rather than the differences. 16 ÷ 14 = 1.14; 19 ÷ 16 = 1.19; 24 ÷ 19 = 1.26; 30 ÷ 24 = 1.25; 36 ÷ 30 = 1.2. The scale is roughly geometric with a ratio somewhere around 1.2, rather than arithmetic with a fixed gap. A new step between 19 and 24 sitting on that pattern would be about 19 × 1.1 ≈ 21, which is also close to the geometric mean of 19 and 24, around 21.4. So B fits best. Option D is the tempting answer for anyone who looks at the differences — 2, 3, 5, 6, 6 — and sees no pattern. Type scales are almost always ratio-based rather than difference-based, because apparent size difference is proportional, and recognising that is a useful piece of professional literacy.

**Question 4 (easy) — Prioritisation in a design morning**

It is 9:30 am. Four things need attention:

1. A live page has an error message in light grey on white that several users have reported being unable to read.
2. Your senior designer asked for icon exploration by Wednesday.
3. The design critique is at 11:00 and you agreed to bring your work.
4. A newsletter about a design conference has arrived.

What should you do first?

- A) Start the icon exploration while the morning is quiet.
- B) Raise or fix the unreadable error message.
- C) Get your work ready for 11:00.
- D) Read the newsletter.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Prioritisation questions ask you to weigh impact and urgency together. Item 1 is both: an error message people cannot read means users are stuck at a point where something has already gone wrong for them, which is the worst possible place to lose someone, and it is affecting people today. That outranks everything else. As an associate you may not deploy the fix yourself — the right first action might be to alert your senior designer with the specific colour values — but either way it comes first. Item 3 is time-bound and takes minutes. Item 2 has until Wednesday. Item 4 is least urgent. The pattern to remember: harm to users outranks internal commitments, and an error message nobody can read is a particularly sharp form of harm.

**Question 5 (easy) — Error checking against instructions**

A senior designer asks you to make exactly three changes to a layout: (i) increase body text from 16 to 19 pixels, (ii) change the link colour to the system blue, and (iii) add 8 pixels of space above each heading. You get the file back from a colleague and find: body text is 19, links are system blue, headings have 8 pixels above, and the page margin has been reduced from 24 to 16 pixels. How many of the changes made were requested?

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Count carefully. Four changes were made, but the question asks how many were *requested*. Changes (i), (ii) and (iii) were all requested and all done — three. The margin change was not on the list. The answer is 3. This mirrors an important habit: when you work after being given direction, you deliver exactly what was agreed and flag anything else. The margin change is worth flagging for a specific reason too — reducing a page margin from 24 to 16 affects every page using that template, and it may have been done to accommodate the extra heading space, in which case the colleague has solved a local problem by changing a global value, which is exactly the kind of change that should be discussed rather than made.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Deduction about contrast**

A standard states: "Text smaller than 24 pixels must have a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 against its background. Text 24 pixels or larger, or 19 pixels or larger and bold, must have a contrast ratio of at least 3:1." A designer uses 19 pixel bold text at a contrast ratio of 3.5:1. Which must be true?

- A) It fails the standard.
- B) It meets the standard.
- C) It meets the standard only if the text is also 24 pixels.
- D) The standard does not cover this case.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Work through the conditions. The text is 19 pixels and bold, which places it in the second category — "19 pixels or larger and bold" — where the requirement is 3:1. Its ratio is 3.5:1, which exceeds 3:1, so it meets the standard, giving B. Option A applies the 4.5:1 threshold, which belongs to the first category and does not apply here. Option C misreads the second category as requiring both conditions when it offers two alternative routes into it, separated by "or". Option D overlooks that the case is explicitly covered. The habit being tested is reading a conditional standard precisely, and the specific trap — reading "or" as "and" — is one of the most common errors in applying accessibility criteria, usually in the direction of over-compliance, which wastes effort and makes the standard look unreasonable.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Pattern recognition across screen sizes**

A page's completion rate by viewport width:

| Width | Users | Completed |
|---|---|---|
| Under 400px | 6,000 | 3,000 |
| 400–768px | 9,000 | 7,200 |
| Over 768px | 5,000 | 4,500 |

What does the pattern most strongly suggest?

- A) The page works well overall.
- B) Completion falls sharply below 400 pixels — 50% against 80% and 90% — which points at something in the layout that breaks at the narrowest widths rather than at mobile generally.
- C) Mobile users find the page difficult.
- D) The page should be redesigned for desktop.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Convert to rates: under 400px is 3,000 ÷ 6,000 = 50%; 400–768px is 7,200 ÷ 9,000 = 80%; over 768px is 4,500 ÷ 5,000 = 90%. The interesting feature is not that narrower is worse but *how sharply* the narrowest band drops — the gap between the two mobile bands, 30 points, is three times the gap between the wider two. That pattern suggests a specific breakage rather than a general difficulty, and specific breakages have specific causes: a table that will not reflow, a fixed-width element forcing horizontal scrolling, a button pushed off-screen. Option B says this. Option C describes mobile generally and would send you to redesign the band that performs at 80%. Option A ignores the finding. Option D reads the table backwards.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Error checking an icon set**

Your standard says icons in a set must: be drawn on the same grid, use the same stroke weight, be legible at 16 pixels, and never be the sole indicator of meaning. You review a set where all icons are on a 24-pixel grid, all use a 2-pixel stroke, three of eight are indistinguishable at 16 pixels, and all appear with text labels. How many parts of the standard are unmet?

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Check each requirement. Same grid — met. Same stroke weight — met. Legible at 16 pixels — three of eight fail, so this requirement is unmet. Never the sole indicator — all icons have text labels, so met. One requirement unmet, giving A. Option C is the trap for anyone who counts the three failing icons rather than the one failing requirement, which is the standard confusion between counting items and counting rules. Note also which requirement fails and why it matters: icons that are indistinguishable at their smallest intended size are decoration rather than communication, and the fact that labels are present means users can still proceed — which is exactly why the labelling requirement exists, and exactly why the legibility failure will go unnoticed until somebody removes the labels to save space.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Applied problem solving in a critique**

In a design critique, two colleagues are debating whether a button should be green or blue. You noticed while preparing that the button's text is 14 pixels at a contrast ratio of 3.2:1, which would fail the 4.5:1 requirement for text that size. You are the most junior person present. What is the most sensible thing to do?

- A) Say nothing; the colour debate will resolve itself.
- B) Wait for a pause and raise it as a question — "can I check something? The label is 14 pixels at about 3.2:1, which I think would fail the small-text requirement whichever colour we pick" — bringing the room to a decision the colour choice depends on.
- C) Interrupt to say the colour debate is irrelevant.
- D) Mention it to your senior designer afterwards.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** You have noticed something that constrains the discussion under way rather than being separate from it, which is what makes raising it useful rather than a change of subject. Option B is phrased in the strongest available way for someone at your level: it waits for a pause, states specific values, and frames the observation as a question. It also makes the connection explicit — the contrast requirement applies whichever colour wins, so the debate cannot conclude without it. Option A wastes the observation and lets a critique end on a design that will fail. Option C is likely to be received badly and puts you in conflict with colleagues over the way they are spending their time. Option D is a reasonable backup and misses the moment when the decision is being made.

**Question 10 (moderate) — Deduction about what evidence supports**

In research with eight participants, six could not tell that a section of a page was interactive. Which conclusion is best supported by this evidence alone?

- A) 75% of users cannot tell the section is interactive.
- B) The section should be given a border.
- C) Six of eight participants did not recognise the section as interactive, which is a strong signal that its visual treatment does not signal interactivity — and worth testing alternatives such as a clearer affordance, a change of background, or a conventional control.
- D) The design is bad.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Six of eight is a strong qualitative signal, and C states it at that strength while identifying the mechanism the evidence points at: the visual treatment is not signalling interactivity. That framing matters because it names the problem rather than the solution, and the solution space is genuinely wide — a border, a background change, a shadow, or simply using a standard control instead of a custom one. Option A converts eight participants into a population rate, the commonest overreach in design reporting. Option B jumps to one solution, and it may be the wrong one; a border makes something look like a container as often as a control. Option D is unhelpful to whoever has to act on it. Framing ideas as testable hypotheses is a named skill in your role, and C is what a well-framed one looks like.

**Question 11 (harder) — Prioritisation with competing pressures**

Your senior designer is away. At 10:00 the following are true:

1. A developer needs your final colour values before they can finish a story today.
2. The design critique is at 14:00 and your work is not ready.
3. A content designer needs your confirmation of heading sizes by 12:00 to finalise a template.
4. A colleague has asked you to review their icons this week.

What is the most sensible order?

- A) 2, 3, 1, 4 — your own commitments first.
- B) 1, 3, 2, 4 — unblock the developer, then the content designer, then your own work, then the review.
- C) 3, 1, 2, 4 — deadlines in time order.
- D) 1, 2, 3, 4 — as listed.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Rank by consequence and deadline together. Item 1 blocks another person's work today and probably takes five minutes; leaving a developer to pick colour values themselves is how off-system colours enter a codebase, where they are far harder to remove than to prevent. Item 3 has a hard 12:00 deadline and also blocks somebody, and heading sizes going into a template means the decision propagates to every page using it. Item 2 is your own commitment with a 14:00 deadline and can absorb the earlier interruptions. Item 4 has a week. Option C is defensible and puts a 12:00 task ahead of a five-minute item blocking someone now. Option A ranks by ownership rather than consequence, which is the classic trap.

**Question 12 (harder) — Applied problem solving about brand and accessibility**

A communications team requires a brand colour to be used for body text. The colour gives a contrast ratio of 3.9:1 against white, below the 4.5:1 required for text under 24 pixels. What is the strongest analysis?

- A) The brand colour must be used, so nothing can be done.
- B) The requirement is that the brand is recognisable, not that a specific value appears in every element — so the useful questions are whether a darker variant of the same hue would satisfy both, and whether the brand colour could be used for large headings, where 3:1 applies, while body text uses a compliant tone.
- C) The brand colour should be abandoned.
- D) The text should be made larger so the 3:1 threshold applies.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The strongest analysis separates the brand's actual requirement from the specific instruction, and then finds the route that satisfies both. Brands are recognisable through a palette, a typeface, a tone and a set of proportions, not through one colour value appearing in body text — and most brand palettes already contain a darker variant of the primary hue, precisely because the primary is usually optimised for impact rather than legibility. Option B proposes checking that and also identifies the legitimate place where the brand colour can appear at full strength, which is large headings where the threshold is lower. Option A accepts a conflict nobody has tested. Option C is disproportionate and will be refused. Option D is the dangerous answer: enlarging body text purely to escape a contrast threshold is gaming the criterion rather than meeting its purpose, and it produces a page of oversized, hard-to-scan text that is worse for everyone.

### Preparation tips

- **Practise on real files.** Take a layout and check every spacing value against the system scale. This is exactly the comparison muscle the assessment measures.
- **Look at ratios, not differences, in type scales.** Apparent size difference is proportional.
- **Read conditional standards precisely.** "Or" offers two routes; "and" requires both.
- **Decide what you are counting.** Icons, requirements, participants, values — the question always specifies one.
- **Check contrast against the actual background.** A grey that passes on white may fail on a panel.
- **Ask what an off-system value was solving.** A nudge usually hides a real layout problem.
- **Do a timed drill.** Ten minutes, four questions. You are practising composure, not speed.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Counting failing items instead of failing requirements.**
- **Reading "or" as "and" in an accessibility criterion.** It produces over-compliance and makes standards look unreasonable.
- **Comparing raw counts across groups of different sizes.** Convert to rates.
- **Confusing "could be true" with "must be true".**
- **Ranking by ownership rather than consequence.**
- **Jumping to one visual solution.** Name the problem; the solution space is usually wider than it looks.
- **Gaming a threshold rather than meeting its purpose.** Enlarging text to escape a contrast rule helps nobody.
- **Panicking when questions get harder.** On adaptive tests, that usually means you are doing well.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A numeric reasoning assessment measures how well you work with the numbers your job produces. This is the section people worry about most, and graphic designers often worry about it more than most, on the assumption that a visual discipline is a non-numerical one. That assumption is worth examining, because graphic design is unusually full of numbers: contrast ratios, type scales, line lengths, spacing units, grid columns, optical sizes, viewport widths. Almost every decision you make has a value attached, and a great many of them have thresholds you can pass or fail.

Let us be clear about what the test is not. It is not a maths exam. There are no equations to solve and no formulae to memorise. Most platforms provide a calculator, and where they do not the arithmetic is deliberately simple. What is measured is whether you can read a table correctly, pick the right numbers out of a cluttered display, choose the right operation, and interpret the result sensibly.

The format is usually a timed online test of fifteen to thirty minutes, each item showing a small table, chart or paragraph of figures followed by a multiple-choice question. Time per item is tight, often around a minute, so the real skill is finding the relevant numbers quickly and ignoring the rest.

At associate level the content is modest: percentages, ratios, proportions, simple averages, and reading values from tables. Four techniques carry you through. Work out what the question is asking before looking at the data. Identify the two or three numbers you need. Do the arithmetic one step at a time. Then sanity-check.

One note about the options. Wrong answers are rarely random — they are the results of specific predictable mistakes, so finding your answer among them is not proof that you are right.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The numerical skills tested map onto your named skills:

- **Working with ratios** maps to **Designing for everyone**, where contrast ratios and type relationships are expressed as ratios and misreading one produces an inaccessible design.
- **Reading tables accurately** maps to **Evidence-based design**, where you identify common sources and types of data informing design.
- **Percentages and proportions** map to **Evidence-based design** again, and to **Designing for everyone**, where comparing how different groups fare is how exclusion becomes visible.
- **Understanding scale** maps to **Designing strategically**, where knowing how many people a decision affects is what makes a case for it.
- **Interpreting figures cautiously** maps to **Design communication**, where a decision justified by a misread number is hard to defend twice.
- **Working with proportional systems** maps to **Designing strategically**, where the value of patterns and components lies partly in their internal consistency.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — A completion rate**

Of 2,400 users who reached a page, 1,800 completed it. What percentage completed?

- A) 65%
- B) 70%
- C) 75%
- D) 80%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 1,800 ÷ 2,400 = 0.75, so 75%, and the answer is C. Estimate first as insurance — 1,800 of 2,400 is three quarters, since 1,200 would be half. Options B and D come from dividing by 2,571 and 2,250, the kind of slip a mis-keyed calculator produces. The three-second estimate before every calculation is the cheapest error insurance in this section, and it costs almost nothing under a clock.

**Question 2 (easy) — Reading a contrast ratio**

A standard requires 4.5:1 for text under 24 pixels. Four colour pairings measure 3.1:1, 4.4:1, 4.5:1 and 7.2:1. How many meet the requirement for 16-pixel text?

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The requirement is "at least 4.5:1", so a pairing meets it if its ratio is 4.5 or higher. 3.1 fails. 4.4 fails — and this is the one that catches people, because 4.4 is so close to the threshold that it feels like it should pass, and a designer who rounds it in their head to "about 4.5" has just approved a failing combination. 4.5 meets it exactly, and "at least" includes the boundary. 7.2 passes comfortably. Two pass, giving B. The boundary reading matters in practice as well as in the test: contrast checkers report to one decimal place, and 4.4 is a fail while 4.5 is a pass, with no discretion in between.

**Question 3 (easy) — A type scale ratio**

A type scale steps from 16 to 20 pixels. What is the ratio between the two sizes, to two decimal places?

- A) 1.20
- B) 1.25
- C) 1.33
- D) 0.80

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The ratio of the larger to the smaller is 20 ÷ 16 = 1.25, giving B. Option A is the difference of 4 expressed as a proportion of 20 rather than 16 — that is, 4 ÷ 20 — which is the same denominator error that appears in percentage change questions. Option D inverts the ratio, giving the smaller as a proportion of the larger, which is a true statement answering a different question. Type scales are usually described by their ratio, and common values sit between about 1.125 and 1.618, so recognising 1.25 as a "major third" style scale is a useful piece of literacy — though the arithmetic is the tested part and the vocabulary is a bonus.

**Question 4 (easy) — Line length**

A column is 640 pixels wide and the body text is 16 pixels, with an average character width of 8 pixels. Roughly how many characters fit on a line?

- A) 40
- B) 60
- C) 80
- D) 100

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 640 ÷ 8 = 80 characters, giving C. The reason this calculation is worth knowing is that comfortable line length for continuous reading is widely held to sit between about 45 and 75 characters, so 80 is at or just past the upper edge — which means the arithmetic does not just produce a number, it produces a design judgement. If the column were 800 pixels the line would run to 100 characters, which is uncomfortably long, and the fix is either a narrower measure or a larger type size, since increasing type size reduces characters per line at a fixed width. Being able to do that calculation in your head is what lets you say something specific in a critique rather than "that feels a bit wide".

**Question 5 (easy) — Scale of a decision**

A page is viewed 600,000 times a year. A contrast failure on its error message affects an estimated 4% of viewers who encounter the error. If 10% of viewers encounter the error, how many people a year are affected?

- A) 2,400
- B) 24,000
- C) 60,000
- D) 240,000

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Work in two steps. 10% of 600,000 viewers encounter the error, which is 60,000 people. Of those, 4% are affected by the contrast failure: 4% of 60,000 = 2,400. So A. Option C stops after the first step, which is the most common error in two-stage questions because the first calculation feels complete. The reason this matters beyond arithmetic is that 2,400 people a year unable to read an error message is a figure that gets a colour changed, whereas "the error message contrast is a bit low" does not. Converting a design defect into people is one of the most effective moves available to a designer, and it takes ten seconds.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Comparing groups fairly**

Of 400 users on screens wider than 768 pixels, 360 completed a task. Of 250 users on screens narrower than 400 pixels, 125 completed. Which statement is correct?

- A) Both groups completed at similar rates.
- B) The rate was 90% on wide screens and 50% on narrow screens.
- C) Wide-screen users completed 235 more tasks, so the difference is modest.
- D) The groups cannot be compared because they are different sizes.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Convert each to a rate so the different group sizes stop distorting the comparison. Wide: 360 ÷ 400 = 0.9, which is 90%. Narrow: 125 ÷ 250 = 0.5, which is 50%. Option B states both correctly. Option C compares raw counts across unequal groups, which is the error the question is built to catch. Option D gets it backwards — converting to rates is precisely how unequal groups are compared fairly. A 40 percentage point gap is enormous, and for a graphic designer the likely suspects are specific and checkable: text that reflows into unreadable line lengths, touch targets too close together, a fixed-width element forcing horizontal scrolling, or a layout that pushes the primary action below the fold.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Percentage change**

After a type size increase, the number of users completing a form rose from 720 to 900 a week. What is the percentage increase?

- A) 20%
- B) 25%
- C) 80%
- D) 125%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Percentage change divides the difference by the original value. The difference is 900 − 720 = 180, and 180 ÷ 720 = 0.25, a 25% increase, giving B. Option A is 180 ÷ 900, dividing by the new figure — the most common error in this question type, and one that under-reports every improvement you make, which matters when you are trying to demonstrate that a design change worked. Option D expresses the new figure as a proportion of the old. The rule: for percentage change, the denominator is always where you started from.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Working back to a total**

A report says 15% of users abandon a page, and that this is 4,500 people a month. How many reach the page each month?

- A) 5,175
- B) 6,750
- C) 30,000
- D) 67,500

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** If 15% equals 4,500, the total is 4,500 ÷ 0.15 = 30,000, giving C. Stepwise: 15% is 4,500, so 5% is 1,500, and 100% is 30,000. Verify by reversing — 15% of 30,000 is 4,500. Option A adds 15%; option B is 4,500 × 1.5. This matters because colleagues quote percentages far more readily than base numbers, and 15% of 30,000 is a very different problem from 15% of 300. Establishing the base in ten seconds lets you judge whether a figure deserves the weight it is being given.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Proportional grid**

A 12-column grid spans 960 pixels with 24-pixel gutters between columns. Ignoring outer margins, what is the width of one column?

- A) 56 pixels
- B) 58 pixels
- C) 64 pixels
- D) 80 pixels

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** With 12 columns there are 11 gutters between them, not 12, because gutters sit between columns rather than after each one. The gutters take 11 × 24 = 264 pixels, leaving 960 − 264 = 696 pixels for the columns, and 696 ÷ 12 = 58 pixels each. So B. Option A follows from assuming 12 gutters: 960 − 288 = 672, and 672 ÷ 12 = 56. Option D is 960 ÷ 12, ignoring gutters altogether, which is the commonest error and produces a grid that visibly does not add up. The off-by-one is worth fixing permanently: for n columns there are n − 1 internal gutters, and getting it wrong once in a design system definition confuses everyone who uses it afterwards, because the numbers will never quite reconcile and nobody will be able to say why.

**Question 10 (moderate) — Choosing the right denominator**

A journey shows: 20,000 users start, 16,000 reach a form, 12,000 reach the review page, 11,400 submit. What percentage of users who reached the review page submitted?

- A) 57%
- B) 60%
- C) 71%
- D) 95%

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Identify the group the question names before touching the figures. It asks about users who reached the review page, so the whole is 12,000 and the part is 11,400: 11,400 ÷ 12,000 = 0.95, which is 95%, giving D. Option A uses 20,000, answering a whole-journey question nobody asked. This is the most common trap in funnel data and it determines where design effort goes: 95% of people reaching the review page submit, so the review page is not the problem. The losses are 4,000 between the start and the form, and 4,000 between the form and review — which is where a layout or legibility problem would be worth looking for.

**Question 11 (harder) — Percentage points versus percentages**

Before a redesign, 40% of users completed a task on narrow screens. Afterwards, 52% did. Which statement is accurate?

- A) Completion rose by 12%.
- B) Completion rose by 12 percentage points, which is a 30% increase.
- C) Completion rose by 30 percentage points.
- D) Completion rose by 23%.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The arithmetic gap between two percentages is measured in percentage points: 52 − 40 = 12 percentage points. The relative increase is a separate calculation: 12 ÷ 40 = 0.3, a 30% increase. Option B labels both correctly. Option A uses "12%" loosely, which is ambiguous and usually read as a relative change. Option C misapplies the percentage-point label. Option D divides by the new value. Why this matters beyond the test: "completion rose 30%" and "rose 12 percentage points" describe the identical change, and the first sounds two and a half times more impressive. Being precise about which you mean is part of articulating a design decision honestly, and a colleague who later spots that the flattering figure was chosen will discount everything else you claim.

**Question 12 (harder) — When an average hides something**

Seven users took the following times, in seconds, to find a link: 3, 4, 5, 5, 6, 8, and 84. A colleague reports "the average was 16 seconds". What is the best comment?

- A) The calculation is wrong; the average is 5 seconds.
- B) The calculation is right, but the mean is misleading: six of seven users found the link within eight seconds and one extreme case is pulling the figure up. Report the median of 5 seconds with the range, and treat the 84-second session as the most informative thing in the set.
- C) The figure is fine as reported.
- D) The 84-second case should be excluded.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Check the arithmetic first: the values sum to 115, and 115 ÷ 7 = 16.4, so about 16 is correct and option A is wrong. But no user took anywhere near 16 seconds — six finished within eight, one took 84. The median, the fourth value in the ordered list, is 5, which describes the group well. Option B says this and identifies why the outlier matters most: something about the design defeated that person entirely for well over a minute, and for a graphic designer that is the session worth watching, because it usually reveals a contrast, size or hierarchy failure that the other six worked around without noticing. Option C lets a misleading summary stand. Option D discards the most useful data point in the set.

### Preparation tips

- **Estimate before you calculate.** Three seconds of "about three quarters" catches most serious errors.
- **Read thresholds at the boundary.** "At least 4.5:1" means 4.4 fails and 4.5 passes.
- **Find the denominator first.** Underline the group the question names.
- **Watch for two-stage questions.** Stopping after the first calculation is the commonest error.
- **Remember n − 1 gutters for n columns.** The off-by-one produces grids that do not add up.
- **Convert design defects into people.** "2,400 people a year" gets a colour changed; "the contrast is a bit low" does not.
- **Learn the percentage point distinction.** It decides how your own improvements get reported.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Rounding a contrast ratio in your head.** 4.4 is a fail.
- **Dividing by the wrong total.** Funnel questions are built around this.
- **Confusing percentages with percentage points.**
- **Comparing raw counts across groups of different sizes.**
- **Stopping after the first step of a two-stage calculation.**
- **Assuming a type scale is arithmetic.** It is almost always a ratio.
- **Reporting a mean without checking the spread.**
- **Assuming a visual discipline is a non-numerical one.** Almost every decision you make has a value attached.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately you read. That may feel further from your daily work than the other sections, and it is not: the documents that constrain graphic design in government are unusually precise. Accessibility criteria specify ratios and thresholds. Brand guidelines specify permitted and prohibited uses. Design system documentation specifies when a component may and may not be used. Reading any of those slightly wrong produces a design that fails an audit or a fight you did not need to have.

The classic format presents a short passage — typically three to six sentences of dense workplace prose — followed by statements you must classify as True, False or Cannot Say. True means the statement follows necessarily. False means the passage contradicts it. Cannot Say means the passage neither confirms nor contradicts it. Alongside these you will meet comprehension questions, main-point questions, and questions asking which of four summaries is most accurate.

The golden rule is that you answer only from the passage. Everything you know about accessibility standards, everything your team does, everything obviously true — set it aside. If the passage contradicts what you believe, the passage still wins. This is the biggest single source of lost marks.

Cannot Say deserves particular attention because people avoid it. It feels evasive, so candidates reach for True or False to appear decisive. In practice it is frequently correct.

Small words carry the meaning. "All" is not "most". "May" is not "must". "Should" is not "will". "Or" is not "and" — and that one matters more in your discipline than in most, because accessibility criteria routinely offer alternative routes to compliance separated by "or", and reading them as conjunctions produces unnecessary work and unnecessary arguments.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The verbal skills tested map onto your named skills:

- **Reading precisely** maps to **Designing for everyone**, where you explain the importance of accessible design and describe different access needs. Accessibility requirements are written documents.
- **Distinguishing statement from inference** maps to **Evidence-based design**, where you frame ideas as hypotheses.
- **Identifying the main point** maps to **Design communication**, where you describe methods for explaining design decisions.
- **Understanding qualified language** maps to **Designing strategically**, where knowing whether a pattern is mandatory or recommended tells you how much room you have.
- **Reading for what is absent** maps to **Designing for everyone**, where noticing that a specification never mentions a minimum size has a direct consequence.
- **Following written direction exactly** maps to **Designing together**, where feedback only works if understood as given.

### Practice questions

*Passage A — for Questions 1 to 4*

"Text smaller than 24 pixels must have a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 against its background. Text 24 pixels or larger, or 19 pixels or larger and bold, must have a contrast ratio of at least 3:1. Logotypes are exempt from these requirements. Decorative images that convey no information are exempt, although any text within an image is not."

**Question 1 (easy)** — Statement: "All text must have a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The passage sets a lower threshold of 3:1 for large text and bold text at 19 pixels or above, and exempts logotypes entirely. The statement's "all" contradicts both, so False. This is the most reliable pattern in the section — an absolute term in the statement, exceptions in the passage. It is also a genuinely common misquotation in practice: "everything needs 4.5:1" is the version most people carry in their heads, and applying it to a large heading produces a needlessly dark palette and an argument with whoever chose the brand.

**Question 2 (easy)** — Statement: "Text inside a decorative image is exempt from the contrast requirements." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The final sentence exempts decorative images conveying no information and then states explicitly that any text within an image is not exempt. The statement contradicts this, so False. The item tests whether you read to the end of a sentence that appeared to have finished its work — the clause after "although" reverses the exemption for the specific case the statement asks about. Standards routinely place the qualification in exactly that position, and it is worth slowing down rather than speeding up when you see "although", "except" or "provided that" arriving late in a sentence.

**Question 3 (moderate)** — Statement: "Bold text at 19 pixels needs a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The second sentence sets 3:1 for text that is "24 pixels or larger, **or** 19 pixels or larger and bold". Bold text at 19 pixels satisfies the second route, so the applicable threshold is 3:1, not 4.5:1, and the statement is contradicted. False. This is the "or" trap, and it is the single most consequential misreading in applying contrast criteria. Read as "and", the sentence would require text to be both 24 pixels and bold, which would make the whole clause almost useless and push designers towards unnecessarily dark palettes. Read as written, it recognises that large text and bold text are both easier to read, and either qualifies.

**Question 4 (moderate)** — Statement: "A logotype containing text must meet the 4.5:1 requirement." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** Two provisions bear on this and they point in opposite directions. Logotypes are exempt from the requirements. Text within an image is not exempt. A logotype containing text sits at the intersection, and the passage does not say which provision governs. Because it is genuinely undetermined by the text, the answer is Cannot Say. This is a harder item than it looks and it reflects a real ambiguity that arises constantly in practice — which is why real standards usually address logotypes explicitly. In the workplace the right response is to seek the clarification the document does not give; in the test, the absence of an answer is the answer.

*Passage B — for Questions 5 to 8*

"The review of the service found that users on screens narrower than 400 pixels completed tasks less often than users on wider screens. The review notes that these users also differed in other respects, including connection speed and whether they were using the service away from home. The review did not assess whether the layout reflowed correctly at narrow widths. It recommends that the team consider whether a narrow-width review would be worthwhile."

**Question 5 (easy)** — Statement: "Users on screens narrower than 400 pixels completed tasks less often." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The first sentence states this directly and the statement restates it without addition. True. Note the passage's comparative framing — "less often than users on wider screens" — and the statement respects it. Answer quickly and bank the time for the harder items.

**Question 6 (moderate)** — Statement: "The narrow layout causes users to fail tasks." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The passage reports an association and explicitly names two ways the groups differed: connection speed and whether they were away from home. Either could explain the difference — someone on a slow connection away from home may be interrupted, may not have documents to hand, or may abandon before the page loads, all independently of how the layout behaves. The passage neither asserts nor rules out a layout cause, so Cannot Say. The design consequence is direct: if the layout is at fault you fix the reflow, and if the cause is context you design for interruption and slow loading, which are different pieces of work.

**Question 7 (moderate)** — Statement: "The layout reflows correctly at narrow widths." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The passage says explicitly that the review did not assess whether the layout reflowed correctly. Declared silence establishes nothing in either direction, so Cannot Say. This item is worth dwelling on because the professional temptation runs towards False — given the completion gap, you may well suspect the reflow is broken, and you may be right. The passage supports neither, and marking False would be substituting expertise for evidence, which is exactly what this section penalises. In the workplace the right response is to go and check, which costs ten minutes; in the test, the check is not available and neither is the conclusion.

**Question 8 (harder)** — Statement: "The review recommends a narrow-width review." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The review recommends that the team *consider whether* a narrow-width review would be worthwhile — a recommendation to evaluate an option, including the possibility of concluding it would not be. The statement asserts a recommendation to conduct one, which was not made, so False rather than Cannot Say: the passage tells you what was recommended and it was something different. This distinction pervades review documents and produces the familiar sequence in which a recommendation to consider becomes a commitment on a plan.

*Passage C — for Questions 9 to 12*

"Interactive elements must have a visible focus indicator with a contrast ratio of at least 3:1 against adjacent colours. Touch targets must be at least 24 by 24 pixels, unless an equivalent target of at least 24 by 24 pixels is available elsewhere on the same screen. Meaning must not be conveyed by colour alone. Icons used without a text label must be from the approved set."

**Question 9 (easy)** — Statement: "Meaning may be conveyed by colour alone if the colour is high contrast." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The passage states without qualification that meaning must not be conveyed by colour alone. The statement introduces a condition the passage does not recognise, and high contrast does not address the requirement's purpose, which concerns people who cannot distinguish the colours at all rather than people who cannot see them clearly. False. It is worth being precise about that purpose, because "make it a stronger red" is a common and useless response to this requirement — a stronger red is still only red.

**Question 10 (moderate)** — Statement: "Every touch target must be at least 24 by 24 pixels." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The requirement carries an exception: a smaller target is permitted if an equivalent target of at least 24 by 24 pixels is available elsewhere on the same screen. The statement's "every" drops that, so it is contradicted and False. The exception exists for a practical reason worth knowing: a small inline link inside a paragraph cannot always be enlarged without breaking the text, and the requirement accepts that provided the same action is reachable from a properly sized control nearby. Reading the exception is what lets you keep an inline link rather than fighting to enlarge it.

**Question 11 (harder)** — Statement: "An icon with a text label must be from the approved set." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The final sentence requires icons *used without a text label* to be from the approved set. It says nothing about icons that do have a label — it neither requires them to be from the set nor permits them to be otherwise. Silence leaves the case undetermined, so Cannot Say rather than False. This is the distinction between a rule stating where it applies and a rule stating where it does not, and it is easy to convert the first into the second: a reader may reasonably infer that labelled icons are exempt, and the passage does not say so. In practice this is worth noticing because it is exactly the kind of gap that produces two teams applying the same standard differently, both defensibly.

**Question 12 (harder) — Main point**

Which best captures the main point of Passage C?

- A) Icons must be from the approved set.
- B) Interactive elements must be perceivable and operable regardless of how a user perceives colour or how precisely they can point — through visible focus, adequately sized targets or equivalents, meaning carried in more than colour, and unlabelled icons drawn from a set users can be expected to recognise.
- C) Touch targets must be at least 24 by 24 pixels.
- D) Meaning must not be conveyed by colour alone.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The main point must cover the whole passage without adding anything. Option A is one clause and drops its condition. Options C and D are each true of one sentence and silent on the rest, and C also drops the equivalence exception. Option B captures all four requirements and the logic joining them: each exists so an element works for someone who cannot see colour differences, cannot point precisely, or cannot see the screen at all. Note that B also explains why unlabelled icons must come from an approved set — recognisability — which is the reasoning that makes the rule make sense rather than seeming arbitrary. Main-point items reward the option broad enough to cover everything and disciplined enough to invent nothing.

### Preparation tips

- **Answer only from the passage.** Before marking True, point at the words.
- **Trust Cannot Say.** Especially where you suspect the answer — suspicion is not evidence.
- **Read "or" as "or".** In accessibility criteria it offers alternative routes, and reading it as "and" costs you effort and arguments.
- **Read to the end of the sentence.** "Although" and "except" arrive late and reverse things.
- **Notice exceptions with equivalence conditions.** "Unless an equivalent is available" is a real and usable route.
- **Distinguish stating where a rule applies from stating where it does not.**
- **Separate "consider whether X" from "recommend X".**
- **Practise on real criteria.** Take an accessibility requirement and write three statements — one True, one False, one Cannot Say.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Answering from world knowledge.** The most frequent cause of lost marks.
- **Avoiding Cannot Say.**
- **Reading "or" as "and".** The most costly misreading in your discipline.
- **Carrying "everything needs 4.5:1" in your head.** The passage will have thresholds and exemptions.
- **Marking False because you suspect the untested result.**
- **Converting a scope statement into an exemption.**
- **Treating association as causation.** A passage naming confounds is warning you off.
- **Confusing a true detail with the main point.**

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment presents realistic workplace scenarios and asks what you would do. There is no passage to decode and no arithmetic. You are shown a dilemma of the kind your role produces, given four or five possible responses, and asked which is most effective and often which is least.

This section measures judgement rather than knowledge, and specifically whether your instincts align with what the profession considers good practice at your level. That is the whole calibration. An associate graphic designer who overrules a communications team is overstepping; one who never raises anything is under-performing. Answer as the person your role description says you are — a trainee working under supervision, showing aptitude and potential, expected to notice things and say so.

Four principles carry most scenarios here.

Users come first, and in graphic design that specifically means the users who cannot see, read or operate something. A design that excludes one group is not slightly worse for them; it is unusable, and that asymmetry is why legibility questions rarely lose to aesthetic questions on the merits.

Act within your remit and be transparent at its edges. At associate level the best answers usually involve doing what you can competently do and promptly telling the right person about what you cannot. Neither silent struggling nor reflexive escalation scores well.

Address problems directly and early, with the person involved. Options involving an honest private conversation beat public criticism, silence, or complaining to someone else.

Bring the value, not the opinion. This is the distinctively graphic-design signature of a strong answer. Visual decisions are the ones colleagues feel most entitled to have opinions about, and the most effective response is almost never "that looks wrong" but "that measures 3.1:1, and the requirement for that size is 4.5:1". A number ends an argument that taste cannot.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The judgement dimensions map onto your named skills:

- **Protecting legibility and access** maps to **Designing for everyone**, where you explain the importance of accessible design, describe access needs, and describe how privilege and power influence design and delivery.
- **Explaining a visual decision** maps to **Design communication**, where you explain the importance of articulating design decisions and describe methods for doing so.
- **Working with others' perspectives** maps to **Designing together**, where you explain techniques for bringing together multiple perspectives and the importance of constructive feedback.
- **Using evidence** maps to **Evidence-based design**.
- **Working in cycles** maps to **Iterative design**.
- **Understanding where design sits** maps to **Leading design** and **Designing strategically**.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — You do not know how to do the task**

Your senior designer asks you to "produce an icon set for the new journey by Thursday, consistent with the system". You have drawn icons but never produced a set to a system specification. What is the most effective response?

- A) Say yes, then work it out alone.
- B) Say yes, and add that you have not built a set to a system spec before and would find half an hour going through the grid, stroke and optical size rules really useful before you start.
- C) Say you cannot do it because you have not been trained.
- D) Say yes, and quietly ask another associate to help rather than telling your senior.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** Your role description says explicitly that you work under supervision and need to develop, so asking for guidance is doing your job correctly. Option B combines willingness with honesty and makes a specific, small request — and note how specific it is, naming grid, stroke and optical size rather than asking vaguely for help, which is far easier for a senior to grant and demonstrates you know what you do not know. Option A risks a set that is subtly inconsistent, which is worse than an obviously wrong one because it will ship. Option C is least effective, declining a development opportunity and handing the work back. Option D leaves your senior with a false picture of your skills.

**Question 2 (easy) — Something you notice in live content**

Reviewing a live service, you notice that the "cancel" and "continue" buttons are distinguished only by colour — one green, one red — with no difference in shape, position or label styling beyond the words. What should you do?

- A) Say nothing; the words are different, so it is fine.
- B) Raise it with your senior designer, describing specifically that a user who cannot distinguish red from green has only the labels to rely on, and that the colour is doing work the design is treating as decorative.
- C) Change the colours yourself.
- D) Note it for the next design review.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** The words being different is exactly the argument that makes option A tempting, and it is the wrong reading of the situation. If the labels alone carry the meaning, the colours are decorative and could be removed without loss — but they are not decorative, because a user scanning quickly will use the colour to locate the button before reading it, and for someone who cannot distinguish the two hues that shortcut is unavailable while remaining available to everyone else. Option B names that precisely, which is what makes it actionable. Option C changes a live service beyond your remit. Option D defers a live accessibility issue. Note the useful instinct: when a colour appears to be redundant, ask whether users are actually relying on it, because a redundant cue people use is not redundant.

**Question 3 (easy) — Conflicting instructions**

Your senior designer asked you to finish the icon set this morning. The delivery manager then asks you to spend the morning on slides for tomorrow's show and tell, calling it urgent. What is the most effective response?

- A) Do the slides; the delivery manager is more senior.
- B) Do the icons and tell the delivery manager you are busy.
- C) Tell the delivery manager about the existing task, offer a specific workable option, and ask them and your senior designer to confirm the priority.
- D) Try to do both by working through lunch.

**Correct answer: C. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Two legitimate instructions have collided and you cannot serve both. Resolving it is not yours to impose, but surfacing it promptly and helpfully is. What lifts option C above bare escalation is the offer of a specific option — "I could do the slides this morning and the icons after lunch, if that works" — which turns you from a bottleneck into part of the solution. Option A ranks by seniority; the delivery manager probably does not know about the icons. Option B is abrupt and leaves a real need unmet. Option D is least effective, because working through lunch hides the conflict, sets an unsustainable expectation, and means nobody learns that the team is over-committing you.

**Question 4 (moderate) — A stakeholder asks for something that reduces legibility**

A communications colleague asks you to set body text in the brand's light grey, which measures 3.2:1 against white. The requirement for that size is 4.5:1. What is the most effective response?

- A) Use the light grey; the brand matters.
- B) Explain the measured value and the threshold, and offer the brand's darker grey — or a darkened variant of the same hue — which keeps the palette recognisable while meeting the requirement.
- C) Refuse and use black.
- D) Use the light grey but increase the text size so a lower threshold applies.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** The colleague's concern is legitimate and the specific instruction is not workable, so the answer is a substitution rather than a refusal. Option B does the two things that make a visual argument land: it supplies the measured value, which converts a matter of taste into a matter of fact, and it offers an alternative from within the brand, which addresses what the colleague actually wants. Most brand palettes already contain a darker variant of the primary hue for exactly this reason. Option D is least effective and is the sophisticated-looking trap: enlarging body text purely to escape a threshold games the criterion rather than meeting its purpose, and produces a page of oversized text that scans badly for everyone. Option A publishes text some people cannot read. Option C wins the point and discards the brand unnecessarily.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Feedback that stings**

In a critique, a senior designer says your layout is "cluttered and the hierarchy doesn't work". You think the hierarchy is clear. What is the most effective response?

- A) Explain the hierarchy you intended.
- B) Ask them to describe the order in which they read the page, since if an experienced designer read it in a different order from the one you intended, the hierarchy is not working regardless of what you designed — then fix that.
- C) Accept the criticism and start again.
- D) Ask for the feedback in writing.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Both things can be true: you designed a hierarchy, and the page does not communicate it. Option B converts the feedback into information, and the specific question is the useful one — asking what order somebody read in produces a concrete, checkable answer, whereas asking what they disliked produces opinion. If they read the caption before the heading, you know the caption is too heavy or the heading too light, and you have a measurable fix. Option A is least effective because explaining your intention does not change what a reader experiences, and it teaches the room that giving you feedback means listening to a defence. Option C discards work without extracting the lesson. Option D defers a two-minute conversation.

**Question 6 (moderate) — A pattern you notice**

Auditing a service, you find four different shades of blue used for links across eight pages. What is the most effective response?

- A) Change them all to the system blue.
- B) Document the four values and where each appears, check whether any of them fail contrast, and raise it with your senior designer — noting that the variation is probably a symptom of the system value not being available where those pages were built.
- C) Fix the ones that fail contrast.
- D) Note it in the audit.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Four values where there should be one is a symptom, and the useful work is documenting it precisely and identifying the likely cause. Option B does three things: records the specifics so somebody can act, prioritises by checking which values actually fail rather than treating all four as equally urgent, and — the part that makes it more than proofreading — identifies that designers do not usually invent link colours for pleasure. Four values normally means the token was not available in whatever tool or template those pages were built with, and fixing the eight pages without fixing that produces a fifth value next month. Option A acts unilaterally on eight live pages. Option C fixes the urgent subset and leaves the inconsistency. Option D records without escalating.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Pressure to skip a check**

A delivery manager asks you to sign off a design today, before the accessibility check your team requires, because a build starts tomorrow. What is the most effective response?

- A) Sign it off; the deadline is real.
- B) Explain what the check covers and ask what is possible — whether it can be done in the next hour, or whether the build could start with the components that are already checked — and raise it with your senior designer if no route works.
- C) Refuse and cite the process.
- D) Sign it off and do the check afterwards without telling anyone.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** The deadline is genuine and the check exists for a reason, and option B treats both as real. Asking what is possible frequently resolves it — a contrast and target-size check on one design takes twenty minutes — and the second suggestion is the specifically useful one, because builds rarely need every component on day one and starting with the checked ones costs nothing. Option D is least effective: signing off outside the process and concealing it means nobody knows the design is unchecked, and you have taken on a decision that was not yours. Option A skips the check without asking. Option C cites a rule without engaging with the deadline.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Something outside your remit**

You notice that the printed letter version of a form uses a typeface at 9 point that would be hard for many recipients to read, and the service serves a largely older population. Print belongs to another team. What is most effective?

- A) Ignore it; print is not yours.
- B) Raise it with your senior designer, describing the specific size and the audience, and ask whether there is a route to the team that owns the letters.
- C) Contact the print team directly.
- D) Change the digital version to match.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A user does not experience the screen and the letter as two products; they experience one service, and a letter set at 9 point for an older audience is a legibility failure with the same consequences as a contrast failure on screen. Option B evidences the problem with the specific value and the audience — which is what makes it actionable rather than an opinion about print — and seeks a legitimate route through someone with standing. Option A is least effective, because "not mine" describes an org chart while the user cannot read the letter. Option C acts beyond your remit and risks cutting across conversations you cannot see. Option D changes the half you control to match the half that is wrong.

**Question 9 (harder) — An accessibility problem found late**

Two weeks before launch you find that the focus indicator on every interactive element is a light grey outline measuring 1.6:1 against the background, well below the 3:1 requirement. Fixing it touches every component. What is the most effective response?

- A) Launch and add it to the backlog.
- B) Raise it immediately with your senior designer and the delivery manager, describing precisely what a keyboard user experiences — no visible indication of where they are on the page — and ask for a decision, while checking whether the system's default focus style could simply be reinstated.
- C) Change the focus style yourself across the components.
- D) Launch and note the gap in an accessibility statement.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A focus indicator below the threshold means keyboard users cannot see where they are, which makes the service effectively unusable without a mouse — and weighing that against a launch date is a decision for people with authority, though raising it is entirely yours. Option B does everything in your remit, and the final clause is the practical one that nobody thinks of under pressure: a failing custom focus style has usually replaced a compliant system default, so reinstating the default may fix every component in an afternoon. Option A is least effective, because the backlog is where accessibility problems go to remain unfixed. Option D treats an accessibility statement as a way to launch with a known blocker, which it is not. Option C is well-intentioned and beyond your remit at this scale.

**Question 10 (harder) — Being asked to design around a constraint you doubt**

A developer says a component cannot have more than 4 pixels of internal padding "because of the framework". Research shows the resulting touch targets are too small and users are mis-tapping. What is the most effective response?

- A) Accept it and make the icons smaller so they fit.
- B) Ask what the constraint actually is — whether the framework limits padding specifically, or the component's overall height, or a value somebody set once — since the answer determines whether the target can be enlarged by padding, by height, or by an invisible tap area extending beyond the visible control.
- C) Insist the padding is increased.
- D) Add a note to the design telling users to tap carefully.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** "Because of the framework" is a compressed claim that could mean several things, and the productive move is to establish which. Option B does that and names the third possibility, which is the one that resolves most cases in practice: a touch target does not have to be the same size as the visible control, and extending the interactive area invisibly beyond the graphic satisfies the requirement without changing the layout at all. That is a genuinely useful piece of knowledge to bring to a developer conversation. Option A makes the visible element smaller to fit a constraint, which worsens the original problem. Option C asserts without understanding. Option D is least effective, telling users to compensate for a design defect, which is both futile and the clearest possible statement that the problem has been noticed and not fixed.

**Question 11 (harder) — A mistake of your own**

You realise that a colour token you defined last week, now used across a live service, measures 4.3:1 rather than the 4.5:1 you believed. What should you do?

- A) Correct the token quietly.
- B) Tell your senior designer immediately, explain the measured value and where the token is used, and flag that anything published with it in the last week is below the threshold.
- C) Correct it and mention it at the next stand-up.
- D) Leave it; 4.3 is close enough.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** A token is used everywhere, which is what makes a small error consequential: 4.3:1 rather than 4.5:1 is a failure across every element using it. Option B does the three things that matter — tells the person who needs to know, states the measured value rather than describing the problem vaguely, and identifies the scope, which is the part people skip. Option D is least effective and is the specifically tempting answer: 4.3 looks close, and thresholds do not work that way. A criterion with a stated value has no discretionary zone below it, and "close enough" applied once to a token becomes the standard for every future judgement. Option A corrects the value and leaves published work unexamined. Option C delays disclosure of something affecting a live service.

**Question 12 (harder) — An assumption in the room**

In a session, someone says "we don't need to worry about colour blindness — it's rare". You know the service has around 700,000 users a year and that red-green colour vision deficiency affects roughly one in twelve men. What is the most effective response?

- A) Let it go; the team has decided.
- B) Bring the arithmetic into the room — that even a conservative estimate puts tens of thousands of affected users a year across a service this size — and ask what should happen to them, so the decision is made knowingly.
- C) Argue that all designs must be accessible to everyone.
- D) Raise it privately with your senior designer afterwards.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** The comment rests on an intuition about rarity, and intuitions about rarity collapse against a base number. Around one in twelve men and one in two hundred women have some form of red-green colour vision deficiency, which across 700,000 users implies tens of thousands of people a year — a figure that makes "rare" untenable without anyone having to argue about principles. Option B supplies the arithmetic and then asks a question, which is both the most effective form of challenge available to a junior person and genuinely the right thing to do, since the team may have an answer. Option A is least effective: a decision made on an assumption that is numerically wrong will exclude real people and you had the number. Option C states a true principle in a way that invites a debate about principles, which rarely changes a room. Option D misses the moment.

### Preparation tips

- **Answer as a trainee, not as a hero or a bystander.** Competent action plus prompt, honest communication upwards.
- **Bring the value, not the opinion.** "3.1:1 against a 4.5:1 requirement" ends an argument that "that looks too light" cannot.
- **Put the excluded user first.** A design somebody cannot read is not slightly worse for them.
- **Ask what a constraint actually is.** "Because of the framework" has several possible meanings.
- **Offer an alternative from inside the constraint.** A darker variant of the brand hue beats refusing the brand.
- **Ask what order somebody read in.** It converts hierarchy feedback into something checkable.
- **Read the least-effective question separately.** The worst option usually conceals something or asks users to compensate.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Treating "close enough" as a threshold.** 4.3 is a fail.
- **Assuming a redundant colour cue is unused.** People rely on cues before they read.
- **Gaming a criterion rather than meeting its purpose.** Enlarging text to escape a contrast rule helps nobody.
- **Telling users to compensate for a defect.**
- **Explaining your intention in response to hierarchy feedback.** It does not change what a reader experiences.
- **Fixing instances without asking why the system value was unavailable.**
- **Treating an accessibility statement as a way to launch with a blocker.**
- **Ranking by seniority.** Who is asking matters far less than the consequences.

## Conclusion

You have reached the end, and that is worth a moment. You have worked through forty-eight practice questions across four assessment types and read the reasoning behind every one. That is a substantial piece of preparation and considerably more than most people do.

Look back at what you covered. In the cognitive section you checked spacing values against a scale, read a type scale as a ratio rather than a series of differences, applied a conditional contrast standard without reading "or" as "and", spotted that a sharp drop below 400 pixels pointed at a specific breakage rather than a general difficulty, and found a route that satisfied both a brand and a legibility requirement. In the numeric section you read a threshold at its boundary and saw that 4.4 fails, worked out characters per line and turned it into a design judgement, remembered that twelve columns have eleven gutters, converted a defect into people, and noticed a mean that described nobody. In the verbal section you kept exceptions others drop, read "or" as offering alternative routes, declined to mark False on something you suspected but the passage had not tested, and recognised where a scope statement left a case undetermined. And in the situational judgement section you brought a measured value instead of an opinion, offered a darker brand variant rather than a refusal, asked what a framework constraint actually was, and brought arithmetic to an assumption about rarity.

Notice what unites almost all of it. Every section, in its own idiom, was training you to attach values to visual decisions — because in this context a design decision either lets somebody complete a task or prevents them, and the difference is measurable. That is the single most useful habit available to a graphic designer in government, and it is what turns a discipline everyone feels entitled to have opinions about into one whose judgements can be checked.

A word about where you are. You are an associate graphic designer: a trainee, working under supervision, expected to show aptitude, potential and an understanding of the role. Please read that as the invitation it is. Nobody expects the judgement of someone with a decade behind them. What they are looking for is care, curiosity, honesty about what you do not yet know, and steady improvement. And remember the advantage you have: you have not yet stopped seeing what experienced designers have learned to tolerate.

If you want to keep going from here, a few things will help more than rereading. Get a contrast checker and measure every colour pairing in a service you know, including text on coloured panels rather than only on white. Complete a service using only a keyboard and watch the focus indicator. Look at a page at 400% zoom and see what breaks. Print a page and read it at arm's length. Ask a developer how a spacing token actually reaches the browser. And practise stating findings with their values attached — "3.1:1 at 14 pixels", not "a bit light" — until the precise version is the one that comes naturally.

Finally, for the day itself. An assessment is a snapshot taken on one particular morning. It is not a verdict on your ability, your potential or your future. What determines how far you go is the quality of your attention to the people who have to read these things, and your willingness to keep learning — and someone who has read this far clearly has plenty of both.

Good luck. Go carefully, measure things, and keep looking at the edges.
