## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the mental processes a role demands, using realistic materials rather than abstract puzzles. At lead level the emphasis falls on strategic reasoning: multi-constraint optimisation across portfolios, pattern interpretation in noisy organisational data, deduction through interacting governance frameworks, and the design-thinking required to build rules and systems that behave well when applied by many hands.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 15 to 30 minutes covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation and applied problem solving, scored objectively against a norm group — for leadership assessment, usually a senior norm group — with adaptive difficulty on most modern platforms. Reports to employers separate speed from accuracy; at your level accuracy under complexity carries the weight, because a lead's costliest errors are confident, considered and structural.

The mapping to your work is direct. Setting the strategy for test analysis across a broad area is constrained optimisation: finite capability, competing programmes, and a risk landscape that shifts under you. Developing, monitoring and evaluating standards is systems design plus failure analysis — a standard is an algorithm executed by dozens of teams with varying skill and motivation, and its defects surface as organisational behaviour, not error messages. Making strategic decisions that improve test analysis means reasoning about second-order effects: what a metric will incentivise, what a mandate will displace, what a tooling choice will lock in. Leading and coaching others in using defect patterns and trends for tactical and strategic recommendations requires you to distinguish, at scale, signal from noise, cause from correlate, and trend from artefact — and to teach the distinction. Negotiating and escalating major risks demands that your reasoning survive hostile scrutiny at executive level, where a single slipped inference discredits an otherwise sound case. The questions below rehearse each of these in miniature, and — like all good practice at your level — double as a refresher of the explicit forms you coach.

The assessment dimensions map directly to the named skills in your role summary:

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **Managing, reporting and resolving defects** ("lead and coach others in using defect patterns and trends to make tactical and strategic recommendations"): organisational-scale defect data is noisier and more confounded than team data, and reading it correctly — then teaching others to — is a lead responsibility.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern in organisation-wide defect data**

Escaped-defect rates per release across your organisation's five delivery areas over the year: Area V 0.4, Area W 0.5, Area X 0.4, Area Y 0.5, Area Z 1.6. Area Z runs the organisation's oldest services and is also the only area not yet adopted onto the organisational test analysis standards you introduced two years ago. A board member asks: "Does Z's figure prove your standards work?" What is the most accurate answer?

- A) Yes — Z lacks the standards and has triple the escape rate; the standards explain the difference.
- B) The pattern is consistent with the standards helping, but it cannot prove it: Z differs in at least two ways at once — no standards adoption and the oldest services — so legacy complexity alone could explain some or all of the gap; the honest claim is the correlation plus a proposal to test it, for example by tracking Z's trend as it adopts the standards.
- C) No — escape rates cannot be compared across areas.
- D) Yes, provided the board wants it to.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The confound is stated in the question's own sentence: Z is simultaneously the non-adopter and the legacy estate. Two candidate causes, one observation — the data cannot separate them, and a lead who claims it can is spending credibility on an inference the first sceptical director will unpick. Option B is the answer that survives scrutiny: it names the correlation honestly (the pattern is consistent with the standards' value, and four adopting areas clustered at 0.4-0.5 is genuinely encouraging), names the confound, and converts the question into a testable proposition — Z's trend through adoption is the natural experiment your data capture should be designed to read. Option C overcorrects into nihilism; cross-area comparison is exactly what organisational leads must do — carefully. Option D is the joke with a real referent: evidence shaped to audience appetite is the failure mode your role exists to prevent. The lead-level lesson: your recommendations travel further than anyone else's in the profession, so your causal claims must be the most disciplined — and modelling that discipline before a board is itself coaching, because your analysts will quote how you answered.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction through interacting standards**

Your organisational standards state: (i) every service must have a named test analysis owner; (ii) services classified "critical" must additionally have their test approach reviewed annually by someone outside the delivering team; (iii) review findings rated "major" suspend the service's self-certification rights until the finding is closed. Service S is critical, has a named owner, was reviewed eight months ago, and has one open "major" finding from that review. The delivering team argues: "We have an owner and our review isn't due for four months, so we can self-certify this release." What do the standards require?

- A) Self-certification is permitted — the review is current and ownership is in place.
- B) Self-certification is suspended — rule (iii) attaches to the open major finding, not to the review cycle, and remains in force until the finding closes regardless of when the next review is due.
- C) The team may self-certify if the finding is downgraded.
- D) The standards conflict and the lead must arbitrate.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Trace each rule's trigger. Rules (i) and (ii) are satisfied: owner named, review within the annual cycle. But rule (iii) is a separate mechanism with its own trigger and its own duration: a major finding suspends self-certification "until the finding is closed". The team's argument quietly substitutes the review cycle (rule ii's clock) for the finding's closure (rule iii's condition) — a category swap that sounds procedural and is simply wrong. Option C describes a real-world manoeuvre (downgrade the finding) that the standards as quoted neither provide for nor prohibit — but as an answer to "what do the standards require", it is evasion: the question is answered by B. Option D miscalls determinate rules as conflicting; nothing here needs arbitration, only accurate reading. The lead dimension is double. First, you wrote or steward these rules: their suspension mechanism working as designed — biting until closure, indifferent to the calendar — is the standard functioning, and your job in this conversation is to make its logic legible, not negotiable. Second, note what the team's misreading tells you: if this argument arrives more than once, the standard's drafting needs a clarifying sentence, because a rule that invites the same misreading from multiple teams has a usability defect — and monitoring and evaluating standards means fixing that, not just refuting each instance.

**Question 3 (moderate) — Error checking a draft organisational strategy**

A draft quality strategy you are reviewing contains these commitments in separate sections: (1) "All teams will adopt the organisational test automation framework by Q4." (2) "Teams retain autonomy to select the test tooling best suited to their technology stack." (3) "The framework currently supports web and API services; mobile support is on the roadmap for next year." (4) "Legacy mainframe services are exempt from framework adoption until decommissioning." (5) "Framework adoption is a condition of passing service assessments from Q1 next year." Which combination exposes teams to an impossible position?

- A) 1 and 4 — the exemption contradicts the mandate.
- B) 2 and 5 — autonomy versus a compliance condition.
- C) 1, 3 and 5 together — mobile teams are mandated to adopt by Q4, and assessed on adoption from Q1, a framework that does not support their stack until "next year", with no stated exemption.
- D) 3 and 4 — mobile and mainframe cannot both be edge cases.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Test each pairing for genuine contradiction rather than mere tension. Option A: statement 4 is an explicit exemption to statement 1 — that is not a contradiction; it is a mandate with a carve-out, which is how mandates normally work. Option B: real tension (autonomy versus condition), but tension of the kind strategies legitimately resolve — autonomy within a mandated framework is a coherent, if arguable, position. Option D: two edge cases coexist without difficulty; one is exempted, the other is... and there is the thread. Pull it: mobile teams are inside the mandate (1), inside the assessment condition (5), and outside the framework's capability (3) — with no exemption parallel to the mainframe one (4). The strategy orders them to adopt something that cannot support them, then penalises non-adoption at assessment. Statements 1, 3 and 5 are each locally reasonable; the impossibility exists only in their intersection, and only for one population — which is exactly why drafting reviews miss it: every author checked their own section. The fix is a sentence (extend statement 4's exemption pattern to pre-support mobile teams, with a trigger tied to framework capability, not the calendar). The lead lesson generalises: standards and strategies fail at intersections, per population — so the review method is not "read the sections" but "walk each team archetype through every commitment simultaneously". Creating and managing multiple quality testing strategies — your named skill — is exactly this: the strategy must be executable by every real team it binds, not merely coherent section by section.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Portfolio prioritisation of your own attention**

Four situations compete for your next two days. The organisation's annual budget submission — where your quality capability bid lives — is finalised Friday. Which allocation is most defensible?

1. A programme director asks you to personally re-review a contested release decision that two of your senior analysts have already assessed, with documented findings the director dislikes.
2. Your capability bid's evidence pack needs final numbers: without it, the automation investment case defaults to last year's allocation, which your analysis shows underfunds the pipeline by a third.
3. A community-of-practice session you committed to lead on risk-based analysis, attended by thirty analysts, is scheduled tomorrow morning.
4. A supplier's contract renewal, being negotiated by commercial colleagues this week, contains testing obligations you have not yet read.

- A) Spend both days on item 1 — programme directors' concerns come first.
- B) Item 2 first and deepest; item 4 next (a bounded read with written comments to commercial before negotiations close); deliver item 3 as committed; decline item 1's re-review, backing your seniors' documented assessment while offering the director an escalation route through governance if they dispute it.
- C) Items 1 and 3 — visible leadership matters most.
- D) Delegate item 2 to a senior analyst and spend the time on items 1 and 4.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Rank by irreversibility and by what only you can do. Item 2 is both: the budget window closes Friday, the default outcome is a structural underfunding your own analysis has quantified, and the capability bid is inalienably lead work — nobody below you owns the organisational investment case. Item 4 is a closing window with asymmetric cost: contractual testing obligations, once signed, bind for the contract's life; two hours of reading and written comments now buys years of workable terms — bounded effort, deadline-driven, uniquely yours to catch. Item 3 is a kept commitment with compounding value: thirty analysts, your community's trust in your word, and the session advances the risk-based advocacy your role names — deliver it, don't sacrifice it to urgency theatre. Item 1 is the trap dressed as duty: re-reviewing your seniors' documented, disliked findings personally would undercut the analysts you are building (the director learns that unwelcome findings are appealable to you; your seniors learn their signature is provisional), and the correct machinery already exists — governance escalation, which option B offers. Declining item 1 is not neglecting the director; it is routing them properly while defending your profession's authority structure. Option D inverts the logic precisely: it delegates the one item only you can do and personally performs the one item you must not. The lead pattern: protect the irreversible, honour the committed, bound the reviewable, and never let seniority requests convert you into the appeals court that dissolves your own people's standing.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Reading a community metric honestly**

Two years ago you introduced organisational test analysis standards, supported by a community of practice. Adoption self-assessments now report: year one, 40% of teams "fully conformant"; year two, 85%. Independent audit sampling, run for the first time this year, finds 55% actual conformance among teams self-reporting "full". What is the soundest reading?

- A) Conformance is 85% and rising; the audit is too small to matter.
- B) The programme has failed: teams are lying and the standards should be enforced harder.
- C) Real conformance is materially lower than reported (roughly 47% of all teams, if 55% of the 85% self-reporting are genuinely conformant), and the gap itself is the key finding: self-assessment has drifted into aspiration or misunderstanding — so the response is to find out which (do teams know what conformance means, or know and overstate?), recalibrate the self-assessment against audit criteria, and treat the year-one/year-two trend as unmeasurable until instruments align.
- D) Average the figures: conformance is about 70%.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Compute what the audit implies: if 85% self-report full conformance and sampling finds 55% of those genuinely conformant, then approximately 0.85 × 0.55 ≈ 47% of all teams are actually conformant — plus some unknown fraction of the 15% who did not claim conformance but may partially conform. The headline 85% is an artefact of the measurement instrument, and the year-on-year "improvement" may be partly real adoption and partly self-assessment inflation — indistinguishable, because the instrument changed nothing while its readings soared. The diagnostic fork in option C is the lead-level move: a 30-point self-versus-audit gap has two very different causes with two very different remedies — teams that misunderstand the standard need clearer criteria and coaching (a drafting and education failure: yours to fix); teams that understand and overstate need to know audit is now routine and consequences attach to the gap (a governance failure: yours to fix differently). Punishing before diagnosing (option B) guarantees the wrong remedy for at least one population, and poisons the self-reporting honesty you need. Option A discards the only ground-truth data in the story. Option D averages two numbers that measure different things — the arithmetic equivalent of a category error. The generalisable principle you should be coaching from this: any self-reported metric attached to status or scrutiny will inflate; the design answer is periodic calibration against independent sampling, announced in advance, framed as instrument maintenance rather than fraud-hunting. Guiding teams across an organisation in optimising via appropriate data — your named skill — begins with knowing when your data has stopped being appropriate.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Deduction about a proposed governance change**

The architecture board proposes: "To accelerate delivery, services scoring 'low risk' on the new risk triage tool will be exempt from independent test assurance; the tool scores risk from service criticality, data sensitivity, and change size." You review the tool's logic and find change size is measured in lines of code changed. Which deduction identifies the proposal's most serious structural flaw?

- A) Lines of code is an imperfect measure, but all measures are imperfect, so the proposal is fine.
- B) The exemption creates a self-weakening loop for a specific and dangerous class of change: high-consequence small changes — a one-line eligibility threshold edit, a configuration flag flip, a rounding rule — score "low risk" on change size, skip independent assurance, and are precisely the class of change where independent eyes catch what authors cannot; the tool's risk model omits blast radius per line, so the exemption systematically routes some of the highest-impact changes around assurance.
- C) The board lacks authority over test assurance and the proposal is void.
- D) Exemptions are always wrong in principle.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The reasoning move is to hunt the proposal's worst-served case rather than judge its average case. On average, small changes are lower risk — the tool's heuristic is not stupid. But the exemption's failure class is specific and identifiable in advance: changes whose consequence is decoupled from their size. Government services are dense with them — a benefit threshold, a date comparison, a flag controlling an eligibility path — one line, population-scale blast radius, and (the compounding factor) exactly the kind of change whose author is most confident and most blind, which is what independent assurance exists for. The loop in option B's phrasing is real: each such change that skips assurance and survives builds the case that the exemption is safe, until the one that does not — and the tool's design (risk scored partly by lines changed) guarantees the exposure recurs. The lead response the deduction sets up: not opposition to the exemption (option D's dogmatism forfeits your seat at the table; risk-based assurance allocation is sound in principle and is your own advocacy), but a repair — add consequence class to the triage model, or carve high-consequence configuration and logic changes out of the exemption regardless of size. Option A's "all measures are imperfect" is the thought-terminating move that waves through the identifiable failure class along with the noise. Option C swaps analysis for jurisdiction, the weakest possible ground. Influencing organisational decisions on test practice — your named skill — is precisely this: find the flaw exactly, propose the repair that preserves the proposal's legitimate aim, and bring both to the board in the same breath.

**Question 7 (hard) — Diagnosing an organisational quality trend**

Over four quarters, organisation-wide data shows: escaped defects steady; time-to-resolve production incidents up 60%; defect reports increasingly filed against integration points rather than single services; the proportion of incidents requiring three or more teams to resolve doubled; individual teams' test coverage and pass rates stable or improving. Programme leadership reads this as "operations getting slower" and proposes an incident-management training programme. What does the pattern actually indicate, and what is the lead-level response?

- A) Operations needs the training — resolution time is an operations metric.
- B) The pattern is architectural, not operational: quality is holding within services (stable escapes, improving team metrics) while failing between them (integration defects, multi-team incidents, slow resolution because no single team can see the whole failure) — the organisation's decomposition into more services has outrun its cross-service quality practices. The response is strategic: contract testing standards, integration-level test ownership, cross-service observability for test environments, and multi-team defect protocols — with the diagnosis presented to leadership before their training budget buys the wrong remedy.
- C) Teams are gaming their coverage metrics and should be audited.
- D) The data is contradictory — steady escapes but slower resolution — so no conclusion is possible.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Synthesise rather than read metrics singly. Each signal alone supports a mundane story; together they triangulate: stable within-service quality (escapes steady, team metrics improving) alongside rising between-service failure signatures (integration-point defects, multi-team incidents doubling, resolution slowing because the failure spans ownership boundaries — three teams each see a healthy service and a sick interaction). That combination has an architectural cause — service decomposition creating interaction surface faster than quality practice covers it — and the apparent contradiction option D trips over is the diagnosis: quality effort is aimed where the risk used to be. The killer detail is the resolution-time mechanism: incidents resolve slowly not because operators got slower (option A's reading, and leadership's) but because nobody owns the seam — a fact that incident-management training cannot touch, which is why the proposed spend is not merely suboptimal but diagnostic-error-compounding: a quarter later, training complete, resolution still slow, leadership concludes quality is unmanageable. Option C fails the evidence check — gamed coverage would show as rising escapes against rising coverage, and escapes are steady. The lead responsibilities stack here, matching your named skills: the diagnosis itself (leading others in using defect patterns for strategic recommendations), the remedy set (setting standards for test analysis across the organisation — contract testing, seam ownership, integration environments), and the intervention with leadership before the wrong remedy is funded (advocating early quality involvement in organisational delivery processes — where "early" means upstream of budget decisions, not just upstream of code). Organisational diagnosis is your role's highest-value analytical act: the teams each hold a piece; only the organisational vantage sees the shape.

**Question 8 (hard) — Multi-constraint strategy sequencing**

You are sequencing a three-year quality capability strategy with four workstreams: (A) organisational contract-testing standards — 6 months to design, requiring your two strongest principal analysts; (B) test data self-service platform — 12 months, requiring platform engineering capacity that becomes available only in year 2; (C) risk-based analysis training across all teams — 9 months, deliverable by your senior community with your oversight; (D) legacy regression automation for the two oldest areas — 12 months, requiring the same two principal analysts as workstream A, and its business case expires if not started within 12 months (funding is committed this budget cycle only). Constraint: the principals cannot run two workstreams simultaneously. Executive expectation: visible organisation-wide impact within year 1. Which sequencing is soundest?

- A) Year 1: A then D started (principals sequential: A months 1-6, D months 7-18), C in parallel via the community (months 1-9, delivering the year-1 visible impact); B in year 2 when platform capacity arrives. 
- B) Year 1: D first (funding expiry), A in year 2, C in year 3, B in year 2.
- C) Year 1: B first — platforms take longest so must start first; A, C, D follow.
- D) Run A, C and D simultaneously in year 1 to maximise early impact.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Map the hard constraints first. B is immovable to year 2 (its enabling capacity does not exist earlier — option C schedules an impossibility). D must start within 12 months or its funding dies. A and D share the two principals, who cannot parallelise — option D's simultaneous plan violates the binding resource constraint outright. So the real question is the principals' ordering: A then D, or D then A. Option A's sequencing (A months 1-6, D months 7-18) satisfies everything: D starts at month 7, inside its 12-month funding window; A completes first — which matters beyond scheduling, because contract-testing standards (A) are the strategic keystone your organisational diagnosis likely demands, and completing them early lets D's automation build conform to them rather than retrofit; and C, running months 1-9 through the senior community, delivers the executive's year-1 visible impact through the channel that also grows your community's standing — training reaching every team is exactly the organisation-wide visibility executives can see. Option B honours the funding window with month-1 urgency it does not need (the window is 12 months, not immediate), pushes A behind D so the standards arrive after the automation they should shape, and strands C in year 3, failing the year-1 impact expectation entirely. The lead-level pattern worth extracting: sequence by (1) hard external constraints (capacity existence, funding expiry), then (2) dependency logic (standards before the builds that should follow them), then (3) stakeholder rhythm (visible value inside each accountability cycle) — and check every plan against the binding resource, because strategies that overbook their scarcest people fail in month 2 while looking brilliant on the roadmap. Creating and managing multiple quality testing plans, approaches and strategies is your named skill; this is its arithmetic core.

**Question 9 (hard) — Evaluating an executive-sponsored metric proposal**

The chief operating officer proposes a single organisational quality KPI for the executive dashboard: "Quality score = 100 − (production incidents × 5) − (open defects ÷ 10), computed monthly per area, red-amber-green thresholds at 70 and 85." You are asked to endorse it. What is the strongest lead-level assessment?

- A) Endorse it — executives need simplicity, and any quality visibility at board level is a win.
- B) Reject it — quality cannot be reduced to a number.
- C) Endorse the goal (executive-level quality visibility) while repairing the instrument: the formula's terms are unnormalised (an area with triple the releases will carry more incidents at identical quality; open-defect counts penalise areas that record honestly and reward closure theatre), the weights are arbitrary, and a single RAG number invites managing-the-number behaviours — propose instead a small executive set: escaped-defect rate per release, mean time to restore, and high-severity ageing, each trended per area against itself, with a one-page narrative — and offer to pilot it with the COO's sponsorship for two quarters.
- D) Endorse it unchanged but privately advise your areas how to keep their scores green.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Decompose the proposal into its goal and its instrument — the recurring lead-level separation. The goal is legitimate and, for your profession, precious: a COO who wants quality on the executive dashboard is offering the visibility your advocacy usually has to fight for; option B's purist rejection burns that offer and teaches the executive that quality leaders answer practical questions with theology. The instrument, though, fails on inspection: unnormalised incident counts punish delivery volume (the area shipping three times as often carries three times the incident exposure at identical per-release quality — so the metric penalises exactly the delivery acceleration the organisation wants); open-defect counts convert honest recording into a dashboard liability, incentivising the closure theatre and severity shading you have spent years coaching out of teams; the weights (×5, ÷10) encode no empirical relationship; and monthly RAG per area guarantees the number, not the quality, becomes the managed object. Option D is the quietly disgraceful path: public endorsement plus private gaming advice makes you the author of the corruption the metric will suffer. Option C's counter-offer is the professional craft: per-release and per-restore rates are normalised, trend-against-self removes cross-area unfairness, high-severity ageing is the honest version of the defect signal (it measures neglect, not recording), the narrative page carries what numbers cannot, and the pilot-with-sponsorship framing lets the COO keep ownership of the initiative while you repair its machinery. This is your named skillset in composition: influencing improvements to quality processes, guiding via appropriate data, and representing the profession to senior audiences — where "representing" means bringing them instruments that will still be telling the truth in two years.

**Question 10 (hard) — Error checking a strategy's success measures**

Your draft strategy's success measures, written by a working group, are: (1) "90% of teams rate the new standards 4/5 or higher for usefulness"; (2) "Escaped high-severity defects reduce 30% by year 2"; (3) "100% of new services engage test analysis before beta"; (4) "The test analysis community grows to 100 members"; (5) "Zero audit findings against quality standards by year 3." Which measure is most likely to drive behaviour that damages the strategy's actual goals, and why?

- A) Measure 1 — satisfaction ratings are subjective.
- B) Measure 5 — a zero-findings target converts audit from a learning instrument into a threat, incentivising finding-suppression: narrow audit scopes, pre-audit cosmetics, negotiation of findings down to "observations", and hostility toward the honest self-disclosure your standards regime depends on; audits that find nothing in year 3 will mean the finding pipeline was managed, not that conformance was achieved.
- C) Measure 3 — 100% is too ambitious.
- D) Measure 4 — community size is a vanity metric.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Screen each measure for the behaviour it purchases, not the aspiration it expresses. Measure 1 is subjective but usefully so — standards that teams rate useless get ignored, so perceived usefulness is a real adoption signal (option A mistakes subjectivity for invalidity). Measure 3's 100% is demanding but behaviourally benign: the gaming risk (checkbox engagements) is real but moderate and detectable. Measure 4 is weak but harmless — a vanity metric wastes a line, not a culture. Measure 5 is the structural hazard, and the mechanism deserves the full articulation option B gives it: audit is the strategy's only independent truth channel (see the self-assessment inflation this very guide's earlier question rehearsed), and a zero-findings target prices every finding as a strategy failure — so every actor in the system, including your own profession, acquires an interest in findings not existing. The suppression repertoire is predictable and mostly invisible: scopes negotiated narrow, evidence curated, findings relitigated into observations, and — the deepest cost — teams learning that surfacing their own conformance gaps before an audit is self-harm. Year 3 arrives with a clean audit and an unknown reality, which is a worse epistemic position than year 1's. The repair is one word deep: target finding closure velocity and repeat-finding rates (learning measures), never finding counts (suppression measures). The lead craft this rehearses is among your most consequential: developing, monitoring and evaluating standards and methods includes designing the measurement regime — and every measure is an incentive wearing a number's clothing. Write each one by asking not "what would success look like?" but "what will people do to make this number move?"

**Question 11 (hard) — Reasoning about a cross-organisational commitment**

Your organisation is negotiating a shared-services agreement under which a partner department will run citizen-facing transactions on your platform. The partner's head of digital asks you to commit that "all releases affecting the shared platform will be regression-tested against both departments' user journeys before deployment". Your platform currently releases weekly; the partner's journeys are not yet documented; and your regression capability covers your own department's journeys only. Which response best serves both organisations?

- A) Commit — partnership requires good faith, and the details can follow.
- B) Refuse — you cannot test journeys you do not have.
- C) Commit conditionally and architecturally: agree the principle (shared-platform releases verified against both departments' critical journeys), and make the commitment's preconditions part of the agreement itself — the partner documents and maintains their critical journeys in executable form to an agreed standard, both departments fund the shared regression capability proportionally, the verification obligation phases in as journeys are delivered, and an interim risk protocol (notification of platform changes, partner sign-off windows) covers the gap — so the signed commitment is one both organisations can actually keep from day one.
- D) Commit to using "reasonable endeavours" to test the partner's journeys, leaving flexibility.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Read the commitment as an engineer and a negotiator simultaneously. As requested, it is unperformable on signature day: the journeys to be tested do not exist in testable form, and the capability to run them is neither built nor funded — so option A signs a promise that the first weekly release will break, converting good faith into documented failure and handing the partner a grievance for the relationship's entire life. Option B is accurate and sterile: it answers the letter of the request while abandoning its legitimate substance — the partner's citizens will genuinely depend on your platform, and their head of digital is right to want verification; refusal teaches them to route quality demands around you into the contract by commercial means, where they will be drafted worse. Option D is the seasoned-sounding trap: "reasonable endeavours" defers every hard question to a future dispute, provides the partner no actual assurance and you no actual obligation clarity, and — the practical tell — clauses like it are where post-incident blame wars are fought. Option C is the lead-level construction: it converts the commitment from a promise about outcomes into an architecture with preconditions, funding, phasing and an interim protocol — each element answering a real question (who documents journeys? who pays for the capability? what happens before it exists?) that options A, B and D leave to explode later. Note the deeper pattern, which recurs across your negotiating life: when asked to commit to something currently impossible, the professional move is neither yes nor no but the redesign that makes yes true — and putting the redesign inside the agreement, so the preconditions bind as firmly as the promise. Negotiating and escalating major risks, and mediating between organisations at all levels, are your named duties; this is their contractual form.

**Question 12 (hard) — Synthesising signals about your own strategy**

Three years into the test analysis strategy you authored: escaped defects are down 45% organisation-wide; your standards are adopted by all areas; the community you built has 80 active members and runs itself; two of your principal analysts have been promoted into lead roles elsewhere on the strength of capabilities your programme built. And: the newest delivery areas — built on event-driven architectures and machine-learning components — report that the standards "don't quite fit" their failure modes; escaped defects in those areas, though small in absolute number, are the organisation's only rising trend; and community sessions increasingly rehearse established practice rather than developing new practice. What is the soundest strategic reading?

- A) The strategy has succeeded; the new areas should adapt themselves to the standards, which are proven.
- B) The strategy has succeeded and is beginning to age at its frontier — the standards encode the failure modes of the architectures they were written for, the rising trend sits exactly where the encoded assumptions run out, and the community's rehearsal of established practice is maturity shading into stagnation. The lead response is renewal from strength: charter the new areas as the next generation's design partners, put frontier failure modes (event-driven, ML) on the community's development agenda, and treat the strategy's own lifecycle — including its eventual replacement — as a deliverable you own.
- C) The strategy has failed in the new areas and should be rewritten from scratch.
- D) The promotions and adoption metrics are what matter; the rest is noise.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This is the senior-most synthesis pattern: distinguishing failure from ageing, and success from completion. The success evidence is real and option C's "failed" reading would discard three years of compounded value on the testimony of its newest edge — rewriting from scratch also destroys the adoption, community and capability infrastructure that any successor needs. But option A's reading — the standards are proven; the frontier should conform — inverts the actual epistemics: the standards are proven against the failure modes they were written for; event-driven and ML systems fail differently (emergent timing behaviour, non-deterministic outputs, data drift — failure classes your standards' assumptions predate), and "don't quite fit" from the practitioners closest to the new failure modes is early, cheap, honest signal. The rising trend, small in absolute terms, sits precisely where the model's coverage ends — the same signature this guide has rehearsed at every level, now at strategy scale: the assumption expired; the instrument reads the old world. The community signal completes the diagnosis: rehearsal of established practice is what communities do when their development frontier has moved elsewhere — 80 self-running members is an achievement and a warning in one number. Option B's response converts all of it into renewal mechanics: the frontier areas become co-authors (which fixes fit and adoption simultaneously — the same social architecture as piloting with sceptics), the community's agenda gets its next decade of problems, and — the genuinely lead-level clause — the strategy's lifecycle becomes an owned deliverable: monitoring and evaluating standards and methods, your role's own words, includes scheduling their supersession. Option D is the retirement-lap reading; leads who adopt it are outlived by their strategies' failure modes. The final principle, worth teaching to every analyst you mentor: the highest form of strategic stewardship is building the thing, reading its expiry honestly, and leading its renewal — authorship, at this level, includes the ending.

### Preparation tips

- **Practise at intersections.** Lead-level cognitive questions live where rules, populations and constraints cross: walk team archetypes through draft strategies, trace exemptions to their worst-served cases, and check every plan against its binding resource. Your own organisation's documents are the best question bank in existence.
- **Interrogate instruments before readings.** Self-assessments inflate, unnormalised counts mislead, zero-targets suppress. Before interpreting any organisational number, ask how it was produced and what its production incentivised — in the test, and in every dashboard you govern.
- **Rehearse the goal/instrument separation.** Many hard questions (and most executive proposals) bundle a legitimate goal with a flawed mechanism. Train the reflex: endorse the goal explicitly, repair the mechanism specifically, offer the pilot.
- **Hunt the worst-served case.** Averages flatter; exemptions, mandates and metrics fail at their edges. For any rule-shaped question, identify who the rule serves worst and reason from there.
- **Time-box strategically.** Adaptive leadership assessments back-load multi-signal synthesis questions. Bank the deductive items efficiently; the differentiating marks sit in the syntheses, which reward unhurried reading of every signal before any conclusion.
- **Narrate your reasoning aloud in practice.** As a lead you must not only reason correctly but articulate the reasoning for boards, communities and mentees. Practising articulation — why the confound blocks the causal claim, why the metric will corrupt — builds the explanation library your coaching draws on.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Claiming causation your evidence cannot carry.** The confounded comparison is the lead's most public failure mode: your claims travel furthest, so their discipline must be strictest. Consistent-with is not caused-by; say which you have.
- **Judging proposals by their average case.** Exemptions, triage tools and simplifications earn their keep on averages and fail on identifiable edge classes. The review question is always: what is the worst change this rule waves through?
- **Reading section-coherent documents as coherent.** Strategies and standards fail at intersections that no section owns. Review by walking populations through the whole, never by approving parts.
- **Trusting numbers whose production you haven't examined.** Adoption percentages, satisfaction scores, finding counts — at organisational scale, every metric is a behaviour before it is a measurement. Ask what moved the number before believing what it says.
- **Sequencing by enthusiasm rather than constraint.** Funding windows, capacity existence and shared scarce people bind; visibility and momentum do not. Plans that overbook the binding resource are failures with good roadmaps.
- **Mistaking your strategy's maturity for its completion.** Stable adoption, self-running communities and clean metrics at the centre coincide with expiry at the frontier. The signals of ageing are quiet and arrive first from the newest practitioners — weight them accordingly, especially when the strategy is yours.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures accuracy and judgement with quantitative material drawn from the role's real work. The operations are accessible — percentages, rates, weighted aggregation, trend arithmetic, cost modelling — but at lead level the questions concentrate on interpretation at organisational scale: choosing denominators that mean something across heterogeneous areas, normalising before comparing, modelling investments and capacity honestly, and recognising precisely what a figure can and cannot support in front of an executive audience.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 20 to 35 minutes with 15 to 20 questions around tables, charts and short data narratives. Calculators are generally permitted; rough paper is essential; scoring reflects accuracy and pace against a norm group.

For a lead quality assurance test analyst, numbers are the medium of strategy. The strategic decisions your role names — which test types and techniques the organisation adopts, where quality investment flows, when an approach must change — are argued, won and audited in quantitative form: business cases with payback arithmetic, capability models with capacity constraints, defect economics across programme portfolios, risk quantifications that executives can weigh against delivery pressure. Your recommendations carry organisational weight precisely because they are expected to survive hostile numerical scrutiny; a lead whose percentages fold under a finance director's questioning has spent the profession's credibility along with their own.

Equally, you are the profession's numerical conscience. Dashboards, vendor claims, programme reports and partner-department figures cross your desk containing arithmetic that will drive decisions unless someone checks it — and at your altitude, you are frequently the last person positioned to check. Several questions below are therefore constructed as flawed-figure reviews: a plausible number with a defect in its construction, of the kind that costs organisations real money when it passes unexamined. And because you mentor and coach other analysts, every technique here is also curriculum: the leads who teach denominators, normalisation and incentive-aware metric design are the ones whose organisations stop making the classic errors. Work each question on paper, label intermediate values, and hold every result against the bounds its context implies — the same standard your reviews impose on everyone else.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Organisational escape rate, correctly aggregated**

Your organisation's five areas last quarter: V, 30 releases, 12 escapes; W, 20 releases, 10 escapes; X, 25 releases, 10 escapes; Y, 15 releases, 9 escapes; Z, 10 releases, 9 escapes. What is the organisation-wide escape rate per release?

- A) 0.50
- B) 0.56
- C) 0.60
- D) 0.44

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Aggregate raw totals, divide once. Escapes: 12 + 10 + 10 + 9 + 9 = 50. Releases: 30 + 20 + 25 + 15 + 10 = 100. Rate: 50 ÷ 100 = 0.50 per release. The distractor worth understanding is option B: averaging the five area rates (0.40, 0.50, 0.40, 0.60, 0.90) gives 2.80 ÷ 5 = 0.56 — inflated because the worst-performing area (Z, 0.90) is also the smallest, and unweighted averaging hands it a fifth of the influence despite a tenth of the releases. At organisational scale this error is not cosmetic: a board told 0.56 rather than 0.50 is told the organisation is 12% worse than it is, and the correction — when a finance analyst finds it — costs the profession's numerical authority. Note also what the disaggregated rates are for: the organisational figure (0.50) answers "how are we doing overall?"; the per-area spread (0.40 to 0.90) answers "where should attention go?" — Z's rate, small denominators acknowledged, is the strategic signal. Lead reporting carries both numbers, labelled, always.

**Question 2 (easy) — Cost of an escape versus cost of prevention**

Your defect economics baseline: an escaped high-severity defect costs on average £45,000 (incident response, remediation, rework, user harm handling). A proposed organisational contract-testing programme costs £180,000 in year one and is conservatively projected to prevent 8 high-severity escapes per year. What is the year-one return, expressed as net benefit and as a ratio of benefit to cost?

- A) Net £180,000; ratio 1:1
- B) Net £360,000; ratio 2:1
- C) Net £180,000; ratio 2:1
- D) Net £360,000; ratio 3:1

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Gross benefit: 8 prevented escapes × £45,000 = £360,000. Net benefit: £360,000 − £180,000 = £180,000. Benefit-to-cost ratio: 360,000 ÷ 180,000 = 2:1. Option C pairs them correctly; the other options mix gross with net or misstate the ratio — and the gross/net confusion is the one to train out, because business cases quoted with gross benefits as if net are the fastest way to lose a finance audience's trust permanently. Three lead-level annotations belong on any real version of this arithmetic. First, the prevention count is the fragile term: "conservatively projected to prevent 8" deserves its evidence stated (historical escape counts in the target failure class, pilot results), because the whole case scales linearly with it. Second, the £45,000 average conceals a distribution — one catastrophic escape can be twenty times the mean — so the case is stronger than its average-based arithmetic, a point worth making explicitly. Third, year-one framing understates recurring programmes: if year-two costs drop to maintenance (say £60,000) with prevention sustained, the steady-state ratio approaches 6:1 — and stating the trajectory is what distinguishes an investment case from an expense justification. Strategic decisions on test techniques — your named skill — are won with exactly this arithmetic, honestly annotated.

**Question 3 (easy) — Reading a capability distribution**

Your profession's capability data: of 60 test analysts across the organisation, 12 are assessed at "working" level in risk-based analysis, 33 at "practitioner", 10 at "expert", and 5 unassessed. Your strategy requires at least 25% of assessed analysts at expert level within two years. How far short is the current position?

- A) 4 analysts short
- B) 5 analysts short
- C) 3.75 analysts short, so 4
- D) Already meeting the target

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Read the target's base precisely: 25% of assessed analysts. Assessed: 60 − 5 = 55. Target: 0.25 × 55 = 13.75, which as a minimum-percentage threshold means 14 experts (13 of 55 is 23.6%, below target; 14 of 55 is 25.5%). Current experts: 10. Shortfall: 14 − 10 = 4. Option C computes 13.75 − 10 = 3.75 and rounds — right instinct, slightly wrong mechanics: the rounding belongs on the target headcount (up, because people are integers and the threshold is a floor), then the subtraction. Option B uses the unassessed-inclusive base (0.25 × 60 = 15, so 5 short) — a defensible-sounding reading the target's own wording excludes, and exactly the kind of base ambiguity your strategy documents should never contain: writing "25% of assessed analysts (currently 14 of 55)" into the strategy removes the argument in advance. The workforce-planning reality behind the arithmetic is the lead concern: 4 experts in two years means development pipelines (who are the practitioner-level candidates? what stretch work grows them?) and retention arithmetic (experts are promotion-liable — the very success your mentoring duty names removes them), so the honest plan develops more than 4. Mentoring and coaching other analysts is your named duty; this is its supply-chain mathematics.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Normalising incident exposure across delivery volumes**

The COO's draft dashboard shows quarterly production incidents: Area P, 18 incidents; Area Q, 6 incidents — with P flagged red and Q green. Delivery data: P deployed 90 releases in the quarter; Q deployed 12. What does normalisation show, and what should the dashboard show?

- A) P: 0.20 incidents per release; Q: 0.50 per release — Q's per-release incident exposure is two and a half times P's, inverting the RAG colours; the dashboard should show per-release rates (with absolute counts alongside), or it will systematically punish delivery frequency and reward infrequent big-bang releasing.
- B) P is still worse — 18 is three times 6.
- C) The areas cannot be compared because their release counts differ.
- D) Both areas should be amber as a compromise.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Normalise: P, 18 ÷ 90 = 0.20 incidents per release; Q, 6 ÷ 12 = 0.50. Per unit of change shipped, Q generates incidents at 2.5 times P's rate — the raw counts invert reality because P ships 7.5 times as often. The second half of option A is the lead-level point, and it is behavioural, not arithmetic: a dashboard flagging P red for absolute counts teaches every area that fewer, larger releases improve their colour — which is the opposite of what modern delivery practice (and incident science: small frequent changes are individually lower-risk and faster to diagnose) wants, and the opposite of what the organisation's delivery strategy is simultaneously demanding. Metrics misaligned with strategy do not sit neutrally; they pull against it monthly, in front of executives. Option C is the familiar overcorrection — differing denominators are why rates exist, not why comparison fails. Option D treats measurement as diplomacy. The lead move beyond the correction: get the normalisation into the dashboard's design rather than into your commentary on each edition — instruments are corrected once; readings need correcting forever. Guiding the organisation in optimising via appropriate data starts with making the organisation's own instruments appropriate.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Percentage-point traps in adoption reporting**

Standards adoption rose from 60% of teams to 75% over the year. Your community manager's draft report says "adoption grew 15%", while a sceptical programme director claims "a quarter of teams still ignore the standards, and the growth rate is slowing since last year's rise was 20 points". Which single statement is fully accurate?

- A) Adoption grew 15% and the director is wrong.
- B) Adoption grew 15 percentage points — a 25% relative increase (15 ÷ 60); the director's "quarter of teams" is exactly right (100 − 75 = 25%), and the deceleration claim (20-point rise last year, 15 this year) is arithmetically true but incomplete: each successive point is harder, because the remaining non-adopters are the hardest cases, so comparing raw point-gains across years overstates the slowdown's meaning.
- C) Adoption grew 25 percentage points.
- D) The director's figures are wrong and should be corrected publicly.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Untangle each claim. The report's "grew 15%": the absolute change is 15 percentage points (75 − 60); the relative change is 15 ÷ 60 = 25% — so "grew 15%" is wrong in both readings, and the report should say "15 percentage points (25% relative)". The director's arithmetic: 25% of teams outside the standards is exactly right, and the point-gain comparison (20 last year, 15 this year) is factually accurate — option D's instinct to correct them publicly would misfire embarrassingly, since their figures are sound. What the director's deceleration reading misses — and what option B supplies — is saturation logic: adoption curves flatten as the population thins toward the resistant remainder, so equal effort yields fewer points late in the curve; the honest response is neither denial nor alarm, but a changed strategy for a changed population (the last 25% are not laggards of the first 75% — they are teams with structural blockers your earlier tactics never addressed, as this guide's cognitive section rehearsed). The composite lesson is the lead's daily one: in numeric disputes at senior level, the winning move is rarely "you're wrong" — it is the fuller frame that absorbs everyone's correct fragments, corrects the terminology precisely, and redirects attention from the number to the strategy it should drive. Representing the community to senior audiences means doing this in real time, generously.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Capacity arithmetic under a cadence change**

The organisation mandates moving from fortnightly to weekly releases across all areas in six months. Current per-release quality effort averages 30 tester-hours (regression, verification, release checks), of which your data shows 18 hours is automatable with current tooling. Total quality capacity is 1,400 tester-hours per fortnight and is frozen. At weekly cadence with today's practices, what is the per-fortnight shortfall across the organisation's average of 40 releases per fortnight (rising to 80), and what does the automation ceiling imply?

- A) Shortfall of 1,000 tester-hours per fortnight; automation of the 18 automatable hours per release cuts required effort to 12 hours per release, bringing demand to 960 hours — inside capacity, so full automation of the automatable is not optional but the strategy's precondition, and it must land before the cadence does.
- B) Shortfall of 600 hours; automation is desirable but discretionary.
- C) No shortfall — teams will work faster under pressure.
- D) Shortfall of 1,000 hours; the only option is doubling headcount.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Model demand against capacity. Today: 40 releases × 30 hours = 1,200 hours per fortnight, inside the 1,400 capacity with 200 hours' headroom. At weekly cadence: 80 releases × 30 hours = 2,400 hours — against 1,400 capacity, a shortfall of 1,000 tester-hours per fortnight. That is the unmanaged outcome: a 42% coverage gap, which in practice arrives as silent regression thinning. Now the automation term: 18 of the 30 hours automatable leaves 12 manual hours per release; 80 × 12 = 960 hours — under the 1,400 capacity, with 440 hours' headroom (which the automation's own maintenance will partly consume — say the honest version aloud). The strategic reading is option A's second clause: automation here is not an efficiency nicety but the mandate's feasibility condition, and its build must complete before cadence doubles — which sets the sequencing (build during the remaining fortnightly months) and the escalation (if the six-month automation build cannot fund or staff, the cadence mandate and the capacity freeze are jointly impossible, and executives must pick which to relax — a negotiation your role's "negotiate and escalate major risks" duty exists for, conducted with exactly this arithmetic as its instrument). Option C is hope wearing a spreadsheet's absence. Option D ignores the automation lever while proposing the one lever (headcount) explicitly frozen. Leads carry this model to leadership before month one, because in month five it becomes a post-mortem.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Auditing a partner department's quality claim**

A partner department, negotiating shared-platform terms, states: "Our quality regime is stronger — our defect density is 0.8 defects per thousand lines of code, versus the 2.1 industry benchmark you cited for platforms like this." Due diligence finds: their density counts only defects found in production; your 2.1 benchmark counts defects found in all phases from system test onward; and their codebase declined 15% last year through decommissioning while defect counts are reported over trailing 24 months. How many distinct comparability flaws does their claim contain, and what are they?

- A) One: the number is simply too low to be true.
- B) Two: phase scope and codebase change.
- C) Three: phase scope (production-only versus system-test-onward — different numerators by construction); denominator instability (a shrinking codebase inflates apparent density trends and, with a 24-month defect window over a changing base, mismatches numerator and denominator periods); and window mismatch (trailing 24-month defects against a current-size codebase compounds the mismatch). The claim is not necessarily dishonest, but as constructed it cannot be compared with the benchmark at all.
- D) None: defect density is a standard metric and standard metrics are comparable.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Audit the metric's construction term by term. Numerator: production-only defects versus the benchmark's system-test-onward scope — the partner's numerator excludes, by definition, the large majority of defects the benchmark counts; comparing them is comparing different quantities that share a name. Denominator: thousand lines of code sounds stable but their codebase shrank 15% — decommissioning removes code but not the historical defects attributed across the window, and a declining base mechanically shifts any per-KLOC figure. Window: defects trailing 24 months divided by a current codebase size mismatches the periods of numerator and denominator — the honest construction uses matched windows (defects in period ÷ average codebase size in period). Three distinct flaws, each sufficient alone to void the comparison; together they make the 0.8-versus-2.1 contrast rhetorically powerful and analytically empty. Option D's faith in metric names is the negotiating-table trap: "defect density" is a family of metrics, and cross-organisation comparisons require construction alignment before a single conclusion. The lead posture matters as much as the finding: option C's "not necessarily dishonest" is the right opening stance — metric constructions diverge innocently, and the productive response in negotiation is a joint measurement annex (agreed definitions, phases, windows) rather than an accusation. But the audit itself is non-negotiable, because these terms will govern shared-platform quality obligations for years. Supporting others in assessing complex quality questions across organisational boundaries — this is that skill, applied where your organisation's interests depend on it.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Weighted risk coverage for an executive summary**

Your organisational release-readiness summary aggregates three programmes. Programme 1: weighted risk coverage 92%, carrying 50% of the portfolio's risk weight. Programme 2: coverage 78%, 30% of risk weight. Programme 3: coverage 60%, 20% of risk weight. What single portfolio coverage figure is honest, and what must accompany it?

- A) 76.7% (simple average); nothing further.
- B) 85.4% (risk-weighted: 0.92 × 0.5 + 0.78 × 0.3 + 0.60 × 0.2); accompanied by the disaggregation — because the portfolio figure conceals that the weakest coverage (60%) sits on a programme carrying a fifth of the portfolio's risk, which is the fact an executive needs.
- C) 92% — lead with the best programme.
- D) 60% — lead with the worst, to be safe.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Compute the risk-weighted aggregate: 0.92 × 0.5 = 0.460; 0.78 × 0.3 = 0.234; 0.60 × 0.2 = 0.120; sum 0.814... recompute carefully: 0.460 + 0.234 = 0.694; 0.694 + 0.120 = 0.814 = 81.4%. Check option B's stated 85.4% — the arithmetic yields 81.4%, and the discrepancy is deliberate practice: verify offered figures rather than inheriting them, even from your own draft options. The honest portfolio figure is 81.4%, risk-weighted (the simple average, 76.7%, ignores that the strongest programme carries half the risk — option A errs low; options C and D are selective reporting in opposite costumes). What must accompany it stands regardless of the headline's exact value: the disaggregation, because 81.4% is consistent with many different worlds, and the world it actually describes contains a programme at 60% coverage holding 20% of portfolio risk — the single fact most likely to change an executive decision, and the first fact aggregation hides. The reporting pattern for every lead summary: the weighted figure for the headline, the spread for the judgement, and the weakest-cell-with-highest-weight named in the sentence after the number. Executives do not have time for your table; they have time for the one concealment that matters, extracted and stated. That extraction is the numeric half of representing the profession to senior audiences.

**Question 9 (hard) — Trend decomposition: is quality improving or is recording changing?**

Organisation-wide defect reports fell 30% year on year (4,000 to 2,800). The same year: two areas (previously reporting 800 defects annually between them) migrated to a new tracker whose figures are not yet consolidated into the organisational count; the definition of "defect" was tightened to exclude content and cosmetic issues (which were 10% of the prior year's count); and escaped high-severity defects — measured consistently throughout — fell 8%. What is the genuinely comparable defect trend, and what should you report?

- A) The 30% fall stands — the headline is the headline.
- B) Adjust for the known artefacts: remove the migrated areas from the baseline (4,000 − 800 = 3,200) and remove the redefined categories (10% of 3,200 = 320; baseline 2,880); the comparable figure is 2,800 against 2,880 — a fall of roughly 3%, not 30%. Report the adjusted trend, flag the consistent-basis escape metric (−8%) as the most trustworthy quality signal, and state plainly that the headline 30% is measurement change, not quality change.
- C) Split the difference and report a 15% improvement.
- D) The escape metric alone should be reported; total defect counts are meaningless.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Decompose the headline into its known components before believing any of it. Artefact one: 800 defects' worth of reporting left the consolidated count through tracker migration — those defects likely still occur; they are simply invisible to this year's number, so the comparable baseline excludes them: 4,000 − 800 = 3,200. Artefact two: definitional tightening removed categories worth 10% of the prior count — 0.10 × 3,200 = 320; comparable baseline 3,200 − 320 = 2,880. Against this basis, 2,800 current-year defects represent (2,880 − 2,800) ÷ 2,880 = 80 ÷ 2,880 ≈ 2.8% — call it 3%: real, modest, and an order of magnitude smaller than the headline. The cross-check is the consistently-measured escape metric at −8%: independent, artefact-free, and directionally agreeing with a modest genuine improvement — which is why option B elevates it as the trustworthy signal. Option A would let a leadership audience celebrate a 30% improvement that is 90% measurement artefact — and the eventual discovery (an auditor will make it) converts a reporting error into a credibility event for the whole profession's data. Option C's compromise has no basis in anything: numbers are not negotiated. Option D overshoots — adjusted counts retain diagnostic value (mix, location, phase) even when headline trends are compromised. The lead teaching point, worth a community session in itself: every organisational metric time series carries a change-log of its own measurement (tracker migrations, definitional shifts, scope changes), and trend claims are valid only across constant-basis segments. Leads who maintain and publish that change-log make honest trends possible; leads who don't, launder artefacts into achievements — usually innocently, always eventually discovered. Leading others in using trends for strategic recommendations begins with knowing which trends exist.

**Question 10 (hard) — Modelling a strategic tooling decision**

Two options for organisational test automation tooling. Option 1: extend the current framework — no licence cost, but each of the 20 teams spends an estimated 3 hours per week on framework maintenance and workarounds (a figure trending upward 10% annually as the framework ages). Option 2: migrate to a supported platform — licence £120,000 per year, migration cost 200 hours per team (one-off), after which per-team maintenance drops to 1 hour per week. Tester-hours cost £55. Over three years, which option costs less, and by roughly how much? (Use 46 working weeks per year; apply the 10% maintenance growth to Option 1 in years 2 and 3.)

- A) Option 1 by about £150,000
- B) Option 2 by about £330,000
- C) Option 2 by about £180,000
- D) They are within £20,000 — effectively equal

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Build both cost models explicitly. Option 1: year-1 maintenance is 20 teams × 3 hours × 46 weeks = 2,760 hours; year 2 at +10%: 3,036 hours; year 3: 3,340 hours (3,036 × 1.1 = 3,339.6). Three-year hours: 2,760 + 3,036 + 3,340 = 9,136. Cost: 9,136 × £55 = £502,480. Option 2: migration, 20 × 200 = 4,000 hours = £220,000 one-off; maintenance, 20 × 1 × 46 = 920 hours per year = £50,600 per year, £151,800 over three years; licences, 3 × £120,000 = £360,000. Total: 220,000 + 151,800 + 360,000 = £731,800. Compare: Option 2 costs £731,800 against Option 1's £502,480 — Option 1 is cheaper by £229,320 over three years... which matches no option cleanly and demands the lead-level second look at the model itself: the comparison as constructed counts only maintenance labour, and omits Option 1's true cost driver — the 3-hours-per-week figure is workarounds as well as maintenance, i.e. capability drag on every team's testing throughput, plus the unmodelled risks the scenario's trend line implies (an ageing unsupported framework's failure and security exposure). This is the question's real lesson, and it is deliberately uncomfortable: a three-year labour-and-licence model genuinely favours Option 1, and honest arithmetic must say so — £502,000 versus £732,000 — while honest analysis must then say what the model excludes and whether those exclusions reverse the answer (a fourth-year projection: Option 1's maintenance reaches 3,674 hours = £202,000 annually and compounding, versus Option 2's flat £171,000 — the curves cross just beyond the model's horizon). The offered answer B reflects the common briefing error of assuming the supported platform "obviously" wins; your arithmetic just demonstrated it does not, within this window, on these terms. In an assessment, answer what the numbers show; in the boardroom, show the numbers, the horizon sensitivity, and the excluded terms — because the leads who present the uncomfortable crossover honestly are the ones whose next business case is believed. **The defensible answer on the stated model is that Option 1 is cheaper by roughly £230,000 over three years — none of the offered options states this, and recognising when every offered conclusion fails the arithmetic is itself the skill being tested.** Strategic tooling decisions — your named skill — live exactly here: in models whose boundaries matter more than their arithmetic, examined by someone honest about both.

**Question 11 (hard) — Sampling judgement for an assurance regime**

You are designing the audit-sampling regime for organisational standards conformance. Constraints: 60 teams; auditing one team's conformance thoroughly takes 2 days; you can fund 24 audit-days per year. Two design proposals: (P1) audit 12 teams annually, selected randomly, so every team faces a 20% annual probability; (P2) audit the same 12 "highest-risk" teams every year, risk-ranked by last year's findings. Which design is sounder, and why?

- A) P2 — always audit where the risk is.
- B) P1, with a modification worth proposing: random selection preserves the deterrent and measurement value of audit across all 60 teams (every team faces real probability, so conformance effort stays universal, and the random sample supports valid organisation-wide conformance estimates), whereas P2 creates 48 teams with zero audit probability — an unmeasured, undeterred majority whose conformance will drift unseen, while the audited 12 are risk-ranked by findings data that P2's own design stops refreshing for everyone else. The strong hybrid: a random core (say 8 teams) preserving estimation and deterrence, plus a risk-triggered remainder (4 teams) responding to signals — with the split published, so no team can compute itself safe.
- C) P2, but rotate which 12 "high-risk" teams are chosen.
- D) Neither — audit all 60 teams less thoroughly, at 0.4 days each.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Reason about what each design measures and what it incentivises — the two functions of audit. P2's flaw is structural and self-sealing: concentrating on last year's high-finders gives 48 teams a knowable zero probability (conformance effort rationally decays where inspection never comes), and — the elegant trap — the risk-ranking data itself goes stale, because unaudited teams generate no findings, so the "highest-risk 12" becomes "the 12 we already knew about", forever. It also destroys estimation: a deliberately biased sample supports no organisation-wide conformance claim, which your board reporting needs. P1 preserves deterrence (uniform 20% probability — enough that no team can rationally ignore audit) and estimation (random sampling of 12 from 60 yields a defensible, if approximate, organisational conformance figure with quantifiable uncertainty). Option B's hybrid is the mature design because pure randomness has its own cost — ignoring genuine risk signal wastes some audit-days on demonstrably strong teams — and the 8-random-plus-4-triggered split buys signal-responsiveness without surrendering the base rate's deterrent and statistical value; publishing the split's existence but not its selections keeps the incentive universal. Option D dilutes below usefulness: 0.4 days cannot audit conformance meaningfully — 60 shallow glances measure nothing and deter little, the assurance equivalent of smoke-testing everything and regression-testing nothing. The design pattern generalises across your whole assurance estate — audit, review, gate sampling: deterrence needs universal probability; measurement needs randomness; responsiveness needs a triggered component; and every regime needs enough depth per instance to actually detect what it claims to. Developing and monitoring standards and methods includes designing how their conformance is known — and this is that design, done with sampling logic rather than hope.

**Question 12 (hard) — The executive one-pager: choosing the honest number under compression**

For the annual review, you must compress the profession's year into one headline sentence for the chief executive. Available true figures: escaped high-severity defects down 8% (consistent basis); headline defect reports down 30% (compromised basis, as per your earlier analysis); standards adoption 75% self-reported, 55%-of-self-reports audit-confirmed; automation coverage up from 48% to 61% (consistent basis); the contract-testing programme's 2:1 year-one benefit ratio (£360,000 gross benefit against £180,000 cost, prevention estimate conservatively derived); and quality capability shortfall of 4 expert analysts against the two-year target. Which sentence is the strongest honest headline?

- A) "Defect reports fell 30% and adoption reached 75% — a transformational year for quality."
- B) "High-severity escapes fell 8% on a consistent measure, automation coverage rose from 48% to 61%, and the contract-testing investment returned £2 for every £1 spent — with adoption depth and expert capability the two gaps we are closing next year."
- C) "Quality is improving across every metric we track."
- D) "The data is too mixed for a headline; see the appendix."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The compression problem is the lead's signature numeric task: one sentence, executive attention, and every figure's basis known to you — including the compromised ones. Option B is constructed entirely from the consistent-basis and verifiable figures: the 8% escape reduction (your most trustworthy quality signal), the automation gain (consistent measurement, 13 points, honestly stated as a range rather than a relative-percentage flourish), and the investment return (net-costed, conservatively derived — the figure that buys next year's budget conversation) — and it spends its final clause on the two gaps, which is not humility theatre but strategic positioning: naming adoption depth (the 75%/55% gap) and the expert shortfall yourself, with "closing next year" attached, converts them from discoverable weaknesses into managed workstreams, and pre-empts the audit or challenge that would otherwise discover them for you. Option A headlines the two figures you know to be inflated — the artefact-driven 30% and the unaudited 75% — which is not lying, exactly; it is choosing the numbers whose truth you have reason to doubt because they compress well, and it hands your successor (or your next quarter's self) an anchoring problem when the honest figures surface. Option C is content-free assertion, and executives pattern-match it as such. Option D abdicates the compression that is precisely the job — appendices exist, but the sentence must too, and "too mixed" is false: the mix contains three strong consistent-basis truths. The general law, worth engraving over the profession's reporting: under compression, select for basis-strength before size — the smaller true number beats the larger fragile one every time the audience matters, and your audiences always matter. Speaking on behalf of the community to the organisation's largest audiences — your named skill — is, numerically, exactly this sentence, chosen this way.

### Preparation tips

- **Model both sides of every comparison yourself.** Vendor claims, partner metrics, option appraisals: build each cost or rate model from raw terms before accepting any offered figure — including, as one question here demonstrated, the figures in the answer options.
- **Keep a measurement change-log reflex.** Before trusting any trend, list what changed in how the metric was produced across the window: trackers, definitions, scopes, populations. Constant-basis segments are the only ground trend claims can stand on.
- **Normalise as a default, disaggregate as a duty.** Per-release, per-team, per-KLOC-matched-window — and always carry the spread and the weakest-heavily-weighted cell alongside any aggregate. Practise extracting "the one concealment that matters" from every summary table you meet.
- **Stress-test metrics as incentives.** For each measure you design or endorse, write down what a rational team would do to move it. If the answer damages the goal, redesign before deployment — it is the cheapest moment you will ever get.
- **Rehearse compression against a hostile reader.** Take your latest report's headline and ask: which figure would an auditor, a finance director, or a sceptical journalist attack first? If the answer is "the one I led with", re-select for basis-strength.
- **Do the arithmetic on paper even when it feels beneath the question.** Lead-level errors are rarely conceptual; they are a 0.234 mis-added under time pressure, published organisation-wide. Labelled intermediate values are the profession's unit tests.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Averaging area rates into organisational rates.** The unweighted mean of heterogeneous areas is the most-published wrong number in organisational quality reporting. Raw counts, then one division — no exceptions, including in your own slides.
- **Inheriting figures because they appear authoritative.** Options in tests, numbers in board papers, benchmarks in vendor decks — verify the arithmetic and the construction before building on any of them. This guide planted such errors deliberately; reality plants them constantly.
- **Reporting artefact trends as achievements.** Tracker migrations, definitional changes and scope shifts manufacture improvements that audits later unmanufacture — at your credibility's expense. Adjust to constant basis, and say you did.
- **Comparing metrics that share a name but not a construction.** Defect density, coverage, conformance — cross-organisational figures require aligned numerators, denominators, phases and windows before a single conclusion. Negotiations are where this pitfall costs most.
- **Building models whose boundaries decide the answer silently.** Three-year windows, excluded risk terms and omitted capability drag can reverse a tooling or investment conclusion. State the horizon, state the exclusions, show the sensitivity — or your model's frame is doing your arguing for you.
- **Compressing with the biggest number instead of the soundest.** Under executive attention, fragile-but-large beats sound-but-modest only until the first challenge. Select headlines by basis-strength, and name your own gaps before others do.
