# Lead Service Designer - Psychometric Assessment Resources

## Introduction

Welcome. This guide is written for you as a lead service designer in the UK Government Digital and Data profession — an expert practitioner who influences and mentors others, works with service managers and programme directors to develop design concepts, sets direction and assures the quality of design delivery across teams, and leads multiple or highly complex services. Your role description is notably organisational rather than project-shaped: you gain support for design strategy from senior leaders, communicate design strategy across organisational boundaries, coach others, measurably improve inclusive design practice across multiple teams, embed hypothesis-driven design and iterative working, ensure user insight reaches the wider organisation and across government, and create the conditions in which designers can do good work.

You may be facing a formal assessment as part of a selection process, or you may simply want to re-examine reasoning that has become fluent enough to be worth checking. Both are good reasons, and the second is the more interesting one. At your level the risk is not that you lack technique. It is that technique has become automatic, automatic reasoning feels certain, and nobody in your organisation is positioned to catch you.

Three features of the role shape everything that follows. First, you are accountable for design quality you did not produce, frequently on services you have not seen, judging from outputs rather than from the work behind them. That makes diagnostic reasoning — inferring the shape of a study or a design process from the traces it leaves — a core competence. Second, you synthesise across services: strategic insight comes from noticing what several bodies of evidence, gathered separately for different purposes, jointly imply, including where they contradict one another. Third, your authority is largely informal. You do not line-manage most of the people whose practice you assure, you cannot compel a team, and you influence senior leaders who outrank you. Your influence rests on your evidence being unassailable and your judgement being trusted.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the reasoning your role demands, presented through practical materials rather than abstract puzzles. At lead level the materials are organisational and the reasoning is diagnostic and strategic. You will meet evidence assembled by other people for other purposes, standards applied unevenly across teams, findings that appear to conflict until you establish what each measured, and prioritisation problems whose units are services and programmes rather than tasks.

The format is typically an online test of fifteen to thirty minutes covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective against a norm group of comparable seniority. Adaptive platforms raise difficulty after correct answers, and acuity reports distinguish speed from accuracy, both of which reward precision.

Three things characterise this section at your level.

The first is reasoning at a distance. You rarely see raw evidence. You see an output, a slide, a claim in a board paper, and must judge from visible traces whether the reasoning underneath holds. Reading an output for the shape of the work behind it is a specific, trainable skill, and several items exercise it.

The second is that the interesting questions concern mechanisms rather than instances. When something goes wrong repeatedly, the informative feature is the distribution of the failures. Concentrated in one team, under one condition, among experienced people? That is a process telling you it cannot be complied with. Scattered? That is something else. Reading distributions diagnostically is what separates organisational leadership from very senior practice.

The third is that prioritisation is genuinely hard because nothing dominates. Services differ in time horizon, reversibility, population affected and political weight, and there is no formula. The discriminating factor is whether you reason from value of information — how much does a decision change as a result of design input, and is there still time — rather than from importance, urgency or visibility.

You need to memorise nothing. What you need is the discipline not to let fluency substitute for examination.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The cognitive dimensions map onto the skills named in your role summary:

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Auditing against a standard**

Your organisational standard requires every design output to state: the problem, the evidence base including sample composition, the options considered, the decision and its rationale, the risks accepted, and the date. An output you are assuring states the problem, the decision and rationale, the date, describes its evidence as "based on research with 14 users", and has no risks section or options. How many requirements are unmet?

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Work through all six. Problem, decision and rationale, and date are present. The evidence description gives a count but nothing about sample composition, so that requirement fails. Options considered are absent, so that fails. Risks accepted are absent, so that fails. Three unmet, giving C. Option B usually comes from accepting "research with 14 users" as an evidence base, and that is the error worth dwelling on: a count is not a composition, and the requirement exists precisely to stop provenance being offered in place of it. Note which three failed — sample composition, options and risks — because at your level the pattern matters more than the instance. Those are the three most commonly omitted fields in almost every organisation, which tells you where to point an assurance sample and, more usefully, what to fix in the template rather than in the individual output.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction from a governance rule**

Your standard states: "A service may enter public beta only with design assurance sign-off, unless it replaces an existing service on a like-for-like basis and has been assessed as low risk." A programme lead reports: "We entered public beta last month without design assurance sign-off." Which must be true?

- A) The standard was breached.
- B) It was a low-risk like-for-like replacement.
- C) Either it was a low-risk like-for-like replacement, or the standard was breached.
- D) The programme should be paused.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A conditional with a stated exception, plus facts silent on the exception, licenses only the disjunction. Options A and B each assume a branch. Option D prescribes an action rather than stating what must be true, and the question asks the latter — a distinction worth noticing, because at this level assessments routinely offer a sensible action as a distractor in a logic item, and the sensible action is often what you would in fact do. Only C holds. Note the compound exception: like-for-like *and* low risk. Partial satisfaction is not satisfaction, and it is the most common misreading in real governance conversations. As the person assuring this, your opening move is to establish which route was taken, framed as an enquiry rather than a finding.

**Question 3 (moderate) — Reading a study from its output**

An output states: "Users trust the new identity flow — all 22 participants said they were confident their data would be handled properly." It notes recruitment from the departmental panel, sessions in departmental offices, and the confidence question asked by the facilitator as the closing item. What is the most important observation?

- A) Twenty-two participants is too small a base for a trust claim.
- B) Panel recruitment, departmental setting and a direct closing question all bias in the same direction towards socially desirable responding, so unanimity is evidence about the conditions rather than about the flow.
- C) Confidence about data handling is a different construct from trust.
- D) A validated scale should have been used.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Unanimity is itself the alarm, and the strongest observation explains it. Each feature alone would warrant caution: a departmental panel selects for people already well disposed, a government building primes deference, and a direct closing question from the facilitator invites the answer the participant believes is wanted. Because all three push the same way they compound rather than partly offset, and twenty-two out of twenty-two is close to what the conditions predict irrespective of the flow. Option C is a legitimate secondary point that does not explain the inflation. Option A misdiagnoses validity as size — 220 participants under identical conditions would produce the same result. Option D leaves all three biases untouched. When this feeds back, name the compounding: a team that swaps in a validated scale while keeping the panel and the setting will produce the same finding with better packaging, and will believe it more.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Strategic prioritisation across services**

You lead design across several services and can direct substantial capacity to one this quarter:

1. Service A chooses a delivery model next month, committing five years.
2. Service B is live with a persistent 30% drop-off affecting 400,000 users a year.
3. Service C launches in five weeks with designs signed off.
4. Service D is preparing a spending review submission due in eight months.

Which is strongest?

- A) B, because it affects the most users.
- B) A, because the decision is imminent, commits five years, and design input is the only thing that could still change it — giving it far the highest value of information.
- C) D, because spending review submissions shape long-term investment.
- D) C, because launching untested carries reputational risk.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Value of information is the right frame: how much does a decision change as a result of design input, and is there time? Service A is highest on both — a delivery model choice is exactly where design shifts the answer rather than decorating it, the window is open, and five years of commitment makes a wrong choice enormously expensive. Service B affects more users but has been stable for months and will remain so for three more; deferral costs comparatively little and analytics is likely the better first instrument. Service C is weakest despite sounding prudent: signed-off designs five weeks from launch means findings arrive with nowhere to go. Service D has eight months, and at your level the profile trap takes exactly this form — spending review work attracts attention, and attention is not decision leverage. Saying that to a director without sounding dismissive is the harder half.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Cross-service synthesis**

Three unrelated items reach you in a month. A discovery in one service finds people struggle to prove identity without a passport or driving licence. Analytics in a second show 45% drop-off at identity verification. The contact centre reports identity queries as its highest call driver across five services. What is the strongest synthesis?

- A) Three teams have found usability problems at their identity steps.
- B) The organisation has a cross-cutting identity assurance problem concentrated among people without common photographic documents — a strategic capability question for the roadmap rather than five separate service fixes.
- C) Identity verification is hard and should be simplified.
- D) The findings are unrelated and belong in separate reports.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The synthesis that adds value identifies a shared mechanism and reframes the unit of the problem. Five services showing difficulty at the same step, with a discovery pointing at document availability as the cause and contact centre volume confirming scale, indicates something structural rather than five coincidental interface issues. Option B also draws the consequence: this belongs on a roadmap as a capability question, not in five backlogs where each team builds its own partial workaround — which is wasteful and, more importantly, leaves the excluded population excluded five times over. Option A describes without synthesising. Option C is true and strategically useless, since "simplify it" instructs nobody. Option D misses the pattern. Ensuring insight is shared with and used by the wider organisation is a named part of your role, and this is its characteristic shape.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Scope of a claim heading for a roadmap**

An insight report drawing on twelve studies contains the headline "users across our services want proactive notification". The supporting material comes from four studies, all of services with statutory deadlines. What is the most useful intervention?

- A) Remove the claim.
- B) Note that the evidence comes exclusively from deadline-driven services, so the claim over-reaches as written; narrow it to that service type, or test whether it holds where there is no deadline, before it informs investment.
- C) Commission more studies.
- D) Accept it; four studies is a reasonable base.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The evidence is real and the sentence built on it reaches further than the evidence goes — the most common failure in organisational synthesis and the one with the largest financial consequences, because roadmap claims justify spending. Deadline-driven services supply a specific reason to want proactive notification: you are at risk of missing something. That motivation may not transfer to services used once, voluntarily, or where nothing can be missed. Option B preserves the finding, corrects the scope, and offers two proportionate routes. Option A discards something real. Option D lets an over-generalised claim justify investment across services it was never evidenced for. Option C reaches for the expensive remedy before the free one — narrowing costs nothing.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Diagnosing a systemic problem**

Six teams across your organisation consistently report that user research recruitment produces digitally confident participants in work, despite services serving many who are neither. All six use the central recruitment contract. What is the strongest diagnosis and response?

- A) Each team should supplement locally.
- B) The contract's supply model is structurally mismatched to the organisation's user base; document the pattern across all six teams, specify what suitable supply would look like, take it to the contract owner, and enable partnership routes in the meantime.
- C) The teams need better screeners.
- D) Recruitment should be brought in-house.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Six teams with the same problem and the same supplier is one supply problem, and diagnosing at the right level is the point of your role. Option B does the systemic work — evidence across teams is what moves a contract, and a specification is what an owner can act on — while unblocking teams immediately so six pieces of work are not held hostage to a procurement timeline. Option A accepts the structural failure and distributes its cost across every team indefinitely, producing six private workarounds and no institutional memory of why. Option C misdiagnoses: a screener filters the pool the supplier provides and cannot conjure a participant type the supply does not contain. Option D may eventually be right and is a large claim to make before specifying the requirement. The generalisable pattern: when the same problem appears across many teams, the fix almost never belongs in the teams.

**Question 8 (harder) — Scoping a flaw**

A study you are assuring used a prototype whose save-and-return function was broken throughout. Four findings: (i) participants could not locate eligibility criteria; (ii) participants abandoned when interrupted; (iii) sessions ran long; (iv) participants misread the declaration. Which are compromised?

- A) All four.
- B) None; the fault was incidental.
- C) Findings (ii) and (iii) are directly compromised, since inability to save and return plausibly produced both, while (i) and (iv) concern comprehension of content and are largely unaffected.
- D) Only (ii).

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The skill is scoping precisely rather than treating a flaw as total or trivial. A broken save function makes interruption catastrophic rather than inconvenient, so (ii) is manufactured; it also removes a natural pause, plausibly extending sessions, so (iii) is compromised. Findings (i) and (iv) concern locating and understanding content, which the fault does not touch. Option A discards two sound findings and, at organisational scale, does real damage: a leader who condemns wholesale teaches teams that any admitted fault invalidates everything, which is a direct incentive to conceal faults — and you will then be assuring outputs with the faults edited out. Option B leaves two artefactual findings circulating, where they will justify fixes to problems that do not exist. Option D catches the obvious compromise and misses the duration one.

**Question 9 (harder) — Apparent contradiction between services**

Two teams researching adjacent services reach opposite conclusions about the same user group: one reports a preference for completing in one sitting, the other for saving and returning. Both studies appear sound. What is strongest?

- A) Commission a third study.
- B) Establish what differs between the contexts before treating it as a contradiction — a preference expressed about a fifteen-minute task and one about a three-week process involving third-party documents are answers to different questions, and the synthesis is that preference depends on whether the task can be completed with information the user already holds.
- C) Report both and note the inconsistency.
- D) Treat the larger study as authoritative.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Apparent contradictions between sound studies are usually context differences in disguise, and the diagnostic move is to ask what each participant was responding to. Option B reaches the synthesis, and note that the synthesis is more useful than either original finding — it gives every service in the organisation a rule for deciding which pattern applies, which neither study alone could. Option A spends money answering a question that dissolves on inspection. Option C abdicates precisely the synthesis your role exists to produce, and "note the inconsistency" is how genuine insight gets filed as a caveat. Option D picks a winner on an irrelevant criterion. This shape recurs constantly in cross-service work: the insight lives in the difference between contexts rather than in either result.

**Question 10 (harder) — Reading the distribution of failures**

Your standard requires accessibility assurance before public beta. Across the year, fifteen of nineteen qualifying services complied; the four that did not were all in one directorate, all under ministerial deadline pressure, all designed by experienced practitioners. What is the strongest response?

- A) Report the four and require remediation.
- B) Treat the concentration as diagnostic: four failures in one directorate under identical pressure by people who know the requirement indicates a process that cannot be complied with under deadline conditions — investigate what it demands and how long it takes, build a fast route, and remediate the four alongside.
- C) Remind all designers of the requirement.
- D) Introduce a mandatory pre-beta gate for every service.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The distribution is the most informative feature. Scattered breaches by different people would indicate conduct; four concentrated under identical pressure by experienced practitioners indicate a process that does not survive a deadline. Option B investigates the mechanism and fixes it, while the closing clause preserves the point that a systemic diagnosis is not a reason to leave individual failures unremediated — real users are affected now. Option A treats the instances and guarantees recurrence. Option C is the weakest available response to any compliance failure: people who knew the requirement and missed it under pressure will not change on hearing it again. Option D imposes friction on fifteen compliant services to catch four, which is how organisational burden accumulates and how the next workaround gets invented — and you are the person who will be asked in two years why design governance is so slow.

**Question 11 (harder) — Evidence for an investment decision**

The organisation is deciding whether to invest in a shared identity capability. Available: qualitative findings from three services; analytics showing high drop-off across five; a supplier claim of 94% first-time verification success; a contact centre cost model. What is your most rigorous contribution?

- A) Present the internal evidence supporting investment.
- B) Set out what each source establishes and its limits — including that the supplier's 94% requires interrogation of its denominator and population before it can sit alongside internal drop-off data — and name the one or two unknowns that would most change the decision.
- C) Recommend investment on the converging evidence.
- D) Recommend further research before deciding.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Your contribution at an investment decision is neither advocacy nor delay; it is making the evidence base legible so the decision is taken knowingly. Option B does three things. It states what each source supports, preventing converging evidence being treated as stronger than the sum of its parts — three sources sharing a blind spot converge on the same error. It flags the supplier figure specifically, because a vendor's 94% is very likely measured on users who presented acceptable documents, excluding precisely the population driving your drop-off, and comparing them directly would be a costly error. And it names the residual uncertainty, which tells decision-makers what their choice actually rests on. Option A is advocacy dressed as evidence. Option C may reach the right answer on an unexamined basis. Option D defers a decision the evidence may already support, and leaders who reflexively call for more research find themselves excluded from decisions.

**Question 12 (harder) — A pattern across your own teams**

Reviewing a year of design outputs, you find two teams consistently produce strong work and five consistently weaker. Both strong teams contain designers who came from the same previous employer. What is the most useful inference and action?

- A) The five weaker teams need training.
- B) Something transferable is likely operating in the two strong teams — a routine, a habit, a standard applied before circulation — and the highest-value action is to find out what it actually is by examining how they work, then build it into the organisation's standards and coaching rather than attributing the difference to individual talent.
- C) Recruit more designers from that employer.
- D) Increase assurance scrutiny on the five weaker teams.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The pattern is an opportunity to identify a practice, not to grade people. Designers producing consistently better outputs are almost always carrying a routine — a critique habit, a rule about how claims are written, a check applied before anything circulates — and routines transfer in a way that ability does not. Option B goes and looks, which is the step almost everyone skips in favour of an assumption. Option A posits a knowledge deficit without diagnosing one, and training that does not target the real mechanism reliably fails to change practice. Option C is an unusable inference from a handful of people and misattributes a practice to a pedigree. Option D catches more problems and creates no capacity to prevent them, while consuming your assurance capacity indefinitely. Your role names coaching others and creating the conditions for designers to work well — and a naturally occurring comparison telling you what good looks like in your own context is the best raw material that work will ever get.

### Preparation tips

- **Read outputs for the work behind them.** Practise inferring recruitment, setting and question order from what an output says and omits.
- **Scope flaws precisely.** Wholesale condemnation incentivises concealment, and you will be assuring the edited version.
- **Ask who could not have appeared in this evidence.** The highest-yield question available.
- **Read the distribution of failures.** Concentrated non-compliance diagnoses a process; scattered non-compliance diagnoses conduct.
- **Frame prioritisation as value of information.** Decision leverage and remaining time, never importance or profile.
- **Practise synthesis deliberately.** Take three unrelated pieces of evidence from this month and ask what single thing they might describe.
- **Interrogate vendor figures for population and denominator.** They are almost never comparable to internal metrics.
- **Go and look before inferring a cause.** Especially when the inference flatters an assumption you hold.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Accepting a participant count as a sample composition.** Provenance is not composition.
- **Treating converging evidence as automatically stronger.** Shared blind spots converge too.
- **Reaching for training as the answer to a systemic problem.** Diagnose the mechanism first.
- **Reminding people of rules they knowingly missed.** The weakest available compliance response.
- **Adding friction for the compliant majority.** How burden accumulates and workarounds are born.
- **Picking a winner between contradictory studies.** The insight is in the difference between contexts.
- **Filing a contradiction as a caveat.** That is where organisational insight goes to die.
- **Letting fluency substitute for examination.** At your level the costly errors are the confident ones.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A numeric reasoning assessment measures how accurately you handle quantitative material. At lead level the emphasis is almost wholly on interrogation. You will need to compute correctly, but the questions that matter ask whether a figure means what somebody claims — and in most rooms you enter, you may be the only person who will ask.

The format is typically a timed online test of fifteen to thirty minutes, each item presenting a table, chart or paragraph of figures with a multiple-choice question. Calculators are usually provided and the arithmetic is deliberately tractable. Time per item is tight, so the operative skill is locating what matters and discarding what does not.

The professional stake is large. Quantitative evidence carries unearned authority: a number in a board paper is a fact, a design argument is a view, regardless of the rigour behind either. The figures you encounter are increasingly consequential and increasingly detached from anyone who could explain them — business cases, vendor claims, benefits realisation figures, performance metrics copied between documents for two years without the definition being re-checked. Your role names gaining support for design strategy from senior leaders, and in practice that frequently reduces to whether you can engage credibly with the numbers those leaders are working from.

Four habits carry most of the load. Establish what the question asks before looking at the data. Identify the denominator and the population it represents before calculating. Estimate, calculate, compare. And sanity-check against the world — a figure implying that 40% of users complete a form in under three seconds is measuring something other than what it claims.

Distractors come from a predictable catalogue: wrong denominator, percentage confused with percentage point, new value as the base in a percentage change, rates averaged without weighting, vendor metrics compared with internal ones. Recognising your answer among the options proves nothing; knowing the catalogue lets you see the trap being set.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The numerical skills tested map onto your named skills:

- **Interrogating quantitative claims** maps to your role level's requirement to assure the quality of design delivery across teams.
- **Aggregating across services** maps to **Evidence-based design**, where you analyse, synthesise and use evidence to improve how the organisation works.
- **Comparing rates between user groups** maps to **Designing for everyone**, where you measurably improve inclusive practice across multiple teams.
- **Judging sampling adequacy** maps to **Evidence-based design**, where you embed hypothesis-driven design and guide the organisation in using evidence.
- **Presenting figures at defensible strength** maps to **Design communication**, where you gain support for design strategy from senior leaders.
- **Detecting misleading metrics** maps to **Designing strategically**, where constraints and risks shape design and where a wrong metric shapes it wrongly.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Rate from counts**

Across a programme, 45 of 60 participants completed the core task unaided. What percentage?

- A) 66%
- B) 72%
- C) 75%
- D) 80%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 45 ÷ 60 = 0.75, so 75%, and the answer is C. Estimate first: 45 of 60 is three quarters, since 30 would be half. Options A and B come from dividing by 68 and 62. Even here, the prior question in real work is whether aggregating 60 participants across a programme into one rate is meaningful — if the studies tested different tasks with different populations, the figure is arithmetically correct and referentially empty, which is a distinction you will spend considerable time enforcing in other people's outputs and occasionally in your own summaries.

**Question 2 (easy) — Drop-off against the right base**

A step is reached by 9,600 users and passed by 7,200. What is the drop-off rate?

- A) 25%
- B) 33%
- C) 75%
- D) 30%

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The drop-off is 9,600 − 7,200 = 2,400, measured against those who reached the step: 2,400 ÷ 9,600 = 0.25, or 25%, giving A. Option B divides by those who continued — the most common error in journey analytics, and one that overstates every drop-off. The rule is that drop-off is measured against arrivals. At your level the consequence is not one wrong figure but a wrong ranking: if teams compute steps with different denominators, the organisational picture of where design effort should go is distorted, and you are the person adjudicating that picture.

**Question 3 (easy) — Disaggregated rates**

| Group | Users | Completed |
|---|---|---|
| Digitally confident | 8,000 | 7,200 |
| Low digital confidence | 4,000 | 2,000 |
| Assistive technology users | 2,000 | 500 |

What is the overall rate, and the gap between highest and lowest group rates?

- A) 60% overall; 50 point gap
- B) 69% overall; 65 point gap
- C) 69% overall; 50 point gap
- D) 65% overall; 65 point gap

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Completions total 7,200 + 2,000 + 500 = 9,700, across 14,000 users, so 9,700 ÷ 14,000 = 0.693, about 69%. Group rates are 90%, 50% and 25%, giving a 65 point gap. Hence B. The substantive point belongs in your standards rather than in one report: the overall figure of 69% describes no group in the data, and reporting it alone conceals that assistive technology users fail three times in four. An organisational standard that does not require disaggregation by access need has a hole in it, and the hole is exactly the shape of the users least able to complain.

**Question 4 (easy) — Centre and spread**

Thirteen sessions produced task times in minutes: 5, 6, 7, 8, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 14, 16, 19, 84. Which summary is most defensible?

- A) Mean 16.1 minutes
- B) Median 10 minutes, range 5 to 84, with the 84-minute case described separately
- C) Median 10 minutes
- D) Mean 16.1 minutes, range 5 to 84

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The mean of about 16.1 exceeds eleven of thirteen observations, so it represents almost nobody. The median is the seventh value, which is 10, and describes the group well. But the median alone conceals a session lasting more than eight times the typical duration, which is very likely the most informative observation — something went badly wrong for that person. Option B supplies centre, spread and explicit treatment of the outlier. Option D pairs the range with a misleading centre. Option C buries the extreme case. The rule to enforce organisationally: a central figure never travels alone, and an outlier is a lead rather than noise.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Weighted aggregation**

Four studies: 15 participants at 40% completion; 25 at 60%; 10 at 70%; 30 at 80%. What is the overall rate across all 80?

- A) 62%
- B) 65%
- C) 66%
- D) 68%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Convert each to counts. Study 1: 40% of 15 = 6. Study 2: 60% of 25 = 15. Study 3: 70% of 10 = 7. Study 4: 80% of 30 = 24. Total 6 + 15 + 7 + 24 = 52, across 80 participants, so 52 ÷ 80 = 0.65, which is 65% and the answer is B. Option A is the unweighted mean of 40, 60, 70 and 80, which is 62.5 and over-weights the two smallest studies at the expense of the 30-participant study that should dominate. Always return to counts, and build in a second habit alongside it: re-check the total before it leaves your hands, because at your level a plausible-looking figure travels on your authority and nobody downstream will question it.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Funnel denominators**

Funnel: 160,000 start, 120,000 pass identity, 72,000 upload evidence, 64,800 submit. What proportion of those who passed identity went on to submit?

- A) 40.5%
- B) 54%
- C) 60%
- D) 90%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The stem names users who passed identity, so the denominator is 120,000 and the part is 64,800: 64,800 ÷ 120,000 = 0.54, giving B. Option A uses 160,000, option C is the identity-to-upload rate, option D is the upload-to-submit rate. All are true of this funnel; one answers the question. Strategically, the funnel also tells you where capacity belongs: 40,000 lost at identity and 48,000 between identity and upload, against 7,200 at submission. A team proposing to redesign submission would need a very strong reason, and being able to see that in fifteen seconds is what lets you redirect a programme's plan in the meeting rather than a month later.

**Question 7 (moderate) — A benefits claim**

A benefits paper states a change raised completion from 60% to 72%, describing it as "a 20% improvement". Is that accurate?

- A) No; it is a 12% improvement.
- B) Yes as arithmetic — 12 ÷ 60 = 20% relative increase — but the expression is ambiguous, and the paper should state either "20% relative increase" or "12 percentage points".
- C) No; it is 12 percentage points and calling it 20% is wrong.
- D) No; it should be 16.7%.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The relative increase is 12 ÷ 60 = 0.2, so 20% is defensible arithmetic. The problem is labelling: "a 20% improvement" will be read by many as a rise from 60% to 80%. Option B captures both halves. Option C over-corrects into an error of its own, since relative change is a legitimate expression. Option A confuses the units; option D divides by the new value. At your level this is governance rather than arithmetic: benefits papers routinely select the flattering framing, usually without intent to mislead, and an organisation where nobody asks for both numbers will eventually publish one it cannot defend. Asking for both as a standing expectation, rather than as a challenge to an individual, is the cheapest control available.

**Question 8 (moderate) — A vendor claim**

A supplier states their identity product achieves "94% first-time verification success". Your organisation's rate, measured as verified users divided by users reaching the identity step, is 71%. What is the most important question before comparing?

- A) Whether the 94% is independently audited.
- B) What denominator the supplier uses — specifically whether it counts only users presenting acceptable documents, since excluding those without documents removes exactly the population driving your 29% failure.
- C) Whether the figure is current.
- D) Whether the supplier has other government clients.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Two rates are comparable only if their denominators describe the same population, and vendor metrics frequently do not. If the supplier's 94% counts successes among users who submitted a valid passport or driving licence, the users you are losing have been excluded from the denominator entirely — and adopting the product could leave your failure rate essentially unchanged while the vendor's metric remains perfectly accurate. Option B identifies this. Options A, C and D are reasonable due diligence that does not touch comparability, and asking them instead produces the appearance of scrutiny without its substance, which is worse than asking nothing because it satisfies the room. This single question is among the highest-value contributions available to you in a procurement discussion, and one almost nobody else is positioned to ask.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Reconstructing an implied value**

A business case claims a 5 percentage point completion improvement on 1.6 million annual starts is worth £9.6 million a year. What is the implied value per additional completed application?

- A) £6
- B) £60
- C) £120
- D) £240

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A 5 percentage point improvement on 1.6 million starts gives 0.05 × 1,600,000 = 80,000 additional completions. Dividing: £9,600,000 ÷ 80,000 = £120, so C. Option A divides by 1.6 million, valuing every start. This calculation takes thirty seconds and immediately tells you whether a business case is plausible — £120 per additional completed application may be reasonable for a high-value transaction and implausible for a simple information service. The challenge it enables is far more effective than disputing a total: "so we are valuing each additional completion at £120 — is that the right order of magnitude?" is specific, answerable, and requires accusing nobody.

**Question 10 (harder) — A metric that conflates populations**

A board paper reports "average time to complete: 11 minutes" for a service your evidence shows most users taking over forty minutes across several sittings. What is the likely explanation and the right intervention?

- A) The board paper is wrong and should be corrected.
- B) The metric almost certainly measures single sessions rather than end-to-end journeys, so multi-sitting completions are excluded or counted only for their final short visit, and abandoned sessions may be included — ask for the definition and the distribution, and propose an end-to-end measure alongside it.
- C) The research sample was atypical.
- D) The figures cannot be reconciled.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Session-based analytics and journey-based reality diverge systematically for any service completed over more than one sitting, and the divergence always flatters the service. Someone spending forty minutes across three visits typically appears as one short final session, and abandoned sessions may drag the mean lower. The figure is probably calculated correctly and defined in a way that cannot answer the question the board believes it answers. Option B names the mechanism and proposes a constructive intervention — asking for a definition is not an accusation, and offering an end-to-end measure gives the metric owner something to build rather than something to defend. Option A picks a fight over a number that is not wrong, which is how leads acquire a reputation for obstruction. Option C blames the research. Option D forecloses a useful reconciliation.

**Question 11 (harder) — Sampling adequacy**

A directorate proposes measuring whether a redesign improved completion by running 50 moderated sessions before and 50 after. Baseline is 62%; a 5 percentage point improvement is considered meaningful. What is the strongest response?

- A) Agree; 100 sessions is substantial.
- B) Explain that a 5 point difference on 50 per group is two or three participants and well inside ordinary variation, so the comparison cannot detect it — recommend measuring the rate through live analytics across the full user base and spending the 100 sessions on mechanisms, which moderated research does uniquely well.
- C) Increase to 80 per group.
- D) Run it and report the difference with caveats.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Fifty per group cannot distinguish a five point difference from chance; reliable detection needs several hundred per group. Option B says so and redirects rather than merely objecting, which is the substantive contribution: analytics measures rates across the whole population at negligible marginal cost, while moderated sessions are the only instrument that explains why a rate is what it is. Option A endorses a design that cannot answer its own question. Option C moves towards adequacy without reaching it while still misallocating scarce capacity. Option D is the most damaging at organisational level, because caveated findings from underpowered comparisons get cited without their caveats — and once your organisation establishes that fifty-session comparisons can measure rates, you will be arguing against that precedent for the rest of your tenure.

**Question 12 (harder) — A claim already in a board paper**

A board paper states: "Design research confirms 93% of users can complete the service independently." You trace it to a study of 43 participants, 40 of whom completed, recruited from the departmental panel. The board meets in four days. What is most important?

- A) Ask for the figure to be removed.
- B) Go to the paper's author, explain that 43 panel-recruited participants cannot support a population rate, propose the accurate statement — 40 of 43 panel participants completed unaided — and offer what the organisation does have if the board needs a rate; then confirm the correction is made before the paper is tabled.
- C) Raise it at the board.
- D) Note it for the next assurance cycle.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A qualitative sample from a self-selected panel has become a population statistic in a document informing a decision — a category error rather than a rounding one. Option B does the four things that matter: it goes to the author first, names precisely what the evidence supports, offers an alternative source so the board is not left with a hole where a number was, and closes the loop by confirming the change lands. That last element is most often omitted, and the correction agreed but never made is a familiar organisational failure. Option A asks for removal without a replacement, which invites resistance. Option C corrects the record in the most public and least recoverable setting, embarrasses the author, and converts your assurance role into an adversarial one. Option D allows a board decision to proceed on a claim you know to be unsupported.

### Preparation tips

- **Ask who could not have appeared in this data.** The highest-yield question against any figure.
- **Reconstruct implied unit values.** Thirty seconds of division establishes plausibility.
- **Interrogate vendor denominators.** Vendor and internal metrics are almost never defined alike.
- **Ask for the definition and the distribution.** Most misleading metrics are correctly calculated and wrongly defined.
- **Return to counts before combining rates, and re-check the total.** An aggregation error travels on your authority.
- **Know what samples can detect.** No power calculation needed to see that 50 per group cannot see five points.
- **Trace three figures in current board papers back to source.** More instructive than any drill.
- **Ask for both framings as a standing expectation.** Percentage points and relative change, as a norm rather than a challenge.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Averaging rates without weighting.** The commonest real-world error in aggregated reporting.
- **Comparing vendor rates to internal rates.** Different populations, same-looking numbers, large price tags.
- **Converting qualitative samples into population rates.** The error most likely to reach a board paper.
- **Measuring drop-off against those who continued.** It overstates every step and distorts the ranking.
- **Reporting an aggregate that could be disaggregated.** A figure describing no group conceals the group that is failing.
- **Accepting a mean without a spread or a definition.** Session metrics systematically flatter multi-sitting services.
- **Endorsing an underpowered comparison "with caveats".** Caveats do not travel; the number does.
- **Correcting a board paper at the board.** Go to the author, and confirm the change lands.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately you read. At your level the documents that matter are governance frameworks, organisational standards, audit reports, cross-government guidance, ministerial correspondence, board papers and contracts. A misread exception clause becomes a compliance failure across several teams; a misread audit finding becomes advice given confidently to a director general.

The classic format presents a short passage of dense workplace prose, typically three to six sentences, followed by statements to classify as True, False or Cannot Say. True means the statement follows necessarily. False means the passage contradicts it. Cannot Say means the passage neither establishes nor excludes it. Alongside these come comprehension items, main-point items, and questions asking which of four summaries is most accurate.

The governing rule is that you answer from the passage alone, and here is the part specific to you: expertise actively costs marks. The more governance prose you have read, the more readily you supply the clause you expect, complete the sentence the way such sentences usually end, and feel entirely confident doing it. Candidates at your level underperform on verbal reasoning at rates that surprise them, and this is nearly always the mechanism. The counter-habit must be mechanical: before marking anything True, put your finger on the words.

Cannot Say is the answer experienced leaders avoid most. Forming a view under incomplete information is what your job consists of, and the reflex is deeply worn in. Here it is a liability. If you are filling a gap with knowledge or inference, however reasonable, the answer is Cannot Say.

Small words carry the load. "All" is not "most". "May" is not "must". "Should" is not "will". "Consider whether" is not "recommend". "Solely", "entirely", "unless", "provided that" and "where practicable" govern everything around them. Wrong answers are built almost entirely from swaps of these terms.

There is a direct professional payoff. Your role names communicating design strategy across organisational boundaries, and a great deal of what blocks that is a shared misreading of what some document requires. Reading it precisely is how you find the room everyone believed was not there.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The verbal skills tested map onto your named skills:

- **Reading precisely** maps to setting direction and assuring the quality of design delivery across teams.
- **Separating statement from inference** maps to **Evidence-based design**, where you guide the organisation in how to use evidence.
- **Identifying the main point** maps to **Design communication**, where you gain support for design strategy from senior leaders and communicate across boundaries.
- **Handling qualified language** maps to **Designing strategically**, where you use constraints in technology, systems and policy to shape design — and where knowing which constraints are hard tells you where design can move.
- **Reading for absence** maps to **Designing for everyone**, where noticing that a framework never mentions access needs has consequences at scale.
- **Reading cross-government prose accurately** maps to ensuring insights are used across the organisation and across government, where shared standards are the medium.

### Practice questions

*Passage A — for Questions 1 to 4*

"Organisations must submit an annual design assurance return to the cross-government profession board, unless all design work within the organisation has been delivered under a centrally managed programme. Returns must confirm that accessibility assurance was completed where required, and must list any services where it was not. Where an organisation identifies a service released without required accessibility assurance, it must notify the board within ten working days of identifying it, separately from the annual return. Returns are published across government; notifications are not."

**Question 1 (easy)** — Statement: "Every organisation must submit an annual design assurance return." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The passage attaches an exception: organisations where all design work was delivered under a centrally managed programme are exempt. The statement's "every" contradicts that, so it is False. This is the most reliable pattern in the section — absolute term in the statement, exception in the passage. Note that the exception says "all design work", not "most", which narrows it substantially and supplies the material for Question 3.

**Question 2 (easy)** — Statement: "Notifications of missed accessibility assurance are published across government." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The final sentence states that returns are published across government and notifications are not. The statement contradicts this, making it False. The item tests whether you read past the point where a sentence appeared to finish its work — the semicolon clause carries the operative distinction, and a reader who registered "returns are published across government" and moved on would generalise it. Governance prose places qualifications after semicolons with striking regularity, and slowing down there rather than speeding up is a habit worth building deliberately.

**Question 3 (moderate)** — Statement: "An organisation where nineteen of twenty services were delivered under a centrally managed programme must submit a return." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The exemption applies only where *all* design work was delivered under a centrally managed programme. Nineteen of twenty does not satisfy that, so the exemption does not apply and the requirement stands — the statement follows and is True. The item rewards carrying "all" from the first sentence to a scenario several lines later. It also reflects a recurring organisational error: partial satisfaction of an exception condition is non-compliance, and organisations routinely assume the reverse on the reasoning that they are substantially covered. Catching that before a return is due, rather than after, is squarely your job.

**Question 4 (moderate)** — Statement: "A service released without required accessibility assurance must be notified within ten working days of release." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The passage requires notification within ten working days of *identifying* the service, not of its release. Those triggers can be many months apart — a service released in February and identified in a September assurance review must be notified in September, and would not be retrospectively late. The statement misstates the trigger, so it is False. Small distinction, large consequence: trigger events determine whether an organisation is compliant, and misreading one produces either unnecessary alarm or false comfort, both expensive when reported to a cross-government board. Whenever a passage specifies a deadline, establish what starts the clock before anything else.

*Passage B — for Questions 5 to 8*

"The cross-government review of design assurance found considerable variation between departments. Five departments operate design critique before release; the remainder rely on line management sign-off. Departments with critique reported fewer instances of design decisions being reopened after release, although the review did not examine whether those decisions were better. The review recommends that the profession board consider whether a common assurance approach would be proportionate."

**Question 5 (easy)** — Statement: "Some departments do not operate design critique before release." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The passage states that five departments operate critique and the remainder rely on line management sign-off, establishing directly that some do not. The statement follows and is True. Answer quickly and bank the time. The only conceivable ambiguity — whether "the remainder" might be empty — is closed off by the passage describing what the remainder does.

**Question 6 (moderate)** — Statement: "Design critique produces better decisions." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The passage reports fewer reopened decisions in critique departments and then states explicitly that whether the decisions were better was not examined. Fewer reopenings and better decisions are different things: decisions may be reopened less because they are better, or because critique produces decisions that are more defensible in presentation, or because a critiqued decision carries institutional weight that discourages challenge. The passage flags its own silence, so Cannot Say. A candidate answering True has substituted a professional intuition for what the text supports — and at your level that is exactly the substitution that produces a cross-government recommendation resting on a finding the review declined to make.

**Question 7 (moderate)** — Statement: "The review recommends introducing a common assurance approach." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The review recommends that the board *consider whether* a common approach would be proportionate, which includes the possibility of concluding it would not be. The statement asserts a recommendation to introduce, which was not made, so it is False rather than Cannot Say — the passage tells you what was recommended and it was something different. This pervades review and audit prose, and misreading it produces a familiar sequence: a recommendation to consider becomes a commitment to deliver, is tracked, and is reported against for a year without anyone having decided it was right.

**Question 8 (harder)** — Statement: "Line management sign-off is a weaker form of assurance than design critique." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The passage describes two approaches and reports one difference in outcome while explicitly declining to assess decision quality. It makes no judgement about relative strength. A reader may reasonably infer that critique is stronger, and the passage neither supports nor excludes that, so Cannot Say. What makes this harder than Question 6 is that the passage's structure leans towards critique — mentioned first, associated with a favourable outcome — and structural lean is not content. Noticing when you are responding to a document's implied stance rather than its assertions is among the most transferable things this section trains, and it applies directly to audit reports, which routinely imply considerably more than they will state.

*Passage C — for Questions 9 to 12*

"Design decisions must not be altered to reflect the preferences of stakeholders where doing so would misrepresent the evidence. Presentation may be adapted to the audience provided the substance is unchanged. Where a designer is asked to alter a design rationale, the matter must be raised with the head of profession, who is accountable for the integrity of design outputs. Where design input cannot reach decision-makers before a decision is taken, the reason must be recorded; records are reviewed annually."

**Question 9 (easy)** — Statement: "Design rationale may be presented differently to different audiences." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The second sentence permits adapting presentation to the audience provided substance is unchanged, and the statement claims only that different presentation is permitted. It follows and is True. The statement is weaker than the passage's full provision, omitting the substance condition — but a weaker statement the passage supports is still True. Candidates occasionally mark such statements False for incompleteness, confusing "does not say everything" with "says something wrong".

**Question 10 (moderate)** — Statement: "A designer asked to alter a design rationale must refuse." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The passage establishes that decisions must not be altered where doing so would misrepresent the evidence, and that a request to alter a rationale must be raised with the head of profession. Neither states what the individual designer must personally do at the moment of the request. A duty to escalate is not a duty to refuse — arguably the escalation route exists so that an individual is not left to refuse alone, which is a substantive protection and, in your role, the protection you are supposed to provide. The passage neither imposes nor excludes a duty to refuse, so Cannot Say. Note also that the first sentence's prohibition is itself conditional: alteration is prohibited *where doing so would misrepresent the evidence*, which is narrower than a blanket prohibition.

**Question 11 (harder)** — Statement: "Altering a design rationale at a stakeholder's request always breaches the requirements." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The prohibition is conditional: alteration must not occur *where doing so would misrepresent the evidence*. An alteration that clarifies, reorders or reframes without misrepresenting anything is not caught by it, and the second sentence expressly permits adapting presentation provided substance is unchanged. The statement's "always" contradicts both, so it is False. This is a fine and consequential distinction: read as absolute, the clause makes every stakeholder edit suspect and makes designers defensive about reasonable requests; read as written, it identifies precisely the thing that is prohibited, which is misrepresentation. Standards that get read as absolutes lose their force, because people who know the absolute reading is unworkable start ignoring the clause entirely.

**Question 12 (harder) — Main point**

Which best captures the main point of Passage C?

- A) Design rationales must never be changed.
- B) The substance of design reasoning must not be misrepresented to suit stakeholders, with adaptation permitted in form, escalation provided where independence is pressured, accountability located with the head of profession, and honesty required where timing fails.
- C) The head of profession is accountable for design integrity.
- D) Reasons must be recorded when design input arrives late.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The main point must span the passage while adding nothing. Option A overstates: adaptation of presentation is expressly permitted and the prohibition is specifically on misrepresentation. Options C and D are each accurate about one clause and silent on the rest. Option B captures all four elements and the logic joining them — design input is useful only if it is both honest and timely, so the passage protects honesty with a conditional rule, an escalation route and an accountable owner, and handles timing with a requirement of disclosure rather than a prohibition. Main-point items reward the option broad enough to cover everything and disciplined enough to invent nothing, and three of these four are true details promoted beyond their scope.

### Preparation tips

- **Set expertise aside mechanically.** Before marking True, point at the words.
- **Treat Cannot Say as decisive.** The reflex to form a view is what costs marks here.
- **Circle every qualifier.** All, every, entirely, solely, may, must, should, unless, provided that, where practicable, consider whether, proportionate.
- **Establish what starts the clock.** Deadlines hang on trigger events, and the trigger is often not the obvious one.
- **Notice conditional prohibitions.** "Must not, where X" is much narrower than "must not".
- **Separate "recommends considering X" from "recommends X".** Audit and review prose runs on this.
- **Notice structural lean.** Order of mention and favourable association are not assertions.
- **Practise on cross-government standards.** Write three statements about a page — one True, one False, one Cannot Say.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Answering from professional knowledge.** The dominant error at your level.
- **Completing the clause you expected.** Governance prose has conventions; passages exploit them.
- **Missing "all", "entirely" and "solely".** Partial satisfaction of an exception is non-compliance.
- **Reading a conditional prohibition as absolute.** It makes the standard unworkable and then ignored.
- **Reading fewer reopenings as better decisions.** Passages distinguish outcome proxies from the thing itself.
- **Confusing a rule about outcomes with a rule about conduct.** Different obligations.
- **Responding to implied stance.** Tone and structure are not content.
- **Rushing after a semicolon.** The operative qualification lives there disproportionately often.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment presents realistic workplace scenarios and asks what you would do. There is no passage to parse and no arithmetic. You get a dilemma of the kind your role produces, four or five candidate responses, and a question asking which is most effective and often which is least.

This section measures whether your judgement matches what the profession considers good practice at your level. Your role places you as an expert practitioner who sets direction and assures design quality across teams, leads multiple or highly complex services, gains support for design strategy from senior leaders, coaches others, measurably improves inclusive practice across teams, embeds hypothesis-driven and iterative working, and creates an inclusive, productive environment for designers. The strongest answers involve organisational rather than local action, capability building rather than problem solving, and holding positions in forums where the pressure is real.

Three features distinguish your level.

The first is that you are usually acting on systems rather than instances. A senior designer who fixes a flawed output has done their job. A lead who fixes it without asking why the assurance process did not catch it has done half of theirs. The reliable tell of a strong option is whether it addresses the mechanism that produced the problem.

The second is that your authority is largely informal. You do not line-manage most of the people whose practice you assure, you cannot compel a directorate, and you influence senior leaders who outrank you. Options leading with instruction, mandate or escalation tend to score poorly — not because escalation is wrong, but because it is capital you spend rather than a tool you use freely.

The third is that you absorb pressure downwards. When a director leans on a designer, the designer should not be holding that line. Options that leave a colleague exposed to a power differential you could absorb are weak even when the design position survives.

Four principles carry most scenarios. Users come first, including in work you did not commission. Protect the integrity of the design rationale, and protect the people whose job it is to protect it. Build capability while solving the problem. Calibrate escalation to severity.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The judgement dimensions map onto your named skills:

- **Assuring across boundaries** maps to setting direction and assuring the quality of design delivery across teams.
- **Coaching and capability** map to **Leading design**, where you mentor and coach other designers and create an inclusive, productive environment, and to **Design communication**, where you coach others in communicating design.
- **Influence with senior leaders** maps to **Design communication**, where you gain support for design decisions and strategy from senior leaders.
- **Organisational inclusion** maps to **Designing for everyone**, where you measurably improve inclusive practice across multiple teams and help others mitigate the influence of privilege and power.
- **Embedding practice** maps to **Iterative design**, where you embed iterative design and agile working across teams, and iterate delivery processes or team structures.
- **Working across boundaries** maps to **Designing together**, where you help teams connect and work across organisational boundaries.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — A designer under pressure from a director**

A senior designer tells you a director has asked them to remove an accessibility finding from a board summary because it "muddies the message". They are unsure what to do. What is the most effective response?

- A) Tell them to leave it in and cite your instruction.
- B) Agree with them how the finding should be presented, including mitigation; offer to attend; and take the request itself up with the director yourself, since a director asking for findings to be removed is a matter at your level.
- C) Contact the director and instruct them not to make such requests.
- D) Escalate to the director general.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Two things must happen: the finding survives, and the designer emerges more capable and less exposed. Option B does both. Agreeing the presentation builds their judgement rather than substituting yours; a constructive framing with mitigation gives them something to bring rather than a refusal; offering to attend supplies backing without takeover. Most importantly, taking the request up with the director is your job — a power differential of that size is what your role exists to absorb, and leaving a senior designer to manage it alone is a leadership failure even if the finding survives. Option A is least effective: it leaves them defending a position by proxy, teaches nothing, and reframes integrity as hierarchy. Option C treats a conversation as an instruction you are not positioned to give. Option D escalates far past the point and spends capital you will need.

**Question 2 (easy) — A standard nobody follows**

Your organisation has a design standard that is widely ignored. Designers describe it as unrealistic. What is the strongest approach?

- A) Reissue it with a message from you.
- B) Review recent outputs with designers to establish which requirements are genuinely unworkable and which are merely unclear, revise it with them, and pair it with a lightweight routine — such as design critique — that gives compliance a moment of application and delivers something designers want anyway.
- C) Introduce mandatory compliance checks.
- D) Run training on the standard.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A standard everybody ignores has already failed, and reissuing changes only the date. Option B treats the designers' objection as data — some requirements probably are unworkable under real conditions, and the rest are usually unclear rather than unreasonable. Revising with them produces authorship and therefore defenders, which is the only mechanism by which a standard becomes self-sustaining rather than dependent on your attention. Pairing it with a routine matters because a standard without a moment of application is an aspiration; critique supplies that moment and gives something back. Option A is least effective, being the purest form of appearing to act. Option C enforces a standard people have told you is unrealistic, producing compliance theatre and resentment. Option D is useful and insufficient alone.

**Question 3 (easy) — Conditions for designers to work well**

Designers across your teams report that they are pulled into delivery firefighting, have no time for critique, and rarely see each other. Two have left in six months. What is the strongest response?

- A) Discuss workload with each designer's delivery manager.
- B) Treat it as a structural problem: establish a protected routine that brings designers together regularly for critique and shared work, negotiate with delivery leadership for the time it needs by showing what the current pattern costs in rework and attrition, and change how designers are deployed if single embedding is the cause.
- C) Introduce a wellbeing initiative.
- D) Recruit replacements quickly.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Two departures in six months alongside no critique and no professional contact is a structural pattern, not a series of individual circumstances, and your role explicitly names creating an inclusive, productive environment for designers to work in. Option B addresses the structure on three fronts: a protected routine, the negotiation that makes the routine survive contact with delivery pressure, and a change to deployment if the underlying cause is designers embedded singly with nobody to think alongside. The negotiation detail matters — protected time that has not been paid for in delivery leadership's currency gets cancelled by the third sprint. Option D is least effective: replacing people without changing the conditions produces the same attrition on a lag, at considerable cost. Option A addresses symptoms one at a time. Option C treats a design of work problem as a morale problem.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Assuring across a boundary**

A directorate you do not manage has released a design whose central rationale is unsupported by the evidence cited, and it is being referenced in a spending submission. What is most effective?

- A) Correct the record with the submission's author.
- B) Speak to the designer and their lead first, set out specifically what the evidence supports, and agree how the correction is made and communicated to those already relying on it — with a clear next step if they decline.
- C) Raise it at the departmental board.
- D) Note it for the annual assurance return.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** The claim needs correcting and the relationship needs to survive, since you will be assuring this directorate for years. Option B goes to the people responsible first — they may hold context you lack, and if they do not, the correction lands far better coming from them — and it carries a next step if they refuse. The element most often omitted is communicating to those already relying on the claim, without which a corrected output sits quietly alongside an uncorrected submission. Option A bypasses the designer, damages a relationship, and risks acting on your own misreading. Option C is disproportionate as a first move and converts assurance into confrontation. Option D is least effective by a distance: an annual return is a record, not an intervention, and a spending submission is moving now.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Non-compliance concentrated in one place**

Accessibility assurance was missed on four services, all in one directorate, all under ministerial deadline pressure, all by experienced designers. What is the strongest response?

- A) Report the four and require remediation.
- B) Remediate the four, and separately investigate the assurance process — what it demands, how long it takes, whether a fast route exists — on the reasoning that experienced designers who know the requirement and miss it under pressure are telling you the process cannot be complied with when time is short.
- C) Remind all designers of the requirement.
- D) Introduce a mandatory pre-release gate for every service.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** The concentration is the most informative feature. Scattered breaches would indicate conduct; four concentrated under identical pressure by experienced people indicate a process that does not survive a deadline. Option B handles both layers, and the remediation clause matters because a systemic diagnosis is not a reason to leave individual failures unfixed — real users cannot use four services now. Option A treats the instances and guarantees recurrence. Option C is least effective and is the weakest available response to any compliance failure. Option D imposes friction on every compliant service to catch a localised failure, which is how burden accumulates — and you are the person who will be asked in two years why design governance is so slow.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Competing directorates**

Two directors want your design capacity in the same quarter. One has a failing live service; the other a ministerially visible new programme. Both have raised it with your director. What is most effective?

- A) Split capacity.
- B) Set out for each what design work would deliver, what decision it informs, and the cost of deferral; recommend a sequence with reasoning; and put that recommendation to both directors and your director together rather than negotiating separately.
- C) Prioritise the ministerial programme.
- D) Ask your director to decide.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A prioritisation problem is being conducted as a political one, and the way through is to make the trade-off explicit and simultaneously visible. Option B does the analytical work and handles the politics correctly: putting the recommendation to both at once prevents the parallel-negotiation dynamic in which each director hears something slightly different and trust collapses when they compare notes — which they will. Making a recommendation rather than presenting neutral options is essential; you are the expert on what design can deliver, and abdicating that wastes the role and the seat. Option A is least effective: splitting constrained capacity between two substantial needs reliably produces two efforts too thin to inform either decision. Option C ranks by visibility. Option D discards your judgement, though your director should be in the room.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Evidence against a public commitment**

Design work across services shows a publicly announced channel closure will substantially disadvantage users without reliable digital access. Colleagues propose framing it as "opportunities for future enhancement". What is most effective?

- A) Accept the framing; the commitment is public.
- B) Report accurately and do the constructive work alongside — quantify who is affected, specify what assisted provision would mitigate it, and present finding and mitigation together so the organisation has a route that is neither reversal nor denial.
- C) Report starkly and let the organisation respond.
- D) Escalate before acting.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** The proposed framing protects the organisation from discomfort and produces the worst available outcome: the disadvantage occurs anyway, nobody owns it, and evidence that could have driven mitigation has been defused. Option B keeps the finding intact and makes it actionable. Quantifying who is affected converts an abstract concern into something a delivery plan can address, and arriving with mitigation means the finding is not a problem dropped on people who cannot reverse a ministerial announcement. Option A is least effective — softening findings to accommodate a commitment is the precise failure your standards exist to prevent, and it falls hardest on users least able to absorb it. Option C is accurate and unhelpful; at your level a finding without a route forward gets managed around. Option D is worth doing in parallel and is not a substitute.

**Question 8 (harder) — Inclusion practice that is not improving**

Your role requires measurably improving inclusive design practice across teams. After a year of guidance, templates and talks, outputs still routinely omit disabled users from research and reporting. What is the strongest response?

- A) Produce clearer guidance and repeat the training.
- B) Stop adding guidance and find out what is actually preventing it — most likely that recruitment cannot supply the participants, or that teams believe small samples cannot be disaggregated — then fix the specific blocker, and change what is required so that reporting who was in the sample and what each group experienced becomes a condition of an output being accepted.
- C) Introduce a mandatory inclusion checklist.
- D) Report the failure to the head of profession.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A year of guidance that has not changed practice is evidence that the barrier is not knowledge, and continuing to supply knowledge is the characteristic failure of well-intentioned improvement programmes. Option B does the diagnostic work first, and names the two most likely real blockers: supply, because a team cannot include participants its recruitment route does not reach, and a methodological belief that small samples cannot be disaggregated — which is half true and needs answering rather than overriding. Then it changes the requirement to something feasible: reporting composition and each group's experience is possible with eight participants, while reporting rates by group is not. Option A is least effective, repeating an intervention that has demonstrably not worked. Option C adds a checklist to a system that already has guidance. Option D reports a failure you are accountable for solving.

**Question 9 (harder) — An unowned problem**

Synthesising across services, you conclude the organisation's largest user-facing problem is in transitions between services — users falling between four services that each work adequately alone. No director owns it. What is most effective?

- A) Report it in your annual synthesis.
- B) Build the case with evidence from all four services, quantify the affected population using contact centre and analytics data, identify who would need to own it, and take it to a forum with cross-service authority — accepting that securing an owner for an unowned problem is itself months of work.
- C) Raise it with each of the four service owners separately.
- D) Propose research on the transitions.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Unowned problems are the characteristic failure of service-shaped organisations, and surfacing them is what cross-service synthesis is for. Option B recognises that the hard part is not the insight but the ownership: evidence from all four makes the problem undeniable, quantification makes it comparable to problems that do have owners and budgets, and a forum with cross-service authority is the only route by which anything unowned becomes owned. The closing clause is the realistic one — this is a campaign, not a communication. Option A is least effective: the insight is correct and, filed in an annual synthesis, will change nothing, which is the quiet fate of most organisational insight. Option C produces four conversations in which each owner reasonably concludes it is mostly someone else's problem. Option D proposes more evidence when the evidence is sufficient, which is a comfortable way of deferring a difficult conversation.

**Question 10 (harder) — Assurance failure you signed off**

Nine months ago you assured a design whose supporting research excluded anyone who had contacted the support desk, omitting users who had already struggled. The design is live. What should you do?

- A) Add it to your assurance guidance as a lesson.
- B) Notify the team and decision-makers now, scope precisely which conclusions are compromised and which stand, commission targeted follow-up with the excluded group, and examine why the assurance process did not catch it — then change the process.
- C) Commission follow-up quietly and correct the record if findings differ.
- D) Ask your head of profession how to handle it.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** Four things need doing and option B does all of them. Notification now matters because a live design is running on the findings. Precise scoping matters because conclusions about users who never needed help may remain sound, and overstating the damage is as unhelpful as understating it. Targeted follow-up addresses the gap. And examining the assurance process is the element specific to your level: you signed this off, so the consequential question is not only what the study missed but why your check did not surface it and what to add. Option C is least effective — concealing a known flaw while a live design proceeds is indefensible, and the concealment rather than the original error is what would be remembered. Option A converts a live problem into a training note. Option D is worth doing alongside; routing your own error through someone else reads worse than owning it.

**Question 11 (harder) — Working across organisational boundaries**

Three departments are independently designing the same change-of-circumstances journey, each solving it differently, and users move between all three. You have no authority over the other two. What is strongest?

- A) Focus on your own department's design.
- B) Convene the three departments' design leads, establish whether the user journey is genuinely common, and if so propose a shared pattern or a joint design sprint — accepting that you are building consensus rather than exercising authority, and that a well-evidenced shared user journey is more persuasive than three separate assertions.
- C) Raise it with the cross-government profession board and ask them to mandate a common approach.
- D) Share your design with the other two for information.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Three departments solving one user journey three different ways, while users move between all three, is precisely the waste that cross-boundary working exists to prevent, and your role names helping teams connect and work across organisational boundaries. Option B is calibrated to the authority you actually have: convening is available to you, mandating is not, and establishing whether the journey is genuinely common before proposing a shared solution avoids the frequent failure in which a shared pattern is agreed and then fits nobody. Option A is least effective, accepting a solvable structural waste and leaving users to absorb three incompatible designs. Option C reaches for a mandate before building the case, and boards asked to mandate without evidence decline — spending the ask. Option D shares information without creating anything.

**Question 12 (harder) — A standard that will be resisted**

You intend to require that all design outputs report findings disaggregated by access need. You anticipate resistance: teams will say samples are too small. What is strongest?

- A) Introduce the requirement and handle objections as they arise.
- B) Engage the objection before introducing it, because it is partly correct: small samples cannot produce group rates. Frame the requirement as reporting who was in the sample and what each group experienced, rather than producing group statistics — and pair it with the recruitment support that makes including those participants feasible.
- C) Introduce it as guidance rather than a requirement.
- D) Require it only above a certain sample size.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** The anticipated objection is not obstruction; it is a real methodological point, and a standard that ignores it will be both resisted and technically wrong. Option B resolves the tension by changing what is required: reporting composition and describing each group's experience is entirely feasible with eight participants, whereas reporting completion rates by group is not. That reframe makes the requirement workable while preserving what matters — that an output should never conceal whether disabled users were present or how they fared. The paired recruitment support is the other half, because requiring inclusion without enabling it produces either non-compliance or tokenism, and you will get whichever is easier to fake. Option A is least effective: introducing a requirement you know to be contestable and arguing it case by case spends enormous credibility for a worse outcome. Option C removes the force. Option D exempts exactly the small studies where the omission is most common.

### Preparation tips

- **Ask what an option does to the system.** An intervention depending on your personal attention has not solved anything.
- **Absorb pressure downwards.** If a power differential is above the level of the person facing it, it is yours.
- **Read the distribution of a problem.** Concentrated failures diagnose a process; scattered failures diagnose conduct.
- **Stop adding knowledge when knowledge is not the barrier.** A year of guidance that changed nothing is a diagnosis.
- **Engage the strongest objection before you launch.** A standard that has answered its critics survives without you.
- **Make trade-offs visible to all parties simultaneously.** Parallel private negotiation collapses when parties compare notes.
- **Convene where you cannot mandate.** Most cross-boundary progress is consensus built on evidence.
- **Pay for protected time in delivery's currency.** Time that has not been paid for gets cancelled by the third sprint.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Reissuing a standard nobody follows.** The purest form of appearing to act.
- **Repeating an intervention that has demonstrably not worked.** More guidance is rarely the answer to a supply problem.
- **Adding friction for the compliant majority.** How burden accumulates and workarounds are born.
- **Solving personally rather than changing the mechanism.** It does not survive your calendar.
- **Leaving a colleague to hold a line you should be holding.** A leadership failure even when the position survives.
- **Filing a strategic insight rather than finding it an owner.** Unowned problems stay unowned unless someone campaigns.
- **Reaching for a mandate before building the case.** Boards asked without evidence decline, and you have spent the ask.
- **Replacing people without changing the conditions.** The same attrition returns on a lag.

## Conclusion

You have reached the end, and it is worth registering the scale: forty-eight questions across four assessment types, with the reasoning worked through on each. That is a substantial piece of professional preparation and more than most people at your level undertake.

Look back at the ground. In the cognitive section you read outputs for the work behind them, scoped a flaw precisely enough to say which findings survived, synthesised three unrelated items into a cross-cutting identity problem, diagnosed six teams' recruitment failure as one supply problem, read a concentration of accessibility failures as a process diagnosis, and treated two unusually strong teams as a source of transferable practice rather than a story about talent. In the numeric section you weighted rather than averaged, interrogated a vendor's denominator, reconstructed an implied unit value, recognised a session metric masquerading as a journey measure, and declined to endorse a comparison that could not detect what it was built to find. In the verbal section you carried "all" across five lines, checked what started a deadline clock, noticed a conditional prohibition being read as absolute, separated fewer reopenings from better decisions, and caught yourself responding to structural lean. And in the situational judgement section you absorbed a director's pressure on a designer's behalf, treated designer attrition as a structural problem, stopped adding guidance when guidance was not the barrier, convened three departments you could not compel, and campaigned for an owner for an unowned problem.

Two threads run through it. The first is whether you know the limits of the evidence and will say so — in rooms where saying so is unwelcome, about findings that support conclusions you would prefer, and about your own work. The second is whether you act on mechanisms rather than instances. Between them those are most of what your role consists of. Teams can generate opinions without you and read dashboards without you. What you supply is a design rationale that holds when someone pulls at it, and a practice around you that keeps producing them when you are not looking.

A word about the particular difficulty of your level. The errors that cost you most are no longer the ones you would notice. They are made fluently, in areas where your experience runs deep enough that checking feels unnecessary, and nobody in your organisation is positioned to catch them. The verbal reasoning section is the clearest demonstration, and the pattern generalises to every judgement you make alone. The leads whose judgement holds up across a career kept a habit of deliberate scepticism about their own reasoning long after it stopped feeling necessary — and built structures that would catch them.

If you want to build on this, a few things repay effort more than rereading. Audit a design you personally assured and ask who could not have appeared in its evidence. Trace three figures in current board papers back to source. Go and find out what your strongest teams actually do differently rather than assuming. Ask a lead in another department to critique your organisational standard as harshly as they would a stranger's. And the next time you are about to reissue something, ask instead why the first issue did not take.

Finally, for the day itself. An assessment samples your reasoning on one morning under artificial conditions. It is not a verdict on your judgement or your career. Read the stem twice, set expertise aside in the verbal section, find the denominator, trust Cannot Say, and remember that at your level the strongest answer is almost always the one honest about uncertainty and aimed at the mechanism rather than the symptom.

Good luck. The profession is stronger for leaders who prepare like this, and so are the people who depend on these services.
