# Senior Business Relationship Manager - Psychometric Assessment Resources

## Introduction

Welcome! This guide has been created especially for you as a senior business relationship manager working within the UK Government Digital and Data profession. Whether you are preparing for an internal assessment, applying for promotion, benchmarking your own capability against the role you hold, or simply curious about how psychometric assessments relate to the work you lead, this document will help you practise, reflect, and build confidence.

Psychometric assessments are structured, standardised exercises that measure the mental capabilities and behavioural tendencies that predict success in a role. At your level, the assessments worth taking seriously are job-specific: they draw on the artefacts you genuinely work with — investment pipelines, shared-needs analyses, relationship maturity assessments, spending review submissions, user research findings, service strategies, and the conflicting briefings of stakeholders who each believe their version of events — and on the judgements you genuinely make, such as deciding when a set of similar needs across departments justifies a single shared service, how to evaluate whether a current strategy still meets the business's requirements, and how to keep complex needs understood, prioritised and communicated when the organisation's attention is scarce.

Why do these assessments matter for your role in particular? A senior business relationship manager develops relationships across the organisation to understand business ambitions and ensure digital products and services support them. You lead activities that improve relationship maturity, increase value, improve user experience and increase efficiency; you explore shared needs and encourage re-use, central management and commoditisation aligned to digital, data, technology and security strategies; you ensure strategic demands such as spending reviews are supported; and you partner with others to provide strategic guidance. Numerical reasoning is what lets you interrogate a benefits model, a demand forecast, or a spending review option before you put your name behind it. Verbal reasoning is what lets you read a strategy commitment, a supplier's carefully drafted assurance, or a minister-driven deadline for exactly what it says. Situational judgement is the daily texture of the role: choosing how to act when stakeholders conflict, evidence is incomplete, and your influence — not your authority — is the tool available.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive ability assessment measures the mental processes your role demands, using the materials of the job rather than abstract puzzles. At senior level, the emphasis shifts from simple accuracy toward reasoning under complexity: reconciling sources that disagree, deducing consequences from layered rules, spotting the error that matters among many candidate errors, and prioritising when every item carries a stakeholder's expectations.

Typical format: an online, timed test of 15 to 30 minutes covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective against a norm group — often a managerial or senior-professional norm group, which is worth knowing: your performance is compared with peers, not with the general population. Many platforms adapt difficulty to your answers, and most report speed and accuracy separately. Short, ungraded warm-up questions usually precede the scored test.

For a senior business relationship manager, cognitive assessment is relevant because the role is, at its core, structured sense-making on the organisation's behalf. You lead investigative work into problems and opportunities; you absorb large amounts of conflicting information and use it to produce solutions; you explore shared needs across units whose vocabularies, incentives and data rarely line up; and you evaluate whether current strategies still meet business requirements. Each of those duties is a cognitive task performed in public, with your conclusions carried into governance and spending decisions. A well-designed assessment reproduces those demands in miniature — and employers use it at this level because the cost of muddled reasoning rises with seniority: a mid-level error costs a correction, a senior-level error costs a funded programme pointed at the wrong problem.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The assessment dimensions map directly to the named skills in your role summary:

- **Pattern recognition** maps to your duty to **explore shared needs, encouraging re-use, central management, sharing and commoditisation**: seeing that four units' differently worded requests are one underlying need — or that they genuinely are not — is pattern recognition performed on organisational demand.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Strategic thinking**: defining strategies and policies, and evaluating whether current strategies meet business requirements, requires valid reasoning from commitments and constraints to their consequences — especially when the consequences are unwelcome.
- **Error checking** maps to **Business analysis (IT operations)** at your level: leading the collection of information and creation of recommendations means the inconsistencies between sources are yours to find before a board finds them for you.
- **Reconciling conflicting information** maps to the defining line of your business analysis skill — **absorb large amounts of conflicting information and use it to produce solutions** — and to your duty to ensure complex needs and challenges are understood, prioritised and communicated.
- **Prioritisation** maps to **Ownership and topic**: taking accountability for issues and proactively searching for potential problems only works if you can rank a crowded field by impact, urgency and dependency rather than by noise.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Service focus** and **Service management framework knowledge**: taking inputs and establishing coherent frameworks that work — and knowing the ITIL life cycle well enough to recognise where a problem actually sits within it.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern recognition across demand submissions**

Four business units submit needs to your shared-demand review. Unit A asks for "a tool to track supplier commitments and renewal dates". Unit B asks for "a contract register with alerting before expiry". Unit C asks for "a dashboard of our software licences and when they lapse". Unit D asks for "a case management system for complaints handling". As the senior lead exploring shared needs, which grouping is most defensible?

- A) All four are one shared need for a "tracking tool".
- B) A, B and C share an underlying need — visibility and alerting on time-bound commercial agreements — while D is a distinct need.
- C) Each need is distinct and should be assessed separately.
- D) B and D share a need because both involve deadlines.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Strip each request to its underlying structure. A tracks supplier commitments and renewal dates; B tracks contracts with expiry alerts; C tracks licences and lapse dates. All three reduce to the same pattern: a register of time-bound commercial agreements with forward alerting — different words, one need, and a strong candidate for a single shared solution, which is exactly the re-use and commoditisation your role level exists to encourage. D, despite involving dates somewhere, is structurally different: complaints case management is a workflow with states, parties and resolution paths, not a register with alerts. Option A over-abstracts — at the level of "a tracking tool", everything from contracts to complaints to car parking merges, and a solution scoped that vaguely serves nobody. Option C under-abstracts, forfeiting the efficiency of a shared service and committing the organisation to three overlapping procurements. Option D groups on a surface feature ("deadlines") rather than the underlying structure. The senior habit being tested: abstract to the level where genuine commonality appears, and stop there.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction from strategy commitments**

Your organisation's technology strategy states: (i) any new business application must be cloud-hosted unless a documented exception is approved; (ii) applications handling protected data must complete a security assessment before go-live; (iii) approved exceptions are published in the exceptions register. A unit tells you their new on-premises application went live last month handling protected data, and it does not appear in the exceptions register. Which conclusion must be true?

- A) The application has not completed a security assessment.
- B) The cloud-hosting rule has been breached, because no exception is registered.
- C) Either an approved exception exists that has not yet been published, or the cloud-hosting rule has been breached.
- D) The application must be migrated to the cloud.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Take the rules precisely. Rule (i) permits on-premises hosting only with an approved exception. Rule (iii) says approved exceptions are published in the register — but the application's absence from the register is compatible with two states of the world: no exception exists (a breach of rule (i)), or an exception was approved and the register has not yet caught up (a breach of the publishing practice, but not of the hosting rule). Because the facts cannot distinguish these, only the disjunction in C must be true. Option B treats the register as infallibly current — an assumption, not a fact; registers lag. Option A concerns rule (ii), about which the facts say nothing either way. Option D states a remedy no rule requires — exceptions exist precisely so that some applications legitimately stay on-premises. At senior level this discipline has a name in practice: before you tell a delivery board that a unit has breached strategy, you check whether the paperwork or the behaviour is what actually failed — because the two conversations, and the two remedies, are entirely different.

**Question 3 (easy) — Error checking a spending review table**

A draft spending review submission includes this table of a programme's costs:

| Year | Capital (£k) | Resource (£k) | Total (£k) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Year 1 | 800 | 400 | 1,200 |
| Year 2 | 500 | 600 | 1,100 |
| Year 3 | 200 | 700 | 900 |
| Programme total | 1,500 | 1,700 | 3,300 |

Which figure is wrong?

- A) Year 2 total
- B) The capital programme total
- C) The resource programme total
- D) The overall programme total

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Cross-foot the table — check rows, columns, and the corner, because all three must agree. Rows: 800 + 400 = 1,200 ✓; 500 + 600 = 1,100 ✓; 200 + 700 = 900 ✓. Columns: capital 800 + 500 + 200 = 1,500 ✓; resource 400 + 600 + 700 = 1,700 ✓. Now the corner, two independent ways: summing the year totals gives 1,200 + 1,100 + 900 = 3,200; summing the column totals gives 1,500 + 1,700 = 3,200. Both routes agree on 3,200 — but the table prints 3,300. The overall programme total is the wrong figure, overstated by £100k, so D is correct and options A–C all name verified figures. The likely real-world cause is an edit that reduced a year's costs without the grand total being refreshed — a fossil of an earlier draft. The senior lesson sits in the method: cross-foot every summary table before it leaves your hands, and always compute the corner by two routes, because agreement of two independent paths is what turns "looks right" into "is right". A spending review team will find a £100k discrepancy in minutes, and a submission that fails arithmetic loses the benefit of the doubt on everything else it claims.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Prioritisation across a senior week**

Monday morning. Five items compete for your attention:

1. The spending review team needs your directorate's efficiency narrative by Thursday; your draft exists but two unit heads have not yet confirmed their figures.
2. A relationship maturity workshop you lead with a struggling unit is scheduled for Wednesday; materials are ready, but the unit head hinted Friday that "some of my team think this is a box-ticking exercise".
3. A supplier's monthly report, received this morning, shows a service supporting casework slipped below its availability target for the second consecutive month.
4. A user researcher has shared findings suggesting a flagship internal product is being widely worked around; she asks when you can discuss them.
5. Your team's junior BRM asks for an hour to prepare for her first solo engagement with a director on Friday.

Which sequencing is most defensible for Monday itself?

- A) Chase the two unit heads (1), then engage the supplier issue (3), book the researcher discussion (4), coach the junior (5), and address the workshop scepticism (2) on Tuesday.
- B) Deal with the supplier issue (3) first and fully, then the spending review (1), leaving the rest to later in the week.
- C) Start with the workshop scepticism (2) since Wednesday is closest, then the spending review (1), then the rest.
- D) Coach the junior (5) first as people come first, then handle items in deadline order.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Senior prioritisation is dependency management plus impact, not deadline sorting. Item 1's constraint is other people's time: unit heads need days, not hours, to confirm figures, so the chase must launch Monday even though Thursday feels distant — starting dependency-laden work early is the recurring senior pattern. Item 3 is a second consecutive breach on a casework-supporting service: proactive ownership (your **Ownership and topic** skill: search for potential problems before they mature) says engage it Monday — establish cause, impact and the supplier's recovery plan — before it becomes month three and a formal escalation. Item 4 needs only a booking today; the discussion itself can be mid-week, but a researcher signalling a widely worked-around flagship product deserves a fast, visible response — that is championing user research in practice. Item 5 needs an hour before Friday; committing the slot Monday respects the junior's preparation time without displacing urgent work. Item 2's scepticism is real but is best addressed Tuesday with a call to the unit head — reworking Wednesday's framing after a considered conversation, not a rushed Monday reaction. Option B lets one issue consume the day while the spending review dependency clock runs; option C treats calendar proximity as priority; option D honours a value ("people first") by ignoring the structure of the week. A's logic — launch dependencies, engage emerging risks, book what needs booking, protect development time — is the defensible pattern.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Reconciling conflicting information**

You are investigating why adoption of a shared document platform has stalled. Three sources conflict. The platform team's telemetry shows 3,100 weekly active users, up 5% this quarter. The intranet survey (420 responses, self-selected) shows 61% of respondents "rarely or never" use the platform. Two unit heads tell you separately that "everyone has gone back to email attachments". Which reading best reconciles the evidence?

- A) The telemetry is correct and the survey and unit heads are wrong; adoption is growing.
- B) The survey is correct and the telemetry must be miscounting.
- C) The sources may all be accurate about different things: telemetry counts any weekly activity, however shallow; the self-selected survey over-represents the dissatisfied; and the unit heads report their own units. The next step is to segment telemetry by unit and depth of use.
- D) With sources in conflict, no conclusion is possible until a new, independent study is commissioned.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** This is the defining senior business analysis move: absorb conflicting information and produce a solution, rather than crowning one source and dismissing the rest. Each source has a scope and a bias, and C names them. Telemetry measures behaviour but bluntly — "weekly active" may mean one file opened, so 3,100 actives can coexist with widespread shallow use and attachment workarounds. A self-selected survey measures sentiment among those motivated to respond — often the frustrated — so 61% "rarely or never" overstates the population rate but genuinely signals a sizeable disaffected group. The unit heads offer vivid, local, secondhand evidence: possibly true of their units, unsafe to generalise. These are not contradictions but different instruments pointed at different parts of the same phenomenon, and C's proposed segmentation — by unit and by depth of use — is precisely the analysis that would either reconcile them (growth concentrated in some units, shallow elsewhere) or expose a real measurement fault. Option A and option B each commit the same error in opposite directions: source-picking. Option D is the senior-level failure of nerve — commissioning new research to avoid analysing the evidence already held, at the cost of months. Assessments at your level increasingly test this pattern: the right answer usually honours all the evidence by finding the frame in which it fits together.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Deduction through the ITIL life cycle**

Your service management framework distinguishes: an *incident* (unplanned interruption or degradation), a *problem* (underlying cause of one or more incidents), and a *change* (addition, modification or removal of anything that could affect services). Rules: (i) recurring incidents with a common suspected cause must be linked to a problem record; (ii) a workaround documented on a problem record may be applied to incidents without a change request; (iii) a permanent fix that modifies a production service requires an approved change. The document platform has failed the same way five times this month; a script that restarts the failed component restores service in minutes; the vendor has issued a patch said to remove the fault entirely. Which statement is correct?

- A) The restart script and the vendor patch both require approved change requests.
- B) Five similar incidents should be linked to a problem record; the restart script can be documented as a workaround and applied without a change request; applying the vendor patch requires an approved change.
- C) Once the patch is available, the incidents can be closed without a problem record.
- D) The restart script is a permanent fix and should be recorded as a change.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Map each element to the framework's categories, because the categories carry the rules. Five same-signature failures are recurring incidents with a common suspected cause: rule (i) requires a linked problem record — this is what problem management is *for*, and it is where the investigation and the vendor engagement are coordinated. The restart script restores service without modifying it: that is the definition of a workaround, and rule (ii) explicitly permits applying documented workarounds without a change request — so option A over-controls, and option D miscategorises a recovery action as a modification. The vendor patch modifies the production service permanently: rule (iii) applies, and it needs an approved change — which is also where testing and rollback planning get their governance moment. Option C inverts the logic: the patch's availability makes the problem record *more* important, not less, since the problem record is the vehicle that tracks the permanent fix through change and confirms the incidents stop recurring. Your **Service management framework knowledge** skill at this level — demonstrating the life cycle elements of ITIL — is exactly this ability to route real events through the right process, and the payoff is practical: workarounds keep users working today, problems get root causes, and changes get scrutiny proportionate to risk.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Error checking a benefits logic chain**

A unit's investment case, which you are quality-assuring before it reaches the board, argues: "Automating invoice matching will remove 4,000 hours of manual checking annually (Enabler). This releases two full-time posts (Intermediate benefit). Headcount reduction of two posts saves £90,000 per year (End benefit), which funds the system's £70,000 annual cost with £20,000 net saving (Financial case)." The unit's own workforce plan, attached as an annex, states that the two affected staff will be redeployed to clear the invoice dispute backlog. Which is the critical inconsistency?

- A) 4,000 hours does not equal two full-time posts.
- B) The case claims a cashable £90,000 headcount saving while the annex commits to redeploying both posts, so the saving is capacity released, not cash — and the financial case as stated collapses.
- C) £70,000 annual cost is too high for an invoice matching tool.
- D) Invoice disputes should be automated before invoice matching.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Test each link, then test the links against the annexes — the senior QA habit that catches what section-by-section reading misses. Option A fails as a criticism: 4,000 hours ÷ roughly 1,900–2,000 working hours per post ≈ 2.0–2.1 posts, so that conversion is defensible. Options C and D are opinions imported from outside the document — possibly worth exploring, but not inconsistencies *within* the case. The genuine defect is B: the financial case's £20,000 net saving depends on £90,000 being *cashable* — money that leaves the paybill — while the workforce plan annex commits both posts to redeployment on the dispute backlog. Redeployed posts are a real benefit (capacity released to valuable work) but not a cashable one: the paybill is unchanged, so the honest financial position is £70,000 of new annual cost against £0 cash release, justified — if at all — by the value of clearing the backlog. The case must choose its story: cash the posts or redeploy them; claiming both is the kind of double-count that spending review scrutiny exists to catch. Leading the creation of recommendations, at your level, includes protecting units from their own optimistic drafting — finding this before the board does converts an embarrassment into a better case.

**Question 8 (challenging) — Multi-constraint engagement scheduling**

You must run four sessions in one working week (Monday–Friday, at most one session per day): a spending review options briefing (S) for the executive committee, a shared-needs workshop (W) with four unit heads, a supplier escalation meeting (E), and a user research playback (P). Constraints: (i) the executive committee meets only on Tuesday, and S must be presented there; (ii) the spending review options pack — pre-reading for W — is finalised the day after S, and the unit heads need one full working day with the pack before W takes place; (iii) the supplier's account director is available Monday and Thursday only; (iv) the research playback must be held on the day immediately before W, because its findings feed directly into the workshop. On which days must W and E fall?

- A) W Thursday, E Monday
- B) W Friday, E Monday
- C) W Thursday, E Thursday — impossible, so the week cannot work
- D) W Friday, E Thursday

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Anchor the fixed item first: constraint (i) pins S to Tuesday. Propagate constraint (ii): the pack is finalised Wednesday (the day after S), and the unit heads need one full working day with it — Thursday — before the workshop, so W cannot happen before Friday. W is therefore Friday, which immediately eliminates options A and C. Now place P: constraint (iv) puts it on the day immediately before W, which is Thursday. Finally place E: constraint (iii) allows Monday or Thursday, but Thursday now holds P and the rule is one session per day, so E must be Monday. The unique schedule is E Monday, S Tuesday, P Thursday, W Friday — with Wednesday absorbed by pack finalisation — which is option B. Option D fails because it double-books Thursday against the playback that W's own dependency chain forces there. Note the method, because it generalises: anchor the immovable constraint, propagate each dependency forward, and let eliminations — not intuition — choose among the survivors. Note also the trap: "at least one full day with the pack" is a buffer constraint, and buffer constraints are the ones candidates silently drop under time pressure, both in tests and in real diary planning, where a workshop held before stakeholders could read the pack is a workshop you get to run twice.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Evaluating a strategy against the evidence**

Two years ago your directorate's engagement strategy set three commitments: (a) every business unit has a named senior relationship contact; (b) relationship maturity, self-assessed annually on a 1–5 scale, improves in at least 80% of units year on year; (c) digital investment decisions cite business-unit input in 100% of cases. Current evidence: (a) is met — all 14 units have named contacts; (b) shows 9 of 14 units improved (64%), 3 static, 2 declined — both decliners citing "constant contact churn"; (c) audit shows 11 of 12 decisions cited unit input, and the twelfth was an emergency security patch decision taken in hours. As the senior evaluating whether the strategy meets business requirements, what is the most defensible assessment?

- A) The strategy has failed: two of three commitments are unmet.
- B) The strategy is broadly succeeding: (a) is met, (c)'s single miss is a defensible emergency, and (b)'s 64% against an 80% target is the real shortfall — with contact churn implicated, so the refresh should target continuity of relationships, and the emergency pathway in (c) should be formalised rather than counted as failure.
- C) The strategy has succeeded: 64% improvement still means most units improved.
- D) The self-assessed maturity data is too subjective to evaluate, so commitment (b) should be dropped.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Evaluating strategies — your **Strategic thinking** skill at this level — means neither prosecuting the strategy (option A) nor defending it (option C), but reading each commitment against its evidence and extracting the decision each reading implies. Commitment (a): met, cleanly. Commitment (c): 11 of 12 is literally a miss of a 100% target, but the miss is an hours-scale emergency security decision — the mature response is not to record failure but to notice the strategy never designed an emergency pathway, and to formalise one (perhaps "input within 48 hours post-decision for emergencies"), converting a technical breach into a better policy. Commitment (b) is the substantive shortfall — 64% versus 80% — and the evidence offers a mechanism: both declining units cite contact churn, which points the strategy refresh at relationship *continuity* (handover standards, overlap periods, succession planning for contacts) rather than at more engagement volume. Option A applies a pass/fail frame that discards exactly the diagnostic information a refresh needs. Option C waves the target away — targets exist so that "most improved" is not confused with "enough improved". Option D mistakes imperfect data for useless data: self-assessment is noisy, but two units independently citing the same cause is signal, and dropping the measure would abandon the strategy's only maturity feedback loop. The senior pattern: evaluation's output is not a verdict but a targeted set of adjustments, each traceable to evidence.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Root cause through conflicting process accounts**

Complaints about slow onboarding of new starters have doubled. You lead the investigation. HR says: "We process every request within our 3-day SLA — the delays are IT's." IT says: "We complete account creation within 2 days of a valid request — half the requests arrive incomplete and bounce back." Line managers say: "We submit the form weeks early, then hear nothing until the starter arrives without a laptop." You pull twenty recent cases: median elapsed time from form submission to working equipment is 19 days; the form passes through an HR validation queue (median 3 days), bounces back to managers for corrections in 9 of 20 cases (median 5 days lost), then IT (median 2 days), then equipment dispatch (median 6 days, driven by a stock shortage). Which conclusion do the data best support?

- A) HR is the bottleneck and their SLA should be tightened.
- B) Line managers are at fault for submitting incomplete forms.
- C) Every party's account is locally true; the systemic causes are a form that fails validation 45% of the time and an equipment stock shortage — the rework loop and dispatch delay, invisible in each team's own SLA, consume most of the elapsed time.
- D) The 19-day median is acceptable because each team meets its own SLA.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Add up the accounts and notice they are all true and all incomplete — the signature of a process problem hiding between teams rather than inside one. HR genuinely meets 3 days *within its queue*; IT genuinely meets 2 days *from a valid request*; managers genuinely submit early. But end-to-end, the case data shows where 19 days actually go: validation (3) + a 45% rework loop costing a median 5 days when triggered + IT (2) + dispatch (6, stock-constrained) — with the rework loop and dispatch, which appear in *nobody's* SLA, contributing the bulk of the delay and all of the variance. Option A tightens a queue that is not the constraint. Option B moralises a design defect: when 45% of users fail a form, the form — unclear fields, missing guidance, no inline validation — is the cause worth fixing; blaming users for a bad interface is the opposite of your **User focus** skill. Option D is the most instructive wrong answer: it is precisely how organisations institutionalise poor service — local SLAs all green, users waiting three weeks — and your **Service focus** skill (take inputs and establish coherent frameworks that work) exists to reject it, insisting the framework measure the journey users actually experience. The recommendations write themselves from C's diagnosis: redesign the form with validation at the point of entry, measure the end-to-end journey with a single owner, and fix or buffer the equipment stock position. That movement — from three conflicting accounts to one instrumented journey — is leading investigative work, exactly as your role defines it.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Second-order consequences of a well-meant rule**

To improve engagement consistency, a neighbouring directorate introduced a rule: all business-unit requests to digital teams must be logged through the front-door portal, and un-logged work is not resourced. Six months on: logged demand is up 60%; the backlog has tripled; unit heads praise the transparency; delivery teams report that "urgent small fixes now take six weeks"; and two units have hired their own contractors for quick changes, outside governance. Your directorate is considering adopting the same rule. What is the most defensible advice?

- A) Adopt the rule unchanged: transparency has improved and demand is now visible, which is the point.
- B) Reject the rule: it triples backlogs and drives shadow IT.
- C) Adopt the front-door principle, but add what the neighbouring design lacks: a triaged fast lane for small changes with a service-level commitment, capacity planning that responds to the newly visible demand, and monitoring for shadow-IT symptoms — because the observed harms flow from an unsegmented queue, not from visibility itself.
- D) Pilot the rule in one unit and decide in a year.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Separate the mechanism from the side effects — the analytical move this question exists to test. The rule did two distinct things: it made demand visible (logged demand up 60% — much of that work previously happened informally and invisibly), and it forced all demand, from strategic programmes to ten-minute fixes, through a single undifferentiated queue. The benefits (transparency, praise from unit heads) flow from the first; the harms (tripled backlog, six-week small fixes, contractor shadow IT) flow from the second — and the shadow IT is the system routing around a blockage, a warning sign your role is specifically positioned to read, since ungoverned contractors re-create the security, duplication and data risks that governance exists to prevent. Option A attends only to the mechanism's benefits; option B only to its side effects — both treat a two-part design as one indivisible thing. Option D sounds prudent but buys a year's delay to learn what the neighbouring directorate's six months already teach; piloting is for genuine unknowns, not for avoidable analysis. C is the senior answer because it *redesigns* rather than adopts-or-rejects: keep visibility, segment the queue (fast lane with a commitment for small changes — which removes the incentive to go around), and treat the demand data as input to capacity planning rather than as a backlog to admire. Establishing coherent frameworks that work, in your skill set, means exactly this: frameworks that account for how people will behave when the framework inconveniences them.

### Preparation tips

- **Practise on genuinely conflicting sources.** Take a live question from your work — adoption, satisfaction, demand — and deliberately gather three sources that disagree. Write the one-paragraph reconciliation. This is the highest-value cognitive rehearsal for your level, and it produces useful work as a by-product.
- **Cross-foot every table you meet.** Rows, columns, corner. Make it a reflex on your own submissions and you will make it a reflex under test conditions.
- **Rehearse the abstraction ladder.** When grouping needs or patterns, consciously name the level of abstraction you are using and test one level up (does the grouping dissolve into vagueness?) and one level down (does genuine commonality split apart?).
- **Anchor, then propagate.** In multi-constraint problems, place the fixed element first and let dependencies radiate from it. When two solutions both look legal, the discriminating phrase is usually hiding in a constraint you paraphrased instead of re-reading.
- **Time-box, and bank the easy marks.** Senior norm groups are quick. Answer cleanly gettable questions first; return to the puzzle-like ones. One stubborn question is never worth two straightforward ones.
- **Warm up your working memory.** Before the test: quiet room, notes ready, two or three practice questions to engage the checking mindset. Cold starts cost the first three questions.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Source-picking.** Under pressure, the tempting move with conflicting evidence is to crown the most authoritative-looking source and dismiss the rest. The scored answer — and the professional one — usually reconciles scopes and biases instead.
- **Verdict-first evaluation.** Reading evidence with a pass/fail frame ("has the strategy worked?") discards the diagnostic texture that targeted adjustment needs. Evaluate commitment by commitment.
- **Stopping at the first defensible error.** Senior error-checking questions often contain a plausible near-error (the hours-to-posts conversion) shielding the real one (the cashability double-count). Verify your candidate error is *the* inconsistency, not merely *a* question mark.
- **Local-optimum reasoning.** Any answer that fixes one team's metric while the end-to-end journey stays broken — or adopts a rule's benefits while ignoring how people will route around its costs — is a trap at this level. Ask what the whole system does next.
- **Over-abstraction and under-abstraction.** "Everything is one need" and "everything is unique" are twin failures. The tested skill is finding the level where commonality is real.
- **Letting speed abolish the second read.** The discriminating detail in hard questions is nearly always in a clause you skimmed. Budget ten seconds to re-read the constraint before committing — cheaper than the mark.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures your ability to interpret numerical information accurately, select and execute the right calculations, and — increasingly at senior level — judge what a figure legitimately supports once computed. Calculators are usually permitted; the test is of reasoning, not arithmetic recall.

Typical format: an online, timed test of 20 to 40 minutes built around tables, charts and short data-rich passages, each with two to four multiple-choice questions. Senior-normed versions weight the harder skills: multi-step calculations, weighted figures, compound growth, cost models mixing one-off and recurring elements, and the interpretive judgement questions where every option is a calculation but only one is the *right* calculation for the decision at hand. Speed and accuracy are usually reported separately, against a managerial norm group.

For a senior business relationship manager, numbers are the medium of your most consequential conversations. Spending reviews are numeric: options, phasing, efficiency claims, and the scrutiny of all three. The value cases behind re-use and commoditisation are numeric: the shared platform is justified — or not — by unit costs, adoption projections and avoided duplication. Relationship maturity, user experience and efficiency — the three things your role level explicitly leads improvement in — are all tracked through measures that someone must read intelligently, challenge when flattering, and defend when true. And because you lead investigations and QA the recommendations of others, you are the last line of defence between an optimistic benefits model and a board that will fund it. Employers test numeric reasoning at this level because seniors are rarely asked to *produce* the spreadsheet — they are asked to decide whether to *believe* it, and that is a distinct, testable skill.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Multi-step cost and benefit modelling** maps to your duty to **ensure strategic demands, such as spending reviews, are supported**: options analysis, phasing, and cashable-versus-capacity distinctions are the arithmetic of every submission you shape.
- **Weighted averages and normalised rates** map to **leading activities that improve relationship maturity, increase value, improve user experience and increase efficiency**: improvement claims live or die by whether the measures behind them are weighted and normalised honestly.
- **Compound growth and trend projection** map to **Strategic thinking**: evaluating whether current strategies meet business requirements often turns on whether a demand or cost trend, projected honestly, breaks the strategy's assumptions.
- **Unit economics and comparison of options** map to your duty to **explore shared needs, encouraging re-use, central management, sharing and commoditisation**: the case for one shared service over four local ones is a unit-cost comparison, and you must be able to build and challenge it.
- **Interpreting survey and research data** maps to **User focus**: championing user research includes protecting its numbers — sample sizes, response rates, subgroup differences — from both dismissal and over-claim.
- **Reading complex tables under pressure** maps to **Business analysis (IT operations)**: absorbing large amounts of conflicting information includes conflicting *numerical* information, where the reconciliation is usually a denominator.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Percentage of demand by category**

Your directorate logged 240 demand items this year: 96 were small changes, 84 were enhancements, and the rest were new-service requests. What percentage of demand items were new-service requests?

- A) 20%
- B) 25%
- C) 30%
- D) 35%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** First find the new-service count: 240 − 96 − 84 = 60. Then the percentage: 60 ÷ 240 = 0.25 = 25%, so B. Sanity-check by parts: small changes are 96 ÷ 240 = 40%, enhancements 84 ÷ 240 = 35%, and 40% + 35% + 25% = 100% ✓. The habit of checking that category percentages sum to 100 catches both your own slips and other people's — demand reports where the categories sum to 108% (double-counted items) or 92% (a quietly dropped "miscellaneous") are more common than they should be, and as the person who presents demand shape to boards, you want to find those inconsistencies before the audience does.

**Question 2 (easy) — Reading a maturity assessment table**

Relationship maturity self-assessments (1 = ad hoc, 5 = strategic partner) across your portfolio:

| Unit | Last year | This year |
|---|---|---|
| Operations | 2 | 3 |
| Finance | 3 | 3 |
| HR | 2 | 4 |
| Policy | 4 | 3 |
| Commercial | 3 | 4 |

How many units improved, and what is this year's mean score across the five units?

- A) 3 improved; mean 3.4
- B) 2 improved; mean 3.4
- C) 3 improved; mean 3.2
- D) 4 improved; mean 3.6

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Count improvements by comparing rows: Operations 2→3 improved; Finance 3→3 static; HR 2→4 improved; Policy 4→3 declined; Commercial 3→4 improved. Three units improved. This year's mean: (3 + 3 + 4 + 3 + 4) ÷ 5 = 17 ÷ 5 = 3.4. So A. Two reading disciplines matter. First, "improved" means strictly greater — Finance's unchanged 3 is not an improvement, an error option B anticipates in the other direction. Second, the mean alone hides the story a relationship lead most needs: Policy *declined* from your portfolio's best score, and a falling 4 is a different conversation from a static 2 — the mean of 3.4 (up from last year's 14 ÷ 5 = 2.8) would let you report improvement while your strongest relationship quietly erodes. Leading maturity improvement means reading the movement table, not just the average.

**Question 3 (easy) — Ratio in a shared-service case**

Four units each run their own survey tool, at annual licence costs of £9,000, £7,500, £12,000 and £7,500. A single shared tool with capacity for all four would cost £24,000 per year. What is the ratio of current combined spend to the shared alternative, in simplest form?

- A) 2:1
- B) 3:2
- C) 4:3
- D) 5:4

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Combined current spend: £9,000 + £7,500 + £12,000 + £7,500 = £36,000. Ratio of current to shared: 36,000:24,000. Divide both sides by 12,000: 3:2. So B — the organisation currently spends £1.50 for every £1.00 the shared option would cost, an annual saving of £12,000 or 33% of current spend. Keep the ratio's direction straight (current first, as asked), and note what a senior reader adds beyond the arithmetic: the shared case is only as strong as its hidden costs — migration effort, contract exit timing across four different renewal dates, and whether the shared tool genuinely meets all four units' needs. The ratio opens the commoditisation conversation your role level is charged with leading; it does not close it.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Compound demand growth against capacity**

Demand for your directorate's delivery capacity is 1,000 story points per quarter and growing at 8% per quarter, compounding. Capacity is fixed at 1,150 points per quarter. In which quarter, counting from now (quarter 0), does demand first exceed capacity?

- A) Quarter 1
- B) Quarter 2
- C) Quarter 3
- D) Quarter 4

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Compound forward step by step. Quarter 1: 1,000 × 1.08 = 1,080 — under 1,150. Quarter 2: 1,080 × 1.08 = 1,166.4 — over 1,150. So demand first exceeds capacity in quarter 2, option B. The common error is linear thinking: 8% of 1,000 is 80, and 1,150 − 1,000 = 150, so "capacity holds for 150 ÷ 80 ≈ 2 quarters, breach in quarter 3" — but compounding grows the base each quarter (the second quarter adds 86.4, not 80), pulling the breach earlier. At small percentages and short horizons the linear error is subtle — one quarter here — but over a spending review period it becomes structural: 8% quarterly compounding is (1.08)⁴ ≈ 1.36, a 36% annual increase, not 32%. When you evaluate whether current strategies meet business requirements, demand projections are usually where the strategy's assumptions break first, and knowing *which quarter* the line crosses is what turns a vague "demand is growing" into a scheduled, fundable capacity decision.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Weighted user satisfaction across channels**

A service you champion is used through three channels. This quarter's satisfaction ("satisfied or very satisfied") by channel: web 82% of 5,400 users; phone 64% of 1,800 users; face-to-face 90% of 800 users. What is the overall satisfaction rate across all users, to the nearest percentage point?

- A) 79%
- B) 78%
- C) 80%
- D) 76%

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Weight each channel by its user count. Satisfied users: web 0.82 × 5,400 = 4,428; phone 0.64 × 1,800 = 1,152; face-to-face 0.90 × 800 = 720. Total satisfied = 4,428 + 1,152 + 720 = 6,300. Total users = 5,400 + 1,800 + 800 = 8,000. Overall rate = 6,300 ÷ 8,000 = 0.7875 = 78.75%, which rounds to 79% — option A. The unweighted mean of the three channel rates, (82 + 64 + 90) ÷ 3 = 78.67 ≈ 79%, happens to land close here — a coincidence of these numbers, not a licence for the shortcut, as the weighted and unweighted figures diverge sharply whenever channel sizes and scores are correlated. The figure a senior reader extracts is not the average at all: phone — your second-largest channel — sits 15 points below web and 26 below face-to-face, and 1,800 × 0.36 = 648 dissatisfied phone users is where the improvement leverage lives. Meeting user needs across a variety of channels, as your **User focus** skill puts it, starts by refusing to let a healthy overall figure bury an unhealthy channel.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Spending review options arithmetic**

You are shaping three options for a spending review line currently costing £2.4 million per year:

- Option 1: flat cash — hold spend at £2.4m per year for three years.
- Option 2: hold year 1 at £2.4m, then apply a 5% cash reduction in each of years 2 and 3, each cut applied to the previous year's figure.
- Option 3: invest £300k extra in year 1 (total £2.7m), then reduce to £2.2m in years 2 and 3.

Which option has the lowest total cost over the three years, and by how much versus flat cash?

- A) Option 2; about £354k less than flat cash
- B) Option 2; about £360k less than flat cash
- C) Option 3; £100k less than flat cash
- D) Option 3; £200k less than flat cash

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Compute each three-year total. Option 1: £2.4m × 3 = £7.2m. Option 2 compounds the reduction from year 2: year 1 = £2.4m; year 2 = 2.4 × 0.95 = £2.28m; year 3 = 2.28 × 0.95 = £2.166m (note: 5% off the *previous year's* figure, not off the original baseline — the second cut removes £114k, not £120k); total = 2.4 + 2.28 + 2.166 = £6.846m, a saving of 7.2 − 6.846 = £0.354m ≈ £354k. Option 3: 2.7 + 2.2 + 2.2 = £7.1m, saving £100k. Option 2 is lowest, by about £354k — option A. Option B (£360k) is the flat-reduction error: treating both cuts as £120k off the original baseline (2 × 120k + 120k of double-count confusion) rather than compounding; the compounding difference of £6k looks trivial here but scales with the budget and the horizon — on a £240m programme line the same slip is £600k. Two senior lessons ride along. First, always pin "5% per year" to a baseline year and a start year in writing: "5% from year 1" would have produced a materially different total (2.28 + 2.166 + 2.058 = £6.504m, saving £696k), and ambiguity of exactly this kind surfaces expensively at settlement. Second, note what the arithmetic does not decide: Option 3 costs £254k more than Option 2 over the window but buys a £300k year-1 investment — whether that investment yields recurring savings beyond year 3 is the real strategic question, and a good options paper prices the window *and* names what lies beyond it.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Response rates and representativeness**

Your annual stakeholder survey went to 1,600 staff across four units. Responses: Unit A 180 of 400; Unit B 90 of 600; Unit C 150 of 300; Unit D 120 of 300. Overall satisfaction among respondents is 71%. Which statement should most temper how you report that 71%?

- A) The overall response rate is only 540 ÷ 1,600 ≈ 34%, so the survey is worthless.
- B) Unit B — the largest unit — responded at only 15%, versus 45–50% elsewhere, so the overall figure under-represents your biggest stakeholder population and may not survive reweighting.
- C) Unit C's 50% response rate means its views are over-counted, so its responses should be discarded.
- D) 71% is above the 70% target, so no caveat is needed.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Work the rates: Unit A 180 ÷ 400 = 45%; Unit B 90 ÷ 600 = 15%; Unit C 150 ÷ 300 = 50%; Unit D 120 ÷ 300 = 40%; overall 540 ÷ 1,600 = 33.75%. The structural issue is B's: Unit B contains 600 ÷ 1,600 = 37.5% of the population but only 90 ÷ 540 = 16.7% of the responses — your largest unit speaks with less than half its proportional voice in the headline figure. If Unit B's non-respondents lean less satisfied (and low response often correlates with disengagement), the true organisation-wide figure sits below 71%, and a reweighted estimate should be computed and reported alongside the raw one. Option A commits the dismissal error — a 34% response rate limits precision but hardly renders 540 responses "worthless"; option C proposes discarding your best data rather than weighting appropriately; option D reads a number against a target while ignoring whether the number means what it claims. Championing user and stakeholder research, at your level, includes this exact stewardship: present the figure, present its composition, and pre-empt the board member who will ask "and who didn't answer?"

**Question 8 (challenging) — Unit economics of consolidation**

Six units each run local reporting tools. Annual costs: four small units pay £15,000 each; two large units pay £40,000 each. A central platform would cost £120,000 per year flat, plus £5,000 per unit onboarding (one-off), and would need a £60,000 one-off migration project. Over three years, what is the net saving (or cost) of consolidation versus the status quo?

- A) Net saving of £90,000
- B) Net saving of £150,000
- C) Net cost of £30,000
- D) Net saving of £60,000

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Build each side of the comparison carefully. Status quo annual spend = (4 × £15,000) + (2 × £40,000) = £60,000 + £80,000 = £140,000; over three years = £420,000. Consolidated: platform £120,000 × 3 = £360,000; onboarding 6 × £5,000 = £30,000 one-off; migration project £60,000 one-off; total = £360,000 + £30,000 + £60,000 = £450,000. Difference: £450,000 − £420,000 = £30,000 *more* for consolidation over the three-year window — a net cost, option C. Option B is the error of forgetting both one-off items (£360,000 versus £420,000 looks like £60,000 saved per… and then compounds into £150,000 by also miscounting a year); option A typically comes from dropping the migration project; option D from dropping onboarding. Now the senior reading, which is why this question matters more than its subtraction: the *steady-state* economics favour consolidation — £140,000 falls to £120,000, saving £20,000 every year once the one-offs are paid — and the total one-off investment of £90,000 therefore pays back in £90,000 ÷ £20,000 = 4.5 years. A three-year appraisal window lands before payback, so it shows a modest net cost for a change whose year-4-onwards position is clearly better. The honest options paper states all three figures side by side — window total (−£30,000 over three years), steady-state saving (+£20,000 per year), payback (4.5 years) — because an opponent of change can always pick a window shorter than payback to make a good consolidation look bad, and an advocate can pick a long one to bury the transition cost. Choosing and *declaring* the appraisal window is the senior skill; the subtraction is just the entry fee.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Percentage points, baselines and honest reporting**

A supplier's service credit regime activates if availability falls below 99.0% in a quarter. The service recorded: Q1 99.4%, Q2 99.1%, Q3 98.8%, Q4 99.3%. The supplier's annual report states: "Average availability of 99.15% comfortably exceeded the 99.0% threshold; availability varied by no more than 0.6 percentage points across the year." Which assessment is correct?

- A) The supplier's statement is accurate and complete; no credits are due.
- B) Both quoted figures are arithmetically correct, but the framing conceals that Q3 breached the quarterly threshold — the regime applies per quarter, so a credit is due for Q3 regardless of the annual average.
- C) The average is miscalculated, which invalidates the report.
- D) A 0.6 percentage point range is too large for any threshold regime, so credits are due for the whole year.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Verify the arithmetic first: mean = (99.4 + 99.1 + 98.8 + 99.3) ÷ 4 = 396.6 ÷ 4 = 99.15 ✓; range = 99.4 − 98.8 = 0.6 percentage points ✓. So option C fails — nothing is miscalculated. The deception is structural: the credit regime is defined *per quarter*, and Q3's 98.8% sits below the 99.0% threshold, triggering a credit — a fact the annual averaging is constructed to bury, since averaging is precisely the operation that lets one bad quarter hide inside three good ones. Option A accepts the frame the supplier chose; option D invents a rule about ranges that no regime in the stem contains. This question rehearses a senior skill with a name: *baseline and aggregation vigilance*. Whenever a counterparty reports against a threshold, check that their aggregation period matches the regime's — per-quarter thresholds reported annually, per-service targets reported as portfolio averages, and per-user experience reported as system-wide uptime are the three commonest flattering translations. Managing supplier-facing relationships and holding frameworks coherent means doing this check reflexively, and doing it *before* countersigning the report, because a credit unclaimed in the quarter it arose is, in most contracts, a credit waived.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Efficiency claims under scrutiny**

A unit claims its process improvement "increased efficiency by 40%". Investigating, you find: last year the team of 10 processed 20,000 cases; this year the team of 8 processed 22,400 cases. Which statement most accurately characterises the efficiency change?

- A) The claim is understated: cases per person rose from 2,000 to 2,800, a 40% productivity increase, and total output also rose 12% with 20% less staff.
- B) The claim is correct as stated and needs no qualification.
- C) The claim is overstated: output rose only 12%.
- D) Efficiency cannot be measured because staff numbers changed.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Compute the per-person rate, which is what "efficiency" honestly denotes here. Last year: 20,000 ÷ 10 = 2,000 cases per person. This year: 22,400 ÷ 8 = 2,800 cases per person. Change: (2,800 − 2,000) ÷ 2,000 = 800 ÷ 2,000 = 40% — the claim is exactly right on productivity, and option A adds the two components that make it *more* impressive, not less: total output rose 12% (22,400 vs 20,000) even as headcount fell 20% (10 → 8). Option C commits the revealing error — comparing raw output while ignoring the denominator change — and it is worth noticing that this error usually runs in the *skeptical* direction from reviewers, just as denominators are usually fudged in the *flattering* direction by claimants: both are the same sin. Option D gives up on exactly the normalisation that makes changed circumstances comparable. One senior caveat belongs in any write-up: a 40% per-person jump in one year invites the question *how* — automation, case-mix change (were easier cases routed here?), quality trade-offs (error rates?), or unsustainable effort. Your role's improvement mandate — increase value, improve user experience *and* increase efficiency — treats a productivity number as the start of the quality conversation, not the end of it. Verify the error rate moved the right way before this claim headlines your directorate's efficiency narrative.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Marginal thinking in a demand decision**

Your shared platform team has capacity for 30 onboarding projects this year and 34 units want onboarding. Each onboarding delivers an average £25,000 annual saving to the joining unit. A director proposes hiring two contractors (£90,000 total, one year) to add 6 more onboarding slots. Assuming the savings estimates hold and onboarded units keep saving every year, which analysis best supports a decision?

- A) Reject: £90,000 for 6 slots is £15,000 per slot, and slots are worth only £25,000 once.
- B) Approve: the 4 unmet demands plus 2 further… the contractors enable at most 4 additional real onboardings (34 − 30 = 4 units actually waiting), yielding 4 × £25,000 = £100,000 of *recurring* annual savings for a one-off £90,000 — payback inside year 1 and pure saving thereafter; the two surplus slots have no demand and should not be bought, so negotiate the contractor cost toward 4 slots.
- C) Approve as proposed: 6 slots × £25,000 = £150,000 exceeds £90,000.
- D) Reject: contractors cost more per head than staff, so the work should wait for next year's capacity.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The question tests marginal thinking — pricing what the spend *actually buys*, not what it nominally provides. Demand is 34; capacity is 30; the genuine shortfall is 4 units. Contractors offering 6 slots therefore deliver at most 4 *used* slots; the other 2 are capacity without customers. Option C's arithmetic (6 × £25,000) values slots nobody needs — the classic supply-side error. The value of the 4 real slots is strong precisely because the savings *recur*: £100,000 in year 1 (already exceeding the £90,000 one-off cost), then £100,000 every subsequent year — while the £90,000 never repeats. Option A makes two errors: it prices per nominal slot rather than per used slot, and it treats a recurring saving as one-off. Option D introduces a per-head cost comparison irrelevant to a one-year capacity bridge and quietly costs the organisation a year of £100,000 recurring savings — deferral is never free when benefits recur. B also does what senior reviewers should: it converts analysis into negotiating position (pay for 4, not 6). One honest caveat completes the picture: "average £25,000" savings deserve a look at their distribution — if the four waiting units are the four smallest, their actual savings may sit below average, which is exactly the kind of assumption-check that distinguishes supporting a strategic demand from merely arithmetic-checking it.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Reading a two-way table on investment outcomes**

A review of 80 completed digital investments cross-tabulates whether business units were engaged through a relationship manager during shaping, and whether the investment met its benefits targets:

| | Met benefits target | Missed benefits target | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| BRM-engaged during shaping | 33 | 12 | 45 |
| Not BRM-engaged | 14 | 21 | 35 |
| Total | 47 | 33 | 80 |

Your head of function wants to tell the executive committee: "BRM engagement more than doubles the success rate — this proves we should mandate it." Which is the most defensible version of the claim?

- A) The claim is fine as drafted: 33 successes versus 14 speaks for itself.
- B) BRM-engaged investments met their targets at 73% versus 40% for non-engaged — a strong association that supports expanding engagement, but not proof of causation: bigger, better-run, or better-sponsored initiatives may both seek BRM engagement and succeed for other reasons. Recommend expansion alongside a design that tests the effect — for example, tracking outcomes as engagement rolls out by cohort.
- C) The success rates are 73% versus 40%, which is less than double, so the claim is false and the data should not be presented.
- D) The sample of 80 is too small to say anything.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Compute the rates the claim depends on: engaged 33 ÷ 45 = 73.3%; not engaged 14 ÷ 35 = 40%. The ratio is 73.3 ÷ 40 = 1.83 — a substantial association, just short of "more than doubles", so the draft overstates arithmetically as well as logically. The logical gap matters more: "proves" converts an observational cross-tab into a causal experiment. Engagement was not randomly assigned — units that involve BRMs early may run better initiatives generally, and initiative size, sponsorship quality or delivery method may drive both the choice to engage and the outcome. Option B fixes the arithmetic (state the rates, drop "doubles"), keeps the legitimate strength of the finding (an 33-point gap across 80 investments is well worth acting on), and adds the design that would convert association toward evidence — cohort tracking during rollout, the pragmatic public-sector substitute for a trial. Option A repeats raw counts whose comparison is meaningless across different group sizes. Option C commits the mirror-image error of over-claiming: an overstated draft is corrected, not suppressed; the underlying finding remains genuinely favourable to your function. Option D wields "small sample" as a slogan — 80 outcomes with a gap this large is far from nothing, and dismissing it discards the best evidence your function has. This is the numeric version of your influencing duty: the strongest position in front of an executive committee is the claim that survives the sharpest person in the room — so make that claim, and no more.

### Preparation tips

- **Drill the senior five.** Weighted averages, compound growth, one-off versus recurring costs, per-quarter versus aggregated thresholds, and marginal (used-slot) valuation cover most of what senior numeric tests — and spending reviews — throw at you. Practise the *setup* of each until it is reflexive.
- **Name the denominator, then challenge it.** At senior level, choosing the right denominator is half the marks; noticing that someone else chose a flattering one is the other half. For every rate you read, ask: per what, over what period, for which subgroup?
- **Declare appraisal windows.** For any cost comparison, consciously pick the horizon, and compute steady-state and payback alongside the window total. Practise presenting all three in one sentence.
- **Reverse-check everything that will be quoted.** Multiply your answer back, cross-foot your tables, and recompute the one number a board will remember. Ninety seconds of checking is cheaper than a corrected minute.
- **Use your live submissions as practice.** QA one benefits model or options paper per week with the techniques here — cashability, compounding, weighting, thresholds. The test preparation is free; the organisational value is real.
- **Practise the interpretation question.** Senior tests increasingly ask not "what is the number?" but "what does the number support?" After each calculation you practise, write the one-sentence claim it honestly licenses.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Linear thinking about compound processes.** Growth of 8% per quarter is not 32% per year, and the capacity breach lands earlier than the linear estimate says. Compound forward step by step when the horizon is short; use powers when it is long.
- **Averaging away the story.** Overall means — of satisfaction, availability, maturity — routinely conceal the failing channel, quarter, or unit that the decision actually turns on. Read the components before you bless the average.
- **Accepting the counterparty's aggregation.** Annual averages reported against quarterly thresholds, portfolio figures against per-service targets: always re-aggregate to the regime's own period before agreeing anything.
- **Counting capacity as value.** Slots, licences and seats are only worth what demand will use. Price the marginal used unit, not the nominal one.
- **Mixing cashable and capacity benefits.** Redeployed staff are not savings on the paybill. Every benefits figure should carry its label, and every financial case should survive the question "whose budget line goes down?"
- **Treating association as causation — or dismissing it as nothing.** Observational gaps justify action and further testing; they do not "prove", and they are not "too small to mean anything". Both over-claim and over-dismissal fail the sharpest-person-in-the-room test.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment measures how precisely you understand, evaluate and act on written information — the medium through which nearly all of your influence travels. You are shown short passages of the kind your role handles daily — strategy commitments, governance minutes, supplier assurances, research summaries, escalation emails — and asked what each one guarantees, contradicts, or leaves open. The classic format is True / False / Cannot say, joined at senior level by inference questions, argument-evaluation questions (find the flaw, find the assumption), and clear-writing judgement.

Typical format: an online, timed test of 15 to 30 minutes, with passages of 100–250 words carrying two to four questions each. The governing rule is strict: answer only from the passage, setting aside your considerable background knowledge. Senior-normed tests add subtlety rather than vocabulary — the wrong answers are not silly but *almost right*, differing from the passage by one scope word, one modal verb, or one quietly substituted concept.

For a senior business relationship manager, verbal precision is a core professional instrument. You ensure complex needs and challenges are understood, prioritised and *communicated* — and communication at your level is load-bearing: the sentence you write into a spending review narrative will be quoted back at settlement; the way you summarise a unit's ambition determines whether the digital strategy serves it or misses it; the assurance you accept from a supplier defines what you can later enforce. You also read on others' behalf — distilling a forty-page strategy for a unit head, or a technical assurance for a director — and your distillations must survive comparison with the source. Employers test verbal reasoning at this level because seniors are the organisation's translators, and translation errors at altitude propagate: one over-read commitment or under-read caveat can move money, expectations, and trust in the wrong direction for a year.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **True/False/Cannot-say discipline** maps to **Business analysis (IT operations)**: leading the collection of information means recording what sources actually establish — the boundary between finding, inference and assumption is exactly the T/F/CS boundary.
- **Reading commitments and conditions precisely** maps to your duty to **ensure strategic demands, such as spending reviews, are supported**: settlement letters, strategy commitments and ministerial deadlines are conditional texts, and mis-reading a condition is mis-briefing the organisation.
- **Argument evaluation** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management (IT operations)**: influencing decisions and removing blockers requires you to locate the load-bearing flaw in a persuasive case — a supplier's renewal logic, a unit's resistance narrative — without quibbling its incidentals.
- **Distinguishing needs from desires in written evidence** maps to **User focus**: your skill definition explicitly asks you to explain the difference between user needs and the desires of the user, and that distinction is made or lost in how research and requests are worded.
- **Clear-writing judgement** maps to **Strategic thinking** and your duty to ensure complex challenges are **communicated to relevant audiences**: the version of a message that survives is the one a busy reader cannot misunderstand.
- **Reading between the lines — with restraint** maps to your relationship leadership: noticing what a carefully drafted email avoids committing to is senior craft; inventing meanings it never carried is senior malpractice. The assessment rewards exactly that calibration.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — True/False/Cannot say on a strategy commitment**

Passage: "By the end of the spending review period, the department will reduce its application estate from 420 to no more than 300 applications. Reductions will prioritise applications with fewer than 50 active users. No application supporting a statutory service will be retired without ministerial agreement to a replacement plan."

Statement: "The department has committed to retiring at least 120 applications by the end of the spending review period."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The arithmetic is right — 420 down to at most 300 is a reduction of at least 120 — but check the verb. The passage commits to *reducing the estate*; the statement claims a commitment to *retiring* applications. An estate can shrink by retirement, but also by merger, consolidation into platforms, or reclassification — and the passage's own third sentence shows "retired" is a specific mechanism with its own safeguards, distinct from the headline reduction commitment. Because the passage guarantees the reduction but not the mechanism, the statement is neither guaranteed (not True) nor contradicted (not False): Cannot say. This one-word substitution — "reduce" read as "retire" — is exactly how strategy commitments get misquoted in the wild, and at your level the misquote has consequences: telling a unit head "your application is committed for retirement" when the commitment is estate reduction manufactures resistance that accurate reading would never have provoked.

**Question 2 (easy) — False versus Cannot say on a supplier assurance**

Passage (supplier email): "We can confirm that the March incident did not result in any loss of customer data. Our investigation found the root cause to be a failed storage controller, which has been replaced. We are reviewing whether similar controllers elsewhere in the estate require proactive replacement and will report by the end of the month."

Statement: "The supplier has confirmed that no similar controllers elsewhere in the estate require replacement."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The statement claims the supplier confirmed a conclusion about the wider estate. The passage says the supplier is *reviewing* that exact question and will report by month end — an explicitly open question cannot simultaneously be a confirmed conclusion, so the passage contradicts the statement: False. Note why this is not Cannot say: the passage is not silent about the wider estate — it speaks to it directly, and what it says (review in progress) is incompatible with what the statement claims (confirmation delivered). The False/Cannot-say boundary is the most-missed distinction in these tests, and the reliable test is: does the passage actively conflict with the statement (False), or merely fail to address it (Cannot say)? Professionally, this is the reading that protects you in assurance chains: "they confirmed no data loss" is true and quotable; "they confirmed the estate is safe" is a misquote that becomes *your* assurance the moment you forward it upward — and seniors are exactly the people whose forwarded summaries get treated as due diligence.

**Question 3 (easy) — Main point of a governance paper**

Passage: "Attendance at the design authority has averaged 40% for programme-level items this year, and three decisions have been remade within a quarter after affected units said they were not consulted. The authority's terms of reference date from 2019, before the current federated delivery model. Units now make many technology choices locally under delegated budgets. The secretariat proposes that, rather than enforcing attendance, the authority's scope and membership be redesigned around the decisions that genuinely require central agreement."

Which sentence best states the paper's main point?

- A) Design authority attendance must be enforced more strictly.
- B) The design authority's governance model predates the current operating model, and the proposal is to redesign its scope and membership rather than police attendance.
- C) Units should lose their delegated technology budgets.
- D) The design authority should be abolished.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Trace the paper's argument structure: symptoms (low attendance, decisions remade after consultation failures), diagnosis (2019 terms of reference versus a federated delivery model with delegated budgets — the governance no longer matches how decisions are actually made), and proposal (redesign scope and membership around decisions that genuinely need central agreement). B captures diagnosis and proposal. Option A is the position the paper explicitly argues *against* ("rather than enforcing attendance"). Option C reverses the paper's direction of travel — it treats delegation as the given, not the problem. Option D overshoots: redesign is not abolition, and the paper affirms that some decisions genuinely require central agreement. Main-point questions at senior level reward reading for *argument architecture* — symptom, diagnosis, proposal — rather than for topic. It is also how you should brief such a paper to a unit head in ninety seconds, which is the workplace version of this question.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Needs versus desires in research evidence**

Passage (research summary): "In interviews, 14 of 18 caseworkers asked for the new system to include a built-in chat function 'like the one sales teams use'. Observation sessions showed that caseworkers currently interrupt casework an average of nine times daily to seek colleague input on edge cases, using a mixture of walking over, phoning, and emailing, with a median wait of 25 minutes for email responses. Interviewees consistently linked their chat request to these delays."

Which statement best distinguishes the user need from the expressed desire?

- A) The need is a chat function; 14 of 18 users requested it, which is conclusive.
- B) The underlying need is rapid access to colleague expertise during casework; built-in chat is one candidate solution, whose fit should be tested against alternatives such as a searchable decisions log or a duty-expert rota.
- C) There is no real need, since caseworkers already have three ways to reach colleagues.
- D) The need is to reduce interruptions, so colleague contact should be restricted.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Your **User focus** skill at this level explicitly includes explaining the difference between user needs and the desires of the user — and this passage is a textbook case. The *desire* is specific and solution-shaped: built-in chat, borrowed from a neighbouring team's experience. The *need* is what the observational data reveals: nine daily interruptions seeking colleague input on edge cases, with painful latency (25-minute median email waits). The interviewees themselves connect the two — which makes the desire *evidence about* the need, not the need itself. B holds that structure and draws the correct practical consequence: chat is one candidate response, to be tested against alternatives that might serve the need better (a searchable decisions log attacks the *recurrence* of edge-case questions; a duty-expert rota attacks the latency). Option A converts vote-counting into requirements — 14 of 18 people can accurately report a pain and still propose a suboptimal fix. Option C mistakes the existence of channels for the adequacy of channels; the data shows the channels failing. Option D optimises a metric (interruptions) against the users' actual goal — the interruptions are caseworkers *succeeding* in getting help despite bad tooling. The senior skill: honour the desire as data, name the need behind it, and keep the solution space open long enough to serve the need well.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Inference from a settlement letter**

Passage (extract): "The department's settlement provides £46m for digital and technology over the period, of which £8m is ring-fenced for cyber security improvements. Continued access to the £12m transformation tranche in years 2 and 3 is conditional on the department demonstrating, at the year 1 review point, benefits realisation of at least 60% against the year 1 profile. HM Treasury notes the department's ambition on legacy remediation and expects a costed plan by October."

Which inference is best supported?

- A) If year 1 benefits realisation reaches only 50% of profile, the department loses the entire £46m settlement.
- B) The department can spend the £8m cyber ring-fence on legacy remediation if the October plan justifies it.
- C) At most £38m of the settlement is freely allocable by the department, and £12m of that is at risk if the year 1 review shows benefits realisation below 60% of profile.
- D) HM Treasury has approved the department's legacy remediation plan.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Assemble what the passage guarantees. The £8m ring-fence is committed to cyber, so the freely allocable maximum is £46m − £8m = £38m ("at most", since other conditions may exist beyond the extract). Within that, the £12m transformation tranche for years 2–3 is explicitly conditional on hitting 60% of the year 1 benefits profile — below that, continued access is not demonstrated, so the £12m is genuinely "at risk". C states both, carefully. Option A catastrophises: the condition attaches to the £12m tranche, not the whole settlement — reading conditions as applying to more money than they do is the mirror image of reading them as applying to less, and both mis-brief the organisation. Option B contradicts the meaning of a ring-fence; no mechanism in the passage converts cyber money to legacy money. Option D over-reads a courteous acknowledgement — "notes the ambition and expects a costed plan" is Treasury language for "not yet persuaded; show your work", almost the opposite of approval. This is the reading skill your role level names directly: ensuring strategic demands such as spending reviews are supported begins with knowing, to the pound and the condition, what the settlement text actually says — because every unit head you partner with will ask you "so what does this mean for us?", and your answer becomes their planning assumption.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Argument evaluation: the resistance narrative**

Passage (email from a unit head to your director, copied to you): "We oppose migrating to the shared HR platform. Our current system was configured for our statutory casework and staff know it well. The shared platform failed its first go-live in the pensions unit last year, which proves it cannot handle complex requirements. Moreover, our unit's engagement survey shows staff morale is already low; a forced migration now would make us the least efficient unit in the department."

Which is the strongest criticism of the argument?

- A) The email wrongly claims staff know the current system well.
- B) A single failed first go-live, later context unknown, cannot "prove" general incapacity — and the morale claim assumes without evidence that migration causes the predicted efficiency collapse; the legitimate points (statutory configuration, familiarity, timing) are reasons to shape the migration, not shown reasons to cancel it.
- C) Unit heads should not email directors directly.
- D) Engagement surveys are not valid evidence of morale.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Separate the argument's legitimate observations from its logical over-reaches — because your response, as the relationship lead, must respect the former while dismantling the latter. Legitimate: statutory configuration is a real requirement to be carried into the migration; familiarity is a real transition cost; low morale is a real timing consideration. Over-reached: "failed its first go-live… which proves it cannot handle complex requirements" converts one data point, cause unexamined (first go-lives fail for planning, data and readiness reasons as often as product reasons), into a universal verdict — the classic hasty generalisation; and "would make us the least efficient unit" is an unevidenced prediction stated as fact, resting on the unstated assumption that migration effects and morale effects simply add. B names both flaws *and* preserves the reasonable core, which is what makes it the strongest criticism rather than merely the harshest. Option A disputes a premise the passage gives you no grounds to dispute. Options C and D attack the messenger and the instrument rather than the reasoning. The professional pattern this trains: when you arbitrate resistance, answer the argument's best version — acknowledge the statutory and timing points, then ask for the evidence behind "proves" and "would make". Influence that engages the strongest form of the other side's case is the only influence that survives the meeting.

**Question 7 (moderate) — True/False/Cannot say on relationship maturity reporting**

Passage: "The annual partnering report records that 11 of 14 business units now have engagement plans agreed with their senior relationship manager, compared with 6 last year. Units with agreed plans raised 84% of this year's demand through the front-door process, compared with 51% for units without plans. The report cautions that units which agreed plans early tended to be those with the strongest prior relationships."

Statement: "Agreeing an engagement plan causes units to use the front-door process more."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage reports an association — planned units use the front door at 84% versus 51% — and then, unusually, supplies its own confound: units that agreed plans early were those with the strongest prior relationships, and strong prior relationships could independently drive both plan agreement and front-door usage. The causal statement is therefore not guaranteed (not True), but neither does the passage contradict it (not False) — the plans may well contribute; the evidence simply cannot isolate the effect. Cannot say. This question rewards noticing that the passage *itself* is a careful piece of writing: its final sentence is the author pre-empting the causal over-read, exactly as you should when drafting your own maturity reporting. The workplace version of this discipline: when your function's dashboard shows partnered units performing better, resist the promotional sentence "partnering delivers these results" and write the survivable one — "partnered units show materially better outcomes; some of this reflects which units partner first" — because the second sentence keeps its credibility when a sceptical finance director stress-tests it, and credibility is the asset your influence spends.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Clear-writing judgement for a mixed audience**

You must send one message to all unit heads about a change to demand submission. Which version is best?

- A) "Pursuant to the outcome of the Q2 governance review, the demand intake mechanism will transition to an enhanced triage paradigm, with concomitant adjustments to submission modalities, effective imminently."
- B) "From 1 October, submit all demand through the new front-door form (link below). Items already in the pipeline are unaffected. Small changes will get a decision within 5 working days; larger items go to the monthly board as now. Questions: contact your relationship manager."
- C) "The demand process is changing soon. More details will follow in due course. Please bear with us while we work through the implications."
- D) "As you may be aware, following extensive consultation, and in line with best practice across government, we have been considering how best to optimise our demand management approach, and after careful reflection have concluded that changes are appropriate, which this note summarises at a high level."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Test each against what a busy unit head needs: what changes, when, what they must do, what happens to in-flight items, what improves, and where to ask. B answers all six in four short sentences — date, action, transitional protection ("already in the pipeline are unaffected", the sentence that prevents half the anxious replies), a service commitment that gives units a reason to *welcome* the change, and a named route for questions that routes traffic to relationship managers rather than to a hundred reply-alls. Option A is jargon armour — "paradigm", "modalities", "imminently" — which conceals every operative fact including the date. Option C is a pre-announcement that creates uncertainty and zero capability to act; announcing that news will exist is worse than waiting until it does. Option D spends fifty words clearing its throat about process history and never reaches the change itself. The senior lesson: communication *to relevant audiences* — your role-level phrase — is judged by what the audience can do after reading, and the drafting discipline of B (date first, action second, reassurance third, benefit fourth, route for questions last) is a reusable template for every operational change you will ever announce.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Reading a conditional commitment chain**

Passage (minute of the investment committee): "The committee approved the citizen portal business case, subject to: (a) confirmation of GDS service assessment booking before development spend begins; (b) benefits being re-baselined once the discovery completes, with the case returning to committee only if re-baselined benefits fall more than 20% below the approved profile; and (c) quarterly reporting to the committee. The chair noted that approval lapses if development spend has not begun within six months."

The discovery completes, and re-baselined benefits are 15% below the approved profile. Development spend has not yet begun; five months have passed; the GDS assessment is booked. Which statement is correct?

- A) The case must return to committee because benefits fell below the approved profile.
- B) The team may proceed to development spend without returning to committee, provided it begins within the next month and quarterly reporting continues — the 15% shortfall is inside the 20% tolerance and condition (a) is satisfied.
- C) The approval has lapsed because benefits were re-baselined.
- D) The team may not proceed because any reduction in benefits requires re-approval.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Walk the conditions as a checklist against the facts. Condition (a): assessment booked before development spend — satisfied (booked; spend not yet begun). Condition (b): return to committee is triggered *only if* re-baselined benefits fall *more than* 20% below profile; 15% is within tolerance, so no return is required — which disposes of options A and D, both of which rewrite the tolerance the committee deliberately built (tolerances exist precisely so that expected small movements do not re-consume committee time). Condition (c): quarterly reporting is an ongoing obligation, not a gate. The lapse clause: approval dies if spend has not begun within six months; five have passed, so one month of validity remains — hence B's "provided it begins within the next month", the clause that makes B exactly right rather than roughly right. Option C misreads re-baselining — an *expected activity under* the approval — as a lapse trigger. This checklist-walk is verbatim senior craft: conditional approvals are the normal currency of governance, and the person who can state precisely what is permitted, what is triggered, and what expires — and by when — is the person who keeps programmes moving inside their authority. Mis-read a tolerance in the strict direction and you burn committee time and delivery momentum; mis-read it in the loose direction and you spend money outside approval. Both errors are careers' worth of credibility; the checklist costs ninety seconds.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Synthesis across conflicting written sources**

Passage 1 (supplier performance report): "All eight service levels were met this quarter. User satisfaction with the service desk stands at 4.1/5, based on the post-ticket survey."

Passage 2 (email from an operations manager): "Getting anything fixed takes forever. My team have mostly stopped logging tickets — we fix things ourselves or live with them. The survey only goes to people whose tickets were closed, so of course the scores look fine."

Which conclusion do the two passages together best support?

- A) The supplier is falsifying its performance data.
- B) The operations manager's team is failing to follow process, which explains their poor experience.
- C) The measurement system may be structurally blind to the manager's experience: service levels and post-ticket surveys only observe logged tickets, so ticket avoidance would leave dissatisfaction invisible while every metric stays green — worth testing via unlogged-demand sampling and non-user surveys before the next supplier review.
- D) The two accounts contradict each other, so at least one must be withdrawn.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The senior insight is that these passages do not actually conflict — they describe different populations. Every supplier metric in Passage 1 is conditioned on a ticket existing: service levels attach to logged tickets; the satisfaction survey goes only to closed-ticket users. Passage 2 describes people who have *exited* that population — they no longer log tickets — and the manager even names the mechanism ("the survey only goes to people whose tickets were closed"). A measurement system built entirely on logged demand is structurally incapable of seeing ticket avoidance; indeed, avoidance *improves* the visible metrics by removing hard cases. C draws exactly this conclusion and — critically — converts it into testable next steps: sample the unlogged demand (walk the floor, check the workaround culture), survey non-users, and bring the findings to the supplier review. Option A leaps from "metrics are blind" to "supplier is lying" — no evidence of falsification exists, and the accusation would wreck a relationship that the structural finding could instead improve. Option B blames the users of a failing channel for not using it harder — and note it may be *locally* true (process isn't being followed) while being diagnostically empty about *why*. Option D demands resolution by retraction when reconciliation is available. This is your **Business analysis** skill at its senior definition — absorbing conflicting information and producing a solution — applied to text: the reconciliation of contradictory documents is usually a hidden difference in scope, population, or period, and finding it is what turns two angry emails into one improved measurement framework.

**Question 11 (challenging) — What the passage does not commit to**

Passage (email from a director sponsoring your shared-services proposal): "I read the consolidation paper with interest. The efficiency logic is compelling, and you have clearly done the groundwork with the affected units. I would be comfortable seeing this go to the investment committee in the autumn. You should know that the chief operating officer has expressed some reservations about central platforms following the finance system programme; it would be worth your reflecting on how the paper addresses resilience and exit provisions before submission."

Which reading of this email is most accurate?

- A) The director has committed to sponsoring the paper at the investment committee in the autumn.
- B) The director is signalling conditional support — comfort with an autumn submission, not a commitment to sponsor it — and is telling you that COO-level reservations exist which the paper must visibly answer, particularly on resilience and exit, before it can safely proceed.
- C) The director is politely rejecting the proposal.
- D) The COO has decided to block the proposal at committee.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Read what is said, what is signalled, and what is *not* said — keeping the three distinct. Said: the logic is compelling; groundwork acknowledged; "I would be comfortable seeing this go to committee" — which is an attitude toward the submission, not an undertaking to champion it; "comfortable seeing" commits the director to nothing at the meeting itself, which is why option A over-reads. Signalled: the COO reservation is not gossip — a sponsor mentioning a more senior stakeholder's reservations, twinned with specific homework ("resilience and exit provisions"), is telling you where the paper will be attacked and what the sponsor needs in hand to defend it; experienced readers hear "before submission" as a soft condition. Not said: any rejection (option C misreads engaged, directive feedback as brush-off — rejections do not assign homework), and any COO decision (option D converts "expressed some reservations" secondhand into a settled blocking position; reservations are an audience to be persuaded, not a verdict). B calibrates all three registers correctly. This calibration — hearing conditionality inside encouragement, and instructions inside observations, without hallucinating commitments or conflicts that are not there — is the daily verbal skill of senior relationship work; the assessment merely scores it. The practical move the email actually requires: strengthen the resilience and exit sections, then brief the director *that you have done so*, converting their soft condition into their talking points.

### Preparation tips

- **Read with three registers.** For any workplace passage, practise separating what it *states*, what it *signals*, and what it *omits* — and answer test questions from the first register only, unless the question explicitly asks for inference.
- **Build a scope-word reflex.** All, only, at least, more than, unless, subject to, would be comfortable, expects — circle them mentally on first read. Senior test items and senior correspondence alike hide their meaning in these hinges.
- **Walk conditions as checklists.** For conditional passages (approvals, settlements, standards), list each condition and tag it satisfied / breached / not yet triggered before touching the options. The discipline eliminates the "roughly right" answers that plague fast readers.
- **Practise the reconciliation move.** When two texts conflict, hunt for the scope difference — population, period, definition — before judging either wrong. Your own inbox supplies daily practice material.
- **Draft, then cut, then front-load.** For clear-writing questions and real communications: what must the reader do, by when, and what protects them? Say that first. Everything explanatory comes after the operative facts.
- **Rehearse needs-versus-desires.** Take three recent stakeholder requests and write one sentence each: the solution they asked for, and the need the evidence supports. The gap between the sentences is where your role adds its value — and where a test question is probably waiting.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Substituting a neighbouring concept.** "Reduce" is not "retire"; "reviewing" is not "confirmed"; "comfortable seeing it submitted" is not "will sponsor it". The wrong options at senior level are one concept-swap away from the passage — match the exact word, not the gist.
- **Reading conditions onto the wrong money or the wrong scope.** A condition on a £12m tranche is not a condition on a £46m settlement; a per-quarter threshold is not an annual one. Attach every condition to precisely the object the text attaches it to.
- **Answering from experience instead of the passage.** The more senior you are, the more plausible your background assumptions — and the more marks they cost. If the passage does not guarantee it, it is not True, however often you have seen it happen.
- **Confusing the strongest criticism with the harshest.** Argument-evaluation questions reward locating the load-bearing flaw while preserving the argument's legitimate content. Options that attack the messenger, the instrument, or an undisputed premise are decoys.
- **Missing the passage's own caveat.** Well-drafted sources often pre-empt the over-read ("units which agreed plans early tended to be those with the strongest prior relationships"). The test — and careful professional reading — expects you to *use* that sentence, not skim past it.
- **Treating signals as commitments.** Corridor language, sponsor comfort, and noted ambitions are real information and belong in your planning — but the moment you brief them as commitments, you have converted your inference into someone else's promise. Keep the registers separate in tests, and ruthlessly separate in writing.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment (SJT) presents realistic workplace dilemmas and asks you to judge the effectiveness of possible responses — most effective, most and least effective, full rankings, or independent effectiveness ratings. There is rarely a perfect answer; the scoring key, built with experienced practitioners and occupational psychologists, rewards consistent preference for responses that are active rather than passive, collaborative rather than unilateral, honest rather than convenient, and user- and outcome-focused rather than self-protective.

Typical format: 15 to 25 scenarios over 30 to 45 minutes, often untimed. Senior-normed SJTs differ from junior ones less in format than in the *shape* of the dilemmas: the scenarios put legitimate goods in tension — candour against confidentiality, pace against inclusion, a stakeholder's trust against an organisational duty — and the response options are all superficially defensible. The differentiator at your level is second-order thinking: what does each response teach the organisation, what precedent does it set, and what happens to the relationship, the team, and the system after the immediate move is made.

For a senior business relationship manager, the SJT is the closest of the four assessments to a work sample. Your role-level duties are behavioural through and through: lead activities that improve relationship maturity; ensure complex needs and challenges are understood, prioritised and communicated; partner with others to provide strategic guidance; promote sustained investment in user-centred approaches; take accountability for issues and proactively search for potential problems. None of these is performed at a desk alone — each is performed in rooms, in inboxes, and in moments where several defensible actions compete and your choice becomes the culture. Employers use SJTs at senior level because technical capability is assumed; what distinguishes performance is judgement under relational and political load, and that is precisely what these scenarios sample.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Accountability scenarios** map to **Ownership and topic** at your level: taking accountability for issues that occur — including ones you did not cause — and proactively hunting problems before they surface, rather than managing your distance from them.
- **Competing-stakeholder scenarios** map to **Stakeholder relationship management (IT operations)**: negotiating, influencing, removing blockers, and dealing with challenging situations while keeping long-term strategic relationships intact — the key rewards moves that solve the issue *and* strengthen the relationship's future.
- **Strategic tension scenarios** map to **Strategic thinking**: providing guidance to others on working in the strategic context, especially when local pressures and strategic commitments pull apart — the key rewards responses that serve both or honestly sequence them.
- **User advocacy scenarios** map to **User focus**: promoting sustained investment in user-centred approaches means defending research and user needs when they are politically inconvenient — and representing users who are not in the room.
- **Evidence-versus-relationship scenarios** map to **Business analysis (IT operations)**: leading investigations whose findings someone powerful will not like, and communicating recommendations persuasively without bending them.
- **Capability-building scenarios** map to your leadership of others: growing the judgement of the business relationship managers around you — the key rewards responses that develop people, not just resolve incidents.

### Practice questions

For each scenario, choose the option indicated (most effective, or most and least effective as stated).

**Question 1 (easy) — The finding the sponsor dislikes**

Your investigation into a director's cherished proposal — a bespoke scheduling system for her unit — concludes that an existing shared platform meets 90% of the need at a third of the cost. The director, who has championed your function to her peers, phones you: "I hope your paper isn't going to embarrass me at the board." What is the most effective response?

- A) Soften the paper's conclusion to "further options analysis required" to protect the relationship.
- B) Tell her the findings stand, brief her on them fully before the paper circulates, and work with her on the strongest version of her unit's case — including where the shared platform genuinely falls short and what would close the 10% gap.
- C) Submit the paper as written without further contact, since the evidence speaks for itself.
- D) Ask your head of function to deliver the difficult message instead.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The relationship and the evidence both survive in B, and only in B. The findings stand — communicating recommendations persuasively, in your skill set, never means bending them — but the *handling* is where senior craft lives: a full private briefing before circulation removes the ambush (which is what "embarrass me" actually fears), and co-developing the honest version of her case — the real 10% gap, the costed route to closing it — converts her from opponent of your paper into owner of a better position. She championed your function because it was useful; B is the move that keeps it useful when the news is bad, which is the only kind of usefulness that compounds. Option A trades your function's analytical integrity for one director's comfort; the board will eventually fund on your papers, and papers known to soften under pressure are worthless to everyone, including her. Option C is honest but relationally reckless — surprise is a choice, and it is never the effective one. Option D outsources the hardest conversation in your own portfolio, teaching your head of function that you carry relationships only in fair weather. The senior pattern: never trade the finding; always invest in the landing.

**Question 2 (easy) — Credit and visibility for your team**

At a directors' meeting, your director praises "the outstanding shared-needs analysis" that reshaped the investment pipeline. The analysis was substantially built by one of your business relationship managers, who is not present and is currently under-confident after a difficult year. What is the most effective response?

- A) Accept the praise on behalf of the function and move on; correcting a director mid-meeting is poor form.
- B) Name the BRM in the meeting as the analysis's author, then tell her afterwards exactly what was said and by whom — and find her the next stretch piece of work with director-level visibility.
- C) Tell the BRM afterwards that the directors liked the work, keeping the meeting itself brief.
- D) Suggest the BRM present the analysis at a future meeting to earn the credit directly.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Attribution in the room is cheap for you and compounding for her: a named author in a directors' meeting acquires a professional identity in exactly the forum where reputations are formed, and doing it takes one sentence that costs you nothing but reflected glory you did not earn. The follow-through completes the development move — under-confident people discount secondhand praise, so "exactly what was said, and by whom" makes it undiscountable, and the next visible stretch assignment converts a moment into a trajectory. That is what leading a team of relationship managers means in practice: engineering the conditions in which your people's work meets its audience. Option A quietly banks her work as institutional credit — the small, common theft that under-confident high performers notice most and forgive least. Option C delivers the news but forfeits the room: the directors still believe what they were allowed to believe. Option D is a genuinely good *addition* to B but a poor substitute — it defers attribution to a hypothetical future meeting while today's incorrect impression sets. SJT keys at leadership level consistently reward the pairing B exemplifies: correct the record where the record was made, and convert recognition into development.

**Question 3 (easy) — The proactive problem search**

Reviewing supplier reports across your portfolio, you notice a pattern nobody has raised: three different services, all dependent on the same subcontracted data centre, have each logged single minor incidents in the past month — different symptoms, same window, same underlying site. All three services are green; no stakeholder is complaining. Your week is already oversubscribed. What is the most effective response?

- A) Note the observation and revisit next month if the pattern continues; three minor incidents on green services do not justify displacement of committed work.
- B) Raise it now: ask the supplier for the site's incident and maintenance history, alert the three service owners to the potential common dependency, and commission a view of what a full site failure would take down — treating an hour today as insurance against a correlated outage.
- C) Report the pattern to your director and await direction.
- D) Log a risk in the register so the observation is formally recorded.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This scenario tests the exact sentence in your **Ownership and topic** skill: *be proactive in searching for potential problems* — and then acting on what the search finds. The pattern you have spotted is the classic precursor signature of a correlated failure: independent-looking minor incidents sharing a hidden common dependency, each too small to alarm its own service owner, visible only from your cross-portfolio seat. That seat is the point of your role — nobody else sees all three reports — so "someone would have noticed" is false: the someone is you. B's three moves are proportionate, not heroic: an information request, an alert to the owners (who can each check their contingency), and an impact view — perhaps two hours total against the possibility of three services failing together on the day the data centre has its bad day. Option A confuses *green* with *safe*: status dashboards report each service against its own thresholds and are structurally blind to correlation, which is precisely why the pattern reached this month unnoticed. Option C converts your insight into your director's task — escalation without analysis, the junior version of the move. Option D creates a record without creating any change in the world; risk registers are where observations go to be safe from action. The senior pattern: weak signals plus a common-cause hypothesis equals act now, lightly — because the cheap moment to handle a correlated risk is before it correlates.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Most and least effective: the spending review squeeze on user research**

Drafting the directorate's spending review options, the finance lead proposes deleting the user research budget line from year 2 onward: "It's £180k of nice-to-have, and everything else in the options is contractually committed — it's the only discretionary line left." The submission is due in eight days. Your role explicitly includes promoting sustained investment in user-centred approaches. Identify the **most** and **least** effective responses.

1. Accept the deletion — protecting delivery contracts is objectively the priority — and plan to rebuild the research budget in a future year.
2. Escalate immediately to your director, stating that you cannot support a submission that cuts research.
3. Bring evidence to the finance lead within two days: what the research line delivered (rework avoided, adoption gains, the failed-feature spend it prevented), then jointly build an option that scales research to the settlement rather than deleting it — presenting research as risk mitigation on the £Xm of committed delivery, not as a discretionary comfort.
4. Agree to the deletion in the options paper, but plan to fund research informally from other lines if the settlement allows.

- A) Most effective: 3; Least effective: 4
- B) Most effective: 2; Least effective: 1
- C) Most effective: 3; Least effective: 2
- D) Most effective: 1; Least effective: 4

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Response 3 does what "promote sustained investment in user-centred approaches" actually requires under fiscal pressure: it fights with evidence, in the finance lead's own language. The reframe is the decisive move — research is not a discretionary line *beside* the committed delivery, it is risk mitigation *on* the committed delivery: £180k that protects £Xm of contractual spend from building the wrong things is one of the best-leveraged lines in the submission, and the products the finance lead is protecting are precisely what the research de-risks. Scaling options ("full / reduced / paused-with-restart-costs") give the settlement room to move without deleting the capability. And it works the problem *with* the finance lead in the available eight days, preserving that relationship for the next squeeze. Response 4 is least effective because it is the worst of every world: the deletion becomes official (option papers are quoted for years), while the informal workaround — funding a deleted line from other budgets — is exactly the ungoverned behaviour that destroys financial credibility when found, and it will be found; it also teaches your team that the response to losing an argument is quiet non-compliance. Ranking the middle: response 1 is a genuine position honestly held, but it concedes a false dichotomy without testing it — capitulation dressed as realism; response 2 escalates prematurely (no evidence assembled, no joint option attempted) and spends director capital on a fight not yet had at the right level — but at least it is transparent and keeps the research question alive. Hence 3 over 2 over 1 over 4. The senior pattern: defend user-centred investment as delivery insurance, with numbers, at the table where the cut is being drafted — and never sign one thing while planning another.

**Question 5 (moderate) — The two directors and the single truth**

Directors of two units you partner with are in open dispute. Unit A's director claims Unit B's slow data-sharing sign-off is blocking her flagship service; Unit B's director claims Unit A's team submitted an incomplete request and briefed against him before he had seen it. Each has separately asked you to "make clear to the centre" that the other is at fault. Your own review of the correspondence shows both claims are partially true: the request was incomplete, *and* Unit B sat on it for three weeks before saying so. What is the most effective response?

- A) Brief the centre neutrally that "both parties share responsibility" and let governance allocate fault.
- B) Support Unit A's director — a flagship service outweighs process complaints — while privately telling Unit B's director you understand his position.
- C) Get both directors in one room with the timeline: acknowledge each grievance's valid core, get agreement on a completed request and a dated sign-off, and propose fixing the process gap both fell into — a request standard and a response-time expectation — so the dispute cannot recur.
- D) Decline to be involved: inter-unit disputes are for their common director general, not for a relationship manager.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Both directors have asked you to be their advocate; the role requires you to be the *system's* advocate instead — and C is what that looks like operationally. The timeline is your instrument: it lets you acknowledge each valid core (the request *was* incomplete; the three-week silence *was* a failure) without adjudicating a winner, which matters because facilitating discussions across contested ground — a named capability at the lead end of your skill set that this scenario rehearses — depends on both parties trusting your version of the facts. The forward moves are what actually serve the flagship service: a completed request, a dated commitment, and — the senior addition — fixing the *process gap* both fell into, because a dispute this symmetrical is nearly always a missing standard wearing two people's faces. Option A is accurate but inert: "both share responsibility" resolves nothing, schedules nothing, and prevents nothing; it is the report-shaped version of walking away. Option B trades your integrity to the more strategically glamorous party — and the private sympathy call to Unit B is the kind of two-faced move that surfaces, verbatim, at exactly the wrong moment; once discovered, you are no one's trusted broker ever again. Option D forfeits the core of the role: disputes *about digital delivery between units* are not above a senior relationship manager's station — they are the job description in its most concentrated form. The pattern: facts on the table, grievances honoured, commitments dated, system fixed.

**Question 6 (moderate) — The pre-briefed conclusion**

Your director tells you before your shared-needs investigation is complete: "Between us, the DG has already decided the answer is one consolidated platform — shape your findings accordingly. It'll land better for all of us." Your evidence so far genuinely points toward consolidation for three of the five capability areas, but firmly against it for the other two, where unit needs diverge sharply. What is the most effective response?

- A) Shape the findings as requested — three-fifths alignment is close enough, and the DG's instinct is broadly vindicated anyway.
- B) Tell your director the evidence supports consolidation in three areas and opposes it in two; propose presenting it exactly that way — as a strong endorsement of the DG's direction with two evidence-based exceptions that will protect the programme from its most likely failure modes — and offer to brief the DG together.
- C) Complete the report per the evidence and submit it through normal channels without engaging your director's request further.
- D) Ask to be removed from the investigation, since its independence has been compromised.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The pressure is real but the framing is the way through: your evidence is not *against* the DG — it is the difference between the DG's direction succeeding and failing. B refuses the shaping (the findings stay exactly as the evidence has them) while doing the persuasive work your skill set demands: "strong endorsement with two evidence-based exceptions" is the same facts as "three out of five", arranged to be hearable — and it is *true*, which is what distinguishes framing from shaping. The two divergent areas are precisely where a forced consolidation would generate its workarounds, its shadow systems and its eventual public failure; presenting them as protective exceptions serves the DG's actual interest (a programme that works) over the DG's stated preference (a tidy answer). Offering the joint briefing keeps your director inside the solution rather than exposed by your defiance — influence upward, done with the chain rather than around it. Option A participates in laundering evidence — and when the two forced areas fail, the report with your name on it said they would work. Option C is honest but politically self-blinding: the same report, unbriefed and unframed, invites your director to manage *you* as the problem rather than the evidence as the answer. Option D abandons the investigation to someone more compliant — resignation as integrity theatre, with worse organisational outcomes than staying and framing. The senior pattern, worth memorising: when told what the answer must be, return with what the evidence says, packaged so the powerful can adopt it as their own — and never leave your manager to discover your independence in a meeting.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Most and least effective: the struggling team member**

A BRM in your team is struggling: two unit heads have separately mentioned missed follow-ups, and her pipeline records are weeks stale. You also know she flagged, months ago, that her portfolio — five units including the two complainants — is materially heavier than her peers'. Identify the **most** and **least** effective responses.

1. Rebalance her portfolio immediately and say nothing about the performance concerns, since the workload explains them.
2. Talk with her first: share the specific feedback, hear her account, acknowledge the workload flag she raised and act on rebalancing — and agree a short recovery plan for the two strained relationships, with your visible support in both units.
3. Document the missed follow-ups and stale records formally, so that a performance process is ready if patterns continue.
4. Take the two complaining units into your own portfolio temporarily and let her rebuild with the remaining three.

- A) Most effective: 2; Least effective: 3
- B) Most effective: 1; Least effective: 3
- C) Most effective: 2; Least effective: 4
- D) Most effective: 4; Least effective: 1

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Response 2 is the only option that treats both truths as true: the performance shortfall is real (unit heads' trust is genuinely strained; the records genuinely matter — your function's credibility rests on its data), *and* the organisational failure is real (she flagged the overload months ago and nothing happened — some of this sits with you). The conversation sequences correctly: specifics first, her account heard, the system fixed (rebalancing), and then a recovery plan for the relationships with your visible sponsorship — which tells the unit heads the function has responded and tells her she is being invested in, not managed out. Response 3 is least effective because it inverts the entire situation: it prepares a case against a person whose known, reported, unaddressed overload is the most likely cause — building a paper trail *instead of* a conversation is the definitive self-protective move, and if the file ever surfaced alongside her months-old workload flag, it would indict the manager, not the managed. Ranking the middle pair: response 1 fixes the system but withholds the feedback — she deserves to know what the unit heads are saying, both to repair those relationships and because discovering it later ("everyone knew but me") is corrosive; response 4 relieves pressure fast but at real cost — publicly absorbing her hardest units reads as demotion to every observer, teaches her nothing, and leaves the actual portfolio imbalance intact for whoever holds it next. So 2 over 1 over 4 over 3. The leadership pattern: candour plus system-fix plus visible sponsorship — and never let documentation precede conversation when you own part of the cause.

**Question 8 (challenging) — The strategic guidance you disagree with**

The chief digital officer's new operating model, now formally adopted, routes all business-unit demand through product managers, repositioning your BRM function from demand-shapers to "strategic relationship stewards" — a narrower role you argued against during consultation, unsuccessfully. Your team is deflated; two members are quietly job-hunting; a unit head emails: "So who do we actually talk to now — you or the product people? This feels like a mess." What is the most effective response?

- A) Tell your team and the unit head candidly that you opposed the model, and commit to gathering evidence of its failures for the six-month review.
- B) Implement the model faithfully: work with the product leads to define a crisp interface between the roles, give the unit head a one-page "who to contact" answer agreed with the product community, refocus your team on the strategic-stewardship value the model actually assigns them — and channel your reservations into the six-month review with evidence gathered from making it work, not from watching it fail.
- C) Continue operating broadly as before — units will naturally keep coming to the people they trust, and the model will adjust to reality over time.
- D) Escalate the unit head's confusion to the CDO as early evidence that the model is failing and should be paused.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The decision is made, and made legitimately — you were consulted, you argued, you lost. What remains is the difference between a leader and a casualty of change, and every audience in the scenario is watching which you choose. B is loyal implementation *without* the loyalty lie: the crisp interface with product leads answers the actual operational question (ambiguity between two functions is what the unit head is experiencing — the fix is a defined seam, not a restored past); the one-pager answers the stakeholder question in the only currency that matters (clarity); the team refocus is your deepest duty — two members are job-hunting because they cannot see their value in the new model, and showing them the genuine seniority of strategic stewardship (the work this entire guide describes) is retention through meaning, not spin. And the six-month review channel keeps your dissent alive *in its legitimate venue*, armed with the only evidence that carries weight: that of someone who visibly tried. Option A poisons the well twice — a leader telling stakeholders "I opposed this" licenses every unit to relitigate the model, and "gathering evidence of failure" is sabotage with a clipboard. Option C is passive resistance dressed as pragmatism: it sustains exactly the confusion the unit head is complaining about, and positions your function as the obstacle when the model's friction gets diagnosed. Option D spends escalation currency on one confused email a fortnight into a reorganisation — premature by an order of magnitude, and transparently motivated. The senior pattern: disagree, then commit visibly, define the seams, protect your people's sense of value — and keep your critique for the review, funded by good-faith evidence.

**Question 9 (challenging) — The confidence that constrains you**

A unit head you have partnered with for years tells you in confidence: her unit's board has agreed in principle to outsource its entire back-office function within eighteen months — which would remove the business case for the £2m workflow platform investment your function is about to take to committee on her unit's behalf next week. She asks you to keep the outsourcing plan confidential "until the announcement in about two months". The committee papers are already circulated. What is the most effective response?

- A) Respect the confidence fully: let the committee approve the investment, since the outsourcing may yet fall through, and revisit if the announcement happens.
- B) Tell her the two timelines have collided: you cannot let the committee commit £2m to a case you know may be invalid, so together you need a way to pause the item without exposing her plan — such as her unit requesting a two-month deferral for "internal planning alignment" — and if she cannot agree to any pause, you will have to tell her that you cannot stay silent while the committee decides, and work out with her the minimum disclosure that protects both the money and her announcement.
- C) Quietly tip off the committee chair about the outsourcing plan so the item can be pulled without your fingerprints.
- D) Withdraw the item yourself citing "emerging information", refusing to elaborate to anyone.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Confidences are the working capital of relationship management, but they are held *within* your duties, not above them — and letting a committee spend £2m on a case you privately know may be void is a breach of your accountability that no confidence can license. B navigates the collision in the right order: name the conflict to her honestly (she may not have connected the timelines — stakeholders often compartmentalise); construct the protective route together (a unit-requested deferral is unremarkable governance noise that buys her two months and the organisation its £2m — almost always available, almost always sufficient); and only if every pause is refused, state plainly that silence is not among your options, then negotiate the *minimum* disclosure — perhaps a private word with the chair about a material uncertainty, without naming its nature. That sequencing — transparency with her at every step, escalation only past her refusal, disclosure only at minimum viable scope — is what keeps the relationship recoverable even in the worst branch. Option A gambles public money on "may fall through" while wearing her secret as a blindfold; when the announcement lands eight weeks after approval, the committee will ask who knew, and the answer ends your credibility and hers. Option C protects the money by betraying the person — anonymous leakage is discovered more often than its practitioners believe, and unlike B's worst branch, it was never preceded by honesty. Option D protects the secret and the money but detonates your own standing — an unexplained withdrawal of circulated papers converts you into the mystery, and mysteries get investigated. The senior pattern: when a confidence and a duty collide, take the collision *to the confider first*, engineer the route that honours both, and keep every escalation step transparent to the person it affects.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Most and least effective: the maturity theatre**

Your directorate reports relationship maturity annually to the executive committee. This year's self-assessments, collected by your team, average 3.8 — but your own observation says several units inflated their scores: two "level 4" units routinely bypass the front door, and one has not met its relationship manager in a quarter. Your director is delighted with the 3.8 and has already trailed it to the executive committee as evidence the partnering investment is working. Identify the **most** and **least** effective responses.

1. Report the 3.8 as collected — self-assessment is the agreed method, and the method was followed; note privately that next year's process should include evidence checks.
2. Tell your director before the committee meeting that the 3.8 is unsafe: show the behavioural contradictions, propose reporting the self-assessed figure *alongside* behavioural indicators (front-door usage, engagement frequency) with an honest note on the gap, and frame the mixed picture as the maturing of the measurement itself.
3. Quietly re-score the three inflated units downward before submission, so the reported figure reflects reality.
4. Report the 3.8 but add a verbal caveat at the committee if questioned.

- A) Most effective: 2; Least effective: 3
- B) Most effective: 1; Least effective: 3
- C) Most effective: 2; Least effective: 4
- D) Most effective: 4; Least effective: 1

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Response 2 is the only option that treats the real asset here — the executive committee's trust in your function's reporting — as the thing to protect. The behavioural contradictions you hold (level-4 units bypassing the front door, a quarter of silence) are not awkward footnotes; they are evidence that the headline is unsafe, and your director is about to stake credibility on it. Telling them *before* the meeting is the loyalty move; the triangulated reporting design (self-assessment beside behavioural indicators, gap acknowledged) is the analytical move; and the framing — "the measurement is maturing" — is the persuasive move that lets your director present honesty as progress rather than retreat, which happens to be true: a function that can *detect* its own inflated scores is more mature than one reporting a clean 3.8. Response 3 is least effective and it is worth being precise about why, because its motive is honesty: unilaterally rewriting units' self-assessments corrupts the data twice over — the record no longer reflects either what units said *or* any agreed method, the doctoring is indefensible the moment a unit compares its submission with the report, and it destroys the diagnostic value of the inflation itself (units over-scoring their relationship maturity is *information about the relationship*). Ranking the middle: response 1 is procedurally clean dishonesty — "the method was followed" launders a figure you know misleads, though the private process note at least starts the fix; response 4 is ambush-by-integrity — a verbal caveat *if questioned* means your director learns the number is soft in front of the committee, the worst room for that discovery. So 2 over 1 over 4 over 3. The pattern: bad numbers are briefed upward before the meeting, redesigned into honest ones, and never silently corrected — and the gap between claimed and behaved maturity is itself your richest relationship data.

**Question 11 (challenging) — The user harm nobody owns**

Analysis you commissioned shows that citizens who phone one of your organisation's helplines because they cannot complete the digital service are waiting 40 minutes on average — and 60% of them are calling about the same three form-design problems. The digital service is owned by a product team in another directorate; the helpline is outsourced under a contract managed by a third team; both are "green" on their own metrics. You have no authority over either. What is the most effective response?

- A) Publish your analysis to both teams and their directors, and let the owning teams respond as they see fit — your job was the insight, theirs is the action.
- B) Convene the product team, the contract manager and your own function around the analysis: propose the product team prioritise the three form fixes (quantifying the call-demand reduction each would buy), the contract manager add the fix timeline to the next supplier review, and a named owner — you, until a better one exists — track the end-to-end citizen journey that currently no green dashboard sees.
- C) Escalate to the executive committee that two green-rated teams are jointly causing citizen harm, and recommend a formal service review.
- D) Raise the three form problems with the product team informally and monitor whether call volumes improve over the next two quarters.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This scenario concentrates the whole senior role into one move: a harm visible only from the cross-organisational seat, owned by nobody because every local metric is green — the same structural blindness as the earlier measurement questions, now with forty minutes of a citizen's life attached. B is the answer because it *creates* the missing ownership rather than describing its absence: the convening uses your actual authority (relationships and evidence, not hierarchy); the quantified pitch to the product team ("this fix removes roughly N calls a week") converts your analysis into their prioritisation currency — which is how influence works on teams you do not control; the supplier-review route puts the helpline's role on an existing governance track rather than inventing a new one; and the interim end-to-end ownership — *own an issue until a new owner is found*, your skill set's founding sentence, operating at organisational scale — ensures the journey has an accountable name for the first time. Option A is the insight-lobbing model of analysis: technically complete, organisationally inert — publishing to teams whose metrics are green, without convening or quantifying, predictably produces two polite acknowledgements and no fix. Option C spends the organisation's highest-cost attention before the cheap move (a meeting) has been tried, and arrives accusing two teams who have, by their own instruments, done nothing wrong — creating defensiveness where B creates partnership. Option D is directionally right and structurally weak: informal-plus-monitoring has no timeline, no forcing function, and leaves the citizen journey unowned for two more quarters of 40-minute waits. The senior pattern, and a fitting last lesson: where the organisation's structure leaves a gap, the relationship manager's job is to stand in it — with evidence, with the affected parties convened, and with ownership held until the system grows an owner.

### Preparation tips

- **Study your own role summary before any SJT.** The scoring key for a senior relationship role is effectively a paraphrase of your skill definitions: accountability, proactive problem-searching, persuasive-but-unbent recommendations, user advocacy, strategic framing. Re-read them; then recognise them in the options.
- **Apply the two-audience test.** For each option ask: what does this do to the immediate problem, and what does it teach everyone watching? Senior keys weight the second question heavily — precedent, culture and trust are the real stakes in most scenarios.
- **Practise disagree-then-commit and dissent-with-alternative.** The hardest senior scenarios involve legitimate authority deciding against you, or pressuring your findings. The rewarded pattern is remarkably stable: keep the evidence intact, frame it hearably, brief before meetings, dissent in the right venue, implement faithfully.
- **Locate the least effective option by its motive.** Self-protection (documentation before conversation), secrecy (tip-offs, quiet re-scoring), and passivity (monitoring, noting, awaiting direction) mark the floor of most senior scenarios. Find the floor first; it clarifies the ceiling.
- **Rehearse the collision cases.** Confidence versus duty, relationship versus finding, loyalty versus honesty: write down, from your own career, one real example of each and what you did. The reflection is preparation for the test and supervision for the professional.
- **Answer as your best self on a normal Tuesday.** Not the hero of an imagined crisis, not the exhausted realist of a bad quarter. Keys are calibrated to sustainable, repeatable, senior professionalism.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Advocating when the role requires brokering.** When two stakeholders each demand your allegiance, options that pick a side — or worse, sympathise privately with both — score at the bottom. The rewarded position is the system's advocate: facts tabled, grievances honoured, process fixed.
- **Confusing implementation of a decision you opposed with endorsement of it.** Passive resistance, public dissent, and evidence-gathering-for-failure all score poorly; so does silent capitulation. The key rewards visible commitment plus legitimate-channel critique — both, not either.
- **Protecting numbers instead of trust.** Reporting a figure you know misleads, caveating only if asked, or silently correcting others' data all sacrifice the long asset (credibility of your function's reporting) for the short one (a good meeting). Brief the truth upward early, with a design for reporting it well.
- **Escalating before convening.** Executive attention is the most expensive resource in the scenarios; options that spend it before trying the meeting you could call yourself read as either naivety or theatre. Escalate with evidence, after the cheap move, with your recommendation attached.
- **Letting confidences outrank duties — or breaking them carelessly.** The rewarded pattern in collision cases is always sequenced: honesty with the confider first, engineered routes that honour both obligations, minimum disclosure only past refusal, transparency at every step. Both naive secrecy and casual disclosure score at the floor.
- **Fixing the person when the system is the cause — or vice versa.** Struggling team members with unaddressed workload flags, units inflating scores an incentive invited, teams green on metrics that miss the harm: senior scenarios almost always contain a system defect behind the human one. The strongest options fix both; the weakest punish one while preserving the other.
