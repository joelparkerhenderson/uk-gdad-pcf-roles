## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation and applied problem solving through operational scenarios rather than abstract items. At head level, the scenarios shift altitude: less "diagnose this incident" and more "diagnose this portfolio" — patterns across services, quarters and teams; deductions through policy, contract and architecture simultaneously; error checking of the analyses and assurances others have already signed; prioritisation of organisational attention rather than personal task lists; and problem solving where the constraints are budget, capability and risk appetite as much as dependency and time.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 15 to 30 minutes, adaptive difficulty, with accuracy and speed reported separately against senior norm groups. Expect multi-source questions: a table, a policy extract and a claim that must be reconciled; a pattern whose significance depends on what it implies for strategy rather than for tonight's shift.

Employers use cognitive assessment at this level because the role concentrates *judgement about judgement*. You rarely execute the analysis; you decide whether analyses are sound, whether patterns warrant investment, whether assurances deserve belief, and where the organisation's attention should go. Errors at that layer are expensive precisely because they are wholesale: a head who misreads one capacity trend misallocates a budget line; a head who accepts one flawed assurance model inherits every incident it fails to prevent. The practice below trains the specific reasoning acts your signature commits the organisation to.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **Service focus** and your responsibility for the **strategy roadmap**: seeing the bigger picture across groups of services now includes seeing it across time — which recurring signatures, tooling gaps and demand shifts should shape next year's roadmap rather than tonight's response.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Change management** and **Asset and configuration management**: managing high-impact, complex change and managing service compliance and risk both require deducing consequences through layered rules — policy, contract, architecture — without letting any layer slip.
- **Error checking** maps to **Problem management** and **Continuity management**: ensuring the right actions are taken to investigate, resolve and anticipate problems means auditing the causal reasoning in what you sign; managing and testing runbooks at head level means auditing the assurance, not performing the test.
- **Prioritisation** maps to **Ownership and initiative** and your duty to **address issues across IT operations**: accountability at head level is the allocation of organisational attention — yours, your managers', your budget's — to the risks only your level can retire.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Availability and capacity management** and your duty to ensure **tools are fit for purpose**: identifying capacity issues, implementing the required changes and initiating remedial action are multi-constraint problems where the constraints include procurement, capability and integration, not just arithmetic.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern across a year of majors**

Your annual review tabulates 16 major incidents: 6 traced to a single legacy integration layer; 4 to capacity exhaustion in shared components, each preceded by an unactioned trend warning; 3 to failed high-impact changes; 3 with unique causes. Which reading best serves the strategy roadmap?

- A) Sixteen majors is the headline; target a 25% reduction across the board.
- B) Two structural concentrations dominate: the legacy integration layer (6) is an architectural liability for the roadmap, and the four capacity majors reveal a process gap — warnings are generated but not actioned — which is cheaper to fix than the architecture and should move first.
- C) The three failed changes prove change governance is the priority.
- D) The unique causes show incidents are random; no pattern exists.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Head-level pattern reading means resolving a distribution into differently-shaped problems with differently-shaped fixes. The integration layer's 6 is an *asset* problem: it belongs on the roadmap as remediation or replacement, with investment and time. The capacity cluster's 4 is subtler and more valuable: warnings existed and were unactioned, so the defect is the trend-to-action pathway — a process and ownership fix, achievable in weeks, that converts existing monitoring into prevented incidents. Sequencing the cheap process fix ahead of the expensive architectural one is portfolio logic, and B states both the diagnosis and the sequence. Option A treats the total as the object — across-the-board targets spread attention evenly over concentrated causes, the opposite of what the data supports. Option C elevates the smallest cluster; 3 of 16 merits review, not priority. Option D misreads the residual: three unique causes alongside two concentrations is a normal distribution of operational failure, and the concentrations are the signal. This is your service focus and roadmap duty in one exercise: annual incident data is strategy input, and the skill is reading it as a portfolio of causes, not a count.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction through policy and contract together**

Your release policy: "High-impact changes to citizen-facing services require a tested rollback and CAB approval." Your supplier contract for the managed hosting platform: "Supplier-initiated platform maintenance is not subject to customer change approval, but the supplier must give 10 working days' notice of maintenance affecting service availability." The supplier emails: "Platform upgrade in 12 days; brief availability impact expected on hosted citizen services; as per contract, proceeding under our maintenance provisions." Which statement must be true?

- A) The upgrade requires your CAB's approval because citizen-facing services are affected.
- B) The supplier has breached the contract's notice requirement.
- C) Your change policy does not bind the supplier's maintenance, and the contractual notice condition is met (12 days ≥ 10 working days is not yet established — 12 calendar days may be fewer than 10 working days, so notice compliance actually cannot be confirmed without a calendar).
- D) The supplier's maintenance is exempt from your CAB, and whether the 10-working-day notice is satisfied depends on whether the 12 days contain at least 10 working days — which the email alone does not establish.

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Two instruments govern, and each must be applied to its own subject. Your release policy binds *your* changes; the contract explicitly carves supplier maintenance out of customer approval — so option A fails: the CAB has no purchase, however citizen-facing the impact. The notice question turns on a unit trap: the contract counts *working* days, the email counts days. Twelve calendar days spanning two weekends contains only 8 working days; twelve working days would comfortably satisfy. The email does not say which, so breach can neither be affirmed (option B) nor denied — option D holds exactly this: exemption established, notice undetermined pending a calendar check. Option C reasons correctly and then contradicts its own parenthesis — an option whose conclusion and reasoning diverge is wrong even when the reasoning is right, and spotting that is itself an error-checking exercise. The head-level habit this trains: contracts and policies use defined units (working days, continuous minutes, quarters), and compliance verdicts issued before the units are checked are guesses. Your service compliance and risk duty lives in precisely these definitional seams — as does the practical action: reply seeking the maintenance date, then count.

**Question 3 (easy) — Error checking a compliance dashboard**

Your quarterly compliance dashboard reports: "Runbook currency: 94% (47 of 50 runbooks reviewed within 12 months). Continuity tests: 100% of scheduled tests completed. Configuration accuracy: 98.2% (sample audit)." A footnote reads: "Continuity test schedule revised in Q2; tests for 8 services deferred to next year as their runbooks were under review." What is the most important observation?

- A) The dashboard shows strong compliance across all three measures.
- B) The 100% continuity figure is true of the *revised* schedule but conceals that 8 services went untested this year — the denominator was edited to fit the numerator, and those 8 services' continuity assurance is a year staler than the dashboard's headline implies.
- C) The 94% runbook currency is the weakest figure and should be the priority.
- D) The sample-based configuration figure is meaningless without the sample size.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The dashboard's most dangerous number is its best one. "100% of scheduled tests completed" is literally true and materially misleading: the schedule itself was revised mid-year to remove 8 services, so the metric measures conformance to a shrunk plan, not assurance of the estate. Denominator management — editing the base until the percentage pleases — is the signature pathology of compliance reporting, and the footnote is where it always lives. Option B also names the real-world consequence: 8 services carrying stale continuity assurance, invisible at headline level. Option C picks the number that *looks* weakest, which is the trap's mirror image: 94% with an honest denominator is better information than 100% with a managed one. Option D raises a fair methodological point that is second-order here — a sample audit with undisclosed size is a quality issue; a full metric with an edited base is an integrity issue. Option A is the signature the pathology needs. Your asset and configuration skill at head level — manage service compliance and risk — is largely the discipline of reading dashboards as arguments rather than facts: every percentage has a denominator, every denominator has a history, and the footnotes are load-bearing.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Prioritisation of the head's attention**

Monday, 08:30. Four items in your queue: (i) final sign-off on the monitoring tool procurement — the business case is solid, your operational control managers are unanimous, the deadline is Friday; (ii) a request from your director to "have a look at" a peer division's incident data as a favour, no deadline; (iii) the third consecutive monthly rise in a junior team's attrition, now visible in the workforce report, cause unknown; (iv) a vendor briefing on an AI operations product your roadmap tentatively references for next year. Where does your personal attention go first, and why?

- A) Item (i) — the deadline is nearest and the sum is largest.
- B) Item (iii) — it is the only item where something is genuinely unknown, worsening, and unowned; the procurement is decision-ready and can be signed after, the favour and the briefing can be scheduled.
- C) Item (ii) — director requests take precedence.
- D) Item (iv) — strategy must lead operations.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Distinguish decision-ready from investigation-needed. Item (i) is large but *finished thinking*: unanimous managers, solid case, Friday deadline — it needs your signature and perhaps an hour's final scrutiny, schedulable with certainty. Items (ii) and (iv) are elastic: schedulable favours and briefings. Item (iii) is the discriminating case: a worsening trend, cause unknown, and — the head-level tell — *unowned*: attrition in a junior team shows up in no one else's objectives until it becomes a capability crisis, and building the capability of the team is your named responsibility. Three consecutive rises is a pattern, not noise; the cause (a manager problem? workload? pay banding? a competitor hiring?) determines the remedy, and every month of delay compounds the loss — experienced analysts leave, then the experience gap drives more leaving. First attention goes to commissioning the inquiry: a conversation with the team's manager, exit interview data, a skip-level or two — an hour that starts the diagnosis. Option A confuses size with need; decision-ready items don't improve with earlier attention. Option C converts courtesy into priority. Option D mistakes a vendor's calendar for strategy. The principle, identical in shape to escalation-level triage but at organisational altitude: your attention is the scarcest resource in the function, and it belongs to what is worsening, unknown and unowned — not to what is large, loud or senior.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Integration logic in tooling strategy**

Your roadmap must consolidate monitoring: today, four tools cover infrastructure, application performance, user-experience and security monitoring, each with its own alerting; correlation across them is manual, and post-incident reviews repeatedly cite "correlation delay" as a resolution-time driver. Three consolidation options are costed: (1) replace all four with a single-vendor platform — highest cost, longest migration, one alerting model; (2) keep all four, build an event-correlation layer that ingests from each — lowest cost, fastest, adds a fifth component to operate; (3) consolidate infrastructure and application tools into one platform now (the two whose correlation gap drives most cited delay), defer user-experience and security consolidation, revisit in 18 months. Post-incident data shows 80% of correlation-delay citations involve exactly the infrastructure-application boundary. Which option does the evidence best support?

- A) Option 1 — a single pane of glass is the recognised end-state.
- B) Option 2 — lowest cost and fastest is the fiduciary choice.
- C) Option 3 — it applies 20%-effort-to-80%-cited-problem, tests consolidation on the highest-value boundary before committing to the full end-state, and keeps the security tool's specialised requirements out of a general-purpose migration.
- D) None — more analysis is needed before any choice.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Anchor on the evidence: 80% of the cited correlation delay lives at one boundary. Option 3 spends effort exactly there, and its deferred remainder is not indecision but sequenced learning — the 18-month revisit happens with real data about whether consolidation delivered the resolution-time gains the reviews predict. It also respects a domain fact option 1 flattens: security monitoring has requirements (retention, access separation, audit) that general-purpose platforms satisfy poorly, and forcing it into a single-vendor migration imports risk for the 20% of the problem it barely touches. Option 1's "recognised end-state" is architecture by brochure — end-states are hypotheses until your estate's data supports them. Option 2's correlation layer answers the cost question and dodges the operational one: a fifth component, operated by your team, whose failure modes sit precisely at the correlation point the incidents already cite — complexity added at the exact spot complexity is the problem. Option D fails the evidence already in hand; "more analysis" is the head-level procrastination that dresses as rigour. This is your tools-fit-for-purpose duty exercised as reasoning: fitness is demonstrated against *your* incident data and *your* requirements, sequenced to learn before it commits — and the 80/20 structure of the answer is worth keeping as a template for every integration decision the roadmap contains.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Error checking an availability improvement claim**

A paper for your sign-off claims: "Since the resilience programme began in January, major incidents have fallen from 11 (previous year) to 6 (this year to October) — a 45% reduction demonstrating programme effectiveness. Recommend extending the programme with £250k next year." Independently, you know: this year's figure covers 10 months; two services were migrated out of your estate in March; and the previous year included a single cluster of 4 majors from one firmware defect since fixed by the vendor. What is the soundest assessment?

- A) The reduction is real and the extension justified as argued.
- B) Annualise first: 6 in 10 months projects to ~7.2 in 12 — against 11, a ~35% fall, not 45%. Then adjust the baseline: 4 of the prior year's 11 came from one vendor-fixed defect (leaving ~7 comparable), and the estate shrank by two services in March. The like-for-like comparison is roughly 7 against 7 — the data does not yet demonstrate programme effect, which neither proves nor disproves the programme; the paper must be sent back for an attribution analysis before £250k rides on it.
- C) The programme has failed and should be cancelled.
- D) Incident counts are too noisy to analyse; decide on judgement alone.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Run the three corrections in order. Periods: 6 incidents in 10 months is a 7.2-incident annual pace; comparing 10 months against 12 inflates any reduction — the claimed 45% becomes ~35% before anything else is examined. Baseline composition: a 4-incident cluster from a single, externally-fixed cause is not part of the recurring baseline the programme could have affected; the comparable prior-year figure is nearer 7. Denominator: two services left the estate in March, so even a genuine fall partly reflects a smaller estate. After corrections, the like-for-like picture is approximately flat — which, as B states carefully, does not show the programme failed (option C overshoots symmetrically: perhaps underlying risk rose and the programme held the line; perhaps its benefits lag) but does show the paper's *evidence does not carry its claim*, and £250k should wait for an attribution analysis: which incident classes was the programme designed to prevent, and what happened to those specifically? Option D abandons analysis because the first analysis was flawed — the corrections themselves prove the data is analysable. The head-level point is about your signature: the paper's arithmetic errors each flatter the programme, which is the direction motivated arithmetic always leans; the discipline of annualise-adjust-attribute before funding is how ensuring "the right actions are taken" — your problem management duty's phrasing — applies to programmes and not just incidents.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Deduction about capability coverage**

Your on-call rota requires two qualified incident leads available at all times. Qualification requires completing your incident leadership programme (6 months) plus shadowing (3 months). Today you have 5 qualified leads. Known futures: one retires in 4 months; one starts maternity leave in 6 months (12 months away); promotion of one into a day-role that removes rota eligibility is agreed for month 8. Two candidates entered the programme last month. When does coverage first become impossible to guarantee, and what does that imply now?

- A) Month 8 — when the third lead leaves the rota, 2 remain, exactly meeting the requirement with zero resilience; but "two available at all times" across leave and sickness cannot be guaranteed with only 2 qualified people, so the genuine exposure begins at month 6 when the pool drops to 3.
- B) Month 12 — when the maternity leave begins.
- C) Never — the two candidates qualify in time.
- D) Month 4 — the retirement immediately breaks the requirement.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Track the pool arithmetic against the requirement's real meaning. Pool: 5 now; 4 from month 4 (retirement); 3 from month 6 (maternity — note it starts at month 6 per the stated timeline: "in 6 months"); 2 from month 8 (promotion). The candidates entered the programme last month: 6 months' programme + 3 months' shadowing completes around month 8–9 — arriving at or after the crunch, not before (option C's comfort requires ignoring the shadowing). Now interpret the requirement: "two available at all times" is not "two employed" — with a pool of exactly 2, any leave, sickness or training day breaks guarantee; sustained compliance needs a pool of 4–5 given normal absence rates. So the hard breach risk is month 8, but the *planning* exposure begins at month 6 with a pool of 3 — and option A holds both layers. Option D misreads 4 remaining as breach; option B misorders the timeline. The implications now, month 0: accelerate the two candidates if quality permits (shadowing overlapping the programme's tail?), recruit or borrow a qualified lead, negotiate the promotion's rota-exit date, and — the durable fix — treat qualified-lead coverage as a monitored capacity metric with the same projection discipline you apply to storage. Building the capability of the team, your role summary's phrase, is exactly this: capability is capacity with a nine-month lead time, and heads who model it arithmetically stop discovering rota crises four weeks out.

**Question 8 (moderate) — The anomaly in a familiar report**

Your monthly service report has shown user-experience availability within 0.2 points of infrastructure availability for eighteen consecutive months. This month: infrastructure 99.7%, user-experience 97.1% — a 2.6-point gap — while incident counts, change volumes and desk contacts are all normal. The report's commentary does not mention the gap. What is the best first move?

- A) Accept the report; all the operational indicators are normal.
- B) Treat the divergence of two normally-coupled metrics as the month's most important finding despite — indeed because of — the normal indicators everywhere else: commission a specific investigation (measurement change? new user path outside infrastructure monitoring? synthetic-check blind spot? user-experience tool defect?) and ask why the commentary process failed to surface a 13-fold departure from the historical gap.
- C) Ask for the user-experience figure to be re-measured; it is probably wrong.
- D) Report the infrastructure figure upward this month since the two usually agree.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Eighteen months of tight coupling makes the *relationship* between these metrics an instrument in its own right — and this month the instrument moved while every conventional indicator stayed still. That combination is the interesting case: if incidents, changes and contacts are normal but users experienced 2.9% unavailability (versus infrastructure's 0.3%), then either something real is harming users invisibly to your operational telemetry (a new failure mode below alerting thresholds; a path — CDN, third-party dependency, client-side — outside infrastructure monitoring) or one measurement system has silently changed or broken. Every branch matters: two are estate problems, one is an instrumentation problem, and all three are invisible to a process that only investigates when operational indicators move. Option C picks one branch by assumption — "probably wrong" is a hypothesis, not a finding, and re-measurement without investigation would bury the estate branches. Option D is the disqualifying move: knowingly reporting the flattering metric because the honest one is inconvenient converts an anomaly into a concealment. Option A outsources attention to indicators the anomaly has just demonstrated are insufficient. B's second limb is equally head-level: the commentary's silence on a 13-fold gap departure is a *reporting process* defect — someone compiled that page without the relationship check — and fixing the process (automated divergence flags, commentary standards) is what prevents next month's silent anomaly. Your user focus skill at this level — insight into how user needs and experience move over time — depends on exactly these instruments staying trustworthy; the head's job is noticing when they stop.

**Question 9 (hard) — Multi-constraint remediation sequencing**

Audit has issued three findings with deadlines: (F1) legacy integration layer lacks failover — remediate within 12 months (est. 8 months' work, £400k); (F2) monitoring gaps on two citizen services — remediate within 6 months (est. 3 months, £80k); (F3) continuity tests outstanding for 8 services (from Question 3) — complete within 9 months (est. 4 months elapsed, mostly coordination). Constraints: the same two senior engineers are critical-path for F1 and F2; continuity tests (F3) require change-freeze windows that conflict with F1's migration phases; budget releases quarterly at £150k. Which plan satisfies all deadlines?

- A) Sequence F1 first (months 1–8), F2 next (9–11), F3 last (12–15) — biggest risk first.
- B) F2 first (months 1–3, engineers dedicated, £80k inside Q1's release), F3 in parallel from month 1 (coordination-led, using pre-migration freeze windows, completing ~month 5), then F1 from month 4 with both engineers freed (months 4–11, £400k spread across remaining quarterly releases) — F2 done month 3 (deadline 6), F3 month 5 (deadline 9), F1 month 11 (deadline 12).
- C) F1 and F2 in parallel from month 1, sharing the engineers; F3 from month 6.
- D) Request deadline extensions for all three before planning.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Test each plan against deadlines, resources and windows. Option A sequences by risk size and breaches immediately: F2's 6-month deadline dies at month 9, and F3's tests collide with F1's migration freezes throughout. Option C's parallelism is fictional — two critical-path engineers shared across F1 and F2 halves both, pushing F2 toward month 6 (knife-edge) and F1 toward month 16 (breach); resource-constrained parallelism is sequential work with coordination overhead added. Option D concedes before analysing — and B proves the concession unnecessary. Walk B: F2's short, cheap, deadline-tight work takes the engineers first and lands month 3, comfortably inside its 6-month limit and Q1's £150k release covers its £80k. F3 is coordination-led — it doesn't need the engineers — and running it *before* F1's migration begins converts the freeze-window conflict into a scheduling convenience: pre-migration months are freeze-rich. F1 then gets both engineers from month 4, an 8-month run landing month 11 against a 12-month deadline, with £400k drawn across Q2–Q4 releases (£150k × 3 = £450k available). Every constraint audits clean, with a month of margin on the binding deadline. The construction heuristics are the transferable content: schedule the deadline-tightest short work first when it shares scarce resources with long work; route non-competing workstreams around the resource constraint entirely; and treat sequencing conflicts (freezes) as potentially *dissolvable by ordering* rather than fixed. This is availability-and-capacity remedial action as your summary means it — identify, implement, initiate — expressed as a feasibility proof you can defend to audit committee line by line.

**Question 10 (hard) — Judging a problem investigation portfolio**

Reviewing your problem management function's quarterly output: 22 problem records closed. You sample the closures: 9 closed with root cause identified and preventative action completed; 6 closed as "no longer reproducible" after low investigation effort; 4 closed with workarounds documented but root cause "not economically justifiable to pursue"; 3 closed with root cause found and preventative actions raised but transferred to delivery teams' backlogs, status unknown. Which assessment best serves the function?

- A) A 100% closure achievement — the function is performing.
- B) Four distinct closure qualities are being counted as one outcome: the 9 are genuine; the 6 "not reproducible" closures at low effort are a recurrence pool (intermittent problems close themselves and return); the 4 economic judgements may each be sound but need a visible risk-acceptance record with an owner, not a closure; and the 3 with actions lost into unowned backlogs are prevention promised but not delivered. The function needs closure categories with different exit criteria, recurrence tracking against "not reproducible" closures, and a followed-through path for transferred actions.
- C) The 6 "not reproducible" closures should be prohibited; problems must stay open until reproduced.
- D) The sample is too small to conclude anything about the function.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** "Closed" is doing four different jobs in this portfolio, and head-level review means separating them because each has a different failure mode. The 9 full closures are the function working. The 6 "not reproducible" closures are individually reasonable and collectively a signature: intermittent problems — the hardest and often most important class — present exactly this way, and a function under closure-count pressure learns to let them close; the control is not prohibition (option C would freeze the register with unfalsifiable open records) but *recurrence tracking*: tag such closures, and when the same signature returns, it reopens with its history attached rather than starting as a new record. The 4 economic closures contain a category error your compliance-and-risk duty should catch: "root cause not worth pursuing" is a *risk acceptance*, and risk acceptances need owners, review dates and visibility in the risk register — filed as mere closures, they become permanent invisible acceptances no one ever re-examines as the cost-benefit shifts. The 3 transferred-action closures are the quiet defect with the largest prevention leak: the problem function's output was handed to backlogs nobody audits, so "preventative measures determined" became "preventative measures deferred indefinitely" — the fix is a tracked handover with status visibility, which is precisely your summary's "co-ordinate the team to investigate problems, implement solutions and take preventive measures" applied across team boundaries. Option A reads the count; option D hides behind sample size when the finding is structural (categories and their exit criteria), not statistical. The instrument to leave with: closure quality categories, each with its own exit criterion and follow-up — because what gets counted as done defines what the function actually does.

**Question 11 (hard) — Strategic deduction from user-channel data**

Five-year channel data for a citizen service your centre supports: phone contact share fallen 60%→35%; digital share risen 30%→58%; assisted-digital stable at ~7%. Overnight digital usage (22:00–06:00) has tripled and now carries 18% of digital transactions. Your operational posture is unchanged from five years ago: full support 08:00–18:00, skeleton overnight cover, maintenance windows concentrated 01:00–04:00. Deduce the strategic misalignments and the appropriate roadmap response.

- A) None — the service meets its availability targets on current measures.
- B) Three misalignments: support weighting still mirrors the old channel mix (phone-heavy day support for a now digital-majority service); maintenance windows sit inside a window now carrying material and growing usage — the "quiet hours" assumption has expired; and the availability measures themselves, set in the phone era, likely under-weight overnight digital harm. Roadmap response: rebalance support capability toward digital-incident response, re-derive maintenance windows from current usage-trough data with a periodic review trigger, and re-specify availability measures to weight when users actually transact — while protecting the stable assisted-digital minority in any change.
- C) Close the phone channel to fund digital support, since the trend is clear.
- D) Move maintenance to daytime, when staff are plentiful.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The deduction runs from data to expired assumptions to roadmap items. Assumption one: support posture was designed when contact was phone-heavy and daytime; at 58% digital, incident response capability (who is skilled, when they are rostered) should track where failures now harm users — a capability rebalance, not just a rota change. Assumption two: the 01:00–04:00 maintenance concentration was rational when overnight was dead; overnight digital has *tripled* to 18% of transactions, so the windows now sit on live usage — and the correct fix is not a one-off move but *re-derivation from current trough data plus a review trigger*, because the pattern that expired once will expire again (this is the same "temporal separation is capacity you must not spend by accident" logic as shared-peak analysis, applied to time-of-day). Assumption three is the subtle one: measures themselves age — availability targets weighted for phone-era usage can be met while overnight digital users absorb unmeasured harm, which is why option A's "meets its targets" is not reassurance but the symptom (compare the dashboard question: the instrument, not just the estate, needs auditing). The assisted-digital caveat is user focus at head level, verbatim from your summary — "meeting the needs of users across a variety of channels" and "how user needs have changed over time": a stable 7% minority through a period of channel upheaval indicates a population that *cannot* shift, and strategies that fund the trend by cutting the tail (option C) convert channel evolution into exclusion of precisely the users public services exist to reach last. Option D solves staffing by scheduling outages into peak usage — posture serving the organisation instead of the user. B is the full strategic read: user data ages operational assumptions silently; the roadmap's job is to re-derive them on evidence, on a cycle, with the vulnerable minority protected by design.

**Question 12 (hard) — The assurance chain under load**

A ministerial priority service launches in 6 weeks and will route through your command and control centre. Assurances you currently hold: the delivery programme reports "monitoring integration complete" (their own test); your tooling team reports the integration "receiving events but not yet validated against failure scenarios"; the continuity runbook exists but is untested ("no test window before launch," says the programme); and your on-call rota for the launch period shows the qualified-lead thinning from Question 7 already beginning. The programme director asks you to confirm to the senior responsible owner that "command and control is ready". What does sound reasoning require your confirmation to contain?

- A) Confirm readiness — the components exist, and the programme's own test covers the integration.
- B) Decline to confirm anything until every element is fully tested, whatever the launch consequence.
- C) A differentiated assurance: state what is verified (events flowing), what is claimed but unverified by you (integration under failure scenarios — with a proposal to run scenario validation in weeks 1–2), what is untested with a mitigation (runbook — desk walkthrough plus an on-call specialist retainer for the launch month, and a test scheduled at the first post-launch window), and what you are resourcing to guarantee (named incident leads for the launch period, borrowed if needed) — so the SRO receives a readiness statement whose every clause is either evidenced or accompanied by its compensating control and residual risk.
- D) Confirm readiness verbally but keep an internal note of the gaps in case of later challenge.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The question is what "ready" may honestly mean over your signature, and C answers with the instrument senior assurance actually requires: a differentiated statement in which every clause carries its evidence status. Walk its anatomy, because the anatomy is the lesson. *Verified*: events flowing — your team's own observation; assert it plainly. *Claimed but unverified*: the programme's "integration complete" rests on their test, and your tooling team — your evidence — says failure scenarios are unvalidated; the difference between "they tested it" and "we verified it" is the entire content of an assurance function (a head who countersigns others' self-assessments adds no assurance, only a signature), and C converts the gap into a two-week validation plan rather than a stand-off. *Untested with compensating control*: the runbook cannot be environment-tested before launch — a fact, not a failing — so the honest move is naming the mitigation (walkthrough, specialist retainer, scheduled post-launch test) and the residual risk it leaves; this is your continuity duty operating under constraint, which is where it mostly operates. *Resourced commitment*: the rota thinning is yours to fix and yours to guarantee — named leads, borrowed if necessary — because launch-period incident leadership is the one element no compensating control substitutes for. Option A signs the programme's homework. Option B is the assurance function's other failure mode: binary readiness standards that no real launch meets convert your confirmation into either an obstacle or, under pressure, a rubber stamp — differentiated assurance exists precisely so that neither happens. Option D is the integrity trap in senior clothing: confirming publicly while filing private reservations is concealment with an audit trail — worse than concealment, because the note proves you knew. The transferable template for every "confirm readiness" moment your role contains: verified / claimed-unverified with a plan / untested with mitigation and residual / committed-and-resourced. An SRO served that statement can make a launch decision; a minister's office served option A's version discovers the difference during the launch.

### Preparation tips

- **Practise on your own governance artefacts.** Last quarter's compliance dashboard, one audit response, one funding paper: re-read each hunting denominators, baselines and evidence-status per clause. Your estate's documents are the highest-fidelity question bank available.
- **Build the annualise-adjust-attribute reflex.** Every improvement claim crossing your desk gets the three corrections — period, baseline composition, denominator — before its conclusion gets your belief. Two minutes, most errors caught.
- **Model your capability pipeline numerically.** Qualified-role coverage against known departures and training lead times, projected eight quarters out. It converts workforce surprises into scheduled decisions, and the arithmetic is exactly what assessments test.
- **Rehearse differentiated assurance.** For the next readiness or compliance statement you sign, draft it in the four-clause form: verified, claimed-unverified-with-plan, untested-with-mitigation, committed. The format survives timed conditions once it is habitual.
- **Sequence one multi-constraint plan on paper monthly.** Deadlines, shared resources, windows, budget phasing — prove feasibility line by line. Audit responses and roadmap phasing both improve, and so does your speed on the hardest question class.
- **Time-box, then review your errors by type.** Under the clock, note which questions you missed and whether the cause was reading, arithmetic, or premature closure. At senior level the distribution is diagnostic: most heads miss by closing early, not by calculating wrongly.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Signing the best-looking number.** The 100% with an edited denominator is worse information than the 94% with an honest one. Read footnotes as the main text.
- **Confusing size with need for attention.** Decision-ready items, however large, need signatures; worsening-unknown-unowned items need you. Allocate accordingly, on tests and Mondays alike.
- **Architecture by end-state.** "Single pane of glass" and its cousins are hypotheses. Let your incident data, not the brochure, sequence the roadmap.
- **Accepting self-assessment as assurance.** "The programme tested it" and "we verified it" are different clauses. Differentiate or your signature launders the difference.
- **Treating expired assumptions as facts.** Quiet hours, channel mixes, phone-era measures: usage data ages every operational assumption silently. Re-derive on a cycle.
- **Closure counts as outcomes.** What gets counted as done defines what your functions actually do. Audit the categories behind every count before the count reassures you.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures fluency and rigour with the quantitative material a head of function is accountable for: budgets and their phasing, availability and risk economics, capacity investment cases, workforce and capability arithmetic, supplier commercials, and the KPI structures by which your function is judged and judges others. The format is standard — 20 to 30 minutes online, 15 to 20 questions, calculator permitted, norm-referenced against senior groups — but the content at this level leans hard toward *interrogation*: not "compute the figure" so much as "decide whether the computed figure means what the paper claims it means".

For your role the relevance is structural. You hold budget authority: every business case that reaches you argues in numbers, and approving arithmetic you haven't stress-tested is delegating your authority to whoever built the spreadsheet. You manage service compliance and risk, which is increasingly quantified — risk scores, compliance percentages, error budgets — and each quantification embeds choices (denominators, weightings, exclusions) that your review either audits or inherits. You identify capacity issues and initiate remedial action, which means adjudicating between competing projections. And you report upward: the figures your function sends to directors and boards carry your implicit warranty, so the habits tested here — decomposition, sensitivity, attribution, honest aggregation — are the quality control on that warranty.

Every explanation shows its arithmetic step by step, and — deliberately, at this level — several questions turn on the judgement *around* the arithmetic: which baseline, which denominator, which comparison is honest. That is the examinable skill your signature exercises daily.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Budget and investment arithmetic** maps to your responsibility for the **strategy roadmap** and **tools fit for purpose**: phased spend, cost-per-outcome comparisons, and total-cost-of-ownership reasoning are how roadmap choices are actually made and defended.
- **Risk and availability economics** map to **Asset and configuration management** (service compliance and risk) and **Availability and capacity management**: expected-value reasoning over incidents, error budgets and remediation options is the numerate core of risk acceptance and remedial action.
- **Workforce and capability arithmetic** maps to **building the capability of the team**: pipeline projections, coverage modelling, attrition costs and training lead times — capacity planning where the capacity is people.
- **Supplier and contract figures** map to **Change management** and **Continuity management** at the governance level: SLA verification, penalty and exclusion arithmetic, and the commercial numbers behind release and continuity commitments.
- **KPI structure and aggregation** map to **Service focus**, **User focus** and **Problem management**: choosing measures that reflect user-experienced reality across services and channels, and auditing the counts by which functions demonstrate their own performance.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Budget phasing check**

Your roadmap's tooling consolidation is costed at £520k: £190k in Q1, £150k in Q2, £110k in Q3, £70k in Q4. Finance releases £140k per quarter, with unspent allocation carrying forward. In which quarter, if any, does the plan first exceed available funds?

- A) Q1
- B) Q2
- C) Q3
- D) It never exceeds available funds

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Track cumulative availability against cumulative need. Q1: available £140k, needed £190k — short by £50k immediately. The carry-forward mechanism cannot help in Q1: there is nothing yet to carry. Check the later picture for completeness: by Q2, cumulative release is £280k against cumulative need £340k (still short £60k); by Q3, £420k against £450k (short £30k); by Q4, £560k against £520k — the year funds the plan in total, but the *profile* fails from the first quarter. This is the most common defect in roadmap financials: annual affordability presented as if it were quarterly feasibility. The remedies are standard — rephase the work, negotiate an advanced release, or re-sequence deliverables into the funding envelope — but they only get designed if the head reading the paper runs cumulative-versus-cumulative rather than comparing totals. Thirty seconds of arithmetic; a quarter of delay avoided.

**Question 2 (easy) — Cost of attrition versus cost of retention**

Your workforce data: replacing a senior analyst costs roughly £45k (recruitment, backfill gap, and a productivity ramp of 6 months at 50% effectiveness on a £60k salary — the ramp alone contributing £15k of that total). The junior-team attrition from your cognitive section is running at 6 leavers/year above historical norm. A proposed retention package (progression framework, allowances, training budget) costs £120k/year and is projected to halve the excess attrition. What is the first-year net position, and what should you check before believing it?

- A) Net cost £120k — retention packages are an expense.
- B) Excess attrition costs 6 × £45k = £270k/year; halving saves £135k against a £120k spend — net +£15k in year one, roughly break-even. Check the two load-bearing assumptions: the £45k replacement cost (audit its components against your actual recruitment data) and especially the "halve attrition" projection (what evidence — exit interview alignment with what the package addresses?). Near-break-even in year one with retained capability as the real return.
- C) Savings of £270k make it a clear win.
- D) The package should be rejected because attrition is a market condition beyond influence.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The arithmetic first: excess attrition cost = 6 × £45k = £270k annually. The package's claimed effect halves that: saving £135k. Net: £135k − £120k = +£15k — approximately break-even in cash terms, not the windfall option C claims (C takes the full £270k as saving, an effect-size error: the package addresses half the problem by its own projection). Now the head-level layer, which B makes explicit: a near-break-even case rests entirely on its two soft numbers. The £45k is auditable — your own recent hires give real recruitment and ramp figures. The "halve" is the fragile one: projections of behavioural response deserve interrogation against the diagnosis (if exit interviews cite workload and the package offers allowances, the projection is hope; if they cite progression and the package builds a framework, it has a mechanism). And the honest case for the package is not the £15k — it is that the cash arithmetic merely needs to *not lose* for the capability argument to win: six senior-analyst departures a year is an operational resilience drain (on-call depth, incident experience, mentoring capacity) that the £45k unit cost only partially prices. Option D surrenders a managed variable to fatalism. This is building-the-team arithmetic as your role practises it: retention cases are capacity investment cases, audited the same way.

**Question 3 (easy) — Weighted risk scoring**

Your risk register scores risks as likelihood (1–5) × impact (1–5). Three open risks: R1 likelihood 4, impact 2 (score 8); R2 likelihood 1, impact 5 (score 5); R3 likelihood 3, impact 3 (score 9). The register ranks remediation priority by score: R3, R1, R2. What is the strongest caveat to this ranking?

- A) The ranking is arithmetically wrong.
- B) Multiplicative scoring treats a likely-minor risk (R1) as more urgent than a rare-catastrophic one (R2), but a 1×5 risk — low-probability, maximum-impact — is exactly the class (continuity failures, security breaches) where score-driven deprioritisation is most dangerous: the impact axis is usually capped and non-linear in reality (a "5" can be an order of magnitude worse than a "4"), so tail risks need a floor rule — any impact-5 risk gets senior review regardless of score — alongside the arithmetic ranking.
- C) Likelihood should be weighted double, as it is more measurable.
- D) The register should rank alphabetically to avoid bias.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The arithmetic is right (4×2=8, 1×5=5, 3×3=9; descending: R3, R1, R2 — eliminating A), which is precisely why the question matters: the defect is in the *instrument*, not the computation. Multiplicative scores compress two different quantities into one number and silently assert linearity — that impact 5 is merely 25% worse than impact 4. For operational estates the top of the impact scale is where non-linearity lives: a maximum-impact event (sustained multi-service outage, data breach on a citizen service) is not incrementally worse; it is categorically different, and its low likelihood is often the least reliable estimate on the register (rare events resist estimation). Hence B's floor rule — impact-5 risks receive senior scrutiny regardless of score — a standard maturity feature in risk frameworks, and exactly the kind of instrument-level correction your compliance-and-risk duty exists to impose. Options C and D replace one arbitrary scheme with another. The habit for every scoring system your function operates (risk, incident priority, change impact): run the extremes through the formula and ask whether the ranking it produces is one you would defend to a select committee after the tail event occurred. If not, the formula needs a rule the arithmetic can't supply.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Comparing tooling options on total cost**

Two monitoring consolidation options over 4 years. Option P (platform): £300k implementation + £120k/year licence + one 0.5 FTE administrator (£35k/year all-in). Option Q (build on open source): £150k implementation + £20k/year hosting + 2.0 FTE engineering support (£70k/year each all-in). What are the 4-year totals, and which non-arithmetic factor most deserves weight in the decision?

- A) P: £920k; Q: £790k — Q is cheaper; choose Q.
- B) P: £300k + 4×(£120k + £35k) = £300k + £620k = £920k. Q: £150k + 4×(£20k + £140k) = £150k + £640k = £790k. Q is £130k cheaper over 4 years — but Q's cost is 71% specialist staffing: the decision should weight whether you can *sustain* 2.0 FTE of niche engineering capability (recruitment, retention, key-person risk, and the opportunity cost of those engineers not working the roadmap) against P's contractual certainty; a £130k saving that depends on retaining two specialists in a market that pays more for them is a projection, not a price.
- C) P: £820k; Q: £710k — Q is cheaper.
- D) The options cannot be compared because one involves staff.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Compute both: P = £300k + 4 × £155k = £300k + £620k = £920k. Q = £150k + 4 × (£20k + 2 × £70k) = £150k + 4 × £160k = £150k + £640k = £790k. (Option C's figures drop a year of running costs — the arithmetic near-miss.) Q wins by £130k, about 14% — real but not decisive, and B's second half is the actual decision content. Decompose Q's cost structure: £560k of its £790k is specialist staffing — the cost that behaves least like a price. It carries key-person risk (your continuity of *monitoring* now depends on retaining specific engineers — compare the qualified-lead pipeline arithmetic), market risk (niche skills inflate faster than licence fees), and opportunity cost (2.0 FTE of engineering talent operating a tool is 2.0 FTE not delivering the roadmap those tools serve). P converts those risks into a contract — more expensive, more certain, and transferable to a supplier who carries the specialist bench. Neither answer is universally right; the examinable skill is B's move of *decomposing totals by cost behaviour* (contractual vs. staffing vs. usage-variable) before comparing, because two equal totals with different compositions are different risks. Option D abandons a comparison the decomposition makes perfectly tractable. This is tools-fit-for-purpose governance in its commercial dimension: fitness includes the sustainability of the operating model, and the 4-year spreadsheet is where that question either gets asked or gets discovered.

**Question 5 (moderate) — SLA penalty arithmetic**

Your hosting contract: service credits of 2% of the £80k monthly charge per 0.1 percentage points below 99.9% availability, capped at 20% of the monthly charge. This month the supplier delivered 99.4% availability. What credit applies, and what does the cap structure tell you?

- A) £8,000 — 5 increments of 0.1pp below target × 2% × £80k.
- B) The shortfall is 99.9% − 99.4% = 0.5pp = 5 increments; 5 × 2% = 10% of £80k = £8,000. The cap (20% = £16,000) engages at 1.0pp below target, i.e. at 98.9%: below that, every further point of failure is free to the supplier — so for severe outages the credit regime stops incentivising recovery, and your protection must come from other clauses (termination triggers, service improvement obligations), which is what a head should verify exist before a bad month, not during one.
- C) £16,000 — the cap always applies to any breach.
- D) No credit — 99.4% rounds to 99.9% within tolerance.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The computation: shortfall 0.5 percentage points → 0.5 ÷ 0.1 = 5 increments → 5 × 2% = 10% → £80k × 0.10 = £8,000. Options C and D fail the arithmetic (the cap hasn't engaged; no rounding tolerance exists in the stated terms). But option A, arithmetically identical to B, is the lesser answer, and the difference is the head-level content: B reads the *structure*. Find where the cap binds: 20% ÷ 2% = 10 increments = 1.0pp, so at 98.9% the credits max out at £16,000 — and every additional hour of failure beyond that costs the supplier nothing. For context, 98.9% monthly availability is roughly 8 hours of downtime; a catastrophic 24-hour outage yields the same £16,000 as a bad-but-bounded month. Service credits are therefore an incentive instrument for *moderate* underperformance only; your exposure to severe failure is governed by whatever else the contract contains — termination rights, mandatory improvement plans, liability provisions — and the time to map those is at signature or renewal, in calm. The transferable discipline: for every capped or stepped commercial structure your estate relies on, compute the point where the counterparty's marginal incentive goes to zero, and ensure a different mechanism takes over there. That is service compliance and risk management performed on the contract's arithmetic rather than its adjectives.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Capacity investment timing**

A shared platform runs at 68% peak utilisation, growing 2 percentage points per month. Performance degrades measurably above 85%, and procurement-plus-implementation of the capacity expansion takes 5 months. The expansion costs £200k, and finance asks: "Why buy now? We're only at 68%." What is the correct quantitative answer?

- A) Buy later — 17 points of headroom remain, which is 8.5 months at current growth.
- B) At +2pp/month, 85% is reached in (85 − 68) ÷ 2 = 8.5 months. The expansion takes 5 months, so the decision deadline is 8.5 − 5 = 3.5 months away — "now" is not required, but "within one quarter" is, and any acceleration in growth (check the trend's own trend) shortens it; the honest position for finance is a commitment this quarter with the growth rate monitored monthly against the trigger.
- C) Buy immediately — capacity should always lead demand by a wide margin.
- D) Wait until 85% is reached, then expedite procurement.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The structure is threshold minus lead time. Months to threshold: (85 − 68) ÷ 2 = 8.5. Subtract the 5-month procurement-and-implementation lead: the *order* must be placed by month 3.5, or the platform crosses into degradation before capacity lands. So finance's "why now?" has a precise answer: not now, but within 3.5 months — and B's monitoring caveat is what makes the answer robust rather than merely correct: growth rates drift (the earlier storage question's lesson), so the 3.5-month cushion is itself a projection needing monthly re-verification, and a new workload or seasonal effect can consume it. Option A performs half the calculation — 8.5 months of headroom is true and irrelevant, because headroom must be measured in *decision time* (headroom minus lead time), not in threshold time; presenting 8.5 months to a finance committee when 3.5 is the real number is how capacity crises get scheduled. Option D is option A's terminal form: initiating a 5-month process at the threshold guarantees 5 months of degradation, and "expedite" is not a plan — it is a premium paid for a calendar error. Option C overshoots into unfalsifiable prudence that wastes capital. This is your availability-and-capacity duty verbatim — identify capacity issues, implement required changes, initiate remedial action — with the head-level addition that the *initiation date* is a computed quantity, and defending it to finance in lead-time arithmetic is precisely how remedial action gets funded on time.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Interpreting a fall in reported incidents**

Two quarters after you introduced a new incident categorisation policy (stricter criteria for what counts as an "incident" versus a "service request"), the board pack shows incidents down 30% and celebrates improved stability. Desk contact volumes are flat; monitoring alert volumes are flat; user-experience availability is unchanged. What is the sound interpretation?

- A) Stability has improved 30% as reported.
- B) Every independent indicator (contacts, alerts, user experience) is flat: the 30% fall coincides with the reclassification and most plausibly measures the *policy*, not the estate — some events previously counted as incidents are now counted as requests. The board pack is committing (innocently) a measurement-change attribution error, and the honest correction is to restate the trend on a like-for-like basis (recount prior quarters under the new criteria, or report the combined incident-plus-request operational volume alongside), with the definitional break annotated in the pack permanently.
- C) The desk should be investigated for suppressing contact volumes.
- D) Both things are true: the policy changed and stability improved 30%.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Triangulate. If stability had genuinely improved 30%, correlated indicators should move: fewer incidents ought to mean fewer alerts, fewer contacts, better experienced availability. All three are flat — so the change lives in the one thing that did change: the counting rule. This is a measurement-regime break, the reporting sibling of the "projecting a retired baseline" error: comparing counts across a definitional boundary attributes the definition's effect to the estate. The correction B specifies is the professional standard — restate history under current criteria (or dual-report across the break), annotate the discontinuity in every pack that spans it, and let any *residual* trend claim credit. Option D is worth dwelling on because it is the seductive one: both things could in principle be true, but the flat independent indicators leave no room for a 30% stability effect — D is what a head *wants* to believe with their own policy in the numerator, which is exactly when the discipline matters most. Option C invents suppression where flatness is the evidence *against* anything odd at the desk. The governance sting: you introduced the categorisation policy, and the board is now crediting you for its measurement artefact — a comfortable error, compounding quietly until an incident-heavy quarter "reverses the improvement" and demands explanation. Correcting the record now, from your own side, at the cost of a less flattering pack, is service compliance applied to your own function's reporting — and it is the cheap moment; the expensive one arrives with the reversal.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Expected value across a remediation portfolio**

£300k of discretionary remediation budget; four candidates. Using your estate's incident cost model (staff, recovery, citizen-impact valuation): (W) legacy integration failover, £200k, removes an estimated 2 major incidents/year at £60k each; (X) trend-to-action process automation, £40k, removes an estimated 3 majors/year at £60k each; (Y) monitoring gap closure on two services, £80k, removes 1 major/year at £60k plus an estimated £30k/year of prolonged-minor costs; (Z) continuity test acceleration, £60k, no direct incident reduction but cuts estimated invocation-failure risk, valued at £45k/year expected. Which portfolio maximises first-year expected return within budget?

- A) W + X (£240k): benefit £120k + £180k = £300k.
- B) X + Y + Z (£180k): benefit £180k + £90k + £45k = £315k, leaving £120k unspent.
- C) W + X + Z (£300k): benefit £120k + £180k + £45k = £345k.
- D) X + Y + Z, then reconsider W next year against the remaining case — the ranked returns are X (4.5:1), Z (0.75:1)... recompute: X £180k/£40k = 4.5; Y £90k/£80k ≈ 1.1; Z £45k/£60k = 0.75; W £120k/£200k = 0.6 — so best value-per-pound is X, Y, Z, W; but within the £300k cap, total benefit is maximised by C's £345k, so approve C while noting X carries the portfolio.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Two optimisation questions hide here, and the option set forces you to separate them. Benefit arithmetic first: W = 2 × £60k = £120k/yr; X = 3 × £60k = £180k/yr; Y = £60k + £30k = £90k/yr; Z = £45k/yr. Ratios (benefit per pound): X 4.5, Y 1.125, Z 0.75, W 0.6. If the objective were *return on each pound*, you would buy in ratio order until the budget ran out: X (£40k) + Y (£80k) + Z (£60k) = £180k spent, £315k benefit — option B — leaving £120k, not enough for W (£200k). But the stated objective is *maximum first-year expected benefit within the £300k cap*, and unspent discretionary budget typically returns to the centre: option C spends £300k exactly (W £200k + X £40k + Z £60k) for £345k — £30k more benefit than B — by substituting W+Z (£165k benefit, £260k cost) for Y+Z-plus-nothing. Verify no better combination: X is in every sensible portfolio (highest ratio, lowest cost); X+W+Y = £320k breaks the cap; X+Y+Z = £315k benefit; X+W+Z = £345k. C wins. Option D reasons well, ranks correctly, and then hedges into approving C anyway — its "reconsider W next year" preamble contradicts its conclusion; as in the earlier contract question, an option at war with itself fails even when its components are individually sound. The head-level lessons: state the objective before optimising (ratio-maximising and budget-constrained-benefit-maximising choose different portfolios — B versus C is exactly that difference); and note the fragility worth recording in the approval — every figure is an estimate from your cost model, and X's spectacular ratio (the process fix from your cognitive section, cheap and high-yield) is the robust core of any portfolio, so sequence it first regardless of what else survives scrutiny.

**Question 9 (hard) — The benchmarking claim**

A consultancy's benchmarking report, commissioned by your directorate, states: "Comparable government operations centres run at 2.1 incidents per analyst per day; yours runs at 1.4 — a 33% productivity gap indicating overstaffing of approximately 5 FTE." You establish: the benchmark population's "incident" definition includes service requests (yours does not, since your reclassification); your centre carries the 24/7 on-call obligation in-house while three of the five comparators outsource overnight; and your analysts spend a recorded 25% of time on problem investigation and preventative work the benchmark's activity model doesn't capture. What is the sound response?

- A) Accept the finding — external benchmarks are independent and the directorate commissioned it.
- B) Reject benchmarking entirely as impossible across organisations.
- C) Show the comparison is currently uninterpretable and rebuild it: the definitional mismatch (incidents-plus-requests vs incidents-only) inflates the comparator numerator by an unknown but estimable factor — using your own desk data, requests run at roughly the same volume as incidents, suggesting your like-for-like figure is nearer 2.8; the 24/7 in-house obligation loads roster hours the comparators don't carry; and 25% of recorded time is preventative output the metric counts as idle. Offer the consultancy your data to re-run like-for-like, and present the directorate the corrected picture *including* whatever residual gap survives — with the preventative work's value argued from your incident-reduction data, not asserted.
- D) Quietly increase your incidents-per-analyst figure by reversing the reclassification policy so future benchmarks compare favourably.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Take the three distortions in turn, because each is a standard benchmarking pathology. Definitional: your reclassification (Question 7) means the comparators count a superset of what you count — if requests roughly equal incidents in volume at your desk, your like-for-like rate is ~1.4 × 2 = 2.8 against 2.1, *reversing* the headline's direction (a gap in your favour). Structural: in-house 24/7 coverage requires roster depth that shows up as "analysts" in the denominator while comparators' outsourced overnight staffing sits in a supplier's headcount — an apples-to-staffing-model comparison. Activity-model: 25% of time on problem and preventative work is production the metric reads as slack; its value is demonstrable from your own data (the trend-to-action fix's incident reduction) rather than merely asserted, and option C insists on exactly that evidentiary form. The response's *manner* matters as much as its content: option C engages the benchmark rather than dismissing it (option B), because the directorate commissioned it and "uninterpretable, here is the corrected version" preserves your credibility where "benchmarks are impossible" spends it — and because the corrected comparison might still contain a residual finding worth having; a head who only rebuts benchmarks that flatter is running defence, not analysis. Option A signs a 5-FTE cut on arithmetic you can refute in three paragraphs. Option D is the corrosive one: reshaping your *measurement policy* to game future comparisons — reversing a reclassification you made for operational truth — subordinates your function's information quality to its appearance, the exact inversion of everything your compliance-and-risk duty protects. The durable technique: for any external comparison, audit definitions, structures and activity models *before* the delta, and respond with a rebuilt comparison plus your data — the counterparty who offers their data to the benchmarker is the one the final report believes.

**Question 10 (hard) — Weighted aggregation for the board**

Your board KPI aggregates availability across five services, currently as a simple average. This month: four back-office services at 99.9% each, and the flagship citizen service — carrying 70% of all user transactions — at 97.0%. Simple average: (99.9 × 4 + 97.0) ÷ 5 = 99.32%. The board target is 99.3%. The transaction-weighted figure: back-office services share the remaining 30% of transactions equally (7.5% each). What does weighting show, and what should you report?

- A) 99.32% — target met; report as usual.
- B) Weighted: (97.0 × 0.70) + (99.9 × 0.30) = 67.9 + 29.97 = 97.87% — dramatically below both the simple average and the target, because the failing service is where the users are. Report the weighted figure alongside the simple one, flag that the KPI's construction has been masking user-experienced performance (a 97.0% month on the flagship met a 99.3% target), and propose the board adopt transaction weighting permanently — accepting that this makes your own headline worse this month and honest thereafter.
- C) Weighted: 98.45% — marginally below target; note it verbally.
- D) Report 99.32% this month but adopt weighting from next month so the change doesn't coincide with a bad result.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Compute the weighted figure: flagship contributes 97.0 × 0.70 = 67.9; the four back-office services contribute 99.9 × 0.30 = 29.97; total 97.87% (option C's 98.45% miscarries the weighting). The 1.45-point gap between simple and weighted averages is not a technicality — it is the difference between "target met" and "the service most citizens used delivered 97% availability", roughly 22 hours of user-experienced monthly downtime on the flagship. The simple average conceals it because averaging *services* pretends each service matters equally when one carries 70% of the users — the aggregation-across-different-things error at board level, where its consequences are governance-grade: a board reading 99.32% green will not fund the flagship's remediation. Now the integrity sequencing, which is where B beats D and the question earns "hard": D adopts the honest measure *starting next month*, so that this month's breach reports as a pass and the methodology change never coincides with bad news — measurement timed to flatter, the same pathology as Question 9's option D and the compliance dashboard's edited denominator, executed with better manners. The moment you have computed 97.87%, reporting 99.32% without it is a decision to withhold; option B reports both figures, names the construction defect, and takes the reputational cost of proposing the honest measure in the month it hurts — which is precisely what makes the proposal credible. (A board that later learns the weighting was known and deferred learns something about every number you have ever sent them.) The user-focus core, in your summary's own terms — strategic thinking to provide the best service for the *end user* — is that KPI construction is user representation: whoever chooses the weights decides whose experience the organisation sees. Choose the users' weights, especially the month it costs you.

**Question 11 (hard) — Continuity investment under uncertainty**

A full failover capability for the flagship service costs £450k to build and £50k/year to maintain, and would reduce estimated annual downtime from major events by 20 hours. Your citizen-impact cost model values flagship downtime at £8k/hour, but the model is acknowledged rough: defensible range £4k–£15k/hour. Over a 5-year horizon (ignore discounting), what does the analysis support?

- A) Central case: benefit 5 × 20 × £8k = £800k against cost £450k + 5 × £50k = £700k — net +£100k; proceed.
- B) Run the range, not just the centre. Costs: £700k over 5 years. Benefits: at £4k/hour, 100 hours × £4k = £400k (net −£300k); at £8k, £800k (net +£100k); at £15k, £1.5m (net +£800k). The decision *flips* within the model's own uncertainty range, so the arithmetic alone cannot decide: the case rests on where the true hourly cost sits and on unmodelled factors (reputational and ministerial tolerance for flagship outages, the 20-hour estimate's own reliability). The analysis supports either commissioning a better impact estimate before deciding, or making the decision explicitly on risk appetite with the range disclosed — not presenting +£100k as if it settled the matter.
- C) Reject: at the low end the case loses £300k.
- D) Proceed: at the high end it nets £800k.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The computations: cost = £450k + £250k = £700k; benefit spans 100 hours (20 × 5) priced at the range ends — £400k, £800k, £1.5m; nets of −£300k, +£100k, +£800k. Options A, C and D each pick one point on the range and pronounce — A the centre, C the pessimistic end, D the optimistic — and all three commit the same meta-error: treating a decision that *changes sign inside the stated uncertainty* as if a point estimate could close it. That sign-flip is the analytical finding; B is the only option that reports it. What follows from a sign-flip is procedural, and B names both legitimate paths: reduce the uncertainty (a focused impact study — what do flagship outages actually cost in staff response, user harm, recovery? — is a £20k answer to a £700k question), or decide on explicitly-stated risk appetite with the range in front of the decision-maker, which is what risk appetite is *for* when analysis runs out. Note the contrast with Question 8's £3k-precision portfolio and the earlier resilience-programme case: sensitivity analysis is not decoration — it is the test that sorts decisions arithmetic can make from decisions it can only inform. A head who presents +£100k to an investment committee without the range is not simplifying; they are transferring their uncertainty to people who were not told they were receiving it. Continuity investment lives permanently in this territory — low-frequency, high-impact, soft-costed — and the differentiated honesty of B is the numeric twin of the differentiated assurance in your cognitive section's final question: state what the analysis establishes, what it cannot, and which authority the residue belongs to.

**Question 12 (hard) — The workforce plan's compound arithmetic**

Your 3-year capability plan: the centre needs 24 qualified analysts steady-state. Current: 21 qualified, 5 in training (9-month pipeline, historically 80% completion). Annual attrition among qualified staff runs at 15%, and — from your retention analysis — leavers are disproportionately experienced: assume attrition applies to the qualified pool including newly-qualified. Recruitment into training can run at most 8/year. Does a steady intake of 8/year reach and hold 24, and what does the arithmetic reveal about the plan's real constraint?

- A) Yes, comfortably: 8 recruits against ~3 leavers per year grows the pool rapidly.
- B) Model year by year. Year 1: start 21; attrition ≈ 21 × 0.15 ≈ 3.2 → ~17.8; current trainees deliver 5 × 0.8 = 4 → ~21.8; new intake of 8 is still in the pipeline. Year 2: attrition ≈ 3.3 → ~18.5; year-1 intake delivers 8 × 0.8 = 6.4 → ~24.9. Year 3: attrition ≈ 3.7 → ~21.2; intake delivers 6.4 → ~27.6, but attrition then takes ~4.1 of a 27.6 pool... steady state: pool stabilises where intake-completions equal attrition: 6.4 = 0.15 × P → P ≈ 42.7 — far above 24, so 8/year overshoots eventually; holding 24 needs completions of 0.15 × 24 = 3.6/year, i.e. intake of 3.6 ÷ 0.8 = 4.5/year. The plan reaches 24 during year 2–3 and the real constraints are the transition dip (year 1 runs at ~22 against a 24 requirement) and the 15% attrition itself: at 10% attrition, holding 24 needs only 3 completions (intake ~3.75) — retention is worth more than recruitment capacity.
- C) No: attrition outpaces an 8/year intake permanently.
- D) The question cannot be answered without monthly cohort data.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The model is intake × completion versus pool × attrition, run through time. Year 1 exposes the *transition* problem: the current 5 trainees yield only 4 qualified while attrition removes ~3, so the pool treads water around 21–22 — below the 24 requirement — for the whole pipeline lag; no intake decision now fixes year 1 (the 9-month pipeline is a physical constraint), so year 1 needs bridging tactics: retention focus on the experienced leavers, borrowed qualified staff, or overtime-tolerable rota design — the qualified-lead lesson from your cognitive section at whole-team scale. From year 2, the 8/year intake delivers 6.4 completions against ~3.5 attrition and the pool *grows past* the requirement toward an eventual ~43 steady state — so option C is wrong in the opposite direction to option A: the plan doesn't fail; it eventually over-provisions (a real cost: training capacity, salary drift) — sustained 8/year is the wrong steady-state setting, and ~4.5/year holds 24 once reached. B's closing insight is the strategic one: differentiate the levers. Recruitment capacity (the constraint the plan obsesses over) is not binding after year 2; the binding quantities are the pipeline lag (fixable only by anticipation) and the attrition rate — where a 5-point improvement (15%→10%) roughly halves the required intake, which is why the £120k retention package from Question 2 is not an HR nicety but the cheapest capacity instrument in the plan. Option D demands precision the decision doesn't need — annual cohort arithmetic answers the shape of the plan; monthly data refines it. Building the capability of the team, your summary's flagship phrase, is at head level exactly this: a stock-and-flow system with lags, leaks and levers, and the head who models it chooses levers by arithmetic while the head who doesn't chooses them by anecdote.

### Preparation tips

- **Rebuild one board figure from raw data monthly.** Take a number your function reported upward and reconstruct it — denominator, weights, exclusions, definitional breaks. Finding nothing is the good outcome; the habit is the point.
- **Run sensitivity on every case you sign.** Both ends of the softest estimate: does the decision survive? Sort your decisions into "arithmetic decides" and "appetite decides, arithmetic informs" — and label them honestly in the paper.
- **Keep a lead-time ledger.** For each major capacity, capability and procurement dependency: threshold, current position, growth rate, lead time — and therefore the computed decision date. Review quarterly. It converts most "urgent" investment decisions back into scheduled ones.
- **Practise decomposing totals by cost behaviour.** Contractual, staffing, usage-variable: equal totals, different risks. Apply it to the next two business cases that cross your desk.
- **Audit one scoring or aggregation instrument per quarter.** Risk scores, KPI weights, closure categories: run the extremes through the formula and check the ranking survives a select-committee test.
- **Do timed mixed practice, then classify your misses.** Senior numerical errors cluster in transitions (units, periods, denominators) and in premature closure on point estimates. Knowing your cluster is half the cure.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Annual affordability read as quarterly feasibility.** Cumulative-versus-cumulative or the plan fails in Q1 while the year "works".
- **Point estimates presented where the range flips the sign.** Disclose the range or you are transferring hidden uncertainty to your decision-makers.
- **Measurement changes credited as performance.** Definitional breaks, reclassifications and edited denominators must be annotated and restated — especially when the artefact flatters you.
- **Benchmarks accepted or rejected whole.** Audit definitions, structures and activity models, then rebuild like-for-like. Offer your data; keep the residual finding if one survives.
- **Optimising without stating the objective.** Ratio-maximising and budget-constrained-benefit-maximising choose different portfolios. Name the objective before the arithmetic, or the spreadsheet names it for you.
- **Stock-and-flow systems managed by anecdote.** Workforce, capability, capacity: model intake, leakage and lag, and pull the lever the arithmetic ranks first — it is usually retention, and it is usually underpriced.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment measures exactness in reading, interpreting and reasoning from written material, using the document classes a head of function actually governs by: policy and standards text, contracts and their schedules, audit findings and management responses, board papers, assurance statements, ministerial correspondence trails, and the strategic documents — roadmaps, business cases, workforce plans — where a single loose sentence can commit a function to something its author never intended.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 15 to 25 minutes: passages followed by true/false/cannot-say judgements, best-summary selection, inference identification, argument evaluation and clear-writing judgement. At senior levels the passages lengthen, layer (two or three documents in tension), and reward the reader who tracks precisely what each text commits its author to — because at head level, what documents commit people to is most of what you read them for.

For your role, verbal precision operates at organisational scale. You read upward-bound text before it carries your warranty: assurance statements, audit responses, board narratives. You read downward-bound text before it carries your authority: policies, delegations, standards — where an ambiguity you sign becomes a hundred divergent interpretations on night shifts you will never see. You read inward-bound text adversarially when needed: supplier contracts, benchmark reports, programme assurances, each written by someone whose interests are not fully yours. And you write the texts others will parse this carefully: your role summary's strategic responsibilities — the roadmap, integration decisions, capability plans — all take effect as documents. The questions below train the reading that this traffic demands, including its hardest discipline: refusing to let institutional convenience, seniority or your own prior positions adjust what a text actually says.

The standing rule for true/false/cannot-say items: judge only from the passage. Import neither knowledge nor doubt. At head level a third temptation joins those two — importing *policy intent* ("what the rule was obviously meant to achieve") over policy text. Intent matters when you amend rules; text governs when you apply them.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Precise policy and standards comprehension** maps to **Change management** and your **expert service management framework knowledge**: ensuring release policies and framework-aligned processes are applied across the function starts with reading — and drafting — their conditions, definitions and delegations exactly.
- **Assurance-language discipline** maps to **Continuity management**, **Asset and configuration management** and **Ownership and initiative**: what a test report, compliance statement or readiness confirmation actually establishes, versus what its phrasing invites you to believe, is the difference between managed risk and inherited surprise.
- **Cross-document and supersession reasoning** maps to **Incident management** and **Problem management** at governance level: post-incident reviews, audit trails and management responses are document stacks; leading investigation and prevention includes reading the stack in order and catching where the record diverges from itself.
- **Argument evaluation** maps to **Availability and capacity management**, **Service focus** and your roadmap responsibility: investment cases, supplier claims and strategy papers arrive as arguments; your approval inherits whichever inferential gaps you fail to find.
- **Clear-writing judgement** maps to **Community collaboration** and **User focus**: adapting feedback so it lands and lasts, directing on tools and methods, and representing user needs in decisions all take effect through text that must carry exact meaning to audiences from night-shift analysts to ministers' offices.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — What an assurance statement commits to**

Passage (from a programme's readiness report): "Monitoring integration has been tested by the delivery team and no critical defects remain open against it. Performance testing is scheduled for completion before go-live. The operations acceptance criteria have been reviewed by the programme."

Statement: "The operations acceptance criteria have been met."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage says the criteria have been *reviewed by the programme* — an activity performed on the criteria, not a verdict rendered against them. "Reviewed" is compatible with met, partially met, or found unmeetable; the text simply does not say. Neither is the statement contradicted, so "false" is unavailable: cannot say. This is the assurance-verb discipline in miniature, and it is worth building a small lexicon: *tested* (by whom, against what?), *reviewed* (outcome unstated), *scheduled* (not done), *no critical defects open* (what about high? what was the test's coverage?), *addressed* (the most elastic word in programme English). Readiness reports are built substantially from these verbs, and each one marks a place where a head's follow-up question either lands now or the gap surfaces at go-live. Reading assurance language for what it *commits* rather than what it *suggests* is the first skill of the accountable signatory — and note that the passage's three sentences contain three different commitment levels, which is entirely typical of the genre.

**Question 2 (easy) — Policy text versus policy intent**

Passage (from your organisation's change policy): "Emergency changes may be authorised outside CAB by the duty operational control manager. All emergency changes must be reviewed at the next CAB sitting. Changes authorised under the emergency provision more than three times in any rolling month by the same team trigger a process review of that team's change planning."

Last month, one team used the emergency route three times. Statement: "The team's usage has triggered a process review under the policy."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The trigger is "more than three times" — strictly greater than three. Three uses equals the boundary and does not exceed it: no trigger; the statement is false. The pull toward "true" comes from intent: the clause obviously exists to catch teams over-using the emergency route, three-in-a-month feels like exactly that, and a head who *wrote* the clause may remember what it was for. But application is textual: "more than three" is four, and a review imposed at three would itself be the policy violation — the governance equivalent of an availability target of 99.5% being "nearly breached" at 99.6%. If three-per-month is genuinely the concerning threshold, the correct instrument is amendment ("three or more"), prospectively, through the policy's own change mechanism — not creative reading, which teaches every team that the written rules are drafts and the real rules live in the head's mood. Boundary conditions (*more than*, *at least*, *within*, *no later than*) decide an outsized share of both assessment questions and governance disputes; at your level you sit on both sides — applying them exactly, and drafting them so that exact application produces the outcome you intend.

**Question 3 (easy) — Extracting the operative obligation from audit text**

Passage (audit finding and management response): "Finding 4 (Medium): Configuration records for the secondary data centre could not be reconciled to physical assets within the audit sample; 6 of 40 sampled items showed discrepancies. Management response: Accepted. A full reconciliation of the secondary data centre's configuration records will be completed by 31 October, with quarterly sample-based verification thereafter. Owner: Head of Command and Control."

It is 15 September. Which statement accurately describes your current obligations under this text?

- A) Complete a full reconciliation of all data centres' records by 31 October.
- B) Complete a full reconciliation of the secondary data centre's records by 31 October, and thereafter operate quarterly sample-based verification — both owned by you, with the commitment already made ("accepted"), so the residual question is delivery, not negotiation.
- C) Improve the sample pass rate above 85% by year end.
- D) No obligation yet — audit findings become binding only after the following audit cycle.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Read the response as the contract it is. Scope: "the secondary data centre" — option A inflates it to all centres, a real drafting question (should the reconciliation extend further, given the finding?) but not what the text commits to; if the discrepancy pattern likely generalises, extending scope is a decision to make and record deliberately, not an obligation to misread into existence. Content: two obligations, one dated (full reconciliation, 31 October) and one standing (quarterly verification "thereafter" — which has no end date, a commitment feature worth noticing at signature time, since open-ended obligations accumulate). Status: "Accepted" closes the negotiation phase — options that treat the finding as still contestable (D's invented deferral rule) misread the document's lifecycle stage. Ownership: named as your role, so delivery risk is personally yours to manage from today, and 46 days for a full reconciliation is a plan you should already have. Option C converts the finding's evidence (6 of 40) into a target the text never sets. The head-level habit: management responses are drafted in a hurry and then govern for years — read your own function's past responses as an examiner, because audit will, at follow-up, word by word.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Best summary of a strategy extract**

Passage: "The centre's tooling strategy will prioritise consolidation where incident evidence demonstrates correlation failures between existing tools, and integration elsewhere. Wholesale replacement will be considered only where a tool fails its fitness review and integration costs exceed replacement costs. Fitness reviews will run annually against documented operational requirements, and no procurement will be initiated without a completed review. The strategy explicitly accepts a heterogeneous tooling estate as a legitimate end-state where the evidence supports it."

Which option best summarises the strategy?

- A) "The centre will consolidate its tools into a single platform over time."
- B) "Evidence-gated pragmatism: consolidate where incident data proves correlation failure, integrate otherwise, replace only on failed fitness review plus adverse integration economics, procure nothing without a current review — and heterogeneity is an acceptable outcome, not a transitional state."
- C) "Tool procurement requires an annual review."
- D) "The centre prefers integration to consolidation."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage's architecture is a decision hierarchy with evidence gates: consolidation (gated on incident evidence of correlation failure), integration (the default elsewhere), replacement (double-gated: failed fitness review *and* integration costs exceeding replacement), a procedural lock (no procurement without completed review), and — the sentence most summaries drop — an explicit end-state position: heterogeneity is legitimate when evidenced. Option B preserves every gate and the end-state. Option A asserts the exact end-state the passage disclaims — and it is the summary a vendor, or a hurried director, would *prefer* the strategy to say; watching summaries drift toward what their repeaters want is a head-level occupational study. Option C extracts one procedural clause; option D flattens a conditional hierarchy into a preference. Why it matters beyond the test: strategies are enacted mostly through their summaries — in CAB decisions, procurement papers, corridor explanations — and a strategy whose summary loses its gates becomes, in practice, gateless. The counter-discipline is drafting strategies whose central logic survives compression (the passage's own "consolidate on evidence, integrate otherwise, accept heterogeneity" is nearly summary-proof) and correcting drifted summaries every time you hear one, because the third uncorrected repetition becomes the strategy.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Inference from a workforce policy tension**

Passage 1 (HR policy): "Staff on formal development programmes must be released for all scheduled training modules; operational pressure does not constitute grounds for withdrawal."
Passage 2 (your centre's minimum-staffing standard): "No shift may operate below four qualified analysts. Where staffing would fall below this level, the duty manager must secure cover before the shift begins."
Next Tuesday's night shift roster shows four qualified analysts, one of whom has a scheduled training module that evening under a formal development programme.

Statement: "The documents, taken together, require the duty manager to secure additional cover for Tuesday night."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Chain the obligations. Passage 1 removes one option: withdrawing the analyst from training is prohibited, and operational pressure is expressly excluded as grounds — so the analyst is, for staffing purposes, absent. That takes Tuesday night to three qualified analysts against a floor of four. Passage 2 then fires: where staffing "would fall below" the floor, the duty manager "must secure cover before the shift begins." Both premises are textual; the conclusion follows necessarily; the statement is true. The instructive wrong answer is "cannot say", usually reasoned as "perhaps an exception exists" or "perhaps the module will be cancelled" — imported possibilities again; the passages as given close the deduction. Note what the two documents jointly accomplish, because it is the question's real lesson: neither text mentions the other, yet together they convert a training calendar into a staffing obligation with a deadline. Document *interactions* are where organisations actually operate — and where their gaps actually open. A head reads new policies for their collisions with existing standards (had Passage 2 said "should" rather than "must", Tuesday becomes a judgement; had Passage 1 allowed operational withdrawal, training becomes the shock absorber and your capability pipeline quietly leaks — the workforce arithmetic from your numeric section, made of modal verbs). Your role's building-the-team responsibility depends on exactly this: development commitments that survive contact with the roster, which they only do when the texts make cover mandatory rather than aspirational.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Argument evaluation: the board paper**

Passage (from a paper seeking budget): "Industry analysis shows organisations with AI-assisted operations centres resolve incidents 40% faster. Our resolution times have plateaued for three quarters. Investment in the proposed AI operations platform will therefore reduce our resolution times by approximately 40%, delivering the attached benefits case. Delay carries competitive risk, as peer departments are already procuring similar platforms."

Which is the strongest criticism of the argument?

- A) The 40% industry figure is probably invented.
- B) Three inferential failures compound: the 40% is a cross-sectional association (organisations *with* such centres differ in many ways from those without — maturity, data quality, process discipline — so the figure is not a treatment effect); even as a treatment effect it would not transfer at full size to an estate whose plateau may have entirely different causes (unexamined); and "peer departments are procuring" is social proof, not evidence of fitness for your requirements — the argument moves from correlation to causation to transfer to urgency without earning any of the steps.
- C) AI platforms are overhyped and the paper should be rejected on principle.
- D) The paper should have been longer.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Decompose the argument's chain, because each link fails differently. Link one: "organisations with X do Y better" is cross-sectional association — the classic confound being that adopting X correlates with operational maturity, which independently improves Y; the 40% describes a *population difference*, not what buying X does to you. Link two: even granting a causal effect, transfer requires mechanism-matching — the paper never diagnoses *why* your resolution times plateaued (tooling? process? the correlation-delay findings from your own incident data? capability gaps?), and an AI platform accelerates only the constraints it touches; "approximately 40%" transplants a population statistic onto an undiagnosed estate with false precision. Link three: peer procurement is bandwagon evidence — it may justify *investigation* (peers might know something) but the paper deploys it as urgency, which is how procurement pressure, not fitness evidence, enters business cases. Option B names all three; the compound structure — correlation→causation→transfer→urgency — is the standard anatomy of technology-investment arguments and worth memorising as a checklist. Option A attacks the premise without grounds (and the argument fails even with the premise granted — always the stronger critique). Option C substitutes counter-dogma for analysis; a head who rejects "on principle" is as evidence-free as the paper. The constructive follow-up your roadmap duty implies: return the paper with the diagnosis question — "what constraint does this remove, and what does our incident data say that constraint costs?" — which converts a vendor-shaped case into an evidence-shaped one, or reveals that it cannot be.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Two versions of a runbook clause**

Passage 1 (runbook v3.1, current): "On invocation, the duty manager notifies the head of command and control, who decides within 30 minutes whether to activate the full continuity team."
Passage 2 (draft v3.2, circulated for your approval): "On invocation, the duty manager activates the full continuity team and notifies the head of command and control. The head may stand the team down if activation proves unnecessary."

Which statement best captures the *governance* effect of the proposed change?

- A) The change is cosmetic; the same people are involved either way.
- B) The change moves the default: v3.1 makes activation an opt-in decision held by you inside a 30-minute window (risking delay if you are unreachable, and making activation depend on a single person's availability at the worst possible moment); v3.2 makes activation automatic with your role becoming stand-down authority — faster and more resilient to your unavailability, at the cost of more false-positive activations, whose burden the draft accepts implicitly rather than analysing.
- C) The change removes the head of command and control from continuity decisions entirely.
- D) The change is invalid because runbooks cannot alter decision rights.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The edit swaps a default, and defaults are where documents exercise their deepest governance power. Under v3.1, the safe-by-default state is *inactivity*: the team activates only if you decide, within 30 minutes, presumably at 03:00 — so the clause's failure mode is your unreachability or hesitation, and its cost lands on the incident (delayed continuity response). Under v3.2, the safe-by-default state is *activity*: the team activates unconditionally and your authority operates in the reverse direction (stand-down) — failure mode: unnecessary activations, cost landing on the team (fatigue, disruption, alert-cynicism if false positives are frequent). Neither is universally right; the choice should follow the estate's data — invocation frequency, false-positive rate, the actual cost of a 30-minute delay against the runbook's recovery-time targets — and B's closing observation is the approval-worthy insight: the draft *changes* the trade-off without *stating* it, and your sign-off should require the analysis ("how often have invocations proven unnecessary? what does a 30-minute activation delay cost against our RTOs?") rather than just the diff. Option A is the reading that lets governance changes travel as "wording updates" — the same people, radically different failure modes. Option C misreads: your authority persists, inverted. Option D invents a doctrine; decision rights live *precisely* in documents like runbooks, which is why their edits deserve exactly this scrutiny. The generalisable check for every procedural edit you approve: find the default, find who bears the cost when the default is wrong, and confirm the change's author knows the answer too.

**Question 8 (moderate) — The ministerial correspondence trail**

Passage (three extracts, in sequence): (i) Private office to your director, Monday: "The Minister has asked for assurance that the licensing service outage of 14 May cannot recur." (ii) Your director to you, Monday: "Please draft assurance for the Minister that the 14 May outage cannot recur." (iii) Your problem record, closed June: "Root cause: firmware defect in storage controller, vendor patch applied and verified. Residual risk: the same failure mode is eliminated; however, comparable multi-service impact could arise from other single points of failure in the shared storage layer, remediation of which is on the roadmap for Q3."

Which draft assurance is the only professionally defensible one?

- A) "The Minister may be assured that the outage cannot recur."
- B) "The specific cause of the 14 May outage — a storage controller firmware defect — has been remediated and verified, and that failure mode is eliminated. Comparable impact from related single points of failure in the shared storage layer remains possible until the remediation scheduled for Q3 completes; the interim controls are [X]. We can therefore assure that this outage cannot recur, and that the wider risk it revealed is scheduled for elimination with a defined date."
- C) "Recurrence is unlikely and the service is stable."
- D) "The outage cannot recur, though minor residual risks remain as with any system."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The trail sets a trap of scope: "this outage cannot recur" (true of the verified-patched firmware defect) has drifted, through two forwarding layers, toward "nothing like this can happen again" (false until Q3, per your own record). Each option manages the drift differently. Option A adopts the widest reading and signs it — contradicted by your own closed problem record, and discoverable the moment the Q3 roadmap or the next storage incident surfaces; ministerial assurances are precisely the documents that get re-read after the next failure, beside the record you held when you drafted them. Option D is subtler and worse in kind: it asserts the broad claim, then inoculates with a vague "minor residual risks as with any system" — but your record says the residual is *specific, comparable in impact, and scheduled for Q3 remediation*: describing a named, material risk as generic boilerplate is not caution, it is concealment with a disclaimer's grammar. Option C retreats into adjectives that assure nothing and would invite the private office's next question anyway. Option B does what defensible assurance always does: matches claim-scope to evidence-scope exactly — eliminated failure mode asserted plainly (you have the verification), residual risk named with its mitigation and date (you have the roadmap), and the whole structured so the Minister's office learns both what is safe and what is scheduled. Note that B is also the *politically* stronger draft: it converts the Q3 remediation into a ministerial commitment with a date, which protects its funding — honesty and advantage aligning, as they usually do one incident ahead. The drafting rule your role exercises weekly: assurance language must survive being read aloud at the inquiry into the *next* incident. Draft against that reading.

**Question 9 (hard) — True/false/cannot-say across a document stack**

Passage 1 (supplier master agreement, 2023): "Service credits are the customer's sole financial remedy for availability failures, save where failure results from supplier negligence."
Passage 2 (contract variation, January this year): "Clause 12 (service credits) is amended to increase credit rates as per Schedule A. All other provisions remain in force."
Passage 3 (supplier's letter, July): "Regarding the June outage: as service credits have been applied at the enhanced Schedule A rates, the matter is financially closed under the sole-remedy provision."

Statement: "If the June outage resulted from supplier negligence, the matter is not financially closed by the credits."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Build the stack. The 2023 agreement establishes sole-remedy *with a carve-out*: credits are the only financial remedy "save where failure results from supplier negligence" — negligence sits outside the sole-remedy fence. The variation amends credit *rates* and expressly preserves all other provisions: the carve-out survives intact. The supplier's letter then asserts closure "under the sole-remedy provision" — quietly citing the rule without its exception. Now the statement: it is *conditional* — "if the outage resulted from negligence..." — and conditionals are evaluated on their logic, not on whether the condition holds: given negligence, the carve-out disapplies sole-remedy, credits do not close the matter, and further financial remedy remains open. The conditional is guaranteed by the texts: true. The routes to error are instructive: "cannot say" typically comes from noticing that the passage never establishes whether negligence *occurred* — correct, and irrelevant: the statement doesn't assert negligence, it reasons from it (distinguishing a conditional's truth from its antecedent's truth is a core senior reading skill — most governance analysis is conditional: "if the audit finding is right...", "if the growth rate holds..."). "False" comes from accepting Passage 3's framing — and Passage 3 is the question's real teaching: it is not false, exactly; it is *selectively complete*, citing the provision while omitting the carve-out, and betting the reader remembers the rule better than the exception. Supplier correspondence after expensive incidents is drafted by people who have read the contract that morning; the head who accepts "financially closed" without re-reading clause 12 — carve-out and all — has delegated the organisation's remedies to the counterparty's summary. The practical sequel: whether negligence occurred is now the question that matters, which is why your incident evidence preservation (timelines, communications, the supplier's own status updates) turns out to be a commercial instrument, not just an operational one.

**Question 10 (hard) — Clear-writing judgement: the delegation you are drafting**

You are delegating out-of-hours authority to your three operational control managers. Which drafting best serves the function?

- A) "Operational control managers are empowered to take necessary action out of hours, exercising sound judgement, escalating where appropriate."
- B) "Out of hours, the duty operational control manager holds my delegated authority to: authorise emergency changes within the emergency procedure; invoke continuity runbooks; commit unplanned expenditure to £10k per incident; and stand down non-critical services to protect critical ones. Excluded and reserved to me, waking me if needed: public/ministerial communications; expenditure above £10k; accepting data-loss risk; and any decision to keep a citizen-facing service degraded beyond 2 hours for operational convenience. When a needed decision falls in a gap between these lists, take the reversible option, record the reasoning, and inform me by 09:00. This delegation is reviewed quarterly against the incidents that exercised it."
- C) "All decisions remain mine; call me for any out-of-hours matter of significance."
- D) "The duty manager may do whatever the situation requires; I trust my managers completely."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A delegation is a text whose entire job is to be executable at 03:00 by someone under pressure without its author present — evaluate the four drafts against that test. Options A and D are the same abdication in different registers: "necessary action", "sound judgement", "whatever the situation requires" delegate everything and nothing — the duty manager discovers the *actual* boundaries only afterwards, when a choice is retrospectively judged to have exceeded them, which teaches managers to under-act (the timid failure) or gamble (the career-ending one). Trust language without boundary language is not empowerment; it is transferred ambiguity. Option C is the anti-delegation: it converts every 03:00 decision into a phone call, making your availability the function's single point of failure — the v3.1 runbook defect from Question 7, generalised. Option B exhibits the full anatomy: *enumerated powers* with limits (£10k, within-procedure) so authority is knowable in advance; *enumerated reservations* with the waking instruction explicit — note what is reserved: communications, large money, data-loss acceptance, and prolonged citizen harm, i.e. the decisions whose consequences exceed an incident's boundaries; *a gap rule* — the mark of drafting maturity, because no enumeration is complete, and "reversible option, record, inform by 09:00" converts unforeseen cases from paralysis into principled action; and *a review cycle tied to actual exercise*, which makes the document a living control rather than a filed one. This is your role summary's delegation reality: "potentially be required to address issues across IT operations" works only if authority is distributed with this precision — and the drafting pattern (powers, reservations, gap rule, review) transfers to every authority document you own. A last test worth applying to your real delegations tonight: could your newest duty manager act on them at 03:00 without calling you? If not, the document is a description, not a delegation.

**Question 11 (hard) — The health-check narrative and its data**

Passage (from your own quarterly report to the directorate, drafted by your team): "Team health continues to improve. The latest Agile health check shows 'delivery confidence' up from 3.1 to 3.8 and 'tooling satisfaction' up from 2.9 to 3.4. 'Psychological safety' declined from 3.9 to 3.2, attributed to the restructure period, and is expected to recover. Overall, the team is in its strongest position for two years."

Which criticism of the draft is most important?

- A) The report should not include health-check data at all.
- B) The narrative's headline ("continues to improve", "strongest position in two years") is built on the two rising metrics while the steepest movement in the data — psychological safety falling 0.7, the largest single change in either direction — is subordinated, pre-attributed to a cause ("the restructure") without evidence offered, and dismissed with an expectation ("expected to recover") that no action supports. For a leadership audience, safety is the leading indicator the other two lag: teams stop reporting problems before they stop delivering — so the draft buries the one number that predicts the others' future, and it does so in a report *about* the team signed by the person the safety scores are partly about.
- C) The scores should be reported to one more decimal place.
- D) "Attributed to the restructure period" should name the individuals responsible.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Audit narrative against data, the same discipline as auditing conclusions against evidence in a post-incident review — but aimed at your own function's self-report, where it is hardest. The data: two metrics up (+0.7, +0.5), one down (−0.7). The narrative: improvement asserted twice, the decline framed as explained ("attributed" — by whom, on what evidence? attribution without basis is hope wearing analysis's clothes), and resolved by expectation ("expected to recover" — expectations are not actions; nothing in the draft *does* anything about the fall). B's deeper point is causal ordering: psychological safety is a leading indicator for an operations function specifically — it governs whether near-misses get reported, whether the health check itself stays honest, whether the 03:00 escalation happens — so a safety fall *predicts* future falls in the lagging metrics the narrative celebrates; "strongest position in two years" may be precisely backwards. And the conflict-of-interest layer: this is your report, about your team, in a period whose restructure was your decision — the document every reader will (rightly) read as partly self-assessed, which raises rather than lowers the required candour standard; a directorate that catches one buried decline re-reads all your previous reports. The defensible redraft leads with the decline, the investigation commissioned (skip-levels? exit-interview themes? — compare the attrition arithmetic from your numeric section: these data sets converge), the actions taken, and *then* the genuine improvements — a structure that costs one uncomfortable paragraph and buys the report's credibility whole. Option A throws away the instrument; C polishes irrelevant precision; D converts a systemic reading into a hunt. Your community collaboration skill — identify issues in team dynamics and *rectify* them — includes the textual form: the first rectification is refusing to let your own report look away.

**Question 12 (hard) — Supersession, scope and silence**

Passage 1 (departmental security standard, 2024): "Operational tooling holding citizen data must be hosted within approved UK regions. Exceptions require CISO approval, renewed annually."
Passage 2 (your tooling strategy, signed by you in March): "All monitoring and operational tooling procurements will comply with departmental security standards current at the time of procurement."
Passage 3 (procurement paper for your sign-off, this week): "The proposed incident-analytics platform processes telemetry in the supplier's EU region before storage in the UK region. Telemetry excludes citizen data except where incident records reference user identifiers. A CISO exception was granted to the pilot in 2024."

Statement: "Signing the procurement as presented is consistent with the documents."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Assemble what would need to be true, then check what the texts establish. The standard restricts tooling *holding citizen data* to UK regions, with annually-renewed CISO exceptions. Your strategy binds this procurement to standards "current at the time of procurement". The paper discloses EU-region *processing* and offers two comforts, each of which dissolves under exact reading. First comfort: telemetry "excludes citizen data *except where* incident records reference user identifiers" — an exception clause that concedes the trigger: some telemetry does include identifier-bearing data, so whether the standard applies turns on whether identifier-referencing telemetry constitutes "holding citizen data" and whether transient EU *processing* constitutes "hosting" — definitional questions the three passages do not answer. Second comfort: "a CISO exception was granted to the pilot in 2024" — two gaps in one clause: granted to the *pilot* (scope: does it cover this full procurement?) and in *2024* (currency: exceptions renew annually; is it renewed?). So consistency is not established — but neither is inconsistency: if the exception was renewed and extends to the procurement, or if the definitional questions resolve favourably, signing could be fully compliant. The texts leave it genuinely open: cannot say. Both wrong answers have head-level signatures: "true" is the busy signatory accepting comfort clauses at face value — precisely what the paper's drafting invites; "false" is the compliance reflex that blocks on suspicion without analysis, spending your governance credibility on a paper that may be one renewed exception from clean. The correct real-world action falls straight out of the reading: sign nothing yet; require the exception's current status and scope in writing, and a definitional ruling (CISO or information-governance) on identifier-bearing telemetry — two questions, answerable in days, converting "cannot say" into whichever verdict the facts support. This is the terminal form of the assessment's whole discipline: at your level, "cannot say" is not an answer you accept about your own estate — it is the finding that tells you exactly which questions to ask before your signature turns someone else's ambiguity into your commitment.

### Preparation tips

- **Build the assurance-verb lexicon.** Tested, reviewed, scheduled, addressed, considered, engaged: collect these from real papers that cross your desk, and annotate what each actually committed its author to. The gap between suggestion and commitment is most of senior verbal reasoning.
- **Read one of your own signed documents adversarially each month.** A delegation, a management response, an assurance: read it as the inquiry would after the next incident. Redraft the sentence that fails.
- **Practise conditionals separately from their antecedents.** Governance reasoning is mostly "if X, then what follows" — drill evaluating the logic without being distracted by whether X is true. Question 9's structure recurs constantly in contract and audit work.
- **Find the default in every procedural text.** Who acts without a decision, who bears the cost when the default is wrong. Procedural edits that "just change wording" usually move a default; make finding it reflexive.
- **Audit narrative against data in the next three reports you read.** Count the movements in the data, then check which ones the narrative leads with. The subordinated steepest movement is the finding — in others' reports and, hardest, in your own.
- **Draft for the 03:00 reader.** Every delegation, threshold and instruction you own: could your newest duty manager execute it without calling you? Rewrite until yes. This is simultaneously test preparation and the highest-value writing your role does.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Reading intent over text.** What a rule was meant to catch is amendment material; what it says is application material. Conflating them makes the written rules drafts and your judgement the real policy — expensive in both directions.
- **Accepting comfort clauses at face value.** "Exception granted", "excludes citizen data except...", "reviewed by the programme": every comfort clause has a scope and a currency. Check both before it comforts you.
- **Letting summaries drift toward their repeaters' preferences.** Strategies are enacted through their summaries. Correct drifted ones every time, including your own.
- **Scope drift in assurance.** "This outage cannot recur" and "nothing like this can happen" are different promises. Match claim-scope to evidence-scope exactly, especially in anything ministerial.
- **Vague trust as delegation.** "Sound judgement" and "necessary action" transfer ambiguity, not authority. Powers, reservations, gap rule, review cycle — or it isn't a delegation.
- **Exempting your own documents from your own standards.** The self-report that buries its steepest decline, the strategy summary you let slide, the response you drafted in a hurry: your function's reading discipline is set by what you sign, not what you say.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment (SJT) presents realistic scenarios and asks you to evaluate responses — most effective, least effective, ranked, or rated. Scoring is calibrated against experienced senior practitioners and leadership competency frameworks; options are rarely wrong so much as *differently costly*, and discrimination happens at the level of second-order and organisational effects.

At head-of-function level, the scenario space changes character again. The questions are less about what you do in the room and more about what your choices do to the *system*: what your handling of one manager's failure teaches every manager; what your response to one inconvenient finding does to the probability you are shown the next one; which precedents your exceptions set; how your function's relationships with delivery, audit, suppliers and the centre compound over years. Time horizons stretch — many head-level dilemmas trade this quarter against next year — and almost every scenario contains a version of the same underlying question: will you spend short-term comfort to protect long-term system health, or the reverse?

For your role the mapping is direct. You are responsible for the strategy roadmap (dilemmas of investment, sequencing and sunk cost), integration (dilemmas of standardisation against local autonomy), and building the capability of the team (dilemmas of development against delivery, retention against fairness, succession against loyalty). You hold service compliance and risk (dilemmas of enforcement, exception and disclosure), you lead across IT operations (dilemmas of authority across boundaries you influence but do not command), and you own excellent user outcomes (dilemmas where user interest, organisational convenience and political pressure diverge). The scenarios below are drawn from exactly this territory. Practising them deliberately — with attention to the reasoning, not just the answer — is rehearsal for the moments where your function's culture is actually set.

A calibration note: at this level assessors consistently reward responses that keep accountability attached to authority, protect the integrity of information flows (upward especially), invest in system fixes over instance fixes, make exceptions explicit and priced rather than quiet, and absorb appropriate personal cost to keep organisational signals honest. They penalise comfort purchased with concealment, precedent set by inattention, delegation without authority, and any resolution that teaches people to stop telling you things.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Strategy and investment dilemmas** map to the **strategy roadmap** and **Service focus**: sunk-cost decisions, sequencing under pressure, and defending long-horizon investment against short-horizon demands.
- **Capability and people dilemmas** map to **building the capability of the team** and **Community collaboration**: development versus delivery, succession, retention, feedback that lands, and the health of the team as operational infrastructure.
- **Governance and integrity dilemmas** map to **Asset and configuration management** (service compliance and risk), **Change management** and **Ownership and initiative**: enforcement, exception-setting, disclosure of inconvenient findings, and the honesty of what your function reports.
- **Cross-boundary dilemmas** map to your duty to **address issues across IT operations** and your **expert service management framework knowledge**: exercising influence where you lack command, holding standards across organisational seams, and stewarding process as an asset.
- **Incident and continuity leadership dilemmas** map to **Incident management**, **Problem management** and **Continuity management** at head level: your role during majors, the standards you set for reviews, and assurance under launch pressure.
- **User-outcome dilemmas** map to **User focus** and **Availability and capacity management**: spending availability, weighing minorities, and representing end users when they are not in the room.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Your manager's first major incident, going wrong slowly**

You are visible in the incident channel as a SEV1 unfolds. Your newest operational control manager is leading it — their first major — and their sequencing is suboptimal: they have teams working two hypotheses you judge less likely, and the bridge is slightly behind the incident. Nothing dangerous is happening, but resolution is probably 30–45 minutes slower on this path. What is the most effective response?

- A) Take over the incident lead; users are affected and speed matters.
- B) Stay out entirely; they must learn, and intervention undermines them.
- C) Enter the bridge briefly as a resource, not a replacement: one or two targeted questions ("what would rule out the storage path?") that reshape the reasoning without seizing it, explicit public confidence in their lead, availability signalled — then out, with a proper debrief tomorrow.
- D) Send them private step-by-step instructions through the channel so the bridge doesn't see your involvement.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Compute the real stakes on both sides. The incident's marginal cost of the suboptimal path: 30–45 minutes, explicitly non-dangerous. The developmental stakes: a first major is where an incident leader's identity forms — and where the *team's* belief in that manager forms. Option A spends both to buy back half an hour: the manager learns that majors are the head's job, the team learns their manager leads only until it matters, and every future incident inherits that lesson — a system cost dwarfing the instance benefit (and note your role summary's phrasing: you lead the *building of capability*, which sometimes means paying tuition in minutes of resolution time). Option B overcorrects into abandonment: "learning" without feedback loops is just experience, and a head visibly present but silent while the bridge struggles reads as either indifference or a test. Option D is the worst of both — covert puppetry: the manager executes your reasoning without owning it, learns dependence rather than judgement, and if the channel's members infer the ventriloquism (they usually do), their manager's authority is spent anyway, invisibly. Option C is coaching at operational tempo: questions that upgrade the reasoning while leaving the decisions — and the credit — with the leader; public confidence that *strengthens* their authority precisely when it is fragile; and the debrief where the real teaching happens, out of the pressure, against the timeline. The head-level pattern: in every "your subordinate is doing it imperfectly" scenario, the examined variable is rarely the task — it is what your intervention style does to authority, learning and the team's model of who owns what.

**Question 2 (easy) — The finding you could quietly absorb**

Preparing your annual assurance statement, your compliance lead brings you a discovery: for eight months, a monitoring exclusion list — services deliberately excluded from alerting during a migration — was never cleaned up, and two citizen services ran with materially reduced monitoring. Nothing failed during the window; the gap is now closed. Your assurance statement could truthfully say monitoring controls "are operating effectively" *as of today*. What is the most effective response?

- A) Report controls effective as of today; the gap is closed and nothing failed.
- B) Include the gap in the assurance statement as a control failure discovered and remediated — duration, exposure, why it persisted (no expiry mechanism on exclusion lists), and the systemic fix now implemented (exclusions require end dates and appear on a weekly review) — and commend the compliance lead's discovery visibly.
- C) Include the gap but frame it as a "process improvement opportunity" rather than a failure.
- D) Omit it from the statement but fix the exclusion process quietly.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Separate what the statement is *for* from what it *says*. Assurance exists so that those relying on it can calibrate their trust in your controls; a statement that reports point-in-time truth ("effective as of today") while omitting a known eight-month failure is technically accurate and functionally misleading — the reader's calibration is wrong, and options A and D differ only in self-awareness. The disclosure case is also brutally practical: exclusion lists leave traces (audit sampling, the next engineer who reads the config history, the compliance lead's own records), and an omitted control failure discovered later converts a remediated operational miss into an integrity finding about *you* — the head who knew. Option C is the framing temptation: "improvement opportunity" is the assurance-language equivalent of Question 8's verbal-section boilerplate — describing a specific, material control failure in genre-vocabulary that prevents the reader from weighing it; softened disclosure is disclosure defeated. Option B's components are each load-bearing: duration and exposure let readers calibrate; the *mechanism* (no expiry on exclusions) turns an embarrassing instance into a transferable lesson — this exact defect (temporary states without expiry dates becoming permanent) recurs across operations, and naming it is your problem-management duty applied to your own function; the systemic fix demonstrates the control loop closing; and commending the discoverer is the cultural keystone — the compliance lead who watched what disclosure cost you decides what to bring you next year. That last effect is the question's real subject: heads are shown inconvenient findings at exactly the rate their handling of previous findings deserves. Option B buys, at the price of one uncomfortable paragraph, the continued honesty of your own information supply — the asset your entire role runs on.

**Question 3 (easy) — The roadmap versus the loud quarter**

Six months into the year, delivery pressure across the organisation is intense, and your director suggests: "Pause the trend-to-action automation and the capability programme this quarter and lend those people to the delivery push — the roadmap can absorb a quarter's slip." Your data: the automation is the highest-return item on your roadmap (the 4.5:1 case from your numeric practice), and the capability programme is the fix for the qualified-lead crunch arriving in month 8. What is the most effective response?

- A) Agree fully — directors see the whole picture, and one quarter is recoverable.
- B) Refuse fully — the roadmap is signed strategy and lending operations staff to delivery sets a precedent.
- C) Come back with a differentiated counter-offer built on the numbers: identify what genuinely can slip a quarter cheaply (offer it), show why these two items specifically cannot — the automation's per-quarter deferral cost in prevented incidents, the capability programme's month-8 coverage cliff with its 9-month pipeline — and propose the equivalent staffing from the deferrable work, so the director gets the help and the roadmap keeps its two load-bearing items.
- D) Agree publicly but quietly keep both programmes running at reduced pace, trusting the director won't check.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The director's request contains a category error your data can fix: it treats the roadmap as uniformly deferrable, when its items carry radically different deferral costs. The automation defers at roughly £45k of expected incident cost per quarter (three majors' worth of prevention delayed); the capability programme defers into a *cliff* — the month-8 coverage gap has a 9-month pipeline behind it, so a quarter's pause converts a managed transition into a guaranteed breach (lead-time arithmetic again: some slips cost their duration; slips against pipelines cost their duration *plus* the cliff). Other roadmap items — the 18-month tooling revisit, documentation work, the deferred consolidation phases — genuinely can slip at near-zero cost. Option C trades on exactly this differential: it gives the director real help (which the delivery push may genuinely warrant — option B's flat refusal treats roadmap sanctity as self-evident, spends relationship capital defending your *least* valuable items alongside your most, and teaches the director to route around you next time), while defending the two items whose deferral costs are demonstrable — and it defends them with arithmetic, which directors can carry upward, rather than with assertion, which they cannot. Option A pays the cliff cost invisibly now and visibly in month 8, when "we lent the trainers to the delivery push" will not soften the coverage breach. Option D is the disqualifier: covert non-compliance with an agreed direction — if discovered, you lose the trust that makes every future counter-offer credible; if undiscovered, you have still taught yourself that agreements with your director are optional, a habit with one trajectory. The head-level template: never defend a portfolio uniformly; price the items, concede the cheap ones loudly, and hold the load-bearing ones with numbers.

**Question 4 (moderate) — The star manager and the health-check pattern**

Your strongest operational control manager — best incident leader, best delivery record — shows a pattern in team data: their unit's health-check safety scores are the centre's lowest for the third consecutive cycle, attrition in their team is double the centre's average, and two exit interviews this quarter named the manager's behaviour under pressure ("public dressings-down after incidents") specifically. When you raised the previous cycle informally, they said: "My team performs. The scores reflect high standards." What is the most effective response?

- A) Accept the framing — performance is real, standards are high, and some attrition is the price of excellence.
- B) Move to a formal performance conversation: the pattern (three cycles, double attrition, named behaviours) is now data, not impression; the behaviour is a defined problem with defined change expectations, a support offer (coaching), a review timeline, and clarity that continued pattern has consequences — while being equally explicit about what is valued and worth keeping.
- C) Restructure to move the affected team members away from the manager, preserving the manager's delivery.
- D) Wait one more cycle to be certain the pattern is real.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Run the evidence test the manager's framing invites you to skip. "High standards produce low safety scores" is a testable claim, and this data refutes it: high-standards teams show high challenge *and* high safety (that is what the health check's design measures — compare your verbal section's leading-indicator logic); low safety plus double attrition plus *specific named behaviours* in exits is not rigour's signature, it is attrition-by-fear — and the two exit interviews have converted an impression into a documented pattern. The costs are operational, not soft: that unit under-reports near-misses (safety scores predict incident-reporting honesty), bleeds trained analysts into your pipeline arithmetic at £45k a head, and — the compounding cost — every analyst in the centre knows the scores, knows the exits, and is watching what the head does about a star. Option A prices excellence at the cost of exactly the capability your role exists to build, and teaches the centre that delivery buys behavioural immunity — after which your values documents are decorative. Option D requests a fourth cycle of certainty at ~2 further leavers per cycle; three cycles, corroborated by exits, *is* certainty for management purposes (the "more analysis" procrastination from your cognitive section, wearing HR clothing). Option C is the revealing wrong answer: restructuring around a behaviour problem preserves the behaviour, ships it to a new team, tells the exited team members the organisation saw and chose the manager, and converts a fixable person-problem into a permanent architecture-problem. Option B is management doing its actual job: pattern named as data, expectations and support both explicit, timeline and consequences real, *and* the genuine strengths affirmed — because the goal is recovering the whole manager, and because fairness to them requires the conversation be as clear about what is valued as what must change. Community collaboration in your summary — identify issues in team dynamics and rectify them — has a formal register; this is the scenario where heads either use it or teach everyone it doesn't exist.

**Question 5 (moderate) — The exception that wants to become policy**

A delivery programme with ministerial visibility asks to bypass your release policy's soak-test requirement "just for this launch window" — with a credible technical argument that this specific release's rollback is trivially fast, making the soak test's risk-reduction marginal for this case. Your policy has no fast-track lane. Two other programmes are watching the answer. What is the most effective response?

- A) Refuse: the policy is the policy, and exceptions unravel it.
- B) Approve quietly for this programme only, given the ministerial pressure, and ask them not to publicise it.
- C) Approve it as a *formal, recorded* exception with the risk assessment attached and the compensating condition explicit (the trivial rollback, verified, plus enhanced post-release monitoring) — and use the case to commission the policy amendment it has revealed: a defined fast-track lane with criteria, so the next qualifying release has a legitimate route and the next non-qualifying one has a clear refusal.
- D) Approve it and simultaneously waive the requirement for the two watching programmes to be fair.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The request has two properties that must be handled separately: it is *individually reasonable* (the technical argument is credible — a trivially-fast, verified rollback genuinely changes the soak test's value calculus) and it is *systemically dangerous* (two programmes watching means your answer is precedent regardless of what you intend). Each option manages one property and fumbles the other — except C. Option A honours the system and wastes the case: refusing a technically sound exception teaches programmes that your governance cannot distinguish risk levels, which is the argument *for* routing around it; policies that cannot flex on evidence get evaded rather than amended (your operational-control-level practice met this as the change-classification gaming — the same dynamic one level up). Option B grants the substance and corrupts the form: a quiet exception is a secret rule — the two watching programmes will learn of it (they always do), and what they learn is that exceptions are won by pressure and discretion, not criteria; you have created the fast-track lane anyway, but unwritten, unpriced and awarded by ministerial weight. Option D generalises before analysing: the watchers' releases may not share the trivial-rollback property that justifies this exception — "fairness" between unlike cases is exactly how compensating conditions get detached from the risks they compensate. Option C is exception-handling as governance craft: *recorded* (the exception is visible, auditable, citable), *conditioned* (the justifying property is verified, not asserted, and the compensating control priced in), and — the head-level move — *metabolised*: a reasonable request your policy cannot accommodate is a policy defect discovered in the wild, and the amendment (fast-track criteria) converts one programme's pressure into everyone's legitimate route. The durable principle: every exception request is simultaneously a decision and a data point about your rules; decide the case on its evidence, and feed the data point into the policy — quietly absorbed exceptions become shadow policy, and shadow policy is set by whoever pushes hardest.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Cross-boundary standards without command**

A sister division's operations team — outside your line management — runs monitoring practices that create risk for services *you* are accountable for: their alerting on a shared dependency is configured to notify only their own rota, and during a recent incident your centre learned of the dependency failure eleven minutes late. Their head, your peer, responds to your informal raise with: "Our configuration works for our services. Your eleven minutes is an integration issue, not our problem." What is the most effective response?

- A) Escalate immediately to your shared director with the incident evidence.
- B) Accept the boundary — their estate, their rules — and build detection redundancy on your side only.
- C) Reframe from blame to shared interest before escalating: bring the peer a specific, low-cost proposal (dual-notification on the shared dependency's alerts — a configuration change, not a process change) with the incident timeline showing the mutual exposure (their services depend on the dependency too; next time the eleven minutes may run the other way), and propose making shared-dependency alerting a standing agreement between the centres; escalate jointly-framed ("we need a cross-division standard") only if the peer declines the near-free fix.
- D) Have your engineers subscribe to their alert streams without asking, since the technical access exists.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Your role summary's phrase — "potentially be required to address issues across IT operations" — describes exactly this terrain: accountability that exceeds authority, where influence craft is the actual skill. Diagnose the peer's response first: "not our problem" is wrong on the facts (shared dependency: their services carry the same exposure with roles reversed), which means the disagreement is *informational* before it is political — and option C's move is to make the mutual exposure visible with the timeline before anyone spends escalation capital. The proposal's design does the persuading: dual-notification is a configuration change (near-zero cost to them), narrowly scoped to the shared dependency (no sovereignty claim over their estate), and framed as standing agreement (institutionalised, so it survives both your tenures) — you have made agreement cheaper than continued refusal. Option A escalates a peer conflict upward *first*, which converts a solvable configuration gap into a political event: the shared director now owns a dispute between heads, both heads look unable to collaborate, and even winning costs you the peer relationship every future shared-dependency issue will need. (Escalation is C's *fallback*, jointly-framed as a standards gap rather than a complaint — arriving at the director as system improvement, not tattling.) Option B is the quiet failure: unilateral redundancy is worth building anyway (defence in depth), but as the *whole* answer it accepts a permanent eleven-minute handicap and misses that the systemic fix costs almost nothing. Option D is the disqualifier in technical clothing: unauthorised subscription to another division's alert streams — technically feasible, organisationally corrosive — converts your standing from "peer with a fair request" to "head who helps himself to our systems", poisoning precisely the cooperation the fix requires, and it forfeits the standing-agreement layer that makes the fix durable. The cross-boundary template: mutual-exposure evidence, near-free specific proposal, institutionalised agreement, joint escalation as fallback — in that order, because each step preserves the relationship the next one needs.

**Question 7 (moderate) — The launch pressure returns, one level up**

The ministerial launch from your cognitive section arrives at decision week. Your differentiated assurance (verified/unverified/mitigated/committed) has gone to the SRO — who now phones you directly: "The programme board reads your statement as a yellow light. The Minister has announced the date. I need you to move your assurance to green, or the delay is being attributed to command and control." What is the most effective response?

- A) Move to green — the SRO owns the launch decision and has heard the risks.
- B) Hold the statement exactly as written, restating that its clauses are evidenced; offer the fastest legitimate path to a stronger statement (the failure-scenario validation can complete in 10 days — a dated plan to upgrade the specific unverified clause); make explicit that the launch decision with the current risk profile is the SRO's to take and that your function will support launch fully under either decision; and record the exchange.
- C) Move to green but email your director afterwards noting you did so under pressure.
- D) Refuse and go to the Minister's office directly to correct the record about where delay risk originates.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Parse what is actually being requested: not a launch decision — the SRO already owns that, and your statement doesn't block it — but a *change to your evidence* so the decision reads as riskless. That reframing decides the scenario. An assurance statement's value is that its clauses mean what they say; "moving to green" without the underlying verification would make your function's assurance a negotiable instrument, worthless in exactly the situations it exists for — and option A's comfort ("the SRO has heard the risks") dissolves on inspection: after the statement turns green, the record shows the risks *withdrawn*, and if the unvalidated failure scenarios materialise at launch, the inquiry reads a green assurance you knew was unearned (compare the verbal section's rule: draft against being read aloud at the next incident's inquiry). Option C is the same capitulation with a self-protective memo attached — the private note doesn't restore the SRO's decision-basis or the Minister's; it just proves you knew, which is worse. Option D mistakes the escalation ladder for a weapon: going around the SRO to the Minister's office converts an assurance disagreement into open political warfare, destroys the SRO relationship every future launch needs, and — decisive — is unnecessary, because B's path hasn't been exhausted. Option B holds the only line that matters (the statement says what the evidence says) while doing everything legitimate to help: the 10-day validation plan is the constructive core — it shows the yellow clause is *upgradeable by work, not by pressure*, and hands the SRO a real option (launch now on the honest profile, or launch 10 days evidenced — both defensible); the explicit "the decision is yours, we support either" returns the accountability to where it belongs and refuses the attribution game ("delay attributed to command and control" is pressure's grammar — your answer is a dated plan, which makes any delay a choice with a named price, not your obstruction); and recording the exchange is not paranoia but governance hygiene for precisely such conversations. The head-level principle, worth stating because every senior role meets this scenario eventually: *your assurance is not yours to negotiate — it belongs to everyone who will ever rely on your function's word.* Spend relationship capital, spend convenience, spend the uncomfortable phone call; never spend the meaning of your statements.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Succession and the internal favourite**

Your deputy — loyal, long-serving, competent, and the person who covered your role well during your absence last year — expects the operational control manager vacancy you are about to fill. Your honest assessment: they are strong operationally but have two genuine development gaps against the role (stakeholder influence, and the analytical depth your best external candidates show), and the open recruitment your organisation's rules require has produced a visibly stronger external field. Your deputy has hinted that being passed over would "probably be the sign to move on." What is the most effective response?

- A) Appoint the deputy — retention of a proven insider outweighs marginal candidate differences, and loyalty deserves reward.
- B) Run the process straight, and *invest* in the deputy regardless of outcome: before the panel, an honest conversation about the field's strength and their genuine gaps (which they deserve to hear from you, not infer from a rejection letter); if they are not selected, a serious, resourced development plan targeted at the two gaps with a credible path to the *next* role — and acceptance that they may still leave, which is a real cost you take knowingly rather than a threat that buys an appointment.
- C) Quietly discourage the strongest external candidates so the process produces the deputy legitimately.
- D) Appoint the strongest candidate and let HR handle the deputy's disappointment through normal channels.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Name the pressure precisely: the hint ("the sign to move on") is a retention threat entering a merit process, and the question is whether appointments in your function are decisions or transactions. Option A's real price is invisible until listed: the role gets a manager with known gaps in a centre whose gaps you have been arithmetically tracking all guide long (a weaker appointment is a *capacity* decision); the stronger candidates learn your processes are pre-decided (the external field talks — your future fields weaken); and every ambitious insider learns the actual currency is tenure plus threat, which reprices loyalty across the function. Option C reaches A's outcome through process corruption — a rigged competition is worse than no competition, converting an arguable appointment into an integrity breach with witnesses. Option D gets the appointment right and the leadership wrong: "let HR handle it" outsources the most consequential conversation of your deputy's decade to a process letter — the deputy who covered your role has earned candour from *you*, and D's coldness converts a probably-survivable disappointment into a certain departure, with the story travelling. Option B pays every legitimate cost and no illegitimate one. The pre-panel conversation is the hinge: honest calibration *before* the outcome preserves the deputy's dignity and agency (they can withdraw, compete with open eyes, or start on the gaps now) and drains the result of its ambush quality. The post-decision investment answers the retention concern in the only durable currency — a resourced path to the next role is a bet on them; an appointment conceded to a threat is a payment, and payments recur. And B's final clause is the senior part: accepting they may leave anyway. Heads who cannot tolerate that possibility are governed by it — every merit process in the function is thereafter hostage to whoever hints hardest. Building the capability of the team, your summary's charge, includes the succession pipeline's *integrity*: the people-decisions everyone watches are where that integrity is actually built, or spent.

**Question 9 (hard) — The supplier failure with a co-authored cause**

Your major supplier's platform failed for six hours last month — the worst outage of the year, citizen-visible, ministerial questions asked. Your post-incident analysis, honestly done, finds a co-authored cause: the supplier's change collided with a configuration your own team had modified outside the agreed change window, undocumented; either act alone would have been survivable. Commercially, the sole-remedy carve-out (from your verbal practice) is in play — the supplier's negligence is arguable, and your director wants to pursue maximum recovery, asking you to "keep the analysis focused on their change." The full facts, if surfaced, weaken the commercial claim and embarrass your function. What is the most effective response?

- A) Keep the analysis focused as directed — the supplier's change was genuinely the trigger, your team's contribution is arguable context, and the commercial recovery serves the public purse.
- B) Produce the complete analysis — both causes, documented — and advise your director that the commercial strategy must be built on the full facts: pursue recovery for the supplier's share on honest ground (their change, their process failures), disclose and fix your own team's window breach through your normal problem process, and let the commercial negotiation price both truths — because a claim built on a curated analysis collapses when the supplier's lawyers find the configuration history, taking your credibility and the legitimate half of the claim with it.
- C) Bury both the analysis and the claim — pursuing recovery risks exposing your team's breach, so let the credits stand as settlement.
- D) Disclose your team's contribution to the supplier informally and privately, peer to peer, so the negotiation stays friendly.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The scenario stress-tests whether analytical integrity survives commercial and hierarchical pressure simultaneously — and the practical answer is that integrity *is* the commercial strategy, correctly understood. Walk the discovery logic that option B makes explicit: configuration changes leave histories; a six-hour, ministerially-visible outage guarantees the supplier's own investigation will be thorough; their analysts will find the undocumented modification because that is what competent post-incident analysis does (your own team just demonstrated it). A claim built on the curated version therefore has a failure mode worse than weakness — *collapse at maximum exposure*: mid-negotiation or mid-dispute, the counterparty produces your own configuration history, your analysis is revealed as selective, and everything else your function has ever asserted to them reprices — including the legitimate claim for the supplier's genuine share, which was always winnable on honest ground (their change, their notice failures, their process gaps stand regardless of your team's contribution; co-authorship reduces recovery, it doesn't eliminate it). So option A isn't even the cynical-but-effective choice; it is the strategy with the worst expected value, dressed as pragmatism — and "keep the analysis focused" is your director asking you to do with a post-incident review what Question 7's SRO asked you to do with an assurance: adjust the evidence to fit the desired conclusion. The same answer applies, with the same structure: hold the document, give the stakeholder a better path built on the truth. Option C overcorrects into a different failure — abandoning legitimate public recovery to hide your own breach converts your team's process failure into your institutional cover-up, and the analysis exists; suppressed analyses surface. Option D has a genuine instinct (transparency) deployed through the wrong channel: informal peer disclosure of legally material facts, ahead of your own organisation's position, gives away the negotiation's information structure without authority — candour in negotiations is a *strategy decision* made with your commercial and legal colleagues, not a personal kindness. B's internal component completes it: your team's window breach gets the normal problem treatment (why was an out-of-window change possible and invisible? — that is a controls gap with your name on it), because the embarrassing half of a co-authored cause is also the half you can actually fix. The head-level principle binding Questions 7 and 9: *the documents your function produces — assurances, analyses, records — are load-bearing for people who cannot see the load. Every pressure to curate them is a request to transfer risk, secretly, to whoever relies on them next.*

**Question 10 (hard) — Least effective: the quiet quarter**

An unusually calm quarter: no majors, KPIs green, the board pack writes itself. You have genuine discretionary time for the first time in a year. Four candidate uses:

(1) Run the exercise programme your continuity duty implies but incidents keep displacing: a full failover test on the flagship service, a no-notice out-of-hours invocation drill, and a tabletop for the scenario your risk register rates highest but has never rehearsed.
(2) Advance the roadmap's deferred analytical work: the tooling fitness reviews, the monitoring-coverage reconciliation from the anomaly question, the problem-closure category redesign.
(3) Invest the quarter in the team: the delayed development conversations, skip-levels across all units, the succession and pipeline work your workforce arithmetic flagged.
(4) Use the calm to tighten reporting: rebuild the board pack around the weighted-availability and leading-indicator work, annotate the measurement breaks, pre-empt next quarter's questions.

Which is the LEAST effective use of the quiet quarter?

- A) Option (1)
- B) Option (2)
- C) Option (3)
- D) Option (4)

**Correct answer: D** (least effective)

**Explanation:** All four are legitimate — the question forces portfolio judgement about *what calm is for*, and the discrimination runs on two axes: which work is only possible in calm, and which failure modes are currently compounding. Option (1) scores highest on the first axis: exercises, drills and failover tests structurally require quiet — they are precisely the work that incident pressure displaces (the scenario says so: "incidents keep displacing"), and an untested flagship failover plus an unrehearsed top-rated risk are exactly the exposures a calm quarter exists to retire; if the next quarter is loud, this window may not return for a year. Option (3) scores high on both axes: development conversations and succession work also need calm (they are the first casualties of operational pressure), and the pipeline arithmetic says the people-exposures are compounding *now* — every month of deferred capability work is a month of the month-8 cliff. Option (2) is solid mid-table: the analytical work matters and benefits from focus, but it is *calendar-flexible* — fitness reviews and category redesigns survive being done in a moderately busy quarter; little of it is calm-dependent, and none of it compounds dangerously in a single quarter. Option (4) is least effective — not because reporting hygiene is worthless (the weighted-availability honesty is real, and it appeared as the *right answer* in your numeric section when the alternative was misreporting), but because of what it displaces here: given one rare quarter of genuine discretionary capacity, spending it making the *description* of the function better while the function's untested failover, unrehearsed risks and compounding people-exposures wait is polishing the map instead of the territory — and reporting work is the most schedulable of the four (it improves incrementally, tolerates interruption, and much of it can be delegated to exactly the analytical staff option 2 would deploy). The senior signature being tested: heads reveal their real priorities in discretionary time — and the temptation ranking runs opposite to the value ranking, because reporting work is visible upward while drills at 03:00 are not. A useful closing heuristic for calm periods, worth carrying out of the assessment: *spend quiet on what only quiet enables, and on what compounds while you wait — visibility can be earned in any quarter; readiness and people can't.*

**Question 11 (hard) — The user harm nobody is measuring**

A routine deep-dive by one of your analysts surfaces something the dashboards don't show: the licensing service's assisted-digital channel — the phone-supported route used disproportionately by elderly and vulnerable users, stable at ~7% of transactions in your channel data — has a completion rate that has quietly fallen from 84% to 61% over a year. Cause: three separate "digital-first" changes, each individually approved, each fine for the majority channel, each adding friction to the assisted route (an extra verification step, a shortened session timeout, a callback queue change). No SLA covers assisted-digital completion; no board metric captures it; the service owner (a peer, different directorate) considers the digital-first programme a success on its measured KPIs. What is the most effective response?

- A) Note the finding and file it — no SLA is breached, the service owner's KPIs are green, and cross-directorate measurement gaps are above your pay grade.
- B) Fix what is in your estate quietly (the callback queue change is yours) and leave the rest.
- C) Treat it as the systemic finding it is, on three fronts: immediately, take the evidence to the service owner peer-to-peer — completion-rate series, the three attributable changes, the affected population — framed as "your programme's measured success is hiding an unmeasured harm; here is the data you'd want to see"; structurally, propose assisted-digital completion become a measured KPI with a floor, so the next three changes can't repeat the pattern invisibly; and internally, revert or remediate the change in your own estate now, and commend the analyst's deep-dive visibly — this is exactly the proactive detection your centre exists for.
- D) Escalate directly to the service owner's director with the data, since vulnerable users are being harmed and speed matters more than protocol.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Recognise the failure mode first, because it is the deepest one in this guide: *aggregate optimisation harming an unmeasured minority* — each change individually approved, individually rational, collectively catastrophic for a population that appears in no KPI. This is the weighted-average lesson (your numeric section), the assisted-digital caveat (your cognitive strategy question) and the KPI-construction principle ("whoever chooses the weights decides whose experience the organisation sees") converging on a live case — and your role summary speaks to it directly: *meeting the needs of users across a variety of channels*, *insight into how user needs have changed over time*, *apply strategic thinking to provide the best service for the end user*. A 23-point completion fall means roughly one in four assisted-channel users — the users least able to route around failure — now fail to complete a licensing transaction they previously could. Option A is the abdication with paperwork: "no SLA is breached" is precisely the problem (the absence of the measure *is* the finding), and "above my pay grade" inverts your duty to address issues across IT operations. Option B fixes a third of the harm silently and leaves the mechanism intact — the next digital-first change re-runs the pattern, and the silence wastes the finding's systemic value. Option D has the right urgency and the wrong first move: going over the service owner's head with data they have never seen converts a peer who would likely act (their programme harming vulnerable users is a fact they need before their director does — most owners, shown this series, move) into a defensive party, and it spends the escalation before the cheap move is tried; escalation remains C's implicit fallback if the peer dismisses the evidence. Option C's three fronts map the full repair: *instance* (revert your own change now — you don't need anyone's permission for your own estate), *relationship-mediated instance* (the peer conversation, framed as giving them the data their measures deny them — the same mutual-interest craft as the cross-boundary question), and *system* (the KPI-with-floor proposal, which is the only front that prevents recurrence — measured populations are protected populations). The commendation clause is not decoration: an analyst who deep-dives past green dashboards and finds this deserves to become a story the centre tells, because your function's entire value is noticing what the measures miss — and analysts repeat what heads celebrate. If one question in this guide is the role's essence, it is this one: command and control exists to see what the organisation isn't looking at, and to make it looked-at.

**Question 12 (hard) — Most and least: the last morning of the quarter**

08:30, quarter-end Friday. Four items compete for your personal morning:

(i) Your director calls: a peer head-of-function role is suddenly vacant, and you are asked to give a view *today* on absorbing that function into yours — doubling your scope, with a board paper drafted by Monday.
(ii) The month-8 qualified-lead cliff from your workforce arithmetic arrives early: two resignations landed this morning; next month's on-call coverage for incident leads is now arithmetically impossible without intervention.
(iii) The assurance statement for the ministerial launch (Questions 7's descendant) is due for final signature by noon — your 10-day validation completed yesterday; the results are on your desk, unread.
(iv) Your best operational control manager asks for an hour: they have been offered the vacant peer role in (i) and want your honest counsel before responding today.

Candidate approaches:

(1) Morning on (i) — the scope decision shapes everything else; delegate (iii)'s reading to your deputy, hold (ii) until the workforce meeting Monday, fit (iv) into fifteen minutes.
(2) Sequence by irreversibility and dependency: read (iii) first — one hour, the noon signature is yours alone, and yesterday's validation results are the signature's entire evidential basis; then (iv) in full — the counsel is time-bound today, it directly informs (i) (whether your best manager leaves changes the absorption question's answer), and they asked for honesty, which takes the hour; then give the director a structured interim on (i) — "view by Monday requires these three facts, here is my preliminary frame" — declining the same-day verdict; and convert (ii) today into a 30-minute triage (interim coverage options commissioned from your managers, decision Monday) because the cliff is next month, not next week.
(3) Morning on (ii) — people first, always; ask for the assurance deadline to move, postpone (iv), send the director a holding note.
(4) Morning on (iv) and (i) together — the succession and scope questions are one conversation; sign (iii) on the strength of the team's verbal summary that validation "went fine"; slot (ii) for Monday.

Which is the MOST effective approach, and which is the LEAST effective?

- A) Most: (2); Least: (4)
- B) Most: (1); Least: (3)
- C) Most: (2); Least: (3)
- D) Most: (4); Least: (1)

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Score each approach against the morning's actual structure — deadlines, irreversibility, dependencies, and what only you can do. Approach (2) is the most effective because it alone gets all four properties right. It reads (iii) *personally and first*: the noon signature is the morning's only irreversible act with your name on it, and signing an assurance whose completed validation results you haven't read would repeat — in private, with no pressure as excuse — exactly the failure Question 7 resisted in public; the validation was commissioned *to be the evidence*, so the hour of reading is the signature (note the echo of the launch-assurance principle: the statement means what the evidence says, which requires knowing what the evidence says). It gives (iv) the full hour *second*: the manager's decision is genuinely today-bound, they asked for honest counsel (a head's honest counsel about a role that would promote them elsewhere is career-defining trust — fifteen rushed minutes teaches them what the relationship weighs), and — the dependency insight — their answer is *input* to (i): a scope-absorption view drafted before knowing whether your strongest manager is leaving is built on stale org charts. It converts (i) from same-day verdict to structured interim — which is not delay but *decision hygiene*: doubling your function's scope is the largest decision on the board, and "a view today" for a Monday paper is exactly the pressure-tempo at which heads absorb functions they shouldn't; naming the three facts the view depends on (including (iv)'s outcome and (ii)'s capability picture) is the senior answer the director actually needs. And it right-sizes (ii): two resignations against a next-month cliff is urgent-this-week, not urgent-this-morning — a 30-minute commissioning of options (borrow, accelerate, re-roster) puts the machine to work and reserves the decision for Monday's full information; your workforce arithmetic already mapped this terrain, which is why it takes 30 minutes and not three hours. Approach (4) is least effective because its treatment of (iii) is disqualifying in kind, not degree: signing a ministerial assurance on a verbal "went fine" — with the written results on your desk unread — is the guide's central integrity failure compressed into a single act of convenience; everything else (4) does, including the genuinely clever pairing of (iv) with (i), is financed by that corner-cut, and no scheduling elegance repays it. Approach (3) fails honourably: "people first" misreads which people — (ii)'s people-problem is next month's and delegable today, while (iv)'s is today's and isn't; moving the assurance deadline treats the morning's one fixed, personal obligation as the flexible item. Approach (1) inverts the dependency order — deciding scope before hearing (iv), delegating the one unreadable-by-proxy task — and its fifteen minutes for (iv) is the relationship error (3) makes with the deadline. Hence most (2), least (4): option A. The closing heuristic, which is very nearly this whole guide's summary: *sequence by irreversibility and dependency, not by loudness or rank; do personally what your signature makes personal; and never let scheduling pressure spend what integrity can't buy back.*

### Preparation tips

- **Map your precedent surface.** List the last five exceptions, appointments and enforcement calls you made, and write down what each taught the people watching. Head-level SJT questions are mostly about the watchers; your own history is the best calibration data.
- **Rehearse the integrity holds before you need them.** The assurance that won't move without evidence, the analysis that won't curate, the report that discloses the gap: script your versions now. Under pressure you will perform whatever you rehearsed, including nothing.
- **Practise pricing both sides of people-decisions.** For each retention, succession or performance dilemma, write the visible cost of acting and the compounding cost of not acting. The second column is systematically underestimated; forcing it onto paper corrects the bias.
- **Audit your discretionary time quarterly.** Where calm quarters actually went — drills, people, analysis, or visibility — is your revealed priority order. If it doesn't match your stated one, the SJT you are failing is the real one.
- **Walk one cross-boundary issue through the influence ladder monthly.** Mutual-exposure evidence, near-free proposal, institutionalised agreement, joint escalation. The ladder becomes reflexive only through use.
- **In most/least items, find the disqualifier first.** At head level the least-effective option is usually the one that spends integrity or corrupts a process — often dressed as efficiency. Identify it before ranking the rest; it anchors the whole item.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Instance fixes that leave the system intact.** Quiet reverts, covert workarounds, one-off exceptions: if the mechanism survives your response, the scenario will recur with compound interest — and the assessors know it.
- **Comfort purchased with curation.** Focused analyses, softened findings, green-shifted assurances, buried declines: every senior scenario offers one option that relieves today's pressure by degrading tomorrow's information. It is always the trap, and it is usually the second-most-attractive option.
- **Loyalty and delivery as behavioural immunity.** The star's health-check pattern, the deputy's expectation: rewarding performance with exemption from standards reprices the standards for everyone.
- **Escalation as first resort across boundaries.** Peer conversations with evidence resolve most cross-boundary issues at a fraction of the relationship cost. Climb the ladder; don't start at the top.
- **Deciding at the tempo of the pressure.** Same-day verdicts on structural questions, launches assured by phone, appointments settled by hints: pressure sets tempo to prevent analysis. Naming what the decision needs — and when it can honestly be had — is the senior move.
- **Treating your own conduct as exempt from your function's telemetry.** The health-check about your restructure, the analysis of your team's breach, the report on your own programme: the scenarios where the data implicates you are the ones that set your function's honesty. Handle them as you would want your managers to — because they will.
