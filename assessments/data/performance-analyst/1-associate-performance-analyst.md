## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the mental processes you use every day as an associate performance analyst: spotting patterns in data, checking reports for errors, following logical rules, prioritising tasks, and solving practical problems. Unlike a general intelligence test, it uses realistic workplace material - the dashboards, spreadsheets, KPI tables, and analytics reports you actually handle - so it predicts how well someone will perform in this specific role.

These assessments are usually taken online and are typically timed, most often between 15 and 30 minutes. Questions are multiple choice, and your score is compared against a benchmark group so that everyone is measured fairly and consistently. Many platforms give you a few short, ungraded practice questions first so you can get used to the format before the real test starts. Some modern platforms are adaptive, which means the questions get slightly harder or easier depending on your answers - so do not worry if the questions feel like they are getting tougher; that can actually be a good sign!

For an associate performance analyst, cognitive ability matters because your core responsibility is the accuracy and quality of data and analysis, and how they are used. When you replicate an existing analysis technique, you need to follow the logic precisely. When you produce a standardised report, you need to spot the odd number that does not fit the pattern. When a senior analyst hands you a dataset, you need to check that it looks right before anyone relies on it. Every one of those tasks is, at heart, a cognitive task.

### What it measures for your role

The assessment dimensions map directly onto the skills in your role profile:

- **Analysis and insight**: pattern recognition and logical deduction questions test whether you can apply core techniques for analysis and draw clear, correct conclusions that colleagues can understand and use.
- **Quality assurance of data and analysis**: error-checking questions test whether you can describe and spot basic data issues, and check that data and analysis "look right" - a phrase taken straight from your skill description.
- **Technical understanding (performance analyst)**: questions about tool outputs, tracking data, and chart types test your awareness of analytical tools and technologies.
- **Performance measurement**: prioritisation and rule-following questions test whether you understand how KPIs connect to business objectives.
- **Understanding analysis across the product life cycle**: some questions place data in different service phases (discovery, alpha, beta, live) to test whether you can reason about how measures vary across the life cycle.
- **User-centred analysis**: problem-solving questions often hinge on remembering that everything focuses on the user of the product or service.

### Practice questions

Take your time with these. Work through the logic before checking the answer, and read every explanation - the reasoning is the real lesson.

**Question 1 (easy) - Pattern recognition in a weekly report**

You produce a standardised weekly report of completed applications for a licence-renewal service. The last six weeks show:

Week 1: 1,200 | Week 2: 1,260 | Week 3: 1,320 | Week 4: 1,380 | Week 5: 1,440 | Week 6: ?

Which figure best fits the pattern for Week 6?

A) 1,470
B) 1,500
C) 1,560
D) 1,620

**Correct answer: B) 1,500**

**Explanation:** Look at the difference between each pair of weeks: 1,260 - 1,200 = 60; 1,320 - 1,260 = 60; and so on. The series increases by exactly 60 each week. So Week 6 should be 1,440 + 60 = 1,500. In real reporting work, recognising a steady trend like this helps you spot when a new figure breaks the pattern - which is often the first clue that something has changed in the service or in the data collection.

**Question 2 (easy) - Spotting the data error**

You are checking a table of daily visits to a government service's start page before it goes into a report. The analytics tool never records fractional visits, and the service typically receives between 3,000 and 6,000 visits per day.

Monday: 4,512 | Tuesday: 4,388 | Wednesday: 44,102 | Thursday: 4,455 | Friday: 4,610

Which value most likely contains an error, and why?

A) Monday, because it is the highest normal value
B) Tuesday, because it is the lowest value
C) Wednesday, because it is roughly ten times larger than every other day
D) Friday, because visits should fall at the end of the week

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Wednesday's figure of 44,102 is about ten times the size of every other day and sits far outside the typical range of 3,000 to 6,000. A tenfold jump often indicates a data entry mistake (an extra digit typed in) or a tracking fault such as duplicate tag firing. Options A, B, and D describe values that all sit comfortably inside the normal range - and note that option D relies on an assumption ("visits should fall on Friday") that is not supported by anything in the question. Checking whether data "looks right" by comparing values against a known typical range is one of the basic quality assurance methods named in your skill set.

**Question 3 (easy) - Following a reporting rule**

Your team's rule for the monthly KPI report is: "Include a metric in the exceptions section if it moved more than 10% in either direction compared with last month."

Last month and this month:

- Completion rate: 82% last month, 84% this month
- Average session time: 4.0 minutes last month, 4.6 minutes this month
- Error page views: 900 last month, 980 this month
- Calls to helpline: 2,000 last month, 1,750 this month

Which metrics belong in the exceptions section?

A) Completion rate and error page views
B) Average session time and calls to helpline
C) Only calls to helpline
D) All four metrics

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Work out each percentage change. Completion rate: from 82 to 84 is a rise of 2 percentage points, which is 2/82 = about 2.4% - under the threshold. Average session time: from 4.0 to 4.6 is a rise of 0.6/4.0 = 15% - over the threshold. Error page views: 80/900 = about 8.9% - under. Calls to helpline: a fall of 250/2,000 = 12.5% - over the threshold (the rule says "in either direction"). So the exceptions are average session time and calls to helpline. Applying a written rule precisely, without letting instinct sneak in, is exactly the discipline needed when you replicate existing reporting techniques.

**Question 4 (moderate) - Logical deduction about tracking data**

A senior performance analyst tells you three facts about a service's analytics setup:

1. Every page that has the new tracking tag records the user's journey stage.
2. The "check your answers" page does not record the user's journey stage.
3. All pages in the payment section have the new tracking tag.

Which conclusion must be true?

A) The "check your answers" page is in the payment section
B) The "check your answers" page does not have the new tracking tag
C) The payment section does not record journey stages
D) Some pages in the payment section are missing the tracking tag

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Fact 1 says: tag → records journey stage. Fact 2 says the "check your answers" page does not record the journey stage. If it had the tag, it would record the stage (fact 1) - so it cannot have the tag. That is option B. Option A gets things backwards: since all payment pages have the tag (fact 3) and the tag guarantees recording, the "check your answers" page cannot be in the payment section. Option C contradicts facts 1 and 3 combined. Option D contradicts fact 3 directly. This kind of careful "if-then" reasoning is what you use when diagnosing why a metric is missing from a dashboard.

**Question 5 (moderate) - Prioritising your morning**

You arrive at 9:00 with four tasks. Your team stand-up is at 10:00 and takes 15 minutes.

1. Refresh the standard weekly dashboard - takes 30 minutes; the service manager presents it at 11:00.
2. Reply to a community-of-practice email inviting you to a session next month - takes 5 minutes; no deadline.
3. Investigate why yesterday's data upload shows 0 transactions - takes about 45 minutes; the senior analyst flagged it as blocking today's analysis.
4. Tidy the shared folder structure - takes 60 minutes; no deadline.

Which order makes the most sense?

A) 4, 2, 1, 3
B) 3, 1, 2, 4
C) 1, 3, 2, 4
D) 2, 4, 3, 1

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The zero-transaction upload (task 3) is the highest priority: a senior colleague flagged it as blocking, and if the underlying data is broken, refreshing the dashboard (task 1) might publish wrong figures - accuracy of data is your core responsibility. Starting task 3 at 9:00 finishes it by 9:45, in time for stand-up at 10:00. Then the dashboard refresh (10:15-10:45) is comfortably ready for the 11:00 presentation. The quick email (task 2) fits in afterwards, and the folder tidy-up (task 4) has no deadline. Option C is tempting, but refreshing a dashboard before checking a known data fault risks presenting inaccurate figures - quality comes before speed.

**Question 6 (moderate) - Which chart looks wrong?**

Your standardised monthly report contains four charts. A colleague asks you to sanity-check them before publication:

1. A line chart of weekly digital take-up, ranging from 61% to 66% over 12 weeks.
2. A bar chart of completions by channel: online 8,200; phone 1,400; post 600; total labelled 10,800.
3. A pie chart of device share: mobile 54%, desktop 38%, tablet 8%.
4. A line chart of average satisfaction score (out of 5), ranging from 4.1 to 4.3.

Which chart contains an internal inconsistency?

A) Chart 1
B) Chart 2
C) Chart 3
D) Chart 4

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Check each chart against its own internal logic. Chart 1: percentages between 61% and 66% are plausible and require no internal arithmetic - nothing to fail. Chart 2: add the channel figures: 8,200 + 1,400 = 9,600; 9,600 + 600 = 10,200. But the chart's total is labelled 10,800 - a mismatch of 600. That is a demonstrable internal inconsistency, so B is the answer. Chart 3: the pie chart slices sum to 54 + 38 + 8 = 100% - consistent, as a pie chart must be. Chart 4: scores between 4.1 and 4.3 out of 5 are internally coherent. Two working habits are worth copying from this question. First, always re-add any labelled total yourself - a total is a claim, and claims get checked. Second, be able to *demonstrate* the error before you flag it: "the parts sum to 10,200 but the label says 10,800" is specific, checkable feedback a colleague can act on, whereas "chart 2 looks off" is not. Common causes of this kind of mismatch in real reports include a stale total left over from last month's version, or one channel's figure being updated without refreshing the total - both are exactly the basic data issues your quality assurance skill asks you to describe and catch.

**Question 7 (moderate) - Odd one out in metric definitions**

Three of these describe measures of *user behaviour* on a service; one describes something different. Which is the odd one out?

A) The percentage of users who abandon the form at the address page
B) The average number of attempts before a successful sign-in
C) The number of civil servants who have completed data protection training
D) The proportion of sessions that come from mobile devices

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Options A, B, and D all describe how users behave on the digital service itself - where they drop out, how often they retry, what device they use. Option C measures staff training compliance, which is an internal organisational measure, not a measure of service users' behaviour. Classifying metrics correctly matters when you build performance measurement frameworks: KPIs about user behaviour tell you about the service experience, while internal measures tell you about the organisation. Mixing them up leads to muddled dashboards and muddled decisions.

**Question 8 (moderate) - Deducing the cause of a data gap**

A dashboard shows page-view data for every day in June except 14 and 15 June, where it shows zero. You know:

1. The service was live and receiving users throughout June (the call centre logged normal demand on those dates).
2. A new cookie-consent banner was deployed on the evening of 13 June and rolled back on the morning of 16 June.
3. The analytics tool only records visits from users who accept cookies.

What is the most logical explanation for the gap?

A) The service was actually offline on 14 and 15 June
B) Users stopped visiting the service on those days
C) The new banner prevented cookie consent from being recorded, so the analytics tool logged no visits
D) The analytics vendor deleted the data

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Facts 2 and 3 line up exactly with the gap: the faulty banner was in place precisely during the missing days, and the tool depends on cookie consent to record anything. Fact 1 rules out options A and B - real users were clearly still using the service because the call centre saw normal demand. Option D has no supporting evidence at all. This is a classic real-world scenario: zeros in analytics data usually mean "not measured", not "nothing happened". Understanding the context of the data - one of your quality assurance skills - stops you drawing the wrong insight from a technically accurate chart.

**Question 9 (moderate) - Applying life cycle logic**

Your team supports two services. Service X is in private beta with 500 invited users. Service Y has been live for three years with two million users a year. A colleague proposes using exactly the same KPI targets for both.

Which statement is the strongest logical objection?

A) Beta services should not be measured at all
B) Measures and targets should vary by life cycle stage: a beta service's data answers "is this working and improving?", while a live service's data tracks performance at scale against established benchmarks
C) Two million users is too many to measure accurately
D) The services should use different analytics tools

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Your skill "understanding analysis across the product life cycle" says performance measures relate to business objectives and vary at different stages of the product life cycle. A private beta with 500 users generates small-sample data used to learn and iterate; a mature live service generates large-scale data measured against stable benchmarks. Identical targets would be unrealistic for the beta and uninformative for the live service. Option A is wrong - beta services absolutely should be measured; that is how they improve. Options C and D are irrelevant to the question of targets.

**Question 10 (hard) - Multi-step problem solving**

A senior analyst asks you to reconcile two sources. The analytics tool reports 12,000 completed online applications in July. The case-management system reports 12,900 applications received in July. You establish:

1. The case-management system includes applications from all channels: online, phone, and post.
2. Phone applications in July numbered 620.
3. Postal applications in July numbered 310.
4. The analytics tool loses roughly 1 to 3% of online completions because some users do not accept cookies.

Do the two sources reasonably agree?

A) No - there is an unexplained gap of 900 applications
B) Yes - after removing phone and post, the case system implies 11,970 online applications, and the analytics figure of 12,000 is within the expected cookie-loss tolerance of that figure
C) No - the analytics figure should always be higher than the case system figure
D) Cannot tell without data from June

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Step 1: remove non-online channels from the case system: 12,900 - 620 - 310 = 11,970 online applications. Step 2: compare with analytics: 12,000 vs 11,970 - a difference of only 30, about 0.25%. Step 3: consider known measurement effects: analytics loses 1-3% to cookie refusal, so if anything you would expect analytics to be slightly *lower*; a tiny 0.25% excess is well within normal noise (duplicate sessions, boundary-of-month timing). The sources agree well. This mirrors real reconciliation work: break the total into comparable parts, compare like with like, and judge differences against known tolerances rather than expecting perfection. Option A forgets to remove the other channels; option C gets the direction of cookie loss backwards; option D is unnecessary caution once the July figures reconcile.

**Question 11 (hard) - Error checking a calculated column**

A spreadsheet has three columns: Started, Completed, and Completion rate (Completed divided by Started, shown as a percentage). Which row contains a calculation error?

- Row 1: Started 2,000 | Completed 1,500 | Rate 75%
- Row 2: Started 1,600 | Completed 1,240 | Rate 77.5%
- Row 3: Started 2,400 | Completed 1,850 | Rate 82.4%
- Row 4: Started 3,000 | Completed 2,310 | Rate 77%

A) Row 1
B) Row 2
C) Row 3
D) Row 4

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Check each: Row 1: 1,500/2,000 = 0.75 = 75% - correct. Row 2: 1,240/1,600 = 0.775 = 77.5% - correct. Row 3: 1,850/2,400 = 0.7708... = 77.1%, but the sheet says 82.4% - wrong. Row 4: 2,310/3,000 = 0.77 = 77% - correct. A quick estimation trick helps here: 1,850 is a bit more than three-quarters of 2,400 (three-quarters would be 1,800), so the rate should be only a little above 75% - nowhere near 82%. Estimation-first checking lets you scan a whole report quickly and only reach for the calculator where something smells wrong.

**Question 12 (hard) - Choosing the right technique**

The service manager asks: "Users seem to drop out somewhere in the application journey - can you find out where?" Which analytical approach best answers the question?

A) A funnel analysis showing the percentage of users progressing through each step of the journey, to locate the step with the largest drop
B) A pie chart of traffic sources, to show where users come from
C) A year-on-year comparison of total visits
D) A word cloud of feedback comments

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The question is about *where in the journey* users leave, so the technique must break the journey into steps and measure progression between them - which is exactly what a funnel analysis does. Traffic sources (B) explain where users come *from*, not where they *leave*. A year-on-year total (C) hides everything that happens inside the journey. Feedback comments (D) may add colour later but do not locate the drop-out point systematically. Matching the technique to the question is the heart of "analysis and insight": the right chart answers the question; the wrong chart merely decorates the report.

**Question 13 (hard) - Combining conditions**

Your team's data quality checklist says a dataset may be marked "fit for purpose" only if ALL of these hold:

1. Less than 5% of records have missing values.
2. The date range covers the full reporting month.
3. Totals reconcile with the source system within 2%.

You assess a dataset and find: 3% of records have missing values; the date range runs from the 1st to the 28th of a 31-day month; totals reconcile within 0.8%.

What should you conclude?

A) Fit for purpose - two out of three checks pass, which is a majority
B) Not fit for purpose - condition 2 fails because three days of the month are missing, and the rule requires all conditions to pass
C) Fit for purpose - the missing days probably contain little data
D) Cannot assess without knowing the sample size

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The rule uses ALL - every condition must hold. Conditions 1 (3% < 5%) and 3 (0.8% < 2%) pass, but condition 2 fails: the data stops on the 28th of a 31-day month, so three days are missing. Option A applies majority logic where the rule demands unanimity. Option C makes an unsupported assumption - "probably contains little data" is a guess, and guessing is precisely what quality assurance processes exist to prevent. The right action in real life would be to flag the gap, find the missing days, and only then mark the data fit for purpose. Being the person who catches this before publication is how associate analysts build trust.

### Preparation tips

- **Practise with real artefacts.** Open a dashboard or weekly report at work and quiz yourself: what is the trend? Does anything break the pattern? Do the totals add up? This turns everyday tasks into free practice.
- **Learn to estimate before you calculate.** Rounding numbers to check whether an answer is "in the right ballpark" is faster than precise arithmetic and catches most errors.
- **Read every rule twice.** Many cognitive questions hinge on a single word like "all", "only", or "either direction". Slow down on the rule, then speed up on the arithmetic.
- **Do a timed dry run.** Set a timer for 20 minutes and attempt eight to ten questions. Time pressure feels very different from casual practice, and rehearsing it removes the shock on the day.
- **Be kind to yourself.** As an entry-level analyst you are not expected to score perfectly. Assessments are usually normed against people at a similar career stage. Steady, careful work beats frantic speed.

### Common pitfalls

- **Rushing past the data.** The most common error is answering from a quick glance rather than reading the table properly. Misreading a column header can make every subsequent step wrong.
- **Bringing in outside assumptions.** Answer from the facts given, not from what "usually" happens. If the question does not say Friday traffic falls, do not assume it does.
- **Time-sink questions.** If a question is eating your time, flag it, skip it, and return later. One hard question is never worth three easy ones.
- **Ignoring answer plausibility.** Before selecting, ask: does this answer make sense at this scale? An answer ten times too big usually signals a slipped decimal point.
- **Second-guessing under pressure.** If you followed the logic carefully, trust it. Change an answer only when you can point to the specific mistake in your first reasoning.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures your ability to read, interpret, and make sound decisions from numerical data. It is not a school maths exam: there is no algebra for its own sake and no abstract formulas to memorise. Instead, the questions use the kinds of numbers you meet as an associate performance analyst - completion rates, digital take-up percentages, cost per transaction, visit counts, satisfaction scores, and simple budget figures - presented in tables, charts, and short data summaries.

The role summary for an associate performance analyst says the role requires "numeracy and some experience in analysis or data handling", so this assessment speaks directly to the foundation of your job. The maths itself is deliberately basic: percentages, ratios, averages, differences, and simple projections. What is being tested is whether you can extract the right numbers from realistic data, apply the right operation, and sanity-check the result - quickly and accurately.

Numeric reasoning tests are usually strictly timed, often around 60 to 90 seconds per question, and most allow or provide an on-screen calculator. That tells you something important: the challenge is interpretation, not mental arithmetic. The hard part is choosing *which* numbers to use and *what* to do with them; the calculator handles the rest. Your score is typically compared against a norm group of people at a similar level, so you are being measured fairly against peers, not against senior statisticians.

### What it measures for your role

- **Analysis and insight**: extracting the correct figures from a table and applying core techniques - percentage change, rates, averages - is the numerical core of analysis.
- **Performance measurement**: many questions involve KPIs and targets, testing whether you can tell if a measure is on track against a business objective.
- **Quality assurance of data and analysis**: several questions ask you to verify a calculated figure or spot an implausible one, mirroring how you check that data and analysis look right.
- **Communicating analysis and insight**: choosing the correct percentage or rate is the first step to giving tactical recommendations - a wrong number leads to a wrong decision.
- **Understanding analysis across the product life cycle**: questions may compare a small beta cohort with a large live service, testing whether you handle small and large denominators sensibly.
- **Technical understanding**: data is presented the way analytical tools present it - dashboards, exports, and summary tables - so familiarity with those formats helps you move fast.

### Practice questions

A calculator is allowed for all of these. Show yourself the working, just as the explanations do - the habit of writing down steps prevents most errors.

**Question 1 (easy) - Percentage completion rate**

In one week, 4,500 users started an online passport-renewal application and 3,600 completed it. What was the completion rate?

A) 75%
B) 80%
C) 85%
D) 90%

**Correct answer: B) 80%**

**Explanation:** Completion rate = completed ÷ started = 3,600 ÷ 4,500 = 0.8 = 80%. A quick sanity check: 3,600 is clearly more than three-quarters of 4,500 (three-quarters would be 3,375) but less than nine-tenths (4,050), so 80% sits sensibly between them. Completion rate is one of the most common service KPIs you will report, so this calculation should become second nature.

**Question 2 (easy) - Percentage change**

Visits to a service's guidance page were 15,000 in March and 18,000 in April. What was the percentage increase?

A) 3%
B) 15%
C) 20%
D) 30%

**Correct answer: C) 20%**

**Explanation:** Increase = 18,000 - 15,000 = 3,000. Percentage increase = increase ÷ original = 3,000 ÷ 15,000 = 0.2 = 20%. The most common mistake is dividing by the *new* figure (3,000 ÷ 18,000 = 16.7%) - percentage change is always measured against the starting value. Another common trap is option A: 3,000 is the absolute change, not 3%.

**Question 3 (easy) - Reading a data table**

A monthly channel report shows applications received:

| Channel | January | February |
|---|---|---|
| Online | 6,200 | 6,800 |
| Phone | 1,500 | 1,300 |
| Post | 800 | 700 |

What percentage of February's applications arrived online (to the nearest whole per cent)?

A) 70%
B) 74%
C) 77%
D) 80%

**Correct answer: C) 77%**

**Explanation:** First find February's total: 6,800 + 1,300 + 700 = 8,800. Then the online share: 6,800 ÷ 8,800 = 0.7727... = 77% to the nearest whole per cent. Be careful to use the February column throughout - a very common error under time pressure is mixing months, for example dividing February's online figure by January's total (6,800 ÷ 8,500 = 80%, which is exactly the trap in option D). Digital take-up - the share of transactions completed online - is a headline measure for government services, so this is a calculation you will do often.

**Question 4 (moderate) - Cost per transaction**

A service costs £54,000 a month to run and processed 30,000 transactions this month. Next month, costs are expected to stay the same, but transactions are forecast to rise by 20%. What will the cost per transaction be next month?

A) £1.80
B) £1.50
C) £1.44
D) £2.16

**Correct answer: B) £1.50**

**Explanation:** Step 1: next month's transactions = 30,000 × 1.20 = 36,000. Step 2: cost per transaction = £54,000 ÷ 36,000 = £1.50. Option A (£1.80) is this month's figure (54,000 ÷ 30,000) - correct arithmetic on the wrong month. Option D wrongly *increases* the cost per transaction, but more transactions for the same total cost make each one cheaper, not dearer. Always pause to check the direction of an answer: if volume rises and cost is fixed, unit cost must fall. Cost per transaction is one of the classic government service performance measures, so expect it in real dashboards too.

**Question 5 (moderate) - Averages across a week**

Daily completed applications for one week: Monday 820, Tuesday 790, Wednesday 850, Thursday 910, Friday 880, Saturday 310, Sunday 240. What was the average (mean) per day across the whole week, to the nearest whole number?

A) 686
B) 850
C) 600
D) 743

**Correct answer: A) 686**

**Explanation:** Sum all seven days: 820 + 790 + 850 + 910 + 880 + 310 + 240 = 4,800. Divide by 7: 4,800 ÷ 7 = 685.7... = 686. Option B is roughly the weekday average - a plausible-looking trap if you forget the weekend. Notice the insight buried in the data: weekday demand is around 850 a day while weekend demand collapses to about 275. When you present this in a report, a single weekly mean hides that pattern; a good analyst might show weekday and weekend averages separately. Numeric tests reward the correct calculation; real work also rewards noticing what the number conceals.

**Question 6 (moderate) - Ratios**

A survey of 1,200 service users found 900 rated the service "satisfied" and 300 rated it "dissatisfied". What is the ratio of satisfied to dissatisfied users in its simplest form?

A) 4:1
B) 3:1
C) 900:300
D) 1:3

**Correct answer: B) 3:1**

**Explanation:** The ratio is 900:300. Divide both sides by their highest common factor, 300: 900 ÷ 300 = 3 and 300 ÷ 300 = 1, giving 3:1. Option C is the same ratio but not simplified, and the question asks for simplest form. Option D reverses the order - always match the order of the ratio to the order of the wording ("satisfied to dissatisfied"). Option A would need 960 satisfied users. Ratios turn up when you compare channels, device types, or user groups, and simplifying them makes your reports easier for non-specialist audiences to grasp - part of your communicating analysis and insight skill.

**Question 7 (moderate) - Percentage points versus percentages**

A service's digital take-up rose from 60% in Quarter 1 to 69% in Quarter 2. Which statement is accurate?

A) Take-up rose by 9 percentage points, which is a 15% relative increase
B) Take-up rose by 9%, which is 9 percentage points, so the two phrasings are always interchangeable
C) Take-up rose by 15 percentage points
D) Take-up rose by 6.9%

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The difference between 69% and 60% is 9 *percentage points*. The *relative* increase is 9 ÷ 60 = 0.15 = 15%. These are different things, and confusing them is one of the most common numerical mistakes in performance reporting. Option B is wrong precisely because the phrasings are not interchangeable: "a 9% increase" from 60% would mean 60 × 1.09 = 65.4%, not 69%. When you write reports, saying "take-up rose 9 percentage points to 69%" is unambiguous and protects your readers from misreading - accuracy in how figures are used is explicitly part of your responsibility.

**Question 8 (moderate) - Projecting from a run rate**

By the end of day 12 of a 30-day month, a new online reporting tool has received 3,000 submissions. If submissions continue at the same daily rate, how many will the tool receive in the full month?

A) 6,000
B) 7,500
C) 9,000
D) 4,500

**Correct answer: B) 7,500**

**Explanation:** Daily rate = 3,000 ÷ 12 = 250 submissions per day. Full month = 250 × 30 = 7,500. A faster route: 12 days is 12/30 = two-fifths of the month, so the full month is 3,000 × (30 ÷ 12) = 3,000 × 2.5 = 7,500. When you present a projection like this, always label it as a projection with its assumption ("assuming the current daily rate continues") - real demand often has weekly cycles, as Question 5 showed. Tactical recommendations built on projections must be honest about their assumptions.

**Question 9 (moderate) - Verifying a colleague's figure**

A draft report states: "Error page views fell 25% from 1,600 in May to 1,240 in June." You are checking the report before publication. Is the claim right?

A) Yes - the calculation is correct
B) No - the fall is 22.5%, so the claim overstates the improvement
C) No - the fall is 360 views, which is 29%
D) Cannot tell without July's data

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Fall = 1,600 - 1,240 = 360. Percentage fall = 360 ÷ 1,600 = 0.225 = 22.5%, not 25%. The claim overstates the improvement by 2.5 percentage points. Option C computes 360 ÷ 1,240 = 29% - dividing by the wrong (later) value. Option D is a distractor: checking a May-to-June change needs no July data. This is quality assurance of analysis in its purest form: someone will quote that 25% in a meeting, so catching it before publication protects your team's credibility. Note the tone you would use in real life, too - "I think this should read 22.5%, here is my working" is collaborative and helpful.

**Question 10 (hard) - Multi-step: channel shift savings**

A council service handles 40,000 transactions a year: 70% online at £0.90 each and 30% by phone at £5.10 each. A planned improvement is expected to shift a quarter of the phone transactions to the online channel. How much would the service save per year?

A) £12,600
B) £50,400
C) £15,300
D) £3,000

**Correct answer: A) £12,600**

**Explanation:** Step 1: current phone transactions = 40,000 × 0.30 = 12,000. Step 2: transactions shifting = 12,000 × 0.25 = 3,000. Step 3: saving per shifted transaction = £5.10 - £0.90 = £4.20. Step 4: total saving = 3,000 × £4.20 = £12,600. The key insight is step 3: a shifted transaction still costs something online, so the saving is the *difference* between the channel costs, not the full phone cost. Option C (3,000 × £5.10) makes exactly that mistake. Option D is the count of shifted transactions, not money. Channel-shift arithmetic like this is a staple of government business cases, and as you progress you will help senior analysts build them.

**Question 11 (hard) - Weighted average satisfaction**

Satisfaction is surveyed on three parts of a service:

- Application step: score 4.2 from 1,000 responses
- Payment step: score 3.6 from 500 responses
- Confirmation step: score 4.5 from 500 responses

What is the overall average satisfaction score across all responses?

A) 4.10
B) 4.13
C) 4.30
D) 4.05

**Correct answer: B) 4.13** (to two decimal places)

**Explanation:** Because the parts have different numbers of responses, you must weight by response count. Total score points = (4.2 × 1,000) + (3.6 × 500) + (4.5 × 500) = 4,200 + 1,800 + 2,250 = 8,250. Total responses = 2,000. Weighted average = 8,250 ÷ 2,000 = 4.125, which rounds to 4.13. Option A (4.10) is the simple unweighted average of the three scores ((4.2 + 3.6 + 4.5) ÷ 3) - the classic trap. The unweighted version undercounts the application step, which has twice the responses of each other step. Whenever you combine averages in real analysis, ask "do the groups have equal sizes?" - if not, weight them.

**Question 12 (hard) - Small samples across the life cycle**

Two versions of a sign-up journey are being compared. The live service completed 41,500 of 50,000 attempts last month (83%). A private beta of the redesigned journey completed 44 of 50 attempts (88%). A stakeholder says: "The beta journey is clearly better - take-up is 5 percentage points higher."

What is the most numerically sound response?

A) Agree - 88% is higher than 83%, so the beta is better
B) Point out that the beta figure comes from only 50 attempts, so a difference of a few completions either way would swing the rate substantially; the comparison is suggestive but not yet reliable
C) Disagree - live services always outperform betas
D) Average the two figures to get a combined rate of 85.5%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** With only 50 attempts, each completion is worth 2 percentage points: if just two of the 44 completers had dropped out, the beta rate would be 42/50 = 84% - nearly identical to live. The live figure, by contrast, is based on 50,000 attempts and is very stable. So the honest reading is "promising early signal, keep gathering data", not "clearly better". This connects your numeracy to your life cycle skill: beta-stage data is small by nature, and part of an analyst's value is calibrating confidence to sample size. Option D's averaging is meaningless here, and option C is an assumption, not analysis. Notice that B does not dismiss the stakeholder - it explains the uncertainty helpfully, which is what good communication of insight looks like.

**Question 13 (hard) - Reading a percentage table backwards**

A dashboard shows that 35% of a service's 24,000 monthly sessions come from mobile devices, and that mobile sessions have a completion rate of 65%. Desktop and tablet sessions (the remaining 65% of sessions) have a completion rate of 80%. How many sessions completed in total?

A) 17,940
B) 5,460
C) 12,480
D) 18,600

**Correct answer: A) 17,940**

**Explanation:** Step 1: mobile sessions = 24,000 × 0.35 = 8,400. Step 2: mobile completions = 8,400 × 0.65 = 5,460. Step 3: other sessions = 24,000 × 0.65 = 15,600. Step 4: other completions = 15,600 × 0.80 = 12,480. Step 5: total completions = 5,460 + 12,480 = 17,940. Options B and C are the two partial answers - a strong reminder to re-read the question ("in total") before selecting. There is also an insight for your reports: the mobile completion rate (65%) is well below desktop and tablet (80%), which might prompt a tactical recommendation to investigate the mobile journey - remembering, as always, that everything we do is focused on the user.

**Question 14 (hard) - Budget share and reallocation**

A performance analysis team's annual tools budget is £48,000: 50% on the analytics platform, 25% on survey tools, 15% on dashboard software, and 10% on training. The team decides to cut survey tool spending by a third and move that money to training. How much will the training budget be after the change?

A) £4,800
B) £8,800
C) £9,600
D) £12,000

**Correct answer: B) £8,800**

**Explanation:** Step 1: survey tools budget = £48,000 × 0.25 = £12,000. Step 2: a third of that = £12,000 ÷ 3 = £4,000. Step 3: current training budget = £48,000 × 0.10 = £4,800. Step 4: new training budget = £4,800 + £4,000 = £8,800. Option A is the training budget before the change; option C wrongly moves £4,800 (a third of a mis-remembered figure); option D moves the whole survey budget. Multi-step money questions reward writing each intermediate number down - the errors almost always occur between steps, not within them.

**Question 15 (moderate) - Converting a rate into volumes**

A benefits-enquiry service receives 8,000 calls a month. Analysis shows 45% of calls are about topics fully answered by an existing online guidance page. If better signposting could deflect half of those calls to the guidance page, how many calls would the centre still receive each month?

A) 3,600
B) 4,400
C) 6,200
D) 1,800

**Correct answer: C) 6,200**

**Explanation:** Step 1: calls answerable online = 8,000 × 0.45 = 3,600. Step 2: calls deflected = 3,600 × 0.5 = 1,800. Step 3: remaining calls = 8,000 - 1,800 = 6,200. Options A and D are the intermediate results from steps 1 and 2 - the question asks for calls the centre *still receives*, so you must subtract from the original total. Option B subtracts the full 3,600. This structure - "what share is affected, how much of it changes, what is left" - underpins many tactical recommendations you will make from analysis, so practise reading the final sentence of a question twice before answering.

**Question 16 (hard) - Indexing against a baseline**

A team tracks form-error rates using an index where the January value is set to 100. February's error rate was 8% lower than January's, and March's was 10% higher than February's. What is the index value for March, to one decimal place?

A) 102.0
B) 101.2
C) 98.0
D) 100.0

**Correct answer: B) 101.2**

**Explanation:** Step 1: February = 100 × (1 - 0.08) = 92. Step 2: March = 92 × (1 + 0.10) = 101.2. The trap is option A: adding the changes (-8% + 10% = +2%) treats percentages as if they applied to the same base, but the 10% rise applies to February's *lower* base of 92, not to 100. Sequential percentage changes multiply; they do not add. Indexed measures appear in cross-government reporting because they let services of different sizes be compared on one chart - knowing how they compound will help you both in tests and when you build those charts yourself.

**Question 17 (hard) - Choosing the right denominator**

In one month, a service's start page received 20,000 unique visitors. Of these, 12,000 clicked "Start now", and 9,000 of those completed the application. A colleague drafts two statements for the report:

1. "The completion rate from start of application was 75%."
2. "45% of start-page visitors went on to complete the application."

Which assessment of the two statements is correct?

A) Both statements are correct, and they measure different things
B) Statement 1 is wrong; the completion rate is 45%
C) Statement 2 is wrong; the figure should be 60%
D) Both statements are wrong

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Statement 1: completions ÷ starts = 9,000 ÷ 12,000 = 0.75 = 75% - correct. Statement 2: completions ÷ start-page visitors = 9,000 ÷ 20,000 = 0.45 = 45% - also correct. Both are right because each uses a different, clearly stated denominator. This question rewards precision about "per cent of what?", the single most valuable habit in performance measurement. In real reports, always name the denominator ("of those who clicked Start now...") so specialist and non-specialist readers alike draw the right conclusion - exactly what your communicating analysis and insight skill asks of you. Option C's 60% is 12,000 ÷ 20,000, the click-through rate from the start page - a third valid metric, but not what statement 2 claims.

### Preparation tips

- **Master the big four.** Percentage of a total, percentage change, cost/rate per unit, and weighted averages cover most questions at this level. Practise each until the method is automatic.
- **Write down intermediate steps.** Even with a calculator, jot each partial result. It costs seconds and prevents the between-step slips that cause most wrong answers.
- **Practise with your own dashboards.** Take this week's real figures and ask: what changed, by what per cent, and what is the rate per day? Real data makes practice meaningful and doubles as genuine work insight.
- **Estimate first, then calculate.** Before computing, guess the ballpark ("about 20%", "roughly £13,000"). If the calculator disagrees wildly with your estimate, one of them is wrong - check both.
- **Rehearse under time.** Give yourself 75 seconds per question for a set of ten. Learning what 75 seconds feels like stops panic on test day.
- **Do not fear the maths.** Everything here is entry-level numeracy - exactly what your role profile asks for. Confidence comes from repetition, not talent.

### Common pitfalls

- **Misreading chart and table labels.** Confusing monthly with annual figures, or missing qualifiers like "in thousands", silently multiplies errors. Read the units before the numbers.
- **Dividing by the wrong base.** Percentage change is always relative to the original value; shares are relative to the correct total. Ask "per cent *of what*?" every time.
- **Over-calculating.** If the options are far apart, estimation and rounding find the answer faster than exact arithmetic. Save precision for options that are close together.
- **Time-sink questions.** A multi-stage question that is not yielding should be skipped and revisited. Never let one question consume three questions' worth of time.
- **Confusing percentage points with percentages.** As Question 7 showed, a rise from 60% to 69% is 9 points but 15% - test writers love this distinction.
- **Answering a sub-step.** Long questions often list intermediate results among the wrong options. Re-read exactly what is asked ("in total", "next month", "after the change") before choosing.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment checks whether you can work out exactly what a piece of writing establishes — not what it implies, and not what you already believe to be true.

For an associate performance analyst the material is the writing that surrounds your analysis: a performance measurement framework, a definition of a key performance indicator, a data quality note, guidance on a tool, or a summary of what a service is supposed to achieve.

The usual format gives you a passage and then statements to mark **True**, **False** or **Cannot Say**:

- **True** — the passage establishes it.
- **False** — the passage contradicts it.
- **Cannot Say** — the passage settles it neither way.

Get comfortable with Cannot Say. It is a confident, correct answer rather than an admission that you are stuck, and in a well-built test there will be several.

Two things are worth knowing before you start.

The first is that **definitions in performance frameworks are precise, and small words carry them**. "Completed transactions", "unique users", "per quarter", "excluding resubmissions" — each of these changes what a number means. Your role summary says you are responsible for the accuracy and quality of data and analysis, and that responsibility starts with reading a definition exactly as written.

The second is that **what you know is the main hazard**. You may know perfectly well how a metric is usually calculated. If the passage defines it differently, the passage wins. This feels odd the first few times and quickly becomes natural — and it is genuinely useful, because organisations define the same-sounding metric differently all the time.

### What it measures for your role

- **Reading definitions precisely** maps to **Performance measurement**, where you work with KPIs and frameworks.
- **Reading data quality notes** maps to **Quality assurance of data and analysis**.
- **Reading tool documentation** maps to **Technical understanding (performance analyst)**.
- **Reading a brief accurately** maps to working under guidance on projects with limited scope.
- **Describing findings accurately** maps to **Communicating analysis and insight**.
- **Reading about users** maps to **User-centred analysis**.

### Practice questions

*Passage A — for Questions 1 to 4*

"The service's completion rate is defined as the number of applications submitted divided by the number of applications started, expressed as a percentage, measured per calendar month. An application is counted as started when the user reaches the first question page. Applications abandoned and later resumed within 30 days count once, at the point they were first started. Applications from users who begin more than one application in a month are counted separately. Test transactions are excluded. The rate is reported to one decimal place."

**Question 1 (easy)**

Statement: The completion rate is measured per calendar month.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Stated directly in the definition. A straightforward one, and worth reading carefully anyway — several of the questions below turn on other parts of the same sentence, and getting into the habit of reading a whole definition before answering the first question about it saves time overall.

**Question 2 (easy)**

Statement: An application is counted as started when the user lands on the service's home page.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage says an application counts as started "when the user reaches the first question page", which is not the home page. The passage contradicts the statement. This distinction matters more than it looks: counting from the home page would include everyone who arrived and left, which would produce a much lower completion rate for the same service.

**Question 3 (moderate)**

Statement: A user who starts two applications in the same month is counted once.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage says applications "from users who begin more than one application in a month are counted separately". So two starts count as two. The passage contradicts the statement. Note the metric counts *applications*, not users — a distinction that catches people out, because "unique users" is such a common measure that it is easy to assume it applies here.

**Question 4 (harder)**

Statement: An application started in January, abandoned, and resumed and submitted in February counts towards February's completion rate.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** This is the hardest question in the set and worth working through slowly. The passage says applications resumed within 30 days "count once, at the point they were first started" — so the *start* belongs to January. What it does not say is where the *submission* is counted, and the rate is a ratio of submissions to starts. If both are attributed to January, February is unaffected; if the submission lands in February, February's rate is affected by a submission with no matching start. The passage settles the start and is silent on the submission, so you cannot answer. In real work this is exactly the kind of gap you would take back to whoever wrote the definition.

*Passage B — for Questions 5 to 8*

"The data quality note records that tracking was not functioning on the mobile version of the service between 3 and 11 March. Desktop tracking was unaffected. Figures for March should therefore be treated as incomplete. The analyst has estimated the missing mobile volume by applying February's mobile-to-desktop ratio to March's desktop figures. The estimate is shown separately in the dashboard and is not included in the published headline figure. Comparisons between March and other months should note the gap."

**Question 5 (easy)**

Statement: Desktop tracking worked normally throughout March.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The passage says "Desktop tracking was unaffected", and the outage described is specific to mobile between 3 and 11 March. The passage establishes it. Note that "unaffected" refers to the mobile tracking failure — the passage tells you desktop was not affected by this problem, which is what the statement claims.

**Question 6 (moderate)**

Statement: The published headline figure for March includes an estimate of the missing mobile data.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage contradicts this: the estimate "is shown separately in the dashboard and is not included in the published headline figure". This is good practice being described — an estimate kept visibly separate from measured data — and reading it correctly matters, because someone comparing the headline figure with other months is comparing a number that is missing eight days of mobile traffic.

**Question 7 (moderate)**

Statement: The estimate of missing mobile volume is accurate.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage describes how the estimate was made — February's mobile-to-desktop ratio applied to March's desktop figures — and says nothing about how accurate it turned out to be, which nobody can know, since the actual data was never captured. The method is reasonable and reasonable is not accurate. Distinguishing a described method from a validated result is a habit worth building early.

**Question 8 (harder)**

Statement: Mobile traffic was lower than usual between 3 and 11 March.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The *recorded* mobile traffic was lower, because tracking was not working. Whether the actual traffic was lower is a completely different question, and the passage gives no reason to think users stopped using the mobile service — they just were not counted. Confusing "we did not measure it" with "it did not happen" is one of the most important distinctions in this job, and it is the reason the data quality note exists at all.

*Passage C — for Questions 9 to 12*

"The performance framework sets four key performance indicators for the service: completion rate, user satisfaction, cost per transaction, and time to complete. Targets are set annually by the service owner. The framework states that satisfaction is measured by a survey offered at the end of the transaction, with a target of 80% rating the service good or very good. Cost per transaction is calculated quarterly. The framework notes that time to complete excludes time when the application is awaiting information from a third party. Reporting against the framework is monthly, except for cost per transaction."

**Question 9 (easy)**

Statement: The framework sets four key performance indicators.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Stated directly and then listed. Straightforward — and it is worth counting the list against the number when you read a passage like this, because a mismatch between a stated count and the items listed is a classic error-checking question.

**Question 10 (moderate)**

Statement: Cost per transaction is reported monthly.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage contradicts this twice: cost per transaction "is calculated quarterly", and reporting is monthly "except for cost per transaction". The exception at the end of the passage is what makes this definite. Reading to the end before answering is the lesson here — a reader who stopped after the first sentence about monthly reporting would answer True.

**Question 11 (moderate)**

Statement: The satisfaction figure reflects the views of all users of the service.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The survey is "offered at the end of the transaction", so it reaches people who got to the end. Anyone who abandoned partway through — who may well be the least satisfied — is not offered it at all. So the figure does not reflect all users, and the passage's own description of the method establishes that. This is one of the most useful things to understand about satisfaction data early in your career, because the figure is quoted constantly and the limitation almost never is.

**Question 12 (harder)**

Statement: A service meeting its satisfaction target has 80% of its users rating it good or very good.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This combines the two previous ideas. The target is 80% of *survey respondents* rating the service good or very good — and respondents are people who completed the transaction and chose to answer, not "its users". So a service can hit the target without 80% of users being satisfied. The statement conflates respondents with users, which the passage's own definition contradicts. If you found this one demanding, that is reasonable: it asks you to hold a definition from earlier in the passage while reading a claim that quietly changes one word.

### Preparation tips

- **Read the whole definition before answering anything about it.**
- **Watch small qualifying words.** "Started", "submitted", "per calendar month", "excluding".
- **Check whether a metric counts people or events.** They are different.
- **Treat "Cannot Say" as a real answer** when the passage genuinely does not settle it.
- **Look for the exception at the end.** It often reverses the obvious answer.
- **Distinguish "not measured" from "did not happen".**
- **Ask who a survey actually reaches.**
- **Ask yourself "where does it say that?"** If you cannot point at a sentence, it is Cannot Say.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Answering from how a metric is usually defined.**
- **Assuming a rate counts unique users.**
- **Treating a described method as a validated result.**
- **Reading a tracking outage as a fall in real activity.**
- **Missing the exception in the last sentence.**
- **Treating survey respondents as all users.**
- **Rushing. There is usually enough time for one careful read.**

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment gives you realistic situations from your working life and asks what you would do. The situations are ordinary, and the question is which response an experienced analyst would choose.

For an associate performance analyst the situations involve working under guidance, checking your own work, being asked for numbers quickly, spotting something that looks wrong, and explaining analysis to people who will act on it.

You will usually pick the **most effective** and **least effective** response, or rate each. Scoring is against a key built from experienced practitioners, so you are aiming at professional consensus rather than personal style.

Three things explain most of the answer key.

**You are responsible for the accuracy of your analysis, and that is stated explicitly in your role summary.** It is unusual for an entry-level role to carry that so plainly, and it shapes many of the answers below: checking before publishing, flagging a number you are unsure about, and correcting an error you have already sent are all part of the job rather than signs of inexperience.

**Asking is expected.** You work under the guidance of performance analysts and more senior colleagues. A question early is the arrangement working properly; a wrong number produced confidently is the arrangement failing.

**A number without context can mislead even when it is correct.** Much of the craft of this job is making sure the person receiving your analysis understands what it does and does not show, and that starts now.

### What it measures for your role

- **Accuracy and checking** maps to **Quality assurance of data and analysis**.
- **Working under guidance** maps to your role level's description.
- **Explaining findings** maps to **Communicating analysis and insight**.
- **Understanding what a metric shows** maps to **Performance measurement**.
- **Tool use** maps to **Technical understanding (performance analyst)**.
- **Community engagement** maps to engaging with the performance analysis community.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — A number that looks wrong**

You produce a routine report and one figure is much higher than usual. What is the most effective response?

- A) Check it before sending — look at whether the underlying data changed, whether a filter or date range is different, and whether anything happened in the service that would explain it.
- B) Send it; the tool produced it.
- C) Send it with a note saying the figure looks unusual.
- D) Ask a colleague to look at the whole report.

**Correct answer: A. Least effective: B**

**Explanation:** An unexpected figure is either a real change worth reporting or an error worth catching, and ten minutes tells you which. Option A lists the three things that explain the great majority of such cases. Option B is least effective: your role summary makes you responsible for accuracy, and "the tool produced it" is not a check. Option C is much better than B and still pushes the question onto the reader, who has less context than you. Option D is reasonable if you cannot resolve it yourself, and it is worth trying first.

**Question 2 (easy) — An urgent request**

Someone asks you for a figure by the end of the day. You can produce it, but not check it properly. What is the most effective response?

- A) Send the figure and say clearly that it is unchecked, what you have not been able to verify, and when you could confirm it.
- B) Send the figure.
- C) Say you cannot produce it in the time.
- D) Send an approximate figure rounded heavily.

**Correct answer: A. Least effective: B**

**Explanation:** Option A gives them what they need to decide how much weight to put on it, which is the honest and useful thing — an unchecked figure clearly labelled is often perfectly good enough for the decision they are making. Option B is least effective: an unlabelled unchecked figure will be treated as verified and quoted onward. Option D disguises the uncertainty as precision-by-rounding, which does not tell the reader anything about whether the number is right. Option C withholds something you could provide.

**Question 3 (moderate) — Stuck on a tool**

You have been stuck for most of the morning trying to get a query to produce what you need. What is the most effective response?

- A) Keep going; you will work it out.
- B) Ask a colleague, saying what you are trying to produce, what you have tried and where it is going wrong — because a few minutes of their time saves the rest of your day, and the summary makes it quick for them to help.
- C) Produce the analysis a different way that you know works, even though it takes longer.
- D) Wait until the next team meeting to raise it.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** A morning is long enough to have made a genuine attempt, which is what makes this the right moment to ask. The three-part summary in option B is worth learning by heart — *what I'm trying to do, what I've tried, where it's going wrong* — because it turns a vague interruption into a question someone can answer in two minutes. Option D is least effective: waiting can waste days, and this is exactly what "under the guidance of more senior colleagues" is for. Option A treats struggling alone as a virtue. Option C gets the work done and leaves you unable to do it the efficient way next time.

**Question 4 (moderate) — A finding someone will not like**

Your analysis shows a change the team made has not improved the metric it was meant to improve. What is the most effective response?

- A) Report what you found, clearly and without softening it, and include what the data does and does not show.
- B) Report it, noting that the sample is small so it may change.
- C) Wait another month to see whether the picture changes.
- D) Report the metrics that did improve.

**Correct answer: A. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Reporting what you found is the job, and the "does and does not show" part is what makes it useful rather than blunt — a change that has not improved a metric after one month is a different finding from one that has not after six. Option D is least effective: selecting the favourable metrics is the one response that actively misleads, and it is the habit that, once noticed, means nobody trusts any of your analysis. Option B is fine if the sample genuinely is small and becomes a way of softening findings if used routinely. Option C delays a real result.

**Question 5 (moderate) — An error you have already sent**

You realise a report you sent yesterday contains a mistake. What is the most effective response?

- A) Tell the recipients now, explain what was wrong and what the corrected figures are, and say what you will do to avoid it.
- B) Correct it quietly in the next report.
- C) Wait to see whether anyone noticed.
- D) Tell your manager and ask what to do.

**Correct answer: A. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** People may already be acting on the wrong number, which is what makes speed matter more than comfort. Option A is quick and complete. Option C is least effective: a mistake found later by someone else is far worse than one you corrected yourself, and the delay is what damages trust rather than the error. Option B leaves people with a wrong figure they may already have used. Option D is a perfectly reasonable thing to do *as well*, particularly early on, and it should not delay telling the people who have the wrong number.

**Question 6 (moderate) — A request for a metric that will mislead**

A colleague asks for "the number of users last month". You know the figure available counts sessions, not people. What is the most effective response?

- A) Give the sessions figure, labelled clearly as sessions, and explain the difference — one person visiting four times is four sessions — so they can tell whether it answers their question.
- B) Give the sessions figure as the number of users.
- C) Tell them you cannot answer the question.
- D) Give the sessions figure and let them ask if they need more.

**Correct answer: A. Least effective: B**

**Explanation:** The distinction between sessions and users is one of the most commonly muddled in analytics, and telling someone which one they have got is a small thing that prevents a large error. Option A gives them the figure and the meaning. Option B is least effective: labelling sessions as users produces a number that will be quoted, compared and planned against incorrectly. Option D is close and relies on them knowing enough to ask. Option C withholds a useful figure over a solvable labelling issue.

**Question 7 (harder) — Being asked to explain a change you cannot explain**

A service manager asks why a metric moved last week. You can see the movement but do not know the cause. What is the most effective response?

- A) Say what you can see, say plainly that you do not yet know the cause, and say what you would check and by when.
- B) Offer the most likely explanation.
- C) Say you do not know.
- D) Say it is probably normal variation.

**Correct answer: A. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Option A does three useful things at once: it separates what is observed from what is explained, it is honest about the gap, and it gives the person a time by which they will know more. That combination reads as competent rather than uncertain. Option D is least effective — it offers a specific explanation you have not tested, in a form that sounds reassuring and stops anyone investigating. Option B has the same problem in a more honest form. Option C is honest and leaves them with nothing.

**Question 8 (harder) — Replicating existing analysis**

You are asked to reproduce an analysis a colleague did last quarter. Following their method, you get a noticeably different answer. What is the most effective response?

- A) Go through both versions to find where they diverge — usually a date range, a filter, or a changed data source — and raise it with your colleague with the specific difference.
- B) Use your figure; you followed the method.
- C) Use their figure; they are more experienced.
- D) Report both figures.

**Correct answer: A. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** A difference between two runs of the same method has a specific cause, and finding it is both quick and the whole point — it means either your run is wrong, or theirs was, or something in the data changed, and each matters. Option A names where such differences usually hide. Option C is least effective: deferring to seniority without investigating means a possible error stays in the record, and your colleague would much rather be asked. Option B assumes you are right. Option D publishes a discrepancy without resolving it.

**Question 9 (harder) — Feedback on your analysis**

A senior analyst leaves a lot of comments on a piece of your work. What is the most effective response?

- A) Work through them, make the changes you understand, and ask about any you do not — because the reasoning behind a comment is what stops the same thing recurring.
- B) Make all the changes without asking.
- C) Explain why you did it your way.
- D) Ask a different colleague whether the comments are fair.

**Correct answer: A. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** A heavily commented review early on is somebody investing time in you. Option A gets the value from it, and asking about the ones you do not understand is the part that transfers the skill. Option D is least effective: quietly checking whether the reviewer was fair goes around the person trying to help you and would land badly if they knew. Option C has a place in a conversation, and as a first response it reads as defending rather than learning. Option B is compliant and wastes the lesson.

**Question 10 (harder) — A dashboard nobody uses**

You maintain a dashboard and notice from the access logs that almost nobody opens it. What is the most effective response?

- A) Mention it to your manager or the team it was built for, and ask whether it is still needed or whether it should show something different.
- B) Keep maintaining it; it was requested.
- C) Stop maintaining it.
- D) Add more charts to make it more useful.

**Correct answer: A. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** Noticing this and raising it is genuinely valuable — maintaining an unused dashboard costs your time every month and provides nothing, and the team may have a need it no longer meets. Option A raises it in the most useful form, as a question rather than a complaint. Option C is least effective: quietly stopping means it breaks silently and someone discovers it at the worst moment. Option B accepts a permanent cost without questioning it. Option D adds work to something nobody looks at.

**Question 11 (harder) — Too much work**

You have been given more analysis than you think you can complete this week to a standard you are happy with. What is the most effective response?

- A) Say so now, explain which pieces are at risk, and ask which matter most.
- B) Do your best and see how far you get.
- C) Complete everything, accepting that the checking will be lighter than usual.
- D) Say something at the end of the week if it is not finished.

**Correct answer: A. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** Raising it now lets someone reprioritise while there is still time, and asking which matter most means that if something slips it is the piece the team would have chosen. Option C is least effective and is the tempting one: quietly reducing the checking to fit the volume produces analysis that looks complete and may be wrong, which is worse than analysis that is late and is a direct conflict with your responsibility for accuracy. Option D delivers the information too late to be useful. Option B leaves everyone to discover it at the end.

**Question 12 (harder) — Joining the community**

You are invited to a cross-government performance analysis community session. Your week is busy. What is the most effective response?

- A) Go, because your role summary names engaging with the community and these sessions are where you find out how other people solve problems you are currently solving alone.
- B) Skip it; delivery work comes first.
- C) Go if there is nothing more urgent.
- D) Ask a colleague to summarise it afterwards.

**Correct answer: A. Least effective: B**

**Explanation:** Community engagement is named explicitly in your role at this level, which is unusual and deliberate — early in a specialist career, the fastest route to competence is seeing how other people approach the same problems. Option A treats it as part of the job, which it is. Option B is least effective: "delivery first" is always available as a reason and, applied consistently, means you never go. Option C is the same in softer form, since there is always something more urgent. Option D gets you the headlines and none of the conversations, which are the useful part.

### Preparation tips

- **Check an unexpected figure before it leaves your hands.**
- **Label an unchecked number as unchecked.**
- **Ask for help with a three-part summary of where you are stuck.**
- **Separate what you observed from what you can explain.**
- **Find where two versions of an analysis diverge.**
- **Correct your own errors quickly and completely.**
- **Say what a figure counts — sessions, people, applications.**
- **Raise capacity problems at the start of the week, not the end.**

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Sending a figure because the tool produced it.**
- **Reporting only the metrics that improved.**
- **Offering an untested explanation because it sounds reassuring.**
- **Deferring to a more experienced colleague instead of finding the difference.**
- **Cutting your checking to fit the workload.**
- **Labelling sessions as users.**
- **Fixing a sent error quietly in the next report.**
- **Treating community engagement as optional.**

## Conclusion

You have worked through four assessments built around the reasoning an associate performance analyst actually uses, and it is worth drawing them together.

The cognitive section was about working carefully from what is in front of you — spotting the pattern in a set of figures, following a definition through several conditions, and checking details rather than trusting a first impression.

The numeric section was ordinary arithmetic done accurately under mild time pressure: percentages and their bases, rates, averages, and units. None of it was advanced, and all of it rewarded writing the steps down.

The verbal section asked for something slightly counterintuitive — reading exactly what a definition says and setting aside how the metric is usually calculated. "Started" means what the framework says it means. A tracking outage is not a fall in activity. And a satisfaction target of 80% is 80% of the people who answered a survey, not of your users.

The situational judgement section came back repeatedly to two habits. The first is **checking**: your role summary makes you responsible for accuracy, and almost every strong answer involved verifying something before it left your hands. The second is **saying things early**: asking when stuck, flagging an unchecked figure, correcting your own mistake, telling someone the work will not fit.

Both are worth trusting even when they feel like admissions. Experienced colleagues read an analyst who checks their own work and flags their own uncertainty as someone who can be relied on with bigger things — and they read a confident number that turns out to be wrong as a reason to check everything else you produce.

Good luck. You are at the stage where habits form fastest and matter longest, and working through all of this says something good about how you approach the work.
