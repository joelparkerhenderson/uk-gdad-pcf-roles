# Service Designer - Psychometric Assessment Resources

## Introduction

Welcome. This guide is written for you as a service designer in the UK Government Digital and Data profession — a confident, competent designer who develops designs from evidence of user needs and organisational outcomes, who is trusted to make good decisions, who recognises when to ask for further guidance, who contributes to design concepts, and who interprets evidence-based research and incorporates it into their work. That combination of trust and judgement shapes everything that follows.

You may be preparing for a formal assessment as part of a recruitment or promotion process, or you may simply want to sharpen the reasoning your job depends on. Either way, it is worth being precise about what psychometric assessment is at this level. It is not an intelligence test and it is not a knowledge quiz. It is a standardised sample of the thinking a role demands, and for a competent practitioner that thinking has a particular character: you are rarely choosing between a right answer and a wrong one, and usually choosing between defensible options under incomplete information, competing constraints and real consequences.

Three features of your role shape the questions here. First, you are trusted to make good decisions — which means the assessment will not reward escalating everything, and will reward knowing which decisions genuinely belong to somebody else. Second, you now design strategically: your role names identifying risks and using design to mitigate them, identifying opportunities and using design to take advantage of them, and aligning your work to organisational objectives as well as team goals. That turns prioritisation from an administrative question into a design one. Third, you run design sessions, engage the right people, and give and receive constructive feedback — so several items concern how you get good thinking out of a room rather than out of yourself.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the reasoning your role genuinely uses, presented through practical materials rather than abstract puzzles. At your level the materials get messier and the reasoning less mechanical. You will meet evidence that supports two incompatible readings, service problems whose cause is genuinely ambiguous, design constraints that turn out to be negotiable and others that do not, and prioritisation problems with no dominant option.

The format is usually an online test of fifteen to thirty minutes covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective against a norm group at a comparable level. Many platforms are adaptive, so a test that feels hard is usually one you are doing well on, and most employers receive an acuity report distinguishing speed from accuracy, which rewards precision.

Two things distinguish this section at your level.

The first is that you are expected to reason about risk and opportunity, not only about problems. Your role names identifying potential risks and using design to mitigate them, and identifying opportunities and using design to take advantage of them. That is a forward-looking form of reasoning: given what this evidence shows, what is likely to go wrong, and where is there something available that nobody has taken? Several items below turn on spotting the second-order consequence rather than the immediate one.

The second is comfort with irreducible uncertainty. A number of questions have no clean answer, only a best one, and the discriminating factor is whether you can identify the strongest reading of ambiguous evidence and state its limits rather than collapsing prematurely into confidence. Holding "this is worth acting on" and "this is not yet established" at the same time is uncomfortable, and it is what competent design judgement consists of.

You need to memorise nothing. Everything you need appears in the question. What you need to bring is the discipline not to let familiarity substitute for examination.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The cognitive dimensions map onto the skills named in your role summary:

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **Evidence-based design**, where you analyse, synthesise and clearly explain evidence relevant to users or a service.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Design communication**, where you clearly explain design decisions using appropriate tools and methods. A decision resting on an overreach does not survive its first serious challenge.
- **Error checking** maps to **Designing for everyone**, where you ensure a design meets appropriate standards such as accessibility regulations.
- **Prioritisation and risk reasoning** map to **Designing strategically**, where you identify potential risks and use design to mitigate them, identify opportunities and use design to exploit them, and align to organisational objectives.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Iterative design**, where you prototype at different fidelities and iterate across successive rounds of research.
- **Reasoning about people and positions** maps to **Designing together**, where you identify and engage the right people throughout the design process.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Auditing a design against a standard**

Your team's standard requires every transactional page to: use words users would use, say what happens next, state how long it will take, avoid asking for information already held, and offer a way to get help. You review a page reading: "Your application has been received. Your case will be triaged by the assessment team. Please re-enter your National Insurance number to confirm." The service already holds the National Insurance number, and there is no contact information on the page. How many requirements are unmet?

- A) 2
- B) 3
- C) 4
- D) 5

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Check all five. "Triaged by the assessment team" is internal terminology, so the first requirement fails. The page does indicate what happens next — the case will be assessed — so that one is arguably met, if minimally. No timescale is given, so that fails. It asks for a National Insurance number already held, so that fails. There is no route to help, so that fails. Four requirements unmet, giving C. Option B typically comes from missing the absent help route, which is the easiest to overlook precisely because it is an absence rather than a presence. That is worth generalising: when auditing against a standard, the requirements you miss are usually the ones about something that should be there and is not, rather than something present and wrong.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction from a governance rule**

Your organisation's rule states: "A service may be released to public beta only with sign-off from the service owner, unless it is replacing an existing service on a like-for-like basis and the change has been assessed as low risk." A colleague says: "It went to public beta last week without service owner sign-off." Which must be true?

- A) The rule was breached.
- B) The release was a like-for-like replacement assessed as low risk.
- C) Either it was a low-risk like-for-like replacement, or the rule was breached.
- D) The release was risky.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A conditional with a stated exception, plus facts silent on the exception, licenses only the disjunction. Options A and B each assume a branch. Option D is the distractor that catches people who read quickly — "not assessed as low risk" and "risky" are different claims, and in any case you do not know whether an assessment happened. Only C holds in every case. Note that the exception has two conjoined conditions: like-for-like *and* low risk. A release meeting only one would not qualify, and partial satisfaction of a compound exception is one of the most common misreadings in real governance conversations. As someone trusted to make good decisions, the useful move is to establish which route was taken before forming a view.

**Question 3 (moderate) — Competing explanations**

Completion of an application is 74% for users arriving from a GOV.UK guidance page and 46% for users arriving directly from a search engine. A product manager proposes redesigning the form. What is the most rigorous analysis?

- A) Agree; the form is clearly failing direct arrivals.
- B) The two groups differ in preparation rather than necessarily in what they encounter: users who came via guidance have generally learned what is required and gathered documents, while direct arrivals may be discovering the requirements mid-application — so the highest-value intervention may be at the point of entry rather than inside the form.
- C) A 28 point gap is too large to be explained by anything but the form.
- D) Direct entry to the form should be blocked.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Referral route is a marker for what the user has already done before arriving, and this is one of the most consequential confounds in service analytics. Someone who read a guidance page has typically established their eligibility and assembled their documents; someone landing from a search result may be doing both while the form is open. That difference in preparation can account for much of the gap without the form being worse for anyone. Option B also draws the practical implication, which is what makes it rigorous rather than merely sceptical: a lightweight "what you'll need" step for direct arrivals may be far cheaper and more effective than a form redesign. Option A acts on an unexamined correlation. Option C mistakes effect size for causal certainty, which is a surprisingly common error among people comfortable with data. Option D would harm users who arrive prepared.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Prioritising by what can change**

You have capacity for one substantial piece of work this quarter:

1. A service model decision is taken next month, committing three years of build.
2. A live service has a 32% drop-off at one step, unchanged for months.
3. A team wants pre-launch usability testing on screens already signed off, launching in four weeks.
4. A director wants design support for a business case due in six months.

Which is strongest, and why?

- A) Item 2, because it affects the most users now.
- B) Item 1, because the decision is imminent, commits three years, and design input is the only thing that could still change it — giving it far the highest value.
- C) Item 4, because the director is the most senior sponsor.
- D) Item 3, because launching untested is a risk.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The right frame is how much a decision changes as a result of your input, and whether there is still time for it to. Item 1 scores highest on both: a service model choice is exactly where design shifts the answer rather than decorating it, the window is open, and three years of commitment makes a wrong choice enormously expensive. Item 2 matters greatly but has persisted for months and will persist for three more, so deferring costs comparatively little — and analytics may be the better first instrument there anyway. Item 3 is weakest despite sounding responsible: signed-off screens four weeks from launch means findings arrive with nowhere to go, which is testing for reassurance rather than for decisions. Item 4 has six months. Option C is the seniority trap, and at your level it is a live risk because directors ask directly.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Risk reasoning**

A team proposes replacing a paper form with an online-only service, projecting a 30% reduction in processing cost. About 12% of current applications arrive on paper. What is the most complete risk analysis?

- A) The risk is that 12% of users cannot apply.
- B) The immediate risk is exclusion of the 12%, but the second-order risks matter as much: those users will contact the service by other routes, so contact centre volume will rise; some will apply late or not at all, which may shift cost rather than remove it; and if the paper cohort skews towards people with the greatest need, the policy outcome is affected as well as the service metric.
- C) There is no significant risk; 88% of users are unaffected.
- D) The projected saving is probably overstated.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The strongest analysis follows the consequences beyond the first step, which is precisely what your role means by identifying risks and using design to mitigate them. Removing a channel does not remove the people who used it: they telephone, they visit, they ask a relative, they apply late, or they do not apply at all. Each of those has a cost, and some of those costs land in budgets other than the one showing the saving. The final clause of option B is the most important — if paper applicants skew towards older users, users with lower digital confidence, or users in crisis, then the service is shedding cost by shedding the people it most exists to serve, which is a policy failure and not an efficiency. Option A stops at the first order. Option C treats a majority as sufficient. Option D may be true and is a narrower point that follows from B rather than replacing it.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Strength of a finding**

Ten of fourteen participants failed to notice a "save and return" link. Which conclusion is best supported?

- A) 71% of users do not notice the link.
- B) Discoverability of the link is a substantial problem, evidenced across most participants and varied user groups, warranting design work and a follow-up measure of whether the change helps.
- C) The link should be moved to the top of the page.
- D) The link is invisible.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Ten of fourteen is a strong qualitative signal, strong enough to act on, and B states it at that strength while specifying what acting looks like. Option A converts fourteen participants into a population rate, which is the most common overreach in design reporting and the one most likely to be quoted back at you in a meeting you are not in. Option C prescribes one solution, and at your level the more useful contribution is establishing the problem clearly enough that the design conversation can happen properly — the cause might be position, wording, visual weight, or the user's mental model of the task, and each implies a different fix. Option D overstates: participants did not notice the link, which is different from the link being invisible. Note that B also builds in the follow-up measurement, which is what turns a finding into a change you can defend.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Designing under a constraint**

You need to research and design for a service used mainly by people over 75, many not online. Your organisation's recruitment supplier works exclusively through an online panel, and procurement for a new supplier takes eight weeks. You have five. What is strongest?

- A) Use the online panel and note the limitation.
- B) Work within the five weeks using routes that do not need procurement — partnering with organisations already working with older people, running sessions in community venues or by telephone, and arranging incentives that do not require an online account — while starting procurement in parallel for future rounds.
- C) Delay until a suitable supplier is procured.
- D) Recruit through colleagues' older relatives.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The constraint is procurement, not recruitment, and the strongest analysis separates the two. Partnering with charities, day centres, libraries and local support organisations is a legitimate and widely used route that does not depend on a supplier contract, and telephone or in-person sessions remove the digital barrier the panel creates. Cash or high-street vouchers avoid the online-account problem that quietly excludes exactly this group. Option B also starts procurement anyway, which fixes the structural problem for next time — solving the instance and the system together. Option A produces findings about digitally confident older people, a different population from the one the service serves, and a limitation note does not repair that. Option C forfeits five weeks and, in most organisations, the work then never happens. Option D is not research and would rightly be challenged.

**Question 8 (harder) — Scoping a flaw**

A study you are relying on used a prototype whose "back" function was broken throughout. Four findings: (i) participants could not locate the eligibility criteria; (ii) participants abandoned after taking a wrong turn; (iii) tasks took longer than expected; (iv) participants misread the declaration wording. Which findings are compromised?

- A) All four.
- B) None; it was a minor fault.
- C) Findings (ii) and (iii) are directly compromised, because inability to go back plausibly caused both the abandonment after wrong turns and the extended durations, while (i) and (iv) concern comprehension of content and are largely unaffected.
- D) Only finding (ii).

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The skill is scoping a flaw precisely rather than treating it as total or trivial. A broken back function prevents recovery from a wrong turn, which directly manufactures both abandonment and inflated task times — so (ii) and (iii) cannot be separated from the artefact. Findings (i) and (iv) concern whether participants could find and understand content, which the fault does not touch; someone who could not locate the eligibility criteria could not locate them with a working back button. Option A discards two sound findings. Option B leaves two manufactured findings in circulation, where they will justify design changes to problems that do not exist. Option D catches the obvious compromise and misses the duration one. Being able to say what a team can still rely on is more useful than either wholesale condemnation or wholesale acceptance.

**Question 9 (harder) — Spotting the opportunity**

Analytics show that 40% of users who abandon at the evidence step return within seven days and complete. The team treats the 40% as a success and focuses on the 60% who do not. What is the strongest observation?

- A) Agree; the 60% is the real problem.
- B) The 40% who return are also evidence of a problem — they are making two or more attempts at something that should take one, which costs them time and generates repeat contact — and understanding why they had to leave and what let them come back successfully is likely the fastest route to helping the 60% too.
- C) The 40% should be excluded from the drop-off figure.
- D) The service should send reminders to everyone who abandons.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This is opportunity reasoning, which your role names explicitly. The team has read a number as a success because it ends well, and missed that a returning user is a user who could not finish the first time — almost always because they did not have a document to hand. That is a design problem with a design answer, such as telling people what they will need before they start, or making it genuinely easy to save and resume. Option B also identifies the most valuable move: the returners are a natural experiment. Something enabled them to come back and succeed, and whatever that is may be the mechanism that would help the 60% who could not. Option A accepts the framing and studies only the failures. Option C is a reporting choice that would conceal the phenomenon entirely. Option D leaps to one intervention without understanding the cause, and reminders will not help someone who cannot obtain the document.

**Question 10 (harder) — Reconciling contradictory evidence**

A satisfaction survey of 2,000 users reports 81% positive. Your fourteen sessions found substantial frustration, with eleven participants describing significant difficulty. The contact centre reports the service as its third-highest call driver. How should you synthesise this?

- A) Trust the survey; 2,000 responses outweigh fourteen sessions.
- B) The sources measure different populations and constructs: satisfaction surveys are typically served at the end of a journey so people who abandoned rarely see them, and they often ask about outcome rather than process — while the contact centre volume is behavioural evidence at scale that corroborates the sessions. The picture is a service most people complete, many find hard, and a substantial minority need help with.
- C) The sources conflict irreconcilably.
- D) The service is performing well with minor usability issues.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The apparent conflict dissolves once you name who each source sees and what it measures. Satisfaction surveys reach people who reached the end, and asking whether someone got what they needed is different from asking whether it was hard. Your sessions observed the process. The contact centre data is the pivot and the most useful element of the synthesis: high call volume is behavioural evidence, at scale, that people cannot complete unaided, and it corroborates your qualitative findings using precisely the kind of data that sceptical colleagues find credible. Option B assembles all three without discarding any. Option A defers to scale over relevance. Option C abandons an available reconciliation. Option D is the comfortable reading and ignores the contact centre entirely, which is the single most telling figure in the set.

**Question 11 (harder) — A constraint that may not be one**

A policy colleague states that a service must collect three years of address history because "the policy requires it". Research shows the question causes 18% of users to abandon. What is the most productive line of enquiry?

- A) Accept the requirement and design the question as well as possible.
- B) Ask what the requirement is for and where it is written, on the reasoning that the underlying need — most likely identity verification or fraud prevention — may be satisfiable by other means, and that knowing the need rather than the question opens design options that arguing about the question never will.
- C) Challenge the policy requirement and ask for it to be removed.
- D) Propose making the question optional.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** "The policy requires it" is sometimes exactly true, sometimes a compressed account of a need that could be met differently, and sometimes an inherited practice nobody has revisited. You cannot tell which without asking, and the productive question is not "must we?" but "what is this for?". If the need is identity verification, address history is one instrument among several and the conversation becomes a design conversation. If it is genuinely statutory, you have learned that in one exchange and can design the question properly with your credibility intact — which matters, because a designer who treats every constraint as negotiable is not believed about the ones that are. Option C is a head-on challenge without understanding the basis and will not succeed. Option A gives up before asking. Option D proposes a solution that may quietly break the requirement it was meant to accommodate.

**Question 12 (harder) — A flaw in your own work**

Three weeks after a design decision you supported, you realise the research behind it recruited only users who had previously completed the service successfully. What should you do?

- A) Note it as a limitation in the next report.
- B) Tell the team and the decision-makers now, scope precisely which conclusions are affected and which still stand, and propose targeted follow-up with users who did not complete — since those are the people the study could not see and the ones the design most needs to serve.
- C) Quietly commission follow-up research and correct the record if findings differ.
- D) Say nothing; the design decision was reasonable anyway.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A sample of successful completers cannot see the failures the design was meant to address, and a decision has already been made on it. Option B does the three things that matter: it tells the people who acted on the finding, it scopes the damage precisely rather than either minimising or catastrophising, and it proposes the fix. The scoping matters — conclusions about how successful users navigate the journey may remain perfectly sound, and saying so is accuracy rather than defensiveness. Option A defers disclosure past the point of usefulness. Option C conceals a known flaw while the team continues building on it. Option D is least defensible: reasoning that the decision was probably fine anyway substitutes your guess for the evidence you were relying on. Reporting your own methodological error promptly is uncomfortable and is the clearest available demonstration that your other work can be trusted.

### Preparation tips

- **Follow consequences beyond the first order.** Removing a channel does not remove the people who used it.
- **Ask who could not have appeared in this data.** Sampling frame exclusions are the highest-yield question against any evidence.
- **Scope flaws precisely.** Say which claims a fault compromises and which survive.
- **Frame prioritisation as what can still change.** Decision leverage and remaining time, not importance or profile.
- **Read a good number for the problem inside it.** A 40% return rate is also a 40% failure-first-time rate.
- **Ask what a requirement is for.** It opens options that arguing about the requirement never will.
- **Rehearse the strength-of-claim ladder.** Observation, pattern, supported finding, measured rate.
- **Read the stem's final sentence twice.** Items often ask something narrower than the scenario implies.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Converting participants into percentages.** "Ten of fourteen participants" must never become "71% of users".
- **Treating effect size as causal certainty.** A 28 point gap can be entirely confounded.
- **Stopping at first-order risk.** The second-order effects are usually where the real cost sits.
- **Missing absent requirements when auditing.** What is not there is harder to see than what is wrong.
- **Ranking by sponsor seniority.** Directors ask directly at your level, which makes this a live risk.
- **Condemning a flawed study wholesale.** Teams need to know what they can still rely on.
- **Reading a partially satisfied compound exception as satisfied.** "Like-for-like and low risk" needs both.
- **Prescribing the fix instead of establishing the problem.** It is weaker and forecloses better options.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A numeric reasoning assessment measures how accurately you handle the quantitative material your role produces and consumes. At your level the emphasis shifts from calculating to interrogating. You will still need to compute rates and averages correctly, but the harder items ask whether a figure means what somebody claims it means — which is, not coincidentally, the question you are often the only person in the room positioned to ask.

The format is typically a timed online test of fifteen to thirty minutes, each item presenting a table, chart or short paragraph of figures with a multiple-choice question. Calculators are usually provided and the arithmetic is deliberately tractable. Time per item is tight, so the operative skill is locating what matters and disregarding what does not.

There is a professional stake worth naming. Quantitative evidence carries unearned authority in most organisations: a number in a slide is treated as fact and a design argument as a preference, regardless of the rigour behind either. Designers who cannot interrogate numbers confidently lose arguments they should win, and occasionally win arguments they should lose. Being the person who asks "what is the denominator, and who could not have appeared in this data?" is a substantial part of the value you add — and your role's requirement to align design work to organisational objectives frequently comes down to whether you can engage with the numbers those objectives are expressed in.

Four habits carry most of the load. Establish what the question asks before looking at the data. Identify the denominator and the population it represents before calculating. Estimate, calculate, then compare. And sanity-check against the world — a figure implying that 40% of users complete a form in under three seconds is measuring something other than what it claims.

Distractors are built from a predictable catalogue: wrong denominator, percentage confused with percentage point, new value used as the base in a percentage change, rates averaged without weighting, raw counts compared across unequal groups. Finding your answer among the options proves nothing; knowing the catalogue lets you see the trap being set.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The numerical skills tested map onto your named skills:

- **Reading analytics accurately** maps to **Evidence-based design**, where you analyse, synthesise and clearly explain evidence such as web analytics data.
- **Comparing rates between groups** maps to **Designing for everyone**, where a gap between groups is often the first numerical trace of exclusion.
- **Measuring whether an iteration worked** maps to **Iterative design**, where you iterate and improve based on successive rounds of research.
- **Quantifying risk and opportunity** maps to **Designing strategically**, where identifying risks and opportunities requires knowing how many people are involved.
- **Explaining figures at defensible strength** maps to **Design communication**, where you clearly explain design decisions.
- **Interrogating others' claims** maps to **Designing together**, where you give and receive constructive feedback — including on evidence.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Completion rate**

Of 480 users who started, 360 completed. What percentage completed?

- A) 70%
- B) 75%
- C) 80%
- D) 85%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 360 ÷ 480 = 0.75, so 75%, and the answer is B. Estimate first: 360 of 480 is three quarters, since 240 would be half. Options A and C come from dividing by 514 and 450. Even here, note how you would report it: "75% of users who started completed" is accurate; "75% of users completed" shifts the denominator to everyone who might have started, including people who never reached the service, and that difference matters when the figure is used to argue that a service is working.

**Question 2 (easy) — Drop-off against the right base**

A step is reached by 3,600 users and passed by 2,700. What is the drop-off rate?

- A) 25%
- B) 33%
- C) 75%
- D) 30%

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The drop-off is 3,600 − 2,700 = 900, measured against the number who reached the step: 900 ÷ 3,600 = 0.25, or 25%, giving A. Option B is 900 ÷ 2,700, dividing by those who continued — the most common error in journey analytics, and one that overstates every drop-off it touches. The rule is that a drop-off rate is always measured against the number who arrived. This matters more than it sounds when steps are being compared: if two steps are computed with different denominators, the resulting ranking of where to focus design effort can be simply wrong.

**Question 3 (easy) — Channel rates**

| Channel | Started | Completed |
|---|---|---|
| Online | 12,000 | 9,600 |
| Telephone | 2,400 | 2,160 |
| Paper | 1,600 | 640 |

Which channel has the lowest completion rate, and how many users does it lose?

- A) Paper, 40% rate, 960 users lost
- B) Online, 80% rate, 2,400 users lost
- C) Paper, 60% rate, 640 users lost
- D) Telephone, 90% rate, 240 users lost

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Rates are: online 9,600 ÷ 12,000 = 80%; telephone 2,160 ÷ 2,400 = 90%; paper 640 ÷ 1,600 = 40%. Paper is lowest, and it loses 1,600 − 640 = 960 users. So A. Note what the two figures together tell you, because reporting only one is the mistake. Online loses 2,400 users, more than twice as many as paper, so by volume it is the larger problem. But paper's 40% completion rate signals something badly broken, and paper users are frequently those who cannot use the online channel — which makes their failure rate an inclusion problem rather than an efficiency one. A good report gives both and says which is which.

**Question 4 (easy) — Elapsed journey time**

A journey has: form (30 minutes), wait for reference (3 working days), post evidence (2 working days transit), assessment (5 weeks), decision letter (2 working days). Roughly how long is the journey from the user's perspective?

- A) About 30 minutes
- B) About 5 weeks
- C) About 6 to 7 weeks
- D) About 3 months

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Add the waits, which dominate. Three working days, plus two in transit, plus five weeks, plus two more is roughly five weeks plus seven working days — call it six and a half weeks. The half hour of user activity is negligible. So C. Option A is the instructive trap: it counts only the time the user spends doing something, which is exactly how a service comes to be described internally as "a half-hour application" while the person waits a month and a half. Seeing the whole elapsed journey is close to the definition of your discipline, and it is where the anxiety, the repeat contact and much of the abandonment actually live.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Did the change work?**

Before: 720 of 1,200 users completed a step. After: 950 of 1,400 completed. Did completion improve, and by how much in percentage points?

- A) Yes, by 230 users
- B) Yes, by about 8 percentage points
- C) Yes, by about 13 percentage points
- D) No, the rate fell

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Convert to rates, because the totals differ. Before: 720 ÷ 1,200 = 60%. After: 950 ÷ 1,400 = 67.9%, call it 68%. The improvement is about 8 percentage points, giving B. Option A compares raw completions across different-sized groups, which is meaningless here — more users completed partly because more users started, and crediting the design with the whole increase would be wrong. Option C looks like a miscalculation of the rate. This is precisely the comparison your role requires when iterating a design across successive rounds, and the trap is common in real reporting: "230 more people completed" sounds like a win and may conceal a fall in the rate if traffic rose faster than completions.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Comparing groups**

Of 800 users under 65, 680 completed online. Of 240 users over 65, 72 completed online. What is the gap in percentage points?

- A) 30
- B) 45
- C) 55
- D) 85

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Under-65s: 680 ÷ 800 = 85%. Over-65s: 72 ÷ 240 = 30%. The gap is 85 − 30 = 55 percentage points, giving C. Options A and D are the two group rates offered as if they were the gap, which is a distractor pattern worth recognising — numbers from your working are frequently among the options. A 55 point gap is enormous and would justify immediate investigation of what in the online journey older users cannot get past. Note that comparing 680 with 72 directly would have been meaningless given the group sizes; converting to rates is the only fair comparison, and it is also the only one that makes the inclusion problem visible.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Percentage change**

Weekly completions rose from 640 to 800. What is the percentage increase?

- A) 20%
- B) 25%
- C) 80%
- D) 125%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Percentage change divides the difference by the original: 800 − 640 = 160, and 160 ÷ 640 = 0.25, which is 25%, giving B. Option A is 160 ÷ 800, dividing by the new value — the most common error in this question type and one that under-reports every improvement. Option D expresses the new figure as a percentage of the old. The rule to fix: for percentage change, the denominator is always where you started. Under time pressure the new number is the one most recently in working memory, and it is the wrong one.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Quantifying an exclusion risk**

A service receives 240,000 applications a year, of which 9% arrive on paper. The team proposes closing the paper channel. How many people a year would need another route?

- A) 2,160
- B) 21,600
- C) 26,700
- D) 216,000

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 9% of 240,000 is 0.09 × 240,000 = 21,600, giving B. Option A is 9% of 24,000, option D is 90%. The reason this calculation belongs in a design guide rather than a maths one is what it does to a conversation: "9% of applications" sounds like a rounding error, and "21,600 people a year" does not. Converting a percentage into people is one of the most effective things you can do when arguing about a channel closure, and it takes five seconds. It is also the number that lets you ask the next question, which is what happens to those 21,600 — how many will telephone, how many will apply late, and how many will not apply at all.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Choosing the denominator**

A journey shows 40,000 start, 32,000 pass eligibility, 19,200 upload evidence, 17,280 submit. What proportion of those who uploaded evidence went on to submit?

- A) 43%
- B) 54%
- C) 60%
- D) 90%

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** The stem names users who uploaded evidence, so the denominator is 19,200 and the part is 17,280: 17,280 ÷ 19,200 = 0.9, which is 90%, giving D. Option A uses 40,000, option B uses 32,000, option C is the eligibility-to-upload rate. All four are true of this funnel; one answers the question. Strategically the funnel also tells you where design effort belongs: 12,800 lost between eligibility and upload, against 1,920 at submission. Being able to read that in fifteen seconds is what lets you redirect a team's attention in the meeting rather than a week later.

**Question 10 (harder) — A metric that conflates populations**

A dashboard reports "average time on the eligibility page: 25 seconds", and a product manager concludes the page is easy. Your sessions showed participants spending two to three minutes reading it. What is the most likely explanation and the right response?

- A) The dashboard is wrong and should be disregarded.
- B) The mean probably mixes two populations — people who bounce within a few seconds and people who stay and read — so it describes neither, and the right response is to ask for the distribution rather than to dispute the figure.
- C) The session participants were unusually slow.
- D) The two measures cannot be compared.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A mean time on page combines two very different behaviours: glancing and leaving, and staying and reading. If a substantial share bounce within seconds, they drag the mean far below the experience of anyone actually engaging with the content, and the resulting figure describes nobody. Many analytics implementations also handle the last page of a session or a timeout inconsistently, distorting it further. The productive response is to ask for the distribution, which will typically reveal two clusters, and asking that is not an accusation — the figure is probably calculated correctly and interpreted wrongly. Option A picks a fight over a number that is not wrong. Option C blames the participants. Option D forecloses a reconciliation that is available and useful.

**Question 11 (harder) — Percentage points and relative change**

Completion rose from 45% to 54% after a redesign. Which statement is accurate?

- A) A 9% increase.
- B) A 9 percentage point increase, equivalent to a 20% relative increase.
- C) A 20 percentage point increase.
- D) A 16.7% increase.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The arithmetic gap is 54 − 45 = 9 percentage points. The relative increase is 9 ÷ 45 = 0.2, or 20%. Option B labels both correctly. Option A is ambiguous and generally read as relative, making it wrong. Option C misapplies the percentage-point label. Option D divides by the new value. The professional stake is real: "completion rose 20%" and "completion rose 9 percentage points" describe the same change, and one will appear in a benefits case. You will encounter colleagues who reach for the flattering version, usually without any intent to mislead, and being the person who calmly asks for both is a cheap way to keep the organisation's evidence honest.

**Question 12 (harder) — What a small comparison can establish**

Your team wants to know whether a redesign improved completion. Baseline is 60%, and a 6 percentage point improvement would be meaningful. A colleague proposes 25 moderated sessions before and 25 after. What is the strongest response?

- A) Agree; 50 sessions is substantial.
- B) Explain that a 6 point difference on 25 per group is between one and two participants and well inside ordinary variation, so the comparison cannot detect it — recommend measuring the rate with live analytics across all users, and spending the 50 sessions on understanding mechanisms, which moderated research does uniquely well.
- C) Increase to 40 per group.
- D) Run it and report the difference with caveats.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Twenty-five per group cannot distinguish a six point difference from chance; detecting a difference that small reliably needs hundreds per group. Option B says so and redirects rather than merely objecting, which is the substantive contribution: analytics measures rates across the whole user base at negligible marginal cost, while moderated sessions are the only instrument that explains why a rate is what it is. That division of labour is the recommendation. Option A endorses a design that cannot answer its own question. Option C moves towards adequacy without approaching it while still misallocating scarce sessions. Option D is the most damaging, because a reported difference from an underpowered comparison invites the team to act on noise, and caveats do not travel — the number does.

### Preparation tips

- **Find the denominator first.** Underline the group the question names.
- **Measure drop-off against the number who arrived.** Not the number who continued.
- **Convert percentages into people.** "9% of applications" and "21,600 people a year" are the same fact and only one of them changes a decision.
- **Convert to rates before comparing anything.** Raw counts across unequal groups mislead every time.
- **Add up the waiting, not just the doing.** A journey's real duration is dominated by the gaps.
- **Ask for the distribution.** Most misleading metrics are correctly calculated and wrongly interpreted.
- **Know what samples can detect.** No formal calculation is needed to see that 25 per group cannot see six points.
- **Practise on your own service's numbers.** Calculate every step's loss on a real journey.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Dividing by the wrong total.** Funnel items are built around this single error.
- **Comparing raw completions before and after.** Traffic changes too, and credits the design with an increase it did not produce.
- **Confusing percentages with percentage points.** Both are correct labels for different things.
- **Counting only active time in a journey.** This is how a "half-hour application" comes to mean six weeks.
- **Accepting a mean without checking for clusters.** Bimodal behaviour hides entirely behind an average.
- **Reporting a number from your working as though it answered the question.** Distractors are frequently built from exactly that.
- **Endorsing an underpowered comparison.** Acting on noise is worse than acting on nothing.
- **Leaving a percentage as a percentage.** People do not feel percentages; they feel people.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately you read. In a role that runs on documents — service standards, accessibility requirements, policy summaries, operational procedures, letters sent to users, supplier contracts — reading accuracy is the substrate everything else rests on. A misread requirement produces a design that fails an audit; a misread finding produces a design that fails a user.

The classic format presents a short passage of dense workplace prose, typically three to six sentences, followed by statements to classify as True, False or Cannot Say. True means the statement follows necessarily. False means the passage contradicts it. Cannot Say means the passage neither establishes nor excludes it. Alongside these come comprehension questions, main-point questions, and items asking which of four summaries is most accurate.

The governing rule is that you answer from the passage alone. Your professional knowledge must be set aside entirely — and this becomes harder, not easier, with experience. The more standards you have read, the more readily you supply the clause you expect to be there and feel confident doing it. That is the characteristic error at your level, and it needs a mechanical counter-habit: before marking anything True, put your finger on the words.

Cannot Say deserves particular attention because people avoid it. It feels evasive, so candidates reach for True or False to appear decisive. In practice Cannot Say is frequently correct, and choosing it confidently signals good reading. The diagnostic: if you are filling a gap with knowledge or inference, however reasonable, the answer is Cannot Say.

Small words carry the meaning. "All" is not "most". "May" is not "must". "Should" is not "will". "Encouraged" is not "required". "Consider whether" is not "recommend". "Unless", "provided that", "solely" and "where practicable" govern everything around them. Wrong answers are built almost entirely from swaps of exactly these terms.

There is a specific payoff for a service designer. A great many design opportunities live in the gap between what a policy actually requires and what everyone assumes it requires, and reading the requirement precisely is how you find that gap. This section is that skill under exam conditions.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The verbal skills tested map onto your named skills:

- **Reading precisely** maps to **Designing for everyone**, where you ensure a design meets appropriate standards such as accessibility regulations.
- **Separating statement from inference** maps to **Evidence-based design**, where you analyse, synthesise and clearly explain evidence.
- **Identifying the main point** maps to **Design communication**, where you clearly explain problems and design ideas to others.
- **Handling qualified language** maps to **Designing strategically**, where you align design to organisational objectives expressed in documents full of "should" and "where practicable" — and where knowing which is which tells you how much room you have.
- **Reading for absence** maps to **Designing for everyone**, where noticing that a procedure never mentions people without internet access has a direct consequence.
- **Reading feedback and direction accurately** maps to **Designing together**, where you give and receive constructive feedback.

### Practice questions

*Passage A — for Questions 1 to 4*

"All new public-facing services must meet the accessibility standard before going into public beta. Where a service cannot meet a specific requirement, the team must publish an accessibility statement setting out the gap and the plan to close it. Services already in live operation must meet the standard within 18 months of this policy taking effect. Internal-facing tools are outside the scope of this policy, although teams are encouraged to apply the same standard."

**Question 1 (easy)** — Statement: "Internal tools must meet the accessibility standard." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The passage places internal tools outside scope and says teams are *encouraged* to apply the same standard. Encouragement is not requirement, so the statement's "must" contradicts the passage, making it False. Note the two-part structure — exclusion followed by soft aspiration — which recurs constantly in policy prose and is frequently misreported in both directions: as an obligation by people who want the standard applied, and as a prohibition by people who do not.

**Question 2 (moderate)** — Statement: "A service that cannot meet one requirement has breached the policy." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The passage anticipates this and provides a route: publish an accessibility statement setting out the gap and the plan to close it. Failing to meet a requirement is therefore not itself a breach; the breach would be failing to publish. The statement asserts a breach from the failure alone, contradicting the passage, so it is False. Well-drafted standards commonly work this way — they set an expectation and then define what honesty looks like when it cannot be met. Reading them as absolute produces unnecessary alarm and, more damagingly, encourages teams to conceal gaps rather than declare them, which is the opposite of what the clause is for.

**Question 3 (moderate)** — Statement: "A service that entered public beta last year without meeting the standard breached the policy." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The requirement applies to *new* public-facing services entering public beta, and services already in live operation have 18 months. But you are not told when the policy took effect, so you cannot tell whether a service entering beta last year was covered by it at the time. If the policy took effect this year, that service was not subject to the beta requirement then. Because the fact you would need is absent, the answer is Cannot Say. Whenever a passage sets an obligation relative to a policy's commencement, check whether the commencement date is actually given — it very often is not, and that absence is deliberate.

**Question 4 (harder)** — Statement: "Publishing an accessibility statement is sufficient for a service to comply with this policy." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The passage requires a statement where a specific requirement cannot be met, and specifies what the statement must contain. It does not say that publishing one discharges every obligation — a service might publish a statement and still fail something else the passage does not mention — nor does it say publishing is insufficient. It is silent on the question, so the answer is Cannot Say. Candidates who reason that the passage "provides a route" and therefore taking the route means compliance are supplying a link the text does not make, which is precisely the inference this section penalises and, in practice, precisely how accessibility statements come to be treated as a compliance mechanism rather than a disclosure.

*Passage B — for Questions 5 to 8*

"The review found that applicants using the assisted digital route completed more slowly than online applicants and required an average of 2.3 contacts with the service. The review notes that assisted digital users differed from online users in several respects, including age and first language. The review did not assess whether the completed applications were of comparable quality. It recommends that the service team consider whether additional guidance would reduce the number of contacts."

**Question 5 (easy)** — Statement: "Assisted digital applicants required more than two contacts on average." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The passage states an average of 2.3 contacts, and 2.3 is more than two, so the statement follows and is True. Answer quickly. The only care needed is to notice that the claim concerns assisted digital users specifically, not applicants in general — a statement saying "applicants required more than two contacts" would broaden beyond what the passage supports and would be Cannot Say.

**Question 6 (moderate)** — Statement: "The assisted digital route causes applications to take longer." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The passage reports an association and then explicitly flags that the two groups differed in age and first language. Either could be responsible. The passage neither asserts nor excludes a causal role for the route itself, so the answer is Cannot Say. The design consequence is direct: if the route's design causes the delay you redesign the route, and if assisted digital users are largely people for whom English is an additional language then the answer is translation, interpretation or plainer content. Treating an association as a cause is among the most reliable ways for a team to spend a quarter improving the wrong thing, and the passage's authors were careful precisely to prevent it.

**Question 7 (moderate)** — Statement: "Assisted digital applications are of lower quality." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The passage says explicitly that the review did not assess whether completed applications were of comparable quality. Declared silence establishes nothing in either direction, so the answer is Cannot Say. Candidates sometimes reason that slower completion and more contacts imply worse applications — the inference the item is built to catch — and it could equally run the other way, since 2.3 contacts with the service might produce a better application than one unaided attempt. A review that did not look cannot be evidence about what it did not look at.

**Question 8 (harder)** — Statement: "The review recommends providing additional guidance." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The review recommends that the team *consider whether* additional guidance would reduce contacts — a recommendation to evaluate an option, including the possibility of concluding it would not help. The statement asserts a recommendation to provide, which the review did not make, so it is False rather than Cannot Say, because the passage tells you what was recommended and it was something different. This distinction pervades real review documents and produces a familiar sequence: a recommendation to consider becomes a commitment to deliver, which is tracked and reported against as though someone had decided it.

*Passage C — for Questions 9 to 12*

"Teams should offer users a choice of channel wherever practicable. A digital channel must not be the only option for services used predominantly by people at risk of digital exclusion. Where a team withdraws a channel, it must assess the impact on affected users and record the assessment; records are reviewed annually. Users who need assistance must be able to reach a person by telephone during published service hours."

**Question 9 (easy)** — Statement: "Users must be able to reach a person at any time." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The passage says users *who need assistance* must be able to reach a person by telephone *during published service hours*. The statement drops both qualifications and replaces the second with "at any time", which the passage does not support. So it is False. Two qualifiers removed is a compact demonstration of why small words matter: a statement can be built entirely from the passage's own vocabulary and still assert something the passage does not.

**Question 10 (moderate)** — Statement: "A team that withdraws a channel without recording an impact assessment has breached the requirements." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The passage requires that where a team withdraws a channel it must assess the impact and record the assessment. Withdrawing without recording fails that directly, so the statement follows and is True. Contrast with Question 2 in Passage A, where the passage supplied a route for handling a failure and the breach lay in not taking it. Here the requirement is unconditional. Distinguishing a rule with an escape route from one without is among the most useful habits this section builds, and it transfers directly to reading real standards, where teams routinely assume an escape route exists because one usually does.

**Question 11 (harder)** — Statement: "A digital-only design for a general-population service breaches the policy." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The hard prohibition is specific to services used predominantly by people at risk of digital exclusion, and a general-population service is not described by that condition. But the passage also says teams *should* offer a choice of channel *wherever practicable*, which is a softer expectation whose breach depends on whether offering a choice was practicable — something you are not told. Because the passage neither clearly permits nor clearly prohibits, the answer is Cannot Say. The item rewards holding two provisions in mind and noticing that the narrow scope of the hard rule does not discharge the soft one.

**Question 12 (harder) — Main point**

Which best captures the main point of Passage C?

- A) Digital services are unsuitable for many users.
- B) Users should be able to reach services through a channel that works for them, with digital-only ruled out where exclusion is likely, withdrawals assessed and recorded, and human help available during service hours.
- C) Teams must record an impact assessment when withdrawing a channel.
- D) Users must be able to speak to a person.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The main point must span the passage while adding nothing. Option A overstates a targeted restriction into a general judgement. Options C and D are each accurate about one clause and silent on the rest, making them details — and D also drops both qualifications. Option B captures all four elements and the logic joining them: channel choice is the principle, digital exclusion is the boundary case, impact assessment protects people when things change, and human contact is the backstop. Main-point items reward the option broad enough to cover everything and disciplined enough to invent nothing, and three of these four are true details promoted beyond their scope.

### Preparation tips

- **Point at the words.** Before marking True, identify the phrase that makes it true. Experience makes this harder, not easier.
- **Trust Cannot Say.** If you are supplying a link, that is the diagnostic.
- **Circle every qualifier.** All, some, most, may, must, should, encouraged, unless, wherever practicable, predominantly, who need assistance, consider whether.
- **Look for the missing fact.** Some items are Cannot Say because a commencement date or threshold was never given.
- **Separate rules with escape routes from rules without them.** The first gives False when you assume a breach; the second gives True.
- **Treat declared silence as silence.** A review that did not examine something has told you nothing about it.
- **Distinguish "consider whether X" from "recommend X".** Review prose runs on this.
- **Practise on real documents.** Take a page of your own service standard and write three statements — one True, one False, one Cannot Say.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Answering from professional knowledge.** The characteristic error at your level and beyond.
- **Avoiding Cannot Say.** It converts good reading into wrong answers.
- **Reading "encouraged" as "required".** Policy prose puts soft aspirations next to hard rules.
- **Dropping a qualifier.** "Who need assistance" and "during published hours" are doing real work.
- **Treating association as causation.** A passage naming confounds is warning you off.
- **Inferring from what a review did not examine.** Declared silence is not evidence.
- **Assuming an escape route exists.** Some requirements are unconditional.
- **Treating a compliance route as full compliance.** Publishing a statement discharges one obligation, not necessarily all of them.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment presents realistic workplace scenarios and asks what you would do. There is no passage to parse and no arithmetic. You get a dilemma of the kind your role produces, four or five candidate responses, and a question asking which is most effective and often which is least.

This section measures whether your judgement matches what the profession considers good practice at your level, and level is the whole calibration. Your role description places you as a confident, competent designer who is trusted to make good decisions, recognises when to ask for further guidance, contributes to design concepts, plans and runs design sessions, engages the right people, and gives and receives constructive feedback. The strongest answers therefore involve exercising real professional judgement, holding a position under pressure when the evidence warrants it, and knowing which decisions genuinely belong to someone else.

That last point distinguishes your level from a junior one. A junior designer escalates a policy question. You are expected to have the conversation, understand the constraint, and escalate only what genuinely needs authority you do not hold — decisions that commit the organisation, accessibility blockers close to launch, and anything where your own judgement is compromised by being too close to it.

Four principles carry most scenarios.

Users come first, and specifically the users struggling most. When an option protects someone who cannot complete a journey, it almost always outranks one protecting a schedule, a budget or somebody's comfort.

Protect the integrity of the evidence and of the design rationale. You are the person expected to say what the evidence supports, including when it is inconvenient. Options that inflate, soften or mislabel findings are close to automatically wrong, however reasonable the motive.

Address problems directly, early, with the person involved. Private, specific, non-accusatory conversation beats public correction, silence, or routing around someone.

Fix the instance, then the system. At your level, spotting the systemic version of a recurring problem — and doing something about it — is a marker of the seniority you are working towards.

One trap: options that are pleasant, and options that are decisive, are both attractive for reasons unrelated to effectiveness.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The judgement dimensions map onto your named skills:

- **Explaining and defending design decisions** maps to **Design communication**, where you clearly explain problems, ideas and decisions using appropriate tools and methods.
- **Protecting inclusion** maps to **Designing for everyone**, where you ensure a design meets appropriate standards and deliver services meeting the needs of all users.
- **Running sessions and engaging the right people** maps to **Designing together**, where you effectively plan and run a design session with your team, users or stakeholders, and identify and engage the right people throughout.
- **Giving and receiving feedback** maps to **Designing together**, where constructive design feedback is named in both directions.
- **Using evidence honestly** maps to **Evidence-based design**, where you analyse and clearly explain evidence and work with researchers and analysts.
- **Risk, opportunity and alignment** map to **Designing strategically**, where you identify risks and opportunities and align to organisational objectives.
- **Supporting colleagues** maps to **Leading design**, where you lead and coordinate design work with support, communicate the value of user-centred design, and support other designers.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — A design session that is producing one voice**

You are running a design session. Twenty minutes in, two senior stakeholders are dominating and the four frontline caseworkers you invited have not spoken. What is the most effective response?

- A) Let it run; the stakeholders know the service well.
- B) Change the structure — ask everyone to write for two minutes, then go round the table, or split into pairs — so the quieter participants contribute without anyone being told to stop talking.
- C) Ask the stakeholders directly to let others speak.
- D) Follow up with the caseworkers separately afterwards.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** The session is producing one perspective when you convened it to produce several, and structural solutions work better than confrontational ones. Writing before speaking gives quieter people time to form a thought and gives you a legitimate reason to change who is talking, without anybody being corrected. Splitting into pairs achieves the same thing faster in a larger room. Option B is squarely within your role, which names planning and running design sessions and engaging the right people throughout the process — adapting the format when it is not working is the skill, not a workaround. Option A is least effective because it wastes the caseworkers' time and produces a design based on how senior people imagine the service works. Option C might succeed and creates unnecessary friction when a structural fix is available. Option D is a useful backup and does not rescue this session.

**Question 2 (easy) — Feedback you disagree with**

In a design critique a senior designer says your service blueprint is "confusing and doesn't show the offline journey". You believe it does. What is the most effective response?

- A) Point out where the offline journey appears.
- B) Ask them to show you where they got lost, on the reasoning that if an experienced designer cannot find it then the blueprint is not communicating it — then fix that.
- C) Accept the criticism and redo it.
- D) Ask for written feedback to consider later.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Both things can be true: the offline journey is on the blueprint, and the blueprint does not communicate it. Option B recognises that and converts feedback into information, which is more useful than establishing who was right and makes senior colleagues willing to review your work again. Option A is least effective because winning the argument leaves the artefact as confusing as it was and teaches the room that giving you feedback is effortful. Option C accepts the verdict without extracting the detail, so you may redo it and reproduce the problem. Option D defers a two-minute conversation. Your role names giving and receiving constructive design feedback, and the receiving half is where most designers have more room to improve.

**Question 3 (easy) — A live problem in something you designed**

Caseworkers report that a form section you designed is producing cases they cannot process, and they have been handling them manually. What is the most effective response?

- A) Explain that the section was research-based and ask them to raise a ticket.
- B) Thank them, ask for specific examples of what users are entering and how many cases are affected, diagnose whether the design invites the misunderstanding, and propose a fix quickly.
- C) Wait for the next research round.
- D) Ask for the section to be reverted.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Caseworkers seeing the same wrong answer repeatedly are supplying high-quality evidence for free, and the first move is to treat it that way. Option B gathers the specifics, because the actual wrong entries usually reveal the misunderstanding immediately, and asks about scale, because six affected cases and six hundred warrant different responses. Option A is least effective: defending a design against evidence that it is not working costs you the design and the caseworkers' willingness to tell you anything next time, which is a far larger loss. Option C wastes weeks when the problem is visible now. Option D reverts before understanding and may reintroduce whatever the section was solving. Being wrong about a design is normal; not noticing, or arguing with the people who noticed, is not.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Scope creep in a design session**

Halfway through a session on the application journey, a stakeholder asks you to also cover a proposed new feature "since everyone's here". What is the most effective response?

- A) Cover it; it is efficient use of the time.
- B) Explain that adding an unrelated topic risks both the session's original purpose and the quality of thinking on the new one, and propose either a short separate session or adding it to the next one with proper preparation.
- C) Refuse, since the agenda is agreed.
- D) Cover it briefly at the end if there is time.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** The stakeholder's instinct is reasonable and their underlying need is legitimate, so the answer is redirection rather than refusal. Adding a topic mid-session has two costs that are easy to underestimate: session time is finite, so something gets squeezed, and a topic raised without preparation gets thin, unstructured attention. Option B names both and offers two routes forward, which is what makes it consensus-building. Option A quietly degrades the session you convened. Option C is defensible and relationally poor, treating an agenda as a wall rather than a reason. Option D is least effective — an unprepared topic discussed in the last ten minutes by a tired room produces conclusions too weak to use and just solid enough to be quoted later as "we discussed that".

**Question 5 (moderate) — Pressure to soften a design position**

You have mapped a journey showing that a service which appears to take thirty minutes actually takes six weeks. A stakeholder asks you to remove the waiting periods from the map. What should you do?

- A) Remove them.
- B) Explain that the waits are the largest part of the user's experience and their removal would make the map describe something users would not recognise — then offer a visual treatment, such as compressing or greying them, so the design steps stay legible.
- C) Refuse and send it unchanged.
- D) Produce two versions.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** The stakeholder has a legitimate concern — they want the design work legible, not lost in grey — and the wrong solution. Option B holds the substance while addressing the concern, and offering a visual treatment turns a disagreement into a design problem, which is both more likely to succeed and more pleasant. It also makes the point in one sentence: a map omitting six weeks of waiting is not a map of the user's experience. Option A means nobody designs for the waiting, which is precisely where users give up, telephone, or assume they have been forgotten. Option C protects the map and discards the relationship. Option D is least effective: two versions of the truth means the convenient one gets used, and you have conceded that the inconvenient one is optional.

**Question 6 (moderate) — A requirement you doubt**

A policy colleague says the service must collect three years of address history because the policy requires it. Research shows the question causes 18% abandonment. What is most effective?

- A) Design the question as well as possible and accept it.
- B) Share the abandonment evidence, ask what the requirement is for and where it is written, and explore whether the underlying need could be met another way — while being ready to accept the constraint if it turns out to be fixed.
- C) Challenge the requirement and ask for it to be removed.
- D) Escalate to your senior designer without speaking to the policy colleague.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** "The policy requires it" is sometimes exactly true, sometimes a compressed account of a need that could be met differently, and sometimes an inherited practice nobody has revisited. Option B asks the productive question — not "must we?" but "what is this for?" — because knowing that the need is identity verification opens alternatives that arguing about the question never will, and bringing the abandonment evidence makes the cost concrete. The closing clause matters: some constraints are genuinely statutory, and a designer who cannot accept one is not believed about the ones that are negotiable. Option C is least effective, being a head-on challenge without understanding the basis, which will not succeed and makes the next conversation harder. Option A gives up before asking. Option D bypasses a colleague who has had no chance to explain.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Findings the team does not want**

Research shows a feature the team has built over two months causes problems for nine of twelve participants. You have five minutes at the show and tell. What is the most effective approach?

- A) Lead with the positives and mention the difficulties briefly.
- B) Present what happened clearly and without blame — what participants tried, where they got stuck, what they said — and frame the next step as understanding why, inviting the team into the diagnosis.
- C) Present the failure rate starkly so the team takes it seriously.
- D) Ask your senior designer to present it.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Difficult findings are the most valuable findings, and delivery determines whether they get acted on or defended against. Option B keeps the focus on observed behaviour rather than on the quality of anyone's work, which lets the team engage with the problem instead of their feelings about it, and ending on a question brings the people who built it into the analysis, where they will often see causes you cannot. Option A is least effective: softening a nine-in-twelve failure means two months of effort continues down the same path, which serves nobody, least of all the people who built it. Option C is accurate and adversarial, and a defensive room learns nothing. Option D is understandable and deprives you of the experience and the standing.

**Question 8 (harder) — An accessibility blocker close to launch**

Two weeks before launch you find that a key step cannot be completed with a screen reader. Fixing it delays launch. What is most effective?

- A) Launch and add it to the backlog.
- B) Raise it immediately with the delivery manager and service owner, describe precisely what cannot be done and by whom, ask for an explicit decision, and offer any interim route that would let affected users complete the journey another way.
- C) Attempt the fix yourself in the two weeks.
- D) Launch and publish an accessibility statement noting the gap.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A step that cannot be completed with a screen reader means some people cannot use the service at all, and weighing a delay against an exclusion is a decision for people with authority to make it. Raising it, precisely and now, is squarely yours. Option B does everything in your remit: surfaces the problem while there is time, describes the impact specifically rather than vaguely, asks for an explicit decision rather than letting it drift, and looks for an interim route so the choice is not simply launch-or-delay. Option A is least effective, because "add it to the backlog" is where accessibility problems go to remain unfixed while real people cannot use a public service. Option D looks responsible and frequently is not — an accessibility statement is for gaps you are genuinely closing, not a mechanism for launching with a known blocker. Option C risks a fortnight and leaves nobody with authority aware there is a problem.

**Question 9 (harder) — A problem that belongs to someone else**

Research shows your users routinely fail at a step owned by another department, before reaching your service. Your team says it is out of scope. What is most effective?

- A) Accept it; you cannot fix another department's service.
- B) Evidence the problem and its effect on your users, find a route to raise it with the other department through someone with standing to do so, and separately design what your own service can do to reduce the harm — such as explaining the earlier step, or handling people who arrive without having completed it.
- C) Contact the other department directly yourself.
- D) Document it in the findings and move on.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** This is the characteristic service design situation: the user's journey does not respect organisational boundaries even though budgets do. Option B does three things — evidences the problem, seeks a legitimate route through someone with standing, and asks what your own service can do meanwhile, which is usually more than a team assumes. That last element is what turns an out-of-scope complaint into design work. Option A is least effective, because "out of scope" describes a budget rather than a person's experience, and a user who fails before reaching you has still failed to get the thing government said they could have. Option C may be reasonable depending on your organisation, and going unaccompanied risks cutting across conversations and relationships you cannot see. Option D records without addressing.

**Question 10 (harder) — Consensus that would harm users**

The team has converged on a solution everyone likes, which your evidence indicates will not work for the users who struggle most. Reopening it means reversing a decision people are relieved to have made. What is most effective?

- A) Let it go; consensus has value.
- B) Raise it once, clearly and specifically, showing what the evidence indicates will happen to those users and what a mitigation might look like — then if the team proceeds, ensure the risk is recorded and propose measuring the outcome after release.
- C) Escalate to the service owner over the team's head.
- D) Keep raising it until the team changes course.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** You have a professional obligation to say the thing, and you also have to work with these people next week. Option B threads that: raise it once and properly, with evidence and a constructive mitigation rather than a veto; then, if the decision stands, do the two things that protect users and your position — get the risk documented so the decision is knowing rather than accidental, and set up a measurement so reality gets a vote later. Option A is least effective because consensus that harms the users who struggle most is not a value, and silence makes you complicit in a decision you had the evidence to inform. Option C escalates before using the direct route and costs you the team's trust. Option D substitutes persistence for persuasion, and a designer who relitigates every decision stops being heard on any of them.

**Question 11 (harder) — A flaw in your own work**

You realise that research you relied on for a design decision recruited only users who had previously completed the service. The decision is now in build. What should you do?

- A) Note it as a limitation next time.
- B) Tell the team and decision-makers now, scope precisely which conclusions are affected and which still stand, and propose targeted follow-up with users who did not complete.
- C) Quietly commission follow-up and correct the record if findings differ.
- D) Say nothing; the decision was reasonable anyway.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** A sample of successful completers cannot see the failures the design was meant to address, and something is being built on it. Option B does the three things that matter: tells the people who acted on the finding, scopes the damage precisely rather than minimising or catastrophising, and proposes the fix. The scoping is important — conclusions about how successful users navigate may remain sound, and saying so is accuracy rather than defensiveness. Option A defers past usefulness. Option C conceals a known flaw while the build proceeds. Option D is least effective: reasoning that the decision was probably fine substitutes your guess for the evidence you were relying on, and if it emerges later, the concealment will be the thing people remember rather than the original error.

**Question 12 (harder) — Design work that never lands**

Across three sprints, design work has consistently arrived after build decisions were taken. The team is appreciative and nothing changes. What is most effective?

- A) Accept it; the pace is the product manager's call.
- B) Bring specific examples to the team's retrospective showing where earlier design input would have changed a decision, propose a concrete change such as a design checkpoint before stories are committed, and secure your senior designer's or the delivery manager's support for it.
- C) Work faster so design arrives sooner.
- D) Stop producing design work until the team commits to using it.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** This is a process problem and needs a forum, evidence and a proposal. Option B supplies all three, and the final clause is what distinguishes it from a junior version of the same answer: securing support from someone with influence over how the team plans is what turns a good suggestion into a change. Specific examples are essential, because "design arrives too late" is a complaint while "these three decisions would have gone differently" is a case. Option A gives up on something your role can influence — applying iterative principles and agile methodologies is a named skill, and shaping how design fits the cycle is part of it. Option C treats a sequencing problem as a speed problem and will exhaust you. Option D is least effective, because withholding design work punishes users and the team rather than the process, and it costs the relationships you need.

### Preparation tips

- **Answer as a trusted practitioner.** Exercise judgement, hold positions the evidence supports, escalate only what needs authority you lack.
- **Start from the struggling user.** When an option protects someone who cannot complete the journey, assume it is strongest until you find a reason otherwise.
- **Prefer structural fixes to confrontational ones.** Changing a session's format beats telling someone to stop talking.
- **Redirect rather than refuse.** "Not that, but here is what would work" outperforms "no" in nearly every stakeholder scenario.
- **Raise it once, properly.** One well-evidenced challenge plus a documented risk beats repeated objection.
- **Bring mitigation with bad news.** A finding plus a route forward is actionable; a finding alone invites softening.
- **Look for the option that fixes the instance and the system.** At your level that is the signature of the best answer.
- **Discuss real cases with colleagues.** Your organisation's actual norms are what this section measures.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Defending a design against evidence.** It costs the design and the goodwill of whoever told you.
- **Accepting "out of scope" as an answer about a person's experience.** Budgets have boundaries; journeys do not.
- **Making two versions of the truth.** The convenient one gets used.
- **Treating an accessibility statement as a way to launch with a blocker.** It is for gaps you are genuinely closing.
- **Winning the argument about feedback.** If an experienced designer got lost, the artefact is unclear regardless.
- **Softening difficult findings.** It lets wasted effort continue and helps nobody.
- **Treating consensus as a terminal value.** A comfortable decision that harms excluded users is not a good outcome.
- **Escalating over a colleague's head as a first move.** Except where users are at risk, use the direct route first.

## Conclusion

You have reached the end, and it is worth pausing over what that involved: forty-eight questions across four assessment types, with the reasoning worked through on each. That is a substantial piece of professional preparation, and rather more than most people do.

Look back at the ground. In the cognitive section you audited a page against a standard and caught the requirement that was absent rather than wrong, separated preparation from interface as explanations for a 28 point gap, followed a channel closure through to its second-order costs, read a 40% return rate as a 40% failure-first-time rate, and scoped a prototype fault precisely enough to say which findings survived it. In the numeric section you measured drop-off against the right base, converted a percentage into people so that 9% became 21,600, distinguished percentage points from relative change, recognised a mean sitting in the gap between two clusters, and declined to endorse a comparison too small to detect what it was built to find. In the verbal section you read "encouraged" as encouragement, spotted a missing commencement date, separated a rule with an escape route from one without, and refused to treat a compliance route as full compliance. And in the situational judgement section you fixed a lopsided design session structurally rather than confrontationally, held the waiting periods on a map under pressure, raised an accessibility blocker with a decision request rather than a complaint, and told a team that research you had relied on could not see the failures it was meant to find.

Notice what unites almost all of it. Every section, in its own idiom, was testing whether you know the limits of what you have and will say so — the limits of a sample, of a metric, of a policy requirement, of a design decision made on comfortable evidence. That is not an accident of how the guide is written. It is what makes a designer trusted to make good decisions, which is the phrase your own role description uses. Teams can generate opinions without you and read dashboards without you. What you bring is a design rationale that holds up when someone pulls at it.

A word about your level. Being trusted is a real threshold. It means nobody proofreads your reasoning before it reaches a decision, and the standard you hold yourself to becomes the standard the service gets. It also means the errors that matter most are no longer the ones you would notice — they are the ones you are confident about. That is the honest case for practising this kind of reasoning deliberately rather than trusting experience: experience makes you fluent, and fluency makes overreach feel like insight.

If you want to build on this, a few things repay effort more than rereading. Walk a real service end to end, including the offline parts, and time it honestly. Read the letters your service sends, because letters are frequently the part of a journey nobody designed and the part that generates most of the phone calls. Ask an analyst how a metric you rely on is defined rather than calculated; the answers are often surprising. Take a policy requirement you have accepted for months and ask what it is actually for. And the next time a number in a slide sounds decisive, convert it into people before you respond to it.

Finally, for the day itself. An assessment samples your reasoning on one morning under artificial conditions. It is not a verdict on your judgement or your career. Read the stem twice, find the denominator, trust Cannot Say, add up the waiting as well as the doing, and remember that the strongest answer is usually the one that is honest about uncertainty rather than the one that sounds most decisive.

Good luck. Go carefully, ask what things are for, and keep zooming out.
