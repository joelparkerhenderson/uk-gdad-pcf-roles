# Business Architect (Trainee Business Architect) - Psychometric Assessment Resources

## Introduction

Welcome! This guide is written especially for you as a trainee business architect. Whether you are brand new to the role or a few months in, this document will help you understand, practise, and feel confident about the psychometric assessments that are commonly used for roles like yours in the UK Government Digital and Data profession.

First, let's answer the obvious question: what is a psychometric assessment? Put simply, it is a structured, standardised way of measuring how you think, reason, and make decisions at work. Instead of asking about your qualifications or experience, these assessments give you realistic tasks - a table of numbers to interpret, a passage of policy text to read, a tricky workplace situation to judge - and look at how you handle them. They are widely used in recruitment, in development programmes, and in professional progression conversations, because they give a fair, consistent picture of your working style and abilities.

Why do they matter for you specifically? As a trainee business architect, your day-to-day work involves developing your knowledge of business architecture frameworks, supporting senior architects to produce clear outputs, and helping during workshops and meetings with stakeholders. That means employers care about how well you can spot patterns and errors, work with figures and percentages, read dense documents carefully, and use good judgement with colleagues and stakeholders. The four assessments in this guide map directly onto those abilities.

3. **Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment** - careful reading of policies, standards, emails, and reports, with True / False / Cannot Say questions.

Please do not just read the answers - try each question honestly first, then compare your reasoning with the explanation. That is where the real learning happens.

There is no pass or fail in this guide. Use it for practice, for self-reflection, and for preparation. Take your time, be kind to yourself when you get things wrong (that is what practice is for!), and remember: every senior business architect you support was once exactly where you are now.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the mental processes you use every day: spotting patterns, reasoning logically, checking for errors, prioritising tasks, and solving problems. Unlike a generic intelligence test, a job-specific version uses practical workplace scenarios and data that mimic your actual role. For you, that means questions built around capability maps, process models, workshop plans, stakeholder lists, and strategy documents - the artefacts a trainee business architect genuinely handles.

These assessments are usually taken online and are typically timed, often 15 to 30 minutes. Questions are objectively scored, and your results are compared against a benchmark group. Many platforms give you a few short, ungraded practice questions first so you can get used to the interface, and some adapt the difficulty of questions based on your previous answers. Employers often receive a breakdown of your speed versus your accuracy rather than a single score, so working carefully matters just as much as working quickly.

Do not worry if this sounds intimidating. Cognitive assessments reward exactly the habits you are already building as a trainee: reading carefully, checking your work, asking "does this make sense?", and thinking step by step. This section will help you practise all of those habits.

### What it measures for your role

Here is how the dimensions of a cognitive assessment map to the specific skills in your role profile:

- **Business architecture**: your awareness of frameworks, principles, and techniques is tested through pattern recognition and classification questions - for example, sorting artefacts into the right framework layer, or spotting an item that does not belong in a capability map.
- **Communicating information**: listening to stakeholder needs and interpreting information is tested through questions where you must extract the essential point from mixed or messy inputs, such as workshop notes.
- **Consultancy**: framing problems that need to be solved is tested through logical deduction questions - working out what must be true, what cannot be true, and what is still unknown.
- **Making and guiding decisions**: recommending decisions and describing your reasoning maps to prioritisation and problem-solving questions, where you must choose the best next step and understand why.
- **Methods and tools**: following advice to apply the most appropriate tools maps to sequencing and process-logic questions - putting steps in the right order and spotting where a process breaks.
- **Strategic thinking**: explaining the strategic context of your work maps to questions that ask you to connect a detailed task to a bigger goal, or to check whether a project design aligns with a stated strategy.

### Practice questions

Take your time with these. Try each one before reading the answer. The difficulty is labelled so you can see your progress.

**Question 1 (easy) - Pattern recognition in capability codes**

Your organisation labels business capabilities with codes. You notice this sequence in the capability model you are helping to update:

CAP-FIN-01, CAP-FIN-02, CAP-FIN-03, CAP-HRM-01, CAP-HRM-02, CAP-HRM-03, CAP-DIG-01, CAP-DIG-02, ?

What is the next code in the sequence?

- A) CAP-DIG-03
- B) CAP-FIN-04
- C) CAP-DIG-04
- D) CAP-HRM-04

**Correct answer: A) CAP-DIG-03**

**Explanation:** The pattern groups capabilities by business area (FIN for finance, HRM for human resources, DIG for digital), and each area runs through numbers 01, 02, 03 before the next area begins. The DIG group has reached 02, so the next code continues that group: CAP-DIG-03. Questions like this test whether you can spot the structure behind a labelling convention - a real skill when you maintain capability models, because using the wrong code creates confusion for everyone who reads the model later.

**Question 2 (easy) - Odd one out**

A senior business architect asks you to gather examples of business architecture outputs for a training pack. Which one of the following is NOT a typical business architecture output?

- A) A business capability map
- B) A value stream diagram
- C) An operating model description
- D) A database index tuning script

**Correct answer: D) A database index tuning script**

**Explanation:** Capability maps, value stream diagrams, and operating model descriptions are all classic business architecture artefacts - they describe what the organisation does and how it creates value. A database index tuning script is a low-level technical artefact belonging to database administration, not business architecture. This kind of classification question checks your growing awareness of business architecture frameworks, principles, and techniques - one of your named skills. As a trainee, being able to tell "business layer" artefacts from "technology layer" artefacts helps you file, reference, and discuss documents correctly.

**Question 3 (easy) - Simple logical deduction**

Your department has a rule: "Every project design document must be reviewed for strategic alignment before it goes to the design authority board." You know the following facts:

- Project Swift's design document went to the design authority board last week.
- Project Heron's design document has not yet been reviewed for strategic alignment.

Which statement must be true?

- A) Project Heron's design document has already gone to the board.
- B) Project Swift's design document was reviewed for strategic alignment.
- C) Project Heron's design will fail its review.
- D) Project Swift's design is strategically aligned.

**Correct answer: B) Project Swift's design document was reviewed for strategic alignment.**

**Explanation:** The rule says review must happen before the board. Since Swift's document reached the board, the review must have happened first - so B must be true. Be careful with the traps: A contradicts the rule (Heron cannot have gone to the board without review). C predicts the future, which the facts do not support. D is tempting, but "was reviewed for alignment" is not the same as "is aligned" - a document can be reviewed and found wanting. This distinction between what is stated and what is assumed is central to consultancy-style problem framing.

**Question 4 (moderate) - Error checking a stakeholder list**

You are supporting a senior architect by checking the stakeholder register for a workshop. The master list says the workshop needs: 2 attendees from Finance, 3 from Operations, 1 from Digital Services, and 2 from Policy. The draft invitation list reads:

1. A. Okafor - Finance
2. B. Singh - Operations
3. C. Murray - Operations
4. D. Evans - Digital Services
5. E. Nowak - Policy
6. F. Ahmed - Finance
7. G. Taylor - Policy
8. H. Li - Digital Services

What is wrong with the draft list?

- A) There is one Finance attendee too many.
- B) There is one Operations attendee missing and one Digital Services attendee too many.
- C) There is one Policy attendee missing.
- D) Nothing is wrong; the list matches the master requirements.

**Correct answer: B) There is one Operations attendee missing and one Digital Services attendee too many.**

**Explanation:** Count each group carefully. Finance: Okafor and Ahmed = 2 (correct). Operations: Singh and Murray = 2, but 3 are required (one missing). Digital Services: Evans and Li = 2, but only 1 is required (one too many). Policy: Nowak and Taylor = 2 (correct). So the list has an Operations shortfall and a Digital Services surplus - answer B. Error checking like this is genuinely part of your role: supporting workshops means making sure the right stakeholders are in the room, and a miscounted invitation list can derail a session before it starts.

**Question 5 (moderate) - Sequencing a workshop process**

A senior architect gives you these five workshop preparation steps, out of order:

1. Send the agenda and pre-reading to confirmed attendees.
2. Confirm the workshop objective with the sponsoring stakeholder.
3. Book a room with accessibility provisions and set up the video call link.
4. Draft the agenda based on the confirmed objective.
5. Collect attendee availability and confirm the date.

Which order is most logical?

- A) 2, 4, 5, 3, 1
- B) 5, 2, 4, 1, 3
- C) 2, 5, 4, 3, 1
- D) 4, 2, 5, 1, 3

**Correct answer: A) 2, 4, 5, 3, 1**

**Explanation:** Work through the dependencies. You cannot draft an agenda (4) until the objective is confirmed (2), so 2 comes before 4 - this eliminates D. You cannot send the agenda to confirmed attendees (1) until both the agenda exists (4) and the date is confirmed (5). Booking the room (3) needs a confirmed date, so 3 follows 5. Option C fails because it drafts the agenda after collecting availability but that is acceptable - the real problem with C is subtler: it works, but option A is stronger because the agenda (4) can be drafted immediately after the objective (2) without waiting for availability, and the room booking (3) rightly follows date confirmation (5), with sending (1) last once everything is fixed. Both A and C place 2 first and 1 last; A is preferred because drafting the agenda before chasing availability means the pre-reading is ready as soon as the date lands, avoiding delay. Sequencing questions reward thinking about what each step needs as an input - exactly the "methods and tools" discipline of applying the right technique at the right point in planning.

**Question 6 (moderate) - Strategic alignment logic**

Your organisation's published strategy has three priorities: (P1) improve citizen self-service, (P2) reduce duplicated back-office systems, and (P3) improve data quality for decision making. You are asked to do a first-pass check on four project proposals and flag any that do not clearly support at least one priority:

- Project Alpha: replace three overlapping case-management systems with one shared platform.
- Project Beta: redecorate the ground-floor meeting rooms.
- Project Gamma: build an online portal so citizens can update their own contact details.
- Project Delta: introduce a single data dictionary across departments.

Which project(s) should you flag?

- A) Beta only
- B) Beta and Delta
- C) Gamma only
- D) Alpha and Delta

**Correct answer: A) Beta only**

**Explanation:** Match each project to the priorities. Alpha consolidates duplicated systems - that is P2. Gamma enables citizen self-service - that is P1. Delta improves consistency and quality of data definitions - that is P3. Beta, redecorating meeting rooms, may be a perfectly reasonable facilities task, but it does not clearly support P1, P2, or P3, so it is the one to flag. Notice that flagging does not mean the project is bad - it means it needs a strategic justification or a different funding route. This is a simplified version of a real trainee task: ensuring design work from projects and programmes aligns with strategy is one of the core duties of your profession.

**Question 7 (moderate) - Aggregating messy information**

Three teams describe the same activity in their process documents:

- Team 1 calls it "Verify applicant identity".
- Team 2 calls it "ID checking".
- Team 3 calls it "Customer identity validation (step 4)".

A senior architect asks you to consolidate the three process documents into one capability view. What is the most sensible first move?

- A) Keep all three names so no team is upset.
- B) Treat them as three different capabilities because the names differ.
- C) Confirm with the teams that the three names describe the same activity, then record it once under a single agreed name with the variants noted.
- D) Pick Team 1's name because they wrote their document first.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Aggregating and disaggregating complex information from across the organisation is a core business architect duty, and the first rule is: same thing, one name. Option B creates artificial duplication - exactly what a capability view exists to remove. Option A defeats the purpose of consolidation. Option D guesses instead of verifying, and "first written" is not evidence of "best name". Option C does two important things: it checks the assumption with the people who know (the teams), and it preserves traceability by noting the variant names. As a trainee you would do this under guidance, but the reasoning pattern - verify, unify, keep the audit trail - is one to learn early.

**Question 8 (moderate) - Prioritisation under time pressure**

It is 09:15 on Tuesday. You have four tasks:

1. Print and lay out materials for a stakeholder workshop that starts at 10:00 in your building.
2. Reply to an email from a peer asking for last month's workshop notes, needed "sometime this week".
3. Finish the first draft of a capability description due to your senior architect by the end of Thursday.
4. Re-book a meeting room for next Monday's team catch-up because of a double booking.

What is the most sensible order to tackle them?

- A) 3, 1, 4, 2
- B) 1, 4, 3, 2
- C) 1, 3, 4, 2
- D) 2, 1, 3, 4

**Correct answer: C) 1, 3, 4, 2**

**Explanation:** Task 1 is urgent and immovable - the workshop starts in 45 minutes and materials must be ready, so it comes first. After that, compare the rest by deadline and effort. Task 3 has the nearest hard deadline (Thursday) and the largest effort, so it deserves the next block of focused time. Task 4 matters before Monday, but re-booking a room is quick and can slot in as a short break from drafting. Task 2 is explicitly flexible ("sometime this week") and quick, so it can safely come last. Option B puts the quick room re-booking ahead of the substantial Thursday draft, which risks nibbling away your focused time; option A misses the workshop; option D answers a low-urgency email while the clock runs down on the workshop. Prioritisation questions test the judgement behind "recommend decisions and describe the reasoning behind them" - always be ready to say why your order is right.

**Question 9 (moderate) - Spotting an inconsistency in a capability model**

You are proofreading a capability model summary before it goes to stakeholders. It contains these four statements:

1. "The organisation has 12 top-level capabilities."
2. "Each top-level capability contains exactly 3 sub-capabilities."
3. "The model contains 38 sub-capabilities in total."
4. "Every sub-capability belongs to exactly one top-level capability."

Which pair of statements cannot both be true?

- A) Statements 1 and 2
- B) Statements 2 and 3
- C) Statements 1 and 4
- D) Statements 3 and 4

**Correct answer: B) Statements 2 and 3**

**Explanation:** If there are 12 top-level capabilities (statement 1) and each contains exactly 3 sub-capabilities (statement 2), and every sub-capability belongs to exactly one parent (statement 4), then the total must be 12 x 3 = 36 sub-capabilities. Statement 3 claims 38, which contradicts that total. Strictly, the contradiction involves statements 1, 2, 3, and 4 together, but among the offered pairs, 2 and 3 is the clash the question targets: "exactly 3 each" cannot produce a total of 38 from 12 parents. Checking internal consistency before documents reach stakeholders is a classic trainee support task - a stakeholder who spots the arithmetic error will doubt the rest of the model too.

**Question 10 (hard) - Multi-step logical deduction**

Four programmes (P, Q, R, S) each align to exactly one strategic priority: citizen services, data quality, or system consolidation. You know:

- Exactly two programmes align to citizen services.
- P and Q align to different priorities.
- R aligns to data quality.
- S aligns to citizen services.

Which of the following must be true?

- A) P aligns to citizen services.
- B) Q aligns to system consolidation.
- C) Exactly one of P and Q aligns to citizen services.
- D) No programme aligns to system consolidation.

**Correct answer: C) Exactly one of P and Q aligns to citizen services.**

**Explanation:** Work it through step by step. R takes data quality and S takes citizen services. Exactly two programmes align to citizen services, and S is one of them - so exactly one more programme from {P, Q} must be citizen services. P and Q align to different priorities, which is consistent with exactly one of them taking citizen services (the other takes data quality or system consolidation). So C must be true. A is only possibly true (it could be Q instead). B is only possibly true (the non-citizen-services one of P and Q could take data quality). D is possibly false for the same reason. The skill here is separating "must be true" from "could be true" - the same discipline you need when you aggregate claims from different parts of the organisation and have to state only what the evidence supports.

**Question 11 (hard) - Process fault diagnosis**

Your team's document approval workflow is: Draft -> Peer review -> Senior architect review -> Publish to the repository. A stakeholder reports that a published operating model description contains a section that the senior architect never saw. Assuming everyone followed the workflow as they understood it, which explanation is most plausible?

- A) The peer reviewer deleted the section before senior review.
- B) The section was added after senior architect review but before publication.
- C) The senior architect reviewed the section but forgot.
- D) The document was never peer reviewed.

**Correct answer: B) The section was added after senior architect review but before publication.**

**Explanation:** Diagnose by matching the symptom to the workflow. The symptom is: published content the senior architect never saw. Option A would result in the section being absent from the published version, not present - it explains the opposite symptom. Option C contradicts the premise that the architect never saw it (and "forgot" is an assumption about memory, not a workflow fault). Option D would not by itself explain the senior architect missing content - senior review happens regardless of whether peer review occurred. Option B fits exactly: an edit inserted between the final review and publication is the only step where unseen content can enter a published document. The practical lesson for your role: version control between sign-off and publication matters, and workflows need a "no edits after final review" rule. Fault-diagnosis questions reward tracing each option's consequences and comparing them with the observed symptom.

**Question 12 (hard) - Connecting detail to strategy**

Your organisation's strategy says: "We will reduce the cost of back-office operations so that more funding reaches frontline services." You are helping map current projects to this strategy. A project proposes to automate invoice processing, which will cost 200,000 pounds to build and is forecast to save 80,000 pounds per year in staff time that can be redeployed to frontline casework. A stakeholder argues the project does not support the strategy "because it costs money rather than saving it."

Which is the strongest response to evaluate the stakeholder's claim?

- A) The stakeholder is right: any project with an upfront cost contradicts a cost-reduction strategy.
- B) The stakeholder is wrong: the project supports the strategy because recurring savings exceed the one-off cost within three years, and the savings are redeployed to the frontline, which is the strategy's stated purpose.
- C) The stakeholder is right: 80,000 pounds is less than 200,000 pounds.
- D) The stakeholder is wrong: all automation projects always support all cost-reduction strategies.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The strategy has two parts: reduce back-office cost, and shift funding to the frontline. The project's recurring saving of 80,000 pounds per year passes the one-off cost of 200,000 pounds during the third year (80,000 x 3 = 240,000), and the freed staff time goes to frontline casework - the exact outcome the strategy names. Option A confuses one-off investment with ongoing cost; strategies to reduce operating cost routinely require upfront spending. Option C compares a yearly figure with a one-off figure as if they were the same kind of number - a category error. Option D is right by accident but wrong in reasoning: "always" claims are almost never defensible, and the assessment is testing your reasoning, not just your conclusion. Explaining the strategic context of a piece of work, clearly and fairly, is a named expectation of your strategic thinking skill.

**Question 13 (hard) - Interpreting workshop notes**

During a workshop you took these raw notes: "Ops say handoffs to Finance slow - approx 5 days. Finance say they wait on missing PO numbers from Ops. Digital say new form could capture PO at source. Policy: no objection. Sponsor wants fix before year end."

A senior architect asks: "What is the root cause suggested by the notes, and what is the proposed remedy?" Which summary is most accurate?

- A) Root cause: Finance is slow. Remedy: give Finance more staff before year end.
- B) Root cause: handoffs from Operations arrive without PO numbers, forcing Finance to wait. Remedy: a new digital form capturing the PO number at source, to be delivered before year end.
- C) Root cause: Policy objected to the process. Remedy: escalate to the sponsor.
- D) Root cause: the digital form is broken. Remedy: Operations should stop sending handoffs.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Follow the chain in the notes: the delay appears at the Ops-to-Finance handoff; Finance attributes their waiting to missing PO numbers from Ops; Digital proposes capturing the PO number at source via a new form; Policy raises no objection; the sponsor sets the deadline. Option B reproduces that chain faithfully. Option A blames Finance, but the notes say Finance is waiting, not slow - a subtle but crucial difference. Option C inverts the Policy note ("no objection" is the opposite of an objection). Option D invents a broken form; the form does not exist yet. Turning messy, abbreviated notes into an accurate cause-and-remedy summary is one of the most valuable services a trainee provides in workshops - and misreading who is waiting on whom can sour relationships between teams.

### Preparation tips

- **Practise with your own artefacts.** Spend ten minutes with a capability map or process diagram from your team and ask yourself: what patterns does it use? What would look wrong? This builds the same muscles the assessment tests.
- **Slow down on "must be true" questions.** Trainee-level candidates most often lose marks by choosing something that is merely plausible. Before answering, ask: "Could I invent a situation where this is false?" If yes, it is not a "must".
- **Count things twice.** For error-checking questions, tally categories on paper or on screen. A ten-second recount catches most slips.
- **Do a timed practice run.** Set a timer for 20 minutes and attempt eight of the questions above in one sitting. The goal is calm familiarity with time pressure, not speed for its own sake.
- **Sleep and settle.** Cognitive scores genuinely improve with rest. Take the assessment at a time of day when you are alert, in a quiet space, with water to hand.
- **Use the practice questions platforms offer.** Most assessment platforms give ungraded warm-up questions. Always do them - they cost nothing and remove interface surprises.

### Common pitfalls

- **Treating it like a school test.** Job-specific cognitive assessments use workplace artefacts, not abstract puzzles. If a question mentions a capability model or a stakeholder list, use your working knowledge of what those are for - the context is a clue, not decoration.
- **Rushing the reading.** Many wrong answers come from misreading the question stem ("which cannot be true" versus "which must be true"). Read the final question sentence twice.
- **Getting stuck on one hard question.** These tests often score speed and accuracy separately. If a question is consuming your time, flag it, move on, and return if time allows.
- **Ignoring speed-versus-accuracy trade-offs.** Because employers often see accuracy and speed reported separately, wild guessing to finish fast can hurt you. Aim for steady, checked progress.
- **Assuming adaptivity means failure.** On adaptive platforms, harder questions often mean you are doing well. Do not panic when difficulty rises.


## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment evaluates how well you can analyse, interpret, and make sensible decisions using numerical data. The good news for you: it is business maths, not abstract algebra. Questions are built from graphs, tables, budgets, and schedules - the kinds of real-world data a trainee business architect meets when supporting capability assessments, workshop planning, and strategic reporting. You will work with percentages, ratios, averages, simple projections, and cost comparisons.

Most numeric reasoning assessments are calculator friendly - an onscreen calculator is usually allowed or provided - because employers care about your interpretation of numbers more than your mental arithmetic. That said, the tests are typically strictly timed, so quick and accurate data extraction matters: finding the right row in a table, noticing the units, and choosing the right operation.

As a trainee, you might wonder why numbers matter in a role that is mostly about frameworks, workshops, and communication. Here is why: business architecture runs on evidence. When you help a senior architect show that three systems duplicate each other, or that a process consumes 40 per cent more staff time in one region than another, the argument stands or falls on the numbers behind it. Being comfortable with basic business maths makes you a trustworthy pair of hands.

### What it measures for your role

- **Business architecture**: capability assessments often use maturity scores, headcounts mapped to capabilities, and system counts. The assessment tests whether you can read and compare these figures accurately.
- **Communicating information**: stakeholders send you data in tables, dashboards, and emails. Questions test whether you can extract the correct figure and interpret what it means before you pass it on.
- **Consultancy**: recommendations need evidence. Questions test cost comparisons and simple benefit calculations - the arithmetic behind "we recommend option B".
- **Making and guiding decisions**: several question types present two or three options with costs and benefits, and ask which the numbers support - practice for describing the reasoning behind a recommendation.
- **Methods and tools**: reading outputs from planning and analysis tools (utilisation figures, schedule data) is tested through table and chart interpretation.
- **Strategic thinking**: percentage-of-total and trend questions test whether you can connect detailed figures to the big picture, such as what share of spend supports each strategic priority.

### Practice questions

Have a calculator and some rough paper ready. The explanations show every step of the arithmetic, so you can see exactly where any slip happened.

**Question 1 (easy) - Workshop attendance percentage**

You helped organise a stakeholder workshop. 24 people were invited and 18 attended. What was the attendance rate?

- A) 66%
- B) 70%
- C) 75%
- D) 80%

**Correct answer: C) 75%**

**Explanation:** Attendance rate = attendees divided by invitees, times 100. That is 18 / 24 = 0.75, and 0.75 x 100 = 75%. A quick sense-check: 18 out of 24 is the same as 3 out of 4 (divide both by 6), and three quarters is 75%. Reporting attendance rates is a common trainee task after workshops, and simplifying fractions is often faster than long division.

**Question 2 (easy) - Simple budget remainder**

Your team has a training budget of 3,000 pounds for the year. So far you have spent 450 pounds on a business architecture foundation course and 275 pounds on a workshop facilitation course. How much budget remains?

- A) 2,275 pounds
- B) 2,375 pounds
- C) 2,175 pounds
- D) 2,425 pounds

**Correct answer: A) 2,275 pounds**

**Explanation:** Total spent = 450 + 275 = 725 pounds. Remaining = 3,000 - 725 = 2,275 pounds. The most common slip here is adding 450 + 275 as 700 or 735 - always line up the tens and units. Tracking a small budget, such as team training spend, is a realistic administrative support task at your level, and it is good practice for the larger programme budgets you will meet later in your career.

**Question 3 (easy) - Reading a table accurately**

The table below shows how many business processes each directorate documented this quarter:

| Directorate | Processes documented | Processes still to document |
|---|---|---|
| Finance | 34 | 6 |
| Operations | 28 | 22 |
| Digital | 41 | 9 |
| Policy | 17 | 13 |

Which directorate has documented the highest proportion of its processes?

- A) Finance
- B) Operations
- C) Digital
- D) Policy

**Correct answer: A) Finance**

**Explanation:** The proportion documented = documented / (documented + still to document). Finance: 34 / 40 = 85%. Operations: 28 / 50 = 56%. Digital: 41 / 50 = 82%. Policy: 17 / 30 = 56.7% (rounded). Finance's 85% is highest. The trap is choosing Digital because 41 is the biggest raw number - but the question asks for proportion, not count. Distinguishing "most" from "highest proportion" is one of the most frequently tested skills in numeric reasoning, and it matters in real reporting too: saying "Digital documented the most processes" and "Finance is furthest through its documentation" are both true, and stakeholders need to know which claim you are making.

**Question 4 (moderate) - Percentage change in process time**

A senior architect is showing how a redesigned approvals process has improved. Before the redesign, an approval took an average of 12 working days. After the redesign, it takes an average of 9 working days. What is the percentage reduction in approval time?

- A) 33%
- B) 25%
- C) 3%
- D) 20%

**Correct answer: B) 25%**

**Explanation:** Percentage reduction = (old - new) / old x 100. That is (12 - 9) / 12 x 100 = 3 / 12 x 100 = 25%. The classic trap is dividing by the new value instead of the old one: 3 / 9 = 33%, which is answer A. Remember: percentage change is always measured against the starting point. In business change reporting this distinction is important and occasionally political - a 25% reduction and a 33% reduction are very different claims, and your senior architect will thank you for getting it right.

**Question 5 (moderate) - Capability maturity averages**

Your organisation scores capability maturity from 1 (initial) to 5 (optimised). A capability assessment gives these scores:

| Capability | Maturity score |
|---|---|
| Customer case management | 3 |
| Payments processing | 4 |
| Identity verification | 2 |
| Reporting and analytics | 2 |
| Correspondence handling | 4 |

What is the mean maturity score, and how many capabilities score below that mean?

- A) Mean 3.0; two capabilities below
- B) Mean 3.0; three capabilities below
- C) Mean 2.5; two capabilities below
- D) Mean 3.2; three capabilities below

**Correct answer: A) Mean 3.0; two capabilities below**

**Explanation:** Sum the scores: 3 + 4 + 2 + 2 + 4 = 15. Divide by the number of capabilities: 15 / 5 = 3.0. Now count scores strictly below 3.0: Identity verification (2) and Reporting and analytics (2) - that is two capabilities. Customer case management scores exactly 3, which is equal to the mean, not below it, so it does not count. Reading "below" strictly (not "at or below") is exactly the kind of precise interpretation that assessments and real capability reports both demand. Summaries like "two of our five assessed capabilities fall below average maturity" often go straight into stakeholder packs, so the counting rule matters.

**Question 6 (moderate) - Ratios in team composition**

A programme board wants the ratio of business architects to projects kept at 1:4 or better (meaning each architect supports at most 4 projects). The architecture team currently has 3 business architects supporting 14 projects. Next quarter, 4 more projects will start and one architect will retire. How many architects must be recruited to restore the 1:4 ratio next quarter?

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) None; the ratio is fine

**Correct answer: C) 3**

**Explanation:** Next quarter there will be 14 + 4 = 18 projects and 3 - 1 = 2 architects. To keep each architect at no more than 4 projects, you need at least 18 / 4 = 4.5 architects - and since you cannot hire half a person, round up to 5 architects. With 2 remaining, the team must recruit 5 - 2 = 3. The important habit here is rounding up when the quantity is people needed to meet a standard: 4 architects would give 18 / 4 = 4.5 projects each, which breaks the 1:4 rule. Capacity and workload figures like these appear regularly in the strategic planning that you support in an administrative capacity.

**Question 7 (moderate) - Percentage of spend by strategic priority**

You are helping prepare a slide showing how the change budget maps to strategy. The budget is:

| Strategic priority | Spend |
|---|---|
| Citizen self-service | 1.8 million pounds |
| System consolidation | 1.2 million pounds |
| Data quality | 0.6 million pounds |
| Not mapped to strategy | 0.4 million pounds |

What percentage of the total budget is mapped to the "System consolidation" priority?

- A) 25%
- B) 30%
- C) 35%
- D) 40%

**Correct answer: B) 30%**

**Explanation:** Total budget = 1.8 + 1.2 + 0.6 + 0.4 = 4.0 million pounds. System consolidation share = 1.2 / 4.0 = 0.30 = 30%. Two traps: first, forgetting to include the "not mapped" 0.4 million in the total (giving 1.2 / 3.6 = 33.3%, close to answer C); second, misreading which row the question asks about. Include every row in the denominator unless the question says otherwise. Incidentally, the "not mapped to strategy" line is exactly the kind of figure a business architect highlights: 10% of change spend with no strategic justification is a finding worth raising.

**Question 8 (moderate) - Cost comparison of workshop venues**

Your team needs a venue for a full-day stakeholder workshop for 30 people. You gather these quotes:

- Venue A: room hire 400 pounds, catering 12 pounds per person.
- Venue B: room hire 250 pounds, catering 18 pounds per person.
- Venue C: all-inclusive package at 25 pounds per person.

Which venue is cheapest for 30 people, and by how much compared with the next cheapest?

- A) Venue A, by 30 pounds
- B) Venue B, by 10 pounds
- C) Venue C, by 10 pounds
- D) Venue C, by 40 pounds

**Correct answer: C) Venue C, by 10 pounds**

**Explanation:** Cost each venue in full for 30 people, keeping fixed and per-person costs separate. Venue A: 400 + (12 x 30) = 400 + 360 = 760 pounds. Venue B: 250 + (18 x 30) = 250 + 540 = 790 pounds. Venue C: 25 x 30 = 750 pounds. Venue C is cheapest at 750 pounds; the next cheapest is Venue A at 760 pounds, so the saving is 760 - 750 = 10 pounds. Two lessons here. First, a low room hire (Venue B's 250 pounds) can be swamped by a high per-person cost - always compute the total, never judge by one component. Second, the margin between options can be small: 10 pounds either way. In a real venue decision you would then weigh non-price factors too, such as step-free access and hearing-loop provision, which UK public sector events must take seriously. But when the question asks only for the cheapest, trust the completed arithmetic.

**Question 9 (hard) - Multi-step budget projection**

A programme spends 60,000 pounds per month on contractors. From next month, the programme will reduce contractor spend by 15% and redirect half of the saving into staff training. How much will be redirected into staff training each month?

- A) 9,000 pounds
- B) 4,500 pounds
- C) 6,000 pounds
- D) 3,000 pounds

**Correct answer: B) 4,500 pounds**

**Explanation:** Step 1: calculate the saving. 15% of 60,000 = 0.15 x 60,000 = 9,000 pounds per month. Step 2: half of that saving goes to training: 9,000 / 2 = 4,500 pounds per month. The trap answer is A (stopping after step 1) - multi-step questions often list each intermediate result as an option, so always re-read the question to confirm which quantity is actually asked for. Here the question asks for the redirected amount, not the saving. When you support strategic planning administratively, you will often trace money through exactly this kind of two-step logic: a saving is made, then split between destinations.

**Question 10 (hard) - Interpreting a utilisation table**

The architecture practice tracks how architect time is used. Here is last month's breakdown for the team's 4 architects (each works 160 hours per month):

| Activity | Hours (whole team) |
|---|---|
| Project design reviews | 224 |
| Stakeholder workshops | 96 |
| Capability modelling | 160 |
| Administration | 64 |
| Training and development | 96 |

What percentage of total team hours went on stakeholder workshops and capability modelling combined?

- A) 25%
- B) 35%
- C) 40%
- D) 45%

**Correct answer: C) 40%**

**Explanation:** Total team hours = 4 architects x 160 hours = 640 hours. Check the table adds up: 224 + 96 + 160 + 64 + 96 = 640 (it does - always verify). Workshops plus capability modelling = 96 + 160 = 256 hours. Percentage = 256 / 640 = 0.4 = 40%. A useful shortcut: 256 / 640 simplifies by dividing both by 64, giving 4 / 10. Utilisation analysis like this feeds real decisions about where the practice spends its effort, and as a trainee you may well be the person compiling the figures - so practising the "does the table total match the expected total?" check is time well spent.

**Question 11 (hard) - Weighted scoring of options**

A senior architect asks you to total a weighted options appraisal for three software tools that support capability modelling. Scores are out of 10; each score is multiplied by the criterion weight, then summed.

| Criterion | Weight | Tool X | Tool Y | Tool Z |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ease of use | 3 | 8 | 6 | 7 |
| Accessibility compliance | 4 | 6 | 9 | 7 |
| Cost | 2 | 5 | 4 | 8 |
| Integration with existing tools | 1 | 9 | 7 | 5 |

Which tool wins the weighted appraisal?

- A) Tool X
- B) Tool Y
- C) Tool Z
- D) Tools Y and Z tie

**Correct answer: C) Tool Z**

**Explanation:** Compute each weighted total row by row, writing each product down. Tool X: (3 x 8) + (4 x 6) + (2 x 5) + (1 x 9) = 24 + 24 + 10 + 9 = 67. Tool Y: (3 x 6) + (4 x 9) + (2 x 4) + (1 x 7) = 18 + 36 + 8 + 7 = 69. Tool Z: (3 x 7) + (4 x 7) + (2 x 8) + (1 x 5) = 21 + 28 + 16 + 5 = 70. Tool Z wins at 70, narrowly ahead of Tool Y at 69. The result is close, which is exactly why method matters: a single transposed digit would flip the outcome, and a transposed digit feels exactly like a correct answer until you re-add the column. In a timed test, do each row's multiplication once, write it down, and re-add the totals at the end rather than trusting a running total in your head. Notice also that Tool Y tops the accessibility criterion but still loses overall - weights change outcomes, which is why appraisal weights are agreed before scoring, never after. Weighted scoring tables are everywhere in public sector options appraisals, and accessibility compliance carrying the highest weight reflects genuine UK government practice - services must be accessible to everyone.

**Question 12 (hard) - Trend interpretation with percentages**

Quarterly counts of duplicate systems identified across the organisation:

| Quarter | Duplicate systems identified (cumulative) | Duplicate systems retired (cumulative) |
|---|---|---|
| Q1 | 20 | 2 |
| Q2 | 32 | 6 |
| Q3 | 40 | 14 |
| Q4 | 44 | 24 |

Between which two consecutive quarters did the number of systems retired grow by the largest percentage?

- A) Q1 to Q2
- B) Q2 to Q3
- C) Q3 to Q4
- D) The growth rate was the same each quarter

**Correct answer: A) Q1 to Q2**

**Explanation:** Percentage growth = (new - old) / old x 100, applied to the cumulative retired column. Q1 to Q2: (6 - 2) / 2 = 200%. Q2 to Q3: (14 - 6) / 6 = 133% (rounded). Q3 to Q4: (24 - 14) / 14 = 71% (rounded). The largest percentage growth is Q1 to Q2, even though the largest absolute increase (10 systems) came in Q3 to Q4. This is the mirror image of Question 3's lesson: percentage growth and absolute growth can point at different quarters, and both facts can be true at once. When you help draft a consolidation progress report, choose the measure that answers the stakeholder's actual question - and never mix the two in one sentence without saying which is which.

**Question 13 (moderate) - Time planning across a programme**

You are helping schedule capability workshops. Each workshop needs: 2 hours of preparation, 3 hours of delivery, and 1.5 hours of write-up. You have been allocated 40 hours of your time this month for workshop support. Travel and admin will take a fixed 7 hours in total for the month. How many complete workshops can you support this month?

- A) 4
- B) 5
- C) 6
- D) 7

**Correct answer: B) 5**

**Explanation:** Hours available for workshops = 40 - 7 = 33 hours. Hours per workshop = 2 + 3 + 1.5 = 6.5 hours. Number of complete workshops = 33 / 6.5 = 5.08, and since only complete workshops count, round down to 5. Note the direction of rounding: in Question 6 we rounded up (people needed to meet a standard); here we round down (complete units achievable within a limit). Choosing the rounding direction from the real-world meaning, rather than by habit, is a mark of careful numeric reasoning - and scheduling your own workshop support time is about as job-specific as it gets for a trainee business architect.

**Question 14 (hard) - Benefits phasing across years**

A business change is forecast to deliver benefits of 50,000 pounds in year 1, rising by 20% in year 2, and rising by a further 25% in year 3 (each rise is on the previous year's figure). The change costs 150,000 pounds, all paid in year 1. By the end of which year do cumulative benefits first exceed the total cost?

- A) End of year 1
- B) End of year 2
- C) End of year 3
- D) Cumulative benefits never exceed the cost in the first three years

**Correct answer: C) End of year 3**

**Explanation:** Build the benefits year by year. Year 1: 50,000 pounds. Year 2: 50,000 x 1.20 = 60,000 pounds. Year 3: 60,000 x 1.25 = 75,000 pounds. Now accumulate: end of year 1 = 50,000 (below 150,000). End of year 2 = 50,000 + 60,000 = 110,000 (still below). End of year 3 = 110,000 + 75,000 = 185,000, which exceeds 150,000 for the first time. Two things to notice. First, successive percentage rises compound - the 25% rise applies to 60,000, not to the original 50,000, so year 3 is 75,000, not 62,500. Second, the question asks about cumulative benefits versus cost, so you must keep a running total rather than comparing single years. Benefit profiles like this appear in the business cases that business architects help assemble, and "when do we break even?" is one of the first questions any programme board asks.

### Preparation tips

- **Rebuild your percentage toolkit.** Three formulas cover most questions: part / whole x 100 (what percentage), whole x rate (percentage of), and (new - old) / old x 100 (percentage change). Write them on a card and practise until they are automatic.
- **Always find the total first.** For any table question, compute or verify the total before answering. Many questions hinge on the denominator, and checking that a table's rows sum to the stated total catches errors early.
- **Mind the units and scales.** Watch for "in thousands", "in millions", per-person versus total, monthly versus annual. Underline the units in the question before you calculate.
- **Practise with a calculator you will actually use.** If the test provides an onscreen calculator, practise with a basic onscreen calculator rather than your phone's scientific one, so the interface feels familiar.
- **Estimate before you calculate.** A rough estimate ("about three quarters, so around 75%") tells you instantly if your precise answer is in the right region.
- **Build number confidence gently.** If arithmetic makes you anxious, ten minutes of daily practice beats a three-hour cram. Confidence with everyday business maths grows quickly with little-and-often exposure.
- **Learn the common fraction-percentage pairs.** Knowing instantly that 1/4 is 25%, 1/3 is about 33%, 3/4 is 75%, and 1/5 is 20% lets you sense-check answers in seconds. Add 1/8 (12.5%) and 2/3 (about 67%) once the first set feels automatic - these six pairs cover a surprising share of workplace percentage questions.
- **Verify tables before trusting them.** As in Question 10, add up the rows and compare with any stated total. This habit both catches question traps and mirrors real practice: architects who verify source data before analysing it produce reports that survive scrutiny.

### Common pitfalls

- **Misreading chart and table labels.** Confusing monthly with annual data, cumulative with per-quarter figures (as in Question 12), or missing a scale qualifier like "in thousands" causes more lost marks than hard arithmetic does.
- **Dividing by the wrong base.** Percentage change is measured against the old value; proportions are measured against the full total including rows you might overlook (like "not mapped" budget lines).
- **Over-calculating.** Sometimes simplifying a fraction (18/24 = 3/4) or estimating is faster and safer than long division. Do not perform a five-step calculation when a two-step one answers the question.
- **Stopping one step early.** Multi-step questions list intermediate results as trap options. Re-read the question to confirm which quantity is asked for before you answer.
- **Time-sink questions.** If a multi-stage problem is eating your time, skip it and bank the easier marks first. You can return later - an unanswered easy question is the most expensive mistake in a timed test.
- **Rounding the wrong way.** Round up when calculating resources needed to meet a requirement; round down when counting complete units achievable within a limit.


## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment evaluates how well you understand, analyse, and draw correct conclusions from written business information. It mirrors the reading demands of your actual role: internal standards, governance policies, emails from senior architects, workshop briefs, and programme reports.

The format is distinctive and takes a little getting used to. You are given a passage of text, then a series of statements. For each statement you answer:

- **True** - the statement definitely follows from the passage.
- **False** - the statement definitely contradicts the passage.
- **Cannot Say** - the passage does not give enough information to decide either way.

The golden rule: answer based strictly on the text provided, ignoring your outside knowledge. Even if you happen to know that something is true in the real world, if the passage does not say it, the answer is Cannot Say. Some assessments also include multiple-choice comprehension questions, which ask you to identify the best summary, the correct application of a rule, or the writer's main point.

Verbal reasoning tests are usually time-pressured - often less than a minute per question - to simulate workplace reading under deadlines. For a trainee business architect, this assessment is especially relevant: your role profile asks you to listen to the needs of design and business stakeholders and interpret information, and much of that information arrives in writing. Misreading a governance rule or a stakeholder email creates real problems, so employers test this skill carefully.

### What it measures for your role

- **Communicating information**: the heart of this assessment. Interpreting stakeholder needs from emails and documents, and distinguishing what was actually said from what you assumed, is tested in every question.
- **Business architecture**: passages use framework and governance language - capability, operating model, design authority - and test whether you extract rules from standards documents accurately.
- **Consultancy**: identifying what advice or recommendations might be appropriate starts with reading previous work by others correctly - a named behaviour at your level.
- **Making and guiding decisions**: several questions test whether a conclusion is supported by the evidence in the text - the reading equivalent of describing the reasoning behind a recommendation.
- **Methods and tools**: passages include procedural instructions (how to use a repository, how to run a review), testing whether you can follow written methods precisely.
- **Strategic thinking**: questions ask you to identify the strategic purpose stated in a document, separating the goal from the mechanism.

### Practice questions

For each passage, read carefully, then answer every question from the text alone. Explanations point back to the exact wording, so you can see precisely why each answer is what it is.

**Passage 1 - Internal standard: design review governance**

"All programmes must submit design documentation to the Design Authority at least ten working days before their scheduled review. Programmes classified as high-risk must additionally submit a business impact statement. The Design Authority meets fortnightly. Where a programme misses its submission deadline, its review will be deferred to the next available meeting. The Design Authority may, at its discretion, grant an expedited review for programmes supporting ministerial priorities."

**Question 1 (easy)** - True, False, or Cannot Say: "Programmes must submit design documentation at least ten working days before their scheduled review."

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** This is a direct restatement of the first sentence: "All programmes must submit design documentation to the Design Authority at least ten working days before their scheduled review." Easy questions like this reward careful, literal matching between statement and text. Check every element - "ten working days" (not calendar days), "before their scheduled review" - before confirming.

**Question 2 (easy)** - True, False, or Cannot Say: "The Design Authority meets every week."

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The passage says "The Design Authority meets fortnightly" - that is, every two weeks. "Every week" directly contradicts "fortnightly", so the answer is False, not Cannot Say. A statement is False only when the text contradicts it; here it plainly does. Knowing the word "fortnightly" matters - UK public sector documents use it often.

**Question 3 (moderate)** - True, False, or Cannot Say: "A low-risk programme is not required to submit a business impact statement."

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The passage states that "Programmes classified as high-risk must additionally submit a business impact statement." The word "additionally" tells us this requirement applies on top of the standard documentation, and only to high-risk programmes. The general requirement (first sentence) mentions design documentation only. So a low-risk programme's stated obligations do not include a business impact statement - the statement is True. Careful readers will note this relies on the passage listing requirements exhaustively; in verbal reasoning tests, a rule stated as applying to one category is taken as not applying to others unless the text says otherwise.

**Question 4 (moderate)** - True, False, or Cannot Say: "A programme supporting a ministerial priority will receive an expedited review."

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The passage says the Design Authority "may, at its discretion, grant an expedited review" for such programmes. "May, at its discretion" means it is possible, not guaranteed. The statement claims it "will" happen - the passage neither confirms this (so not True) nor rules it out (so not False). Cannot Say. Watch for modal verbs: "may", "can", "will", "must" each carry different force, and swapping one for another is the single most common trick in verbal reasoning tests - and a meaningful distinction in governance documents, where discretion and obligation are very different things.

**Question 5 (moderate)** - Multiple choice: A programme missed its submission deadline for a review scheduled at the next Design Authority meeting. According to the passage, what happens?

- A) The programme is cancelled.
- B) The programme's review is deferred to the next available meeting.
- C) The programme must submit a business impact statement.
- D) The programme receives an expedited review.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage states: "Where a programme misses its submission deadline, its review will be deferred to the next available meeting." Option B repeats this rule. Option A invents a consequence the text never mentions. Option C confuses the missed-deadline rule with the high-risk rule. Option D inverts the situation - expedited review is a discretionary benefit for ministerial priorities, not a remedy for lateness. Applying the right rule to the right situation is exactly what you do when supporting governance processes.

**Passage 2 - Email from a senior business architect**

"Hi - thanks for offering to help with the operating model pack. Could you pull together the capability heat map and the stakeholder summary by Thursday? The heat map should use the January assessment scores, not the draft February ones, because February hasn't been signed off. Don't worry about the value stream diagrams; Priya is updating those and will send them straight to me. If Finance ask about the pack, please refer them to me rather than sharing anything early. One more thing - the Deputy Director wants the pack to be accessible, so please use the approved template with proper heading styles."

**Question 6 (easy)** - True, False, or Cannot Say: "The heat map should use the January assessment scores."

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The email says directly: "The heat map should use the January assessment scores, not the draft February ones." A literal match. Note the reason given - February "hasn't been signed off" - because understanding why an instruction exists helps you apply it correctly if circumstances change.

**Question 7 (moderate)** - True, False, or Cannot Say: "Priya will send the updated value stream diagrams to you."

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The email says Priya "will send them straight to me" - that is, to the senior architect who wrote the email, not to you. The statement contradicts the text, so it is False. Misreading who receives what is a small error with real consequences: you might chase Priya for diagrams that were never coming to you, or worse, tell someone the pack is incomplete when it is not. Pronoun tracking - who "me", "you", and "them" refer to - is a frequent test point and a frequent source of real workplace confusion.

**Question 8 (moderate)** - True, False, or Cannot Say: "The February assessment scores are inaccurate."

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The email says the February scores are "draft" and "haven't been signed off". Unsigned-off is not the same as inaccurate - a draft may be perfectly accurate but simply awaiting approval. The passage gives no information about the accuracy of the February scores either way, so Cannot Say. This is the "status versus quality" confusion: governance status (signed off, draft, approved) describes where a document is in a process, not how good it is. Business architects live in documents with statuses, so this distinction will serve you daily.

**Question 9 (hard)** - Multiple choice: A colleague from Finance stops you in the corridor and asks to see the current draft of the operating model pack "just for a quick look". Based only on the email, what did the senior architect ask you to do in this situation?

- A) Show Finance the pack but ask them to keep it confidential.
- B) Refer the Finance colleague to the senior architect rather than sharing anything early.
- C) Share only the January heat map, since it is signed off.
- D) Tell Finance the pack does not exist.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The email states: "If Finance ask about the pack, please refer them to me rather than sharing anything early." Option B matches this instruction exactly. Option A shares the pack, which the instruction forbids ("rather than sharing anything early"). Option C invents a partial-sharing rule the email never gives - the instruction covers "anything", not just unsigned-off content. Option D is dishonest and goes far beyond the instruction. Notice that the correct answer requires you to apply a written instruction to a slightly different situation (a corridor conversation rather than a formal request) - the mark of genuine comprehension rather than keyword matching.

**Passage 3 - Extract from a business change programme report**

"The Casework Modernisation Programme remains on track for its March milestone. Of the six business units in scope, four have completed process mapping and two are scheduled to complete by the end of February. Early findings indicate that duplication between the regional intake teams is greater than originally estimated. The programme board has asked the architecture team to quantify the potential staff-time savings from a single national intake process before any decision is taken. No decision on team restructuring has been made, and staff will be consulted before any changes are proposed."

**Question 10 (moderate)** - True, False, or Cannot Say: "All six business units have completed process mapping."

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The passage says "four have completed process mapping and two are scheduled to complete by the end of February". Scheduled to complete is not completed - so the claim that all six are done contradicts the text. Assessments love this gap between planned and actual; so do programme boards. When you draft status summaries, keep "done" and "due" rigorously separate.

**Question 11 (moderate)** - True, False, or Cannot Say: "The programme will merge the regional intake teams into a single national team."

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The passage says the board asked the architecture team "to quantify the potential staff-time savings from a single national intake process before any decision is taken" and explicitly adds "No decision on team restructuring has been made". A merger is under investigation, not decided - the passage neither confirms the merger will happen (not True) nor says it will not (not False). Cannot Say. Treating an option under analysis as a decision already taken is one of the most damaging misreadings a trainee can make - if repeated to staff, it could cause genuine alarm about jobs. The passage's careful wording ("before any decision is taken", "will be consulted") is typical of well-written change communications, and you should learn to read and write with that precision.

**Question 12 (hard)** - True, False, or Cannot Say: "Duplication between the regional intake teams was underestimated at the start of the programme."

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The passage states: "Early findings indicate that duplication between the regional intake teams is greater than originally estimated." If actual duplication is greater than the original estimate, then the original estimate was too low - which is what "underestimated" means. This question tests whether you can recognise the same fact expressed in different words (paraphrase recognition), rather than hunting for matching keywords. One caution: the passage says "early findings indicate", which signals some provisionality - but the statement under test mirrors that same evidential claim, so True is correct. If the statement had said "duplication is definitely twice the estimate", the answer would be Cannot Say.

**Question 13 (hard)** - Multiple choice: Which of the following best summarises the main point of the passage?

- A) The programme is behind schedule because of duplication in intake teams.
- B) The programme is progressing to plan, an unexpected level of intake duplication has emerged, and evidence is being gathered before any restructuring decision.
- C) The architecture team has recommended a single national intake process.
- D) Staff have been consulted about the proposed team restructuring.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B captures all three strands: on track ("remains on track for its March milestone"), the emerging finding ("duplication... is greater than originally estimated"), and the evidence-before-decision stance ("quantify... before any decision is taken"). Option A contradicts the first sentence - the programme is on track. Option C overstates the architecture team's role: they were asked to quantify savings, not to recommend. Option D reverses the timeline: staff "will be consulted", meaning it has not happened yet. Summary questions test whether you can hold a whole passage in balance rather than seizing on one vivid detail - the same skill you use when you support the production of clear business architecture outputs from long source documents.

**Passage 4 - Repository usage guidance**

"All architecture artefacts must be stored in the central repository. Artefacts must be given a status of Draft, In Review, Approved, or Archived. Only Approved artefacts may be shared outside the architecture team. Trainees may create and edit Draft artefacts, and may move an artefact into In Review status once a named senior reviewer has agreed to review it. Only senior architects may set an artefact to Approved. Archived artefacts must not be edited; if an archived artefact needs changing, a new Draft must be created instead."

**Question 14 (moderate)** - True, False, or Cannot Say: "A trainee may set an artefact's status to Approved if a senior architect has verbally agreed the content."

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The passage is explicit: "Only senior architects may set an artefact to Approved." The word "only" excludes everyone else, including a trainee acting with verbal agreement - the mechanism of agreement does not change who may perform the action. The statement contradicts the rule, so it is False. Watch for scenarios that add plausible-sounding justifications ("a senior architect agreed") to an action the text forbids; the justification is a distractor. In real governance, this rule protects you: approval authority sits with the accountable person, and the audit trail must show they exercised it themselves.

**Question 15 (hard)** - Multiple choice: An archived process model needs updating to reflect a new legal requirement. According to the passage, what is the correct procedure?

- A) Edit the archived artefact directly, since legal requirements take priority.
- B) Move the artefact back to Draft status and edit it.
- C) Create a new Draft artefact rather than editing the archived one.
- D) Ask a senior architect to edit the archived artefact.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage says: "Archived artefacts must not be edited; if an archived artefact needs changing, a new Draft must be created instead." Option C follows this exactly. Option A invents a legal-priority exception the text does not contain - real policies may have such exceptions, but this passage does not, and you must answer from the text alone. Option B invents a status transition the passage never describes. Option D fails because the prohibition on editing archived artefacts is absolute in the text - it does not say "except by senior architects". The habit of checking what a rule actually permits, rather than what would be convenient, is core to working with methods and tools under guidance.

**Passage 5 - Workshop briefing note**

"Thursday's capability workshop will run from 09:30 to 12:30 in Conference Room 2, with a hybrid option for remote attendees. The objective is to agree draft definitions for the five customer-facing capabilities; supporting capabilities are out of scope and will be covered in a later session. The facilitator will be the lead business architect. The trainee business architect will capture decisions and actions on the shared board, and will circulate a summary within two working days. Attendees should read the pre-circulated capability definitions before the session. Any attendee who cannot attend should send a delegate with authority to agree definitions on their behalf."

**Question 16 (easy)** - True, False, or Cannot Say: "Supporting capabilities will be discussed at Thursday's workshop."

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The briefing states that "supporting capabilities are out of scope and will be covered in a later session". The statement directly contradicts this, so it is False. Scope statements - what is in, what is out - are among the most important sentences in any architecture document, and workshops drift badly when nobody holds the scope line. As the note-taker, knowing the scope helps you flag out-of-scope discussion in your notes.

**Question 17 (moderate)** - True, False, or Cannot Say: "The trainee business architect will circulate the workshop summary within two calendar days."

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The briefing says the summary will be circulated "within two working days" - the statement says "calendar days". These are different commitments: two working days from a Thursday workshop means by Monday, whereas two calendar days would mean by Saturday. Because the statement substitutes a materially different deadline for the one in the text, it contradicts the passage and is False. If you hesitated between False and Cannot Say, the deciding point is that the passage does state the deadline - it is just a different one. Precision about working versus calendar days matters constantly in public sector service standards and correspondence targets.

**Question 18 (hard)** - True, False, or Cannot Say: "If a listed attendee cannot attend, the workshop objective cannot be achieved."

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The briefing anticipates absence: anyone who cannot attend "should send a delegate with authority to agree definitions on their behalf". This mechanism suggests the workshop is designed to reach agreement even with substitutions - but the passage never states what happens to the objective if someone is absent, with or without a delegate. It neither confirms the statement (not True) nor denies it (not False) - the outcome depends on facts the passage does not give, such as whether a delegate actually attends. Cannot Say. Notice how a well-designed conditional instruction ("should send a delegate") tempts you to infer an outcome; inference beyond the text is exactly what this format penalises.

### Preparation tips

- **Master the three verdicts.** Before the test, be able to recite: True = the text says so; False = the text says the opposite; Cannot Say = the text is silent or non-committal. When unsure, ask "where exactly in the passage is my evidence?" If you cannot point to it, lean towards Cannot Say.
- **Underline modal verbs.** "May", "must", "will", "should", "can" - the strength of these little words decides many answers. Practise noticing them in your own team's documents.
- **Park your knowledge.** You may know how design authorities really work. The test does not care - answer only from the passage. Practise this by deliberately reading passages on topics you know well and answering strictly from the text.
- **Read the statement first, sometimes.** For long passages, reading the question statement first tells you what to scan for. Experiment during practice to find which order suits you.
- **Practise on real documents.** Your organisation's standards, governance terms of reference, and programme reports are ideal practice material. Write three True/False/Cannot Say statements about a document, then challenge yourself (or a fellow trainee) a day later.
- **Build reading stamina kindly.** Dense text under time pressure is tiring. Short daily practice - one passage with five questions - builds speed and confidence better than occasional marathons.
- **Track who does what to whom.** Governance passages are full of actors: programmes, boards, reviewers, trainees, senior architects. Many questions turn on which actor holds an obligation or permission (as in Passage 4's "only senior architects may set an artefact to Approved"). When you read a rule, mentally tag it with its actor before moving on.
- **Watch for "working days", "calendar days", and other qualified units.** Deadlines, notice periods, and service standards in public sector documents almost always qualify their units, and questions exploit the difference. Underline the qualifier the moment you see it.
- **Distinguish scope statements from content.** Sentences saying what a document or meeting does not cover ("supporting capabilities are out of scope") are just as answerable as sentences about what it does cover. Do not skim past them - test writers certainly do not.

### Common pitfalls

- **Bringing in outside knowledge.** The most common error at every level. If the passage does not say it, it is not evidence - even if it is true in real life.
- **Confusing "likely" with "certain".** A passage saying findings "indicate" something, or that an outcome "may" occur, does not support a statement that it "will" occur. Match the strength of the statement to the strength of the text.
- **Keyword matching without understanding.** Distractor options often reuse exact words from the passage in the wrong relationship (as in Question 13's options). Paraphrase-based correct answers (Question 12) often look less similar to the text than wrong ones do.
- **Poor time budgeting.** Spending three minutes memorising a passage before looking at any question wastes time. Skim the passage, read the question, then return to verify against the exact wording.
- **Second-guessing Cannot Say.** Trainees often feel that Cannot Say is a "weak" answer and switch to True or False without evidence. Cannot Say is a full-strength verdict: it says "the text does not support a conclusion", which is often the most professionally responsible answer in real analysis too.
- **Missing negatives and exceptions.** Words like "not", "unless", "except", and "only" reverse or restrict a rule's meaning, and they are easy to skip when reading at speed. If a statement hinges on a rule, re-read the rule slowly and check whether a small word changes everything.


## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment evaluates your decision-making, interpersonal skills, and professional conduct. Instead of testing logic or maths, it presents realistic workplace dilemmas - conflicts, ethical grey areas, competing priorities - and asks how you would respond. There is usually no single objectively right answer; several responses may seem reasonable, but some fit the organisation's values and your role better than others.

The common response formats are:

- **Most effective / least effective**: from a set of possible actions, pick the one you judge most effective and the one you judge least effective.
- **Ranking**: put all the options in order from best to worst.
- **Rating**: rate each option's effectiveness independently.

Unlike the timed tests earlier in this guide, situational judgement assessments are often untimed or generously timed - they measure your natural judgement, not your speed.

For a trainee business architect in the UK public sector, the scenarios revolve around your real working life: supporting senior architects, taking notes in stakeholder workshops, handling requests you are not yet authorised to fulfil, spotting errors in others' work, and balancing learning with delivery. The judging standard reflects UK public sector values: honesty and integrity, putting user needs first, transparency, collaboration across disciplines, accessibility, and inclusion. It also reflects your level - as a trainee, seeking guidance is usually a strength, not a weakness, but passing every problem upwards without thought is not.

### What it measures for your role

- **Communicating information**: scenarios test whether you listen to stakeholders, interpret their needs fairly, and take part in multidisciplinary discussions constructively - especially when messages conflict.
- **Consultancy**: several dilemmas test whether you understand the purpose of consulting in your role, refer appropriately to previous work by others, and frame problems before jumping to answers.
- **Making and guiding decisions**: the core of the assessment - recommending a course of action and being able to explain the reasoning behind it, including identifying disputes between peers and local stakeholders.
- **Business architecture**: scenarios about applying frameworks under guidance test whether you respect the "under guidance" part while still contributing actively.
- **Methods and tools**: dilemmas about tool choice and following advice test whether you balance initiative with the discipline of agreed methods.
- **Strategic thinking**: options are often stronger or weaker depending on whether they serve the strategic purpose of the work, not just the immediate task.

### Practice questions

For each scenario, decide your answer honestly before reading the discussion. The explanations matter more than the "right answer" - they show the reasoning patterns assessors look for.

**Question 1 (easy) - A mistake in your own work**

You circulated the summary notes from yesterday's stakeholder workshop. This morning you realise you recorded a decision incorrectly: you wrote that the Finance representative agreed to the new process, when in fact they agreed only to pilot it in one team. Nobody has noticed yet.

Which is the MOST effective response, and which is the LEAST effective?

- A) Quietly edit the shared document so the record is correct, without telling anyone.
- B) Send a prompt correction to all recipients, clearly marking what changed, and check the correction with the Finance representative.
- C) Wait to see whether anyone notices; if they do, apologise and correct it.
- D) Mention it to your senior architect at your next scheduled catch-up in four days.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C.**

**Explanation:** Option B is honest, fast, and thorough: it corrects the record for everyone who received the wrong version, is transparent about the change, and verifies the correction with the person whose position was misrecorded - which also rebuilds any lost trust. Option A fixes the document but not the misunderstanding: everyone who read the email still believes Finance fully agreed, and a silent edit can look like covering up. Option D is honest but too slow - four days is a long time for a wrong decision record to circulate and be acted on. Option C is least effective because it knowingly leaves a false record in circulation and makes correction conditional on being caught - the opposite of the transparency expected in public service. The general principle: when you find your own error, correct it quickly, visibly, and with the affected people. Assessors consistently reward fast, open self-correction from trainees; nobody expects you to be error-free, but everyone expects you to be straight about it.

**Question 2 (easy) - Asked something beyond your knowledge**

During a workshop you are supporting, a stakeholder turns to you directly and asks: "So what does the architecture team recommend - should we merge these two teams?" The senior architect facilitating has stepped out for five minutes. You do not know the answer, and team mergers are a sensitive topic.

Rank these responses from best to worst:

- A) Say you will make a note of the question and ensure the senior architect addresses it when they return.
- B) Offer your personal view, making clear it is not the team's position.
- C) Say the architecture team never comments on team structures.
- D) Change the subject until the senior architect returns.

**Best to worst: A, B, D, C.**

**Explanation:** Option A is best: it takes the question seriously, is honest about the right person to answer, and uses your actual role in the room - capturing questions and actions. It neither overreaches nor dodges. Option B is second: it is honest and appropriately caveated, but as a trainee offering a personal view on a sensitive restructuring question in front of stakeholders, you risk your view being repeated as "the architecture team said..." regardless of your caveat. Option D avoids the overreach but is evasive and slightly disrespectful to the stakeholder - the question deserves acknowledgement, not deflection. Option C is worst because it is a made-up policy statement: inventing an organisational position is worse than admitting you do not know. The pattern to learn: acknowledge, capture, route to the right person - that is what "supporting during workshops and meetings with stakeholders" looks like when done well.

**Question 3 (moderate) - Spotting an error in a senior colleague's work**

You are formatting a capability heat map produced by a senior business architect, due to go to the programme board tomorrow. You notice that two capabilities appear to have swapped scores: "Payments processing" shows the low score that the assessment workshop gave to "Correspondence handling", and vice versa. You were at the workshop and are fairly confident it is a transposition error - but you are the most junior person involved.

Which is the MOST effective response, and which is the LEAST effective?

- A) Say nothing; a senior architect is unlikely to be wrong, and it is not your place to question their work.
- B) Correct the scores yourself before sending the pack, since you are confident about what the workshop agreed.
- C) Contact the senior architect today, explain what you noticed, reference the workshop record, and ask them to check.
- D) Raise it at the programme board meeting itself so the error can be corrected publicly.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: D.**

**Explanation:** Option C combines everything assessors want to see: you speak up despite being junior (integrity and courage), you do it privately and promptly (respect and urgency), you bring evidence (the workshop record - referring to previous work is a named consultancy behaviour at your level), and you ask them to check rather than asserting they are wrong (appropriate humility - you might be mistaken). Option A fails the organisation: seniority is not infallibility, and a wrong heat map could misdirect board decisions about real services. Option B has the right instinct about the error but the wrong method - changing a senior colleague's professional judgement without telling them breaks trust and bypasses the approval chain, and you might be wrong. Option D is least effective: it lets a known suspected error reach the board, then corrects it in the most embarrassing possible forum, damaging the team's credibility and your relationship with the architect. Speaking up early, privately, and with evidence is the professional gold standard.

**Question 4 (moderate) - Conflicting instructions from two seniors**

Two senior business architects both ask for your support on the same afternoon. Architect One asks you to prepare materials for tomorrow's stakeholder workshop. Architect Two asks you to finish consolidating process documentation "by end of day - the programme office needs it". You cannot do both in the time available. Both colleagues are equally senior.

Rank these responses from best to worst:

- A) Tell both architects about the clash and ask them to agree the priority between them, offering what you can realistically deliver on each.
- B) Work on whichever task was assigned first and let the other architect find out when their task is late.
- C) Stay silent and work late into the evening to attempt both tasks.
- D) Decide yourself which task matters more strategically and do that one, informing the other architect afterwards.

**Best to worst: A, D, C, B.**

**Explanation:** Option A is best because it surfaces the conflict immediately to the people who can resolve it, and comes with a constructive offer ("here is what I can realistically do"). Identifying and articulating disputes - including priority disputes - between peers and stakeholders is literally a named skill behaviour for your role, and it applies to workload conflicts too. Option D is second best: it shows strategic thinking and decisiveness, and it does communicate - but it makes a call that is not yours to make alone as a trainee, and it informs rather than consults. Option C is third: the willingness is admirable, but silently absorbing an impossible workload hides a resourcing problem from the team, risks two half-finished tasks, and sets an unsustainable pattern that harms your wellbeing. Option B is worst: it resolves the conflict by concealment and lets a colleague be surprised by a missed deadline that a single conversation would have prevented. The lesson: conflicts between legitimate demands are normal; hiding them is the only truly wrong move.

**Question 5 (moderate) - Workshop accessibility**

You are helping organise a hybrid capability workshop. The day before, a remote attendee emails to say they use a screen reader, and asks whether the materials will be accessible. You check and discover the pre-read pack is a set of image-heavy slides exported as pictures with no alternative text, prepared by a colleague who has now gone on leave.

Which is the MOST effective response, and which is the LEAST effective?

- A) Reply that unfortunately the materials cannot be changed at this notice, and offer to summarise them verbally during the workshop.
- B) Spend the time before the workshop creating an accessible version - a structured document with headings and text descriptions of each diagram - send it to the attendee, and flag the issue to your senior architect so future packs are accessible from the start.
- C) Forward the attendee's email to the colleague on leave, since they made the pack.
- D) Tell the attendee the pack is optional and they can just attend without reading it.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C.**

**Explanation:** Option B treats accessibility as a need to be met, not a problem to be managed away. It fixes the immediate barrier (the attendee gets usable materials, in time to prepare like everyone else), and it addresses the root cause by flagging the gap so accessible-by-default becomes the team habit. Accessibility is a legal and moral commitment across UK government services and workplaces, and assessors weight it heavily. Option A at least offers something, but it accepts an avoidable exclusion: the attendee is denied equal chance to prepare, and a verbal summary during the session is a workaround, not equality. Option D is worse: it quietly downgrades the attendee's participation - "just attend without preparing" is not what other attendees are being asked to do. Option C is least effective: it dumps the problem on someone who cannot act (they are on leave), leaves the attendee without an answer, and treats an organisational responsibility as a personal one. When an inclusion need surfaces, the effective pattern is: fix it now, fix the system next.

**Question 6 (moderate) - Pressure to skip the method**

You are supporting a capability assessment. The agreed method requires evidence to be gathered from each business unit before scores are assigned. A programme manager, under deadline pressure, says: "Just fill in the scores yourself based on what you reckon - it will be close enough, and we can tidy it up later."

Rank these responses from best to worst:

- A) Explain that scores need evidence to be credible with stakeholders, and offer a practical alternative - such as a clearly-labelled provisional view plus a fast-tracked evidence round - while checking the approach with your senior architect.
- B) Do what the programme manager asks; they are senior to you and own the deadline.
- C) Refuse outright and tell the programme manager that the method cannot be changed under any circumstances.
- D) Say yes to avoid conflict, but secretly gather what evidence you can before filling anything in.

**Best to worst: A, C, D, B.**

**Explanation:** Option A is best because it protects the integrity of the work while genuinely engaging with the programme manager's real problem - the deadline. Explaining why the method exists (evidence gives scores their credibility), proposing a compromise that is honest about its own status ("provisional, pending evidence"), and looping in your senior architect - since method changes are not a trainee's call - together show exactly the blend of consultancy, methods discipline, and escalation judgement your role requires. Option C is second: it reaches the right substantive position (do not fabricate scores) but rigidly, without empathy or alternatives, and "under no circumstances" overstates a trainee's authority just as much as caving does. Option D at least tries to preserve some evidence base, but it is built on a false agreement - the programme manager believes you are doing one thing while you do another, which corrodes trust when discovered. Option B is worst: made-up scores presented as assessment results would mislead every stakeholder who reads them, and "we can tidy it up later" is how provisional guesses become permanent facts. Note the recurring theme: the strongest options pair honesty about constraints with practical help on the underlying need.

**Question 7 (hard) - Confidential information overheard**

While taking notes in a small meeting, you hear two directors discuss - in passing - a possible reorganisation that would affect a team you work with closely. The information is clearly sensitive and was not meant for wider sharing. Later, a friend from that team says over coffee: "You get into all those senior meetings - is something happening to our team? People are worried."

Which is the MOST effective response, and which is the LEAST effective?

- A) Tell your friend what you heard - they deserve to know, and it will leak eventually anyway.
- B) Say you cannot share what is discussed in meetings you support, acknowledge that uncertainty is hard, and suggest they raise their concerns with their line manager; separately, tell the meeting chair that anxiety is building in the team.
- C) Deny that anything is being discussed at all.
- D) Refuse to answer and change the subject abruptly.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A.**

**Explanation:** Trusted access to senior discussions is fundamental to a business architect's ability to work across the organisation - and it survives only if confidences hold. Option B does four things well: it keeps the confidence without lying, it treats your friend's worry with respect rather than brushing it off, it points them to the legitimate channel for their question, and it feeds the human impact back to the chair - who can decide to communicate earlier - without breaching any specifics. Option D keeps the confidence but wastes the chance to respond with empathy or to help the situation; abrupt deflection can also inadvertently confirm suspicions. Option C is a lie: denying a discussion you witnessed is a direct breach of honesty, and when the truth emerges your credibility goes with it. Option A is least effective: it breaches confidentiality on the strength of two rationalisations ("they deserve to know", "it will leak anyway") that assessors specifically look for as red flags. If information genuinely should be shared sooner, the effective move is to advocate for earlier official communication - option B's final step - not to leak it yourself.

**Question 8 (hard) - Disagreement between stakeholders in a workshop**

In a process-mapping workshop you are supporting, representatives from two teams begin arguing about who owns a handoff step. The discussion becomes circular and heated, consuming time needed for the remaining agenda. The facilitator - your senior architect - is momentarily occupied helping a remote attendee reconnect.

Rank these responses from best to worst:

- A) Politely interject: note the disagreement on the board as an open issue with both positions summarised, and suggest the group park it for the facilitator to pick up in a moment.
- B) Let the argument continue - it is not a trainee's place to intervene between senior stakeholders.
- C) Declare which team you think owns the step, to settle the matter quickly.
- D) Whisper to the senior architect to interrupt what they are doing and deal with the argument.

**Best to worst: A, D, B, C.**

**Explanation:** Option A uses the legitimate tools you do have as workshop support: the board, the issue log, and the parking technique. Summarising both positions fairly - identifying and articulating a dispute between stakeholders, which is a named behaviour in your "making and guiding decisions" skill - shows each side they have been heard, which usually de-escalates, and it protects the agenda without anyone needing to win on the spot. Option D gets the right person involved but at the cost of interrupting a remote attendee's access - effectively trading one participant's inclusion for conflict management that option A handles without the trade. Option B mistakes passivity for humility: watching the agenda burn while holding the pen is not deference, it is a missed responsibility. Option C is worst: a trainee ruling on contested ownership exceeds your authority, will likely inflame the losing side, and entangles the neutral architecture function in a turf dispute. The deeper lesson: even at trainee level you are never "just taking notes" - capturing a dispute accurately and neutrally is a real contribution to resolving it.

**Question 9 (hard) - Learning versus delivery tension**

You are three months into your traineeship. Your development plan says you should attend the fortnightly business architecture community of practice session, where this week's topic - capability modelling techniques - directly supports your learning objectives. An hour before the session, your senior architect asks if you can help collate stakeholder feedback this afternoon: "No pressure - it can wait until tomorrow if needed, but sooner is better."

Which is the MOST effective response, and which is the LEAST effective?

- A) Skip the community session; delivery work always comes before personal development.
- B) Attend the session without mentioning the feedback task, and start the collation tomorrow.
- C) Tell your senior architect you have the community session this hour, confirm that starting the collation immediately afterwards (or tomorrow morning) meets their need, and do that.
- D) Skip the session but do not tell the senior architect it existed, so you appear maximally available.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: D.**

**Explanation:** The scenario deliberately includes the signal many candidates miss: "it can wait until tomorrow if needed". Option C simply takes the senior architect at their word - it is transparent about the clash, confirms rather than assumes, and meets both the delivery need and the development need. Developing your knowledge of business architecture frameworks and practice is not a perk; it is a listed duty at your role level, and a trainee who routinely sacrifices structured learning becomes less useful, not more. Option B reaches a similar outcome but silently: the senior architect learns nothing about your constraints and might reasonably expect the work sooner, so small trust is eroded for no benefit. Option A applies a rule ("delivery always first") that the facts contradict - the task explicitly can wait, so the sacrifice buys nothing. Option D is least effective because it adds concealment to the unnecessary sacrifice and optimises for appearing available rather than being effective - a pattern that leads to burnout and stalled development. Assessors read this scenario as a test of honest communication and sustainable self-management, not of raw willingness.

**Question 10 (moderate) - Referring to previous work**

A senior architect asks you to draft a first-cut stakeholder map for a new programme in the licensing business area. You have never made one before. You know the team's repository contains an approved stakeholder map from last year's licensing programme, and the team has a standard stakeholder-mapping template.

Rank these responses from best to worst:

- A) Start from the standard template, study the previous licensing map for structure and likely stakeholders, verify which stakeholders remain current, and note your sources and assumptions for the senior architect's review.
- B) Design your own stakeholder map format from scratch to demonstrate creativity and initiative.
- C) Copy last year's map, change the programme name, and submit it.
- D) Tell the senior architect you have never done one and ask them to do it instead.

**Best to worst: A, D, B, C.**

**Explanation:** Option A is textbook trainee consultancy practice - your skill profile says you work "under guidance to identify what advice, guidance and recommendations might be appropriate, usually by referring to previous work by others", and that is precisely what A does: reuse the approved template and prior map as scaffolding, update rather than assume (stakeholders change in a year), and expose your working so the reviewer can correct it efficiently. Option D is honest and safely avoids a bad output, but it declines the learning opportunity entirely; a better version of D is folded into A - attempt the draft, then seek review. Option B ignores an agreed team method for no reason the scenario supports; initiative is valuable, but reinventing a standard artefact format creates inconsistency and extra review work. Option C is worst: it looks like the fastest option but silently imports a year of staleness - departed stakeholders, changed roles, different programme scope - and presents unverified old work as a current deliverable. The ranking rewards a habit worth internalising early: reuse with verification beats both reinvention and blind copying.

### Preparation tips

- **Anchor on values, not cleverness.** UK public sector scenarios reward honesty, transparency, user focus, collaboration, accessibility, and inclusion. When torn between options, ask: "Which action best serves the people affected, and which could I most comfortably explain afterwards?"
- **Learn your level.** For trainee scenarios, the strong options usually involve speaking up promptly, bringing evidence, offering practical help, and looping in a senior at the right moment. Overstepping (deciding alone) and disappearing (staying silent) are both penalised.
- **Notice the verbs in each option.** "Quietly", "secretly", "wait and see", "refuse outright" are warning signs; "explain", "check", "offer", "flag" usually mark stronger options.
- **Read every option before judging any.** Most/least questions are comparative - an option that seems fine in isolation may be clearly weaker than another.
- **Practise explaining your choice aloud.** After each practice question, say your reasoning in two sentences. Recommending decisions and describing the reasoning behind them is your named skill - the explanation is the skill.
- **Reflect on real moments.** After a real workshop or a tricky email, ask yourself what the "option A to D" versions of your response would have been. Your daily work is a free practice bank.

### Common pitfalls

- **Answering idealistically rather than practically.** Choosing what sounds noble ("work all night to do both tasks") over what actually works (surface the conflict) is a classic error - assessors reward effectiveness, not martyrdom.
- **Selecting passive options.** Options that delay action, wait to be caught, or pass the problem to someone who cannot act are almost always weak, even when they avoid immediate conflict.
- **Overreaching your role.** Settling stakeholder disputes yourself, changing a senior colleague's work without telling them, or inventing organisational positions all exceed a trainee's authority - escalation done well is a strength at your level.
- **Under-reaching your role.** The opposite trap: assuming a trainee should never speak up, never interject, and never question seniors. The strongest options show courage with humility.
- **Ignoring the stated context.** Details like "it can wait until tomorrow" or "the facilitator is momentarily occupied" are placed deliberately. Options that contradict a stated fact are rarely correct.
- **Forgetting the values dimension.** Accessibility, inclusion, and confidentiality are not side issues in UK public sector scenarios - options that trade them away for convenience are heavily penalised.
