There is nothing mysterious about it. Employers across government use these assessments when people apply for roles, move between teams, or take part in development programmes. The good news is that the skills they measure can be practised and improved, just like any other skill.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures how well you think through everyday work problems. Instead of abstract puzzles about shapes and symbols, it uses realistic material from a job like yours: incident tickets, monitoring alerts, runbooks, log entries, and support rotas. The questions ask you to spot patterns, follow rules to a logical conclusion, check details for errors, decide what to do first, and solve small practical problems.

The typical format is an online, timed test lasting between 15 and 30 minutes, with somewhere between 15 and 30 questions. Each question usually has four answer options with exactly one correct answer. Many modern test platforms are adaptive, which means the questions get slightly harder when you answer correctly and slightly easier when you do not — so do not panic if the test feels like it is getting more difficult; that can be a sign you are doing well. Scoring is objective and usually compares your answers with a large group of other test takers. Most platforms give you a couple of ungraded warm-up questions first, so you can get used to the screen layout without any pressure.

Why do employers use this kind of assessment for application operations roles? Because accuracy under time pressure is the heart of the job. When an incident comes in, you need to read it carefully, notice what information is missing, follow the routing rules correctly, and allocate it to the appropriate channel. A small slip — the wrong category, a missed detail, a step done out of order — can slow down the whole team. A cognitive assessment is a safe, low-stakes way to measure exactly that carefulness and clear thinking.

One reassuring point before you start: at associate level, assessments are calibrated for people early in their careers. The questions below start gently and build up. Every single one is based on tasks you genuinely do — identifying and registering incidents, gathering required information, passing problems to your team, and following your team's processes.

### How this assessment maps to your role

Each thinking skill measured by the assessment connects directly to a named skill in your role summary:

- **Pattern recognition** maps to your **Problem management** skill. When you investigate problems in systems, processes and services, the first clue is often a pattern: the same error appearing at the same time each day, the same application failing after each update, the same users reporting the same issue. Spotting the pattern is the start of every investigation.
- **Logical deduction** maps to your **Incident management** skill. Registering incidents and allocating them to the appropriate channel means applying routing rules correctly: if the incident matches this condition, it goes to that channel. Deduction questions practise following rules exactly, without adding assumptions.
- **Error checking** also maps to **Incident management**, because gathering the required information means noticing when a ticket is incomplete or inconsistent — a missing contact detail, a wrong application name, a severity that does not match the description.
- **Prioritisation** maps to your **Ownership and topic** skill. Showing awareness of problem resolution processes includes knowing what needs attention first and when to pass problems on to your team rather than sitting on them.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Problem management** and **User focus** together. Understanding whether a problem is strategic, tactical or operational, and contributing to remedies and preventative measures, means reasoning step by step from evidence — always remembering the user need behind the ticket.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern recognition in ticket references**

Your team's ticketing system creates incident references in a fixed pattern: the letters "INC", then a dash, then the two-digit year, then a dash, then a five-digit number (for example, INC-26-00417). While tidying a spreadsheet, you see these five references:

1. INC-26-00512
2. INC-26-00513
3. INC-2600514
4. INC-26-00515
5. INC-26-00516

Which reference breaks the pattern?

- A) Reference 1
- B) Reference 2
- C) Reference 3
- D) Reference 5

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Work through the pattern piece by piece: "INC", a dash, a two-digit year, another dash, then five digits. References 1, 2, 4 and 5 all follow this exactly. Reference 3 is missing the second dash — it reads "INC-2600514" instead of "INC-26-00514". This is a small detail, but small details matter in your role: a malformed reference can stop a ticket being found later, break links between related records, and slow down an investigation. In the real assessment, check each element of a pattern in order rather than glancing at the whole string, because your eye will happily "auto-correct" a missing character if you let it. Careful, element-by-element checking is exactly the habit you use when gathering the required information for an incident record.

**Question 2 (easy) — Logical deduction from a routing rule**

Your team's routing rule says: "Every incident affecting the payments application must be allocated to the Payments Support channel, unless the incident is flagged as a security issue, in which case it must be allocated to the Security channel." A new incident affects the payments application. Which one of the following must be true?

- A) The incident must be allocated to the Payments Support channel.
- B) The incident must be allocated to the Security channel.
- C) The incident must be allocated to either the Payments Support channel or the Security channel.
- D) The incident should wait until a senior engineer decides.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The rule has two branches. If the incident is not flagged as a security issue, it goes to Payments Support. If it is flagged as a security issue, it goes to Security. The question does not tell you whether the security flag applies, so you cannot be certain of A or B individually — each is only possible, not guaranteed. What you can be certain of is that one of the two branches applies, which is exactly what option C says. Option D invents a step the rule does not contain. Assessments reward this discipline: conclude only what the given information guarantees, nothing more. In your daily work the same discipline keeps allocation accurate — you check the flag first, then route, rather than guessing.

**Question 3 (easy) — Error checking an incident record**

Your team requires every incident record to contain: (i) the affected application name, (ii) the reporting user's contact details, (iii) a severity level from 1 to 4, and (iv) a short description. You are reviewing this draft record before submitting it:

- Application: Case Tracker
- Contact: j.smith@department.gov.uk
- Severity: 5
- Description: User cannot save case notes; error message "save failed" appears each time.

What is wrong with the record?

- A) The application name is missing.
- B) The severity level is outside the allowed range.
- C) The description is too long.
- D) Nothing is wrong.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Check each required field against its rule. The application name is present ("Case Tracker"), so A is wrong. The contact details are present. The description is short, clear, and includes the exact error message — that is a good description, not a fault, so C is wrong. The severity, however, is recorded as 5, and the allowed range is 1 to 4, so the record fails validation. This is precisely the "gathering the required information" part of your **Incident management** skill: before an incident is allocated to a channel, someone must confirm the record is complete and valid. In the assessment, questions like this reward a systematic sweep through every field rather than stopping at the first field that looks fine.

**Question 4 (easy) — Following a runbook sequence**

A runbook for restarting the reporting application lists these steps in order: (1) notify the service desk that a restart is starting; (2) put the application into maintenance mode; (3) restart the application service; (4) run the health check script; (5) take the application out of maintenance mode; (6) notify the service desk that the restart is complete. A colleague performed the steps in this order: 1, 2, 3, 5, 4, 6. What is the risk created by their ordering?

- A) The service desk was never told the restart was happening.
- B) The application was never restarted.
- C) Users could reach the application before the health check confirmed it was working.
- D) There is no risk; the order does not matter.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Compare the performed order with the runbook order step by step. Steps 1, 2 and 3 match. Then the colleague swapped steps 4 and 5: they took the application out of maintenance mode before running the health check. That means real users could reach the application during the window when nobody yet knew whether the restart had worked. If the health check had then failed, users would have been using a broken application. Options A and B are contradicted by the list (steps 1 and 3 were done), and D ignores why the runbook puts verification before release. Runbooks encode your team's hard-won experience — following them in order is a core part of working under direction, and noticing when an order has been changed is a valuable contribution even at associate level.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Pattern recognition in application logs**

You are watching the error log for a document upload application. You notice "timeout" errors at these times: 09:00, 09:30, 10:00, 10:30, 11:00. Each error lasts less than a minute. Which explanation best fits the pattern and would be most useful to mention when you pass this to your team?

- A) The errors are random and there is nothing to report.
- B) The errors occur every 30 minutes on the hour and half hour, which suggests a scheduled task or job may be involved.
- C) The application is completely down.
- D) Users are uploading documents incorrectly.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Look at the spacing: 09:00, 09:30, 10:00, 10:30, 11:00 — a perfectly regular 30-minute interval, aligned to the clock. Regular, clock-aligned errors are a classic signature of something scheduled: a batch job, a backup, a report generation task, or a monitoring probe that briefly overloads the application. Option A ignores an obvious pattern. Option C overstates the evidence — errors lasting under a minute every half hour mean the application is mostly up. Option D invents a cause with no supporting evidence and unfairly blames users. The valuable associate-level behaviour here is exactly what your **Problem management** skill describes: you investigate enough to describe the pattern clearly, then pass the problem to your team with that useful observation attached. "Timeouts every 30 minutes on the hour and half hour" gives an experienced engineer an immediate head start.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Prioritisation of a morning workload**

It is 09:05 on Tuesday. Four items are in front of you:

1. A senior engineer has asked you to gather log files for a live severity 2 incident they are working on right now.
2. Your queue contains six new routine tickets waiting to be registered and allocated.
3. You promised to update a knowledge base article by the end of the week.
4. An automated alert says the test environment (used only by the development team, not by end users) is down.

Which order of attention is most sensible?

- A) 3, 2, 1, 4 — keep your promise first
- B) 1, 2, 4, 3 — support the live incident, then clear the queue, then flag the test environment, then the article
- C) 4, 1, 2, 3 — alerts always come first
- D) 2, 1, 3, 4 — first in, first out

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Weigh urgency and impact together. Item 1 is both urgent and high impact: a live severity 2 incident affects real users now, and a senior engineer is waiting on you — supporting your team's incident work under direction is the core of your role. Item 2 comes next because unregistered tickets may hide something important, and registering and allocating them is your bread-and-butter duty. Item 4 matters, but the test environment affects developers rather than end users, so it can be flagged after the queue — telling your team about it is still worthwhile. Item 3 has a Friday deadline and can safely wait until the urgent work is done. Option A confuses a soft promise with a hard priority; option C treats all alerts as equally urgent regardless of impact; option D uses arrival order instead of importance. Assessments reward reasoning about impact and urgency together, never a single rule applied blindly.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Logical deduction from escalation rules**

Your team's escalation rules state: (i) if a severity 1 incident is not acknowledged within 15 minutes, it must be escalated to the duty manager; (ii) if any incident remains unresolved after 4 hours, it must be reviewed at the next team stand-up. A severity 1 incident was raised at 14:00 and acknowledged at 14:10. It was resolved at 17:30. Which of the following must be true?

- A) The incident was escalated to the duty manager.
- B) The incident must be reviewed at the next team stand-up.
- C) Both A and B.
- D) Neither A nor B.

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Test each rule against the facts. Rule (i): the incident was acknowledged at 14:10, which is 10 minutes after it was raised — within the 15-minute limit — so the escalation condition was never triggered, and A is not required. Rule (ii): the incident was resolved at 17:30, which is 3 hours 30 minutes after 14:00 — under the 4-hour threshold — so the stand-up review condition was never triggered either, and B is not required. Since neither condition fired, the answer is D. The common trap is to feel that a severity 1 incident "must surely" involve escalation or review; but assessments test whether you apply the written rule to the actual numbers, not your general impression. The same precision matters at work: escalating when the rules do not require it wastes senior colleagues' time, while failing to escalate when they do require it delays help.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Error checking a severity mapping table**

Your team uses this severity guide:

| Severity | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Whole service down for all users | Application unreachable for everyone |
| 2 | Major feature down for many users | Document upload failing for all users |
| 3 | Minor feature affected, workaround exists | One report format broken; another format works |
| 4 | Cosmetic issue, no loss of function | Logo displaying at wrong size |

A colleague has drafted four tickets. Which one has been given the wrong severity?

- A) "Application unreachable for everyone" — Severity 1
- B) "Search feature down for all users of the case system" — Severity 2
- C) "Spelling mistake on the help page" — Severity 3
- D) "One export format broken; CSV export still works" — Severity 3

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Check each ticket against the table. A matches the severity 1 example exactly. B describes a major feature down for many users, which fits severity 2. D describes a minor feature affected with a workaround available (CSV still works), which fits severity 3. C, however, is a spelling mistake — a cosmetic issue with no loss of function — which the table maps to severity 4, not 3. Classifying it as severity 3 would make it look more urgent than it is, distorting the queue for the whole team. This is a realistic associate task: severity classification is part of gathering the required information and allocating incidents correctly, and the way to get it right is to match the description to the definitions in your guide, not to your gut feeling about how annoying the issue sounds.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Applied problem solving with monitoring facts**

The web application you support runs on two servers, Server A and Server B, behind a load balancer that shares users between them. Users report that "roughly half the time" pages fail to load, and refreshing sometimes fixes it. Monitoring shows Server A responding normally and Server B not responding at all. What is the most reasonable explanation to note when registering this incident?

- A) The load balancer is broken and must be replaced.
- B) Server B appears to be down, so requests sent to it fail while requests sent to Server A succeed — matching the "roughly half the time" reports.
- C) Users are imagining the problem, because Server A is healthy.
- D) The application code has a bug affecting all servers.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Connect the evidence pieces. The load balancer shares users between two servers. If Server B is down, then approximately half of requests — those routed to Server B — will fail, while the other half succeed on Server A. Refreshing sometimes fixes it because a refresh may be routed to the healthy server. Every reported symptom fits this explanation. Option A blames the load balancer, but the load balancer sharing traffic is exactly what the evidence shows it doing. Option C dismisses users even though monitoring confirms a real fault — remember your **User focus** skill: user reports are evidence, not noise. Option D contradicts the monitoring, which shows Server A responding normally. In your role you would register the incident with this reasoning noted and pass it promptly to your team — a clearly described hypothesis speeds up the fix.

**Question 10 (hard) — Multi-constraint scheduling**

A maintenance task on the HR application needs a two-hour window and must follow these constraints: (i) it must happen between Monday and Friday; (ii) it cannot run during business hours, which are 09:00 to 17:00; (iii) the senior engineer supervising you is only available Monday, Wednesday and Thursday; (iv) the overnight backup runs 22:00 to 00:00 every night, and the task cannot overlap it; (v) the task must finish before 22:00 or start after 00:00, and your team prefers evening slots over early-morning slots. Which slot satisfies all constraints and the team's preference?

- A) Wednesday 17:30 to 19:30
- B) Tuesday 18:00 to 20:00
- C) Thursday 21:00 to 23:00
- D) Monday 10:00 to 12:00

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Apply each constraint as a filter. Constraint (ii) removes D, which sits inside business hours. Constraint (iii) removes B, because the supervising senior engineer is not available on Tuesday — and as a trainee you work under direction, so supervision is a genuine constraint, not a nice-to-have. Constraints (iv) and (v) remove C: a 21:00 start with a two-hour duration runs until 23:00, overlapping the 22:00 to 00:00 backup and breaking the "finish before 22:00" rule. That leaves A: Wednesday 17:30 to 19:30 is outside business hours, on a day the senior engineer is available, finishes well before the backup, and is an evening slot, matching the team's preference. The technique for questions like this is elimination: test every option against every constraint rather than trying to construct the answer in your head. It is quicker and far less error-prone.

**Question 11 (hard) — Classifying a problem's level**

Your team asks you to help sort three problems by level: strategic, tactical, or operational. The problems are: (P1) a single user's saved search keeps disappearing and needs to be restored each time; (P2) the case application's database server is running out of disk space and will need a bigger allocation within the next month; (P3) the case application is built on a software platform whose supplier has announced end of support in three years, so the department will eventually need to replace or re-platform it. Which classification is most reasonable?

- A) P1 strategic, P2 tactical, P3 operational
- B) P1 operational, P2 tactical, P3 strategic
- C) P1 tactical, P2 strategic, P3 operational
- D) P1 operational, P2 strategic, P3 tactical

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Your **Problem management** skill asks you to understand the level of a problem: strategic, tactical or operational. Operational problems are immediate, day-to-day issues affecting current work — P1, a single user's recurring inconvenience handled through routine support, fits that. Tactical problems need planned action over weeks or months and often involve resources or process changes — P2, disk space that must be expanded within a month, is a classic tactical issue: not an emergency today, but it needs a plan soon. Strategic problems affect long-term direction and major decisions — P3, replacing an entire platform because of supplier end of support in three years, will involve budgets, procurement and senior decision makers, which makes it strategic. Options A, C and D each misplace at least one problem. Getting the level right matters because it determines who needs to know and how quickly — an associate who can say "I think this one is bigger than day-to-day" and pass it upward is genuinely helping the team.

**Question 12 (hard) — Combining conditions across a runbook and an alert**

The runbook for the licensing application says: "If the nightly data load fails, re-run it before 08:00. If the re-run also fails, raise a problem record and do not re-run again. If the data load succeeds but takes longer than 90 minutes, add a note to the weekly performance log." This morning you find: the nightly load failed at 02:00; the automatic re-run started at 03:00 and completed successfully at 04:45. What should be done?

- A) Raise a problem record and stop.
- B) Re-run the load a third time to be safe.
- C) Nothing — the re-run succeeded, so no further action is needed.
- D) Add a note to the weekly performance log, because the successful re-run took longer than 90 minutes.

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Walk through the conditions in order. The nightly load failed, so a re-run was required before 08:00 — that happened, starting at 03:00. Did the re-run also fail? No: it completed successfully at 04:45, so the "raise a problem record" branch does not apply, ruling out A. Option B is explicitly against the runbook's spirit and unnecessary after a success. Now the subtle part: the runbook says a successful load that takes longer than 90 minutes must be noted in the performance log. The re-run started at 03:00 and finished at 04:45 — that is 1 hour 45 minutes, or 105 minutes, which is longer than 90 minutes. So the correct action is D. Option C misses this final condition. Assessments include questions like this because real runbooks have exactly these layered conditions, and the engineers who add the most value are the ones who read all the way to the end and apply every applicable rule, not just the first one.

### Preparation tips

- **Practise on your real materials.** With permission, spend ten minutes a day reading closed incident tickets, runbooks, and severity guides in your team's systems. Ask yourself: does this record have all the required fields? Does this severity match the definitions? That is free cognitive practice using the same artefacts assessments imitate.
- **Slow down to speed up.** For error-checking questions, sweep every field or element in order rather than skimming. A methodical pass takes a few seconds longer but avoids the re-reads that really cost you time.
- **Learn your team's rules as written.** Deduction questions punish "what I feel the rule probably means". Read your escalation and routing rules once carefully and notice how conditions like "unless" and "within 15 minutes" change the outcome.
- **Use elimination on hard questions.** For scheduling and multi-constraint questions, test each option against each constraint and cross out failures. It is the fastest reliable method.
- **Time yourself gently.** Try answering three practice questions in five minutes. Build speed gradually — accuracy first, then pace.
- **Rest before the real thing.** Cognitive performance drops when you are tired. A good night's sleep is worth more than an extra hour of cramming.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Adding assumptions to rules.** If a rule does not mention something, the assessment does not want you to invent it. Conclude only what the given facts guarantee.
- **Stopping at the first plausible answer.** Options are often written so an early option looks fine until you check the later ones. Always read all four options.
- **Letting severity feel emotional.** A spelling mistake can feel embarrassing and a timeout can feel scary, but classification follows definitions, not feelings. Match descriptions to your severity guide word by word.
- **Ignoring units and durations.** "1 hour 45 minutes" is 105 minutes. Convert before comparing against thresholds like "90 minutes".
- **Panicking when questions get harder.** On adaptive tests, harder questions often mean you are doing well. Keep your rhythm.
- **Rushing the easy questions.** Easy questions are where careless marks are lost. Bank them with a calm, careful read.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures how confidently and accurately you work with the numbers your job actually produces. For an application operations team, that means ticket volumes, resolution counts, service level agreement (SLA) percentages, response times, uptime figures, and simple trends over days, weeks and months. You will be asked to read small tables and charts, calculate percentages, averages, ratios and rates, and interpret what the numbers mean for the service.

The typical format is an online, timed test lasting 20 to 30 minutes, with 15 to 20 questions. Each question presents some data — often a table — and four answer options. Calculators are usually allowed, and rough paper is your friend. As with cognitive tests, many platforms adapt difficulty to your performance, and scores compare you with a norm group of similar test takers. Speed matters, but accuracy matters more: a steady, careful pace with correct working beats a rushed pace with slips.

Why do employers use numeric assessments for this role? Because operations runs on numbers. Your team reports how many incidents arrived, how many were resolved within SLA, how long the application was available, and whether things are getting better or worse. Even at associate level, you will read these figures in dashboards and stand-ups, and you may help gather them. Someone who can spot that "94% of 50 tickets" and "94% of 500 tickets" are very different situations, or that a rising trend in timeouts deserves a mention, is genuinely useful from day one.

A comforting note: the arithmetic in these assessments is deliberately manageable — percentages, averages, ratios, and differences. Nothing beyond what you learned at school. The skill being tested is applying that arithmetic carefully to workplace data, under mild time pressure, without mixing up what the numbers refer to. Every question below uses data of the kind your team genuinely handles, and every explanation shows the arithmetic step by step.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Percentages and SLA figures** map to your **Incident management** skill. Incident work is measured with percentages: the share of incidents acknowledged on time, resolved within target, or reopened. Registering incidents accurately is what makes those percentages trustworthy in the first place.
- **Averages and rates** map to your **Problem management** skill. Investigating problems often starts with numbers: the average number of errors per day, the rate of failures per week, and whether a remedy actually reduced the numbers afterwards. Contributing to remedies and preventative measures includes helping to check the "before and after" figures.
- **Reading tables and trends** maps to your **Ownership and topic** skill. Showing awareness of problem resolution processes includes reading the team's dashboards, noticing when a number moves in the wrong direction, and passing that observation to your team promptly.
- **Interpreting user-related data** maps to your **User focus** skill. Numbers about complaints, satisfaction scores, and usage tell you about user experience. Understanding what a percentage of affected users really means keeps the user need at the centre of your work.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Percentage of tickets resolved**

On Monday your team received 40 incident tickets and resolved 30 of them the same day. What percentage of Monday's tickets were resolved the same day?

- A) 30%
- B) 70%
- C) 75%
- D) 80%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A percentage is the part divided by the whole, multiplied by 100. Step 1: identify the part (30 resolved) and the whole (40 received). Step 2: divide: 30 ÷ 40 = 0.75. Step 3: multiply by 100: 0.75 × 100 = 75%. So 75% of Monday's tickets were resolved the same day. A common slip is to answer 30% by confusing the count of resolved tickets (30) with a percentage — always ask "out of how many?" before writing an answer. This is exactly the kind of figure your team reports at stand-ups, and understanding how it is calculated helps you spot when a dashboard number looks wrong.

**Question 2 (easy) — Average tickets per day**

Over five days, your team logged the following numbers of new incidents: Monday 22, Tuesday 18, Wednesday 25, Thursday 20, Friday 15. What was the average (mean) number of new incidents per day?

- A) 18
- B) 20
- C) 22
- D) 25

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The mean is the total divided by the number of days. Step 1: add the daily counts: 22 + 18 = 40; 40 + 25 = 65; 65 + 20 = 85; 85 + 15 = 100. Step 2: divide by the number of days: 100 ÷ 5 = 20. The average is 20 incidents per day. A quick sense-check: the average should sit between the smallest value (15) and the largest (25), and 20 does. Sense-checking like this takes two seconds and catches most arithmetic slips. Teams use daily averages to plan cover — if your team knows it averages 20 new incidents a day, it can spot an unusual day (like a 35-ticket day) immediately.

**Question 3 (easy) — Reading an SLA table**

Your team's SLA target is to acknowledge 95% of incidents within 15 minutes. This month's report shows:

| Week | Incidents | Acknowledged within 15 min |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 60 | 57 |
| 2 | 80 | 76 |

In which week (or weeks) did the team meet the 95% target?

- A) Week 1 only
- B) Week 2 only
- C) Both weeks
- D) Neither week

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Calculate each week's percentage separately. Week 1: 57 ÷ 60 = 0.95, so 0.95 × 100 = 95% — exactly on target, and "95% within 15 minutes" is met when the figure is 95% or better. Week 2: 76 ÷ 80 = 0.95, so also exactly 95%. Both weeks meet the target, so the answer is C. Notice the useful shortcut: 95% of 60 is 57 (because 5% of 60 is 3, and 60 − 3 = 57), and 95% of 80 is 76 (5% of 80 is 4, and 80 − 4 = 76). Working out "5% and subtract" is often faster than long division. Being comfortable with the team's SLA arithmetic means you can read the weekly report with understanding rather than just nodding along.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Percentage change in ticket volume**

The reporting application generated 150 incident tickets in March and 180 in April. What was the percentage increase from March to April?

- A) 16.7%
- B) 20%
- C) 30%
- D) 83.3%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Percentage change is the difference divided by the original value, times 100. Step 1: find the difference: 180 − 150 = 30. Step 2: divide by the original (March) figure, not the new one: 30 ÷ 150 = 0.2. Step 3: multiply by 100: 20%. The classic trap is dividing by the new value: 30 ÷ 180 = 0.167, giving 16.7% — that is option A, placed there deliberately. Always divide by the starting value for a change "from" that value. Option C is the raw difference (30) dressed up as a percentage, and D is 150 ÷ 180. A 20% month-on-month rise in tickets for one application is exactly the kind of trend your team would want flagged — it may indicate a problem worth investigating, connecting this arithmetic directly to your problem management work.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Ratio of incidents across applications**

Your team supports three applications. Last month's incident counts were: Case Tracker 90, Document Store 60, Reporting 30. What is the simplest ratio of incidents across Case Tracker, Document Store and Reporting?

- A) 9 : 6 : 3
- B) 3 : 2 : 1
- C) 90 : 60 : 30
- D) 1 : 2 : 3

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A ratio is simplified by dividing every part by the same largest possible number. Step 1: write the raw ratio: 90 : 60 : 30. Step 2: find the highest common factor of 90, 60 and 30, which is 30. Step 3: divide each part by 30: 90 ÷ 30 = 3; 60 ÷ 30 = 2; 30 ÷ 30 = 1. The simplest ratio is 3 : 2 : 1. Options A and C are correct ratios but not fully simplified — option A only divided by 10 — and the question asks for the simplest form. Option D reverses the order, which would wrongly suggest Reporting has the most incidents. Ratios like this help teams see workload balance at a glance: for every one Reporting incident, Case Tracker generates three, which might influence where preventative effort goes.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Converting uptime percentage to downtime**

The case application has an availability target of 99% over a 30-day month. Assuming the application is expected to be available 24 hours a day, how many hours of downtime would exactly meet (but not exceed) the 99% target?

- A) 3 hours
- B) 7.2 hours
- C) 30 hours
- D) 0.72 hours

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Work from the total hours in the month. Step 1: total hours = 30 days × 24 hours = 720 hours. Step 2: 99% availability means 1% downtime is allowed. Step 3: 1% of 720 = 720 ÷ 100 = 7.2 hours. So up to 7.2 hours of downtime in the month still meets a 99% target. Option D slips a decimal place (0.1% rather than 1%); option C confuses 30 days with 30 hours; option A is a guess with no basis in the calculation. Understanding this conversion changes how you read availability numbers: "99%" sounds almost perfect, yet it allows more than seven hours of outage a month — which is why teams supporting important public services often aim for 99.5% or higher, and why every incident's duration matters.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Average resolution time with different group sizes**

Last week your team resolved 20 severity 3 incidents with an average resolution time of 4 hours, and 5 severity 2 incidents with an average resolution time of 10 hours. What was the overall average resolution time across all 25 incidents?

- A) 5.2 hours
- B) 7 hours
- C) 6 hours
- D) 4.8 hours

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** You cannot simply average 4 and 10 to get 7, because the groups are different sizes — that is option B, the classic trap. Instead, weight by group size. Step 1: total time for severity 3 incidents: 20 × 4 = 80 hours. Step 2: total time for severity 2 incidents: 5 × 10 = 50 hours. Step 3: combined total time: 80 + 50 = 130 hours. Step 4: divide by the total number of incidents: 130 ÷ 25 = 5.2 hours. The overall average is 5.2 hours. Sense-check: because there are far more 4-hour incidents than 10-hour ones, the answer should sit closer to 4 than to 10 — and 5.2 does. Weighted averages appear constantly in operations reporting, and recognising when a simple average is wrong is a mark of numerical maturity.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Reading a combined table**

This table shows one month of incidents for the applications your team supports:

| Application | Severity 1–2 | Severity 3–4 | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Case Tracker | 12 | 48 | 60 |
| Document Store | 6 | 34 | 40 |
| Reporting | 2 | 18 | 20 |
| **Total** | **20** | **100** | **120** |

What percentage of all incidents were severity 1–2 incidents on Case Tracker?

- A) 20%
- B) 12%
- C) 10%
- D) 60%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Read the question carefully: it asks for Case Tracker severity 1–2 incidents as a percentage of all incidents. Step 1: find the right cell: Case Tracker severity 1–2 = 12. Step 2: find the right whole: all incidents = 120. Step 3: divide: 12 ÷ 120 = 0.1. Step 4: multiply by 100: 10%. Option A (20%) is what you get if you divide by Case Tracker's own total (12 ÷ 60) — a different question ("what share of Case Tracker's incidents were severe?"). Option B just repeats the raw count. Option D repeats Case Tracker's total. Table questions are mostly about choosing the correct numerator and denominator; say the question to yourself as "12 out of which total?" before you divide. Reading operational tables accurately is a daily skill — dashboards are full of subtotals waiting to be confused with totals.

**Question 9 (hard) — Backlog burn-down rate**

Your team's ticket backlog stands at 84 open tickets. Each working day the team resolves an average of 15 tickets, but an average of 9 new tickets arrive. If these rates continue, after how many working days will the backlog first reach zero?

- A) 6 days
- B) 14 days
- C) 10 days
- D) 9 days

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The backlog changes by the net rate, not the resolution rate alone. Step 1: net reduction per day = tickets resolved − tickets arriving = 15 − 9 = 6 tickets per day. Step 2: days needed = backlog ÷ net rate = 84 ÷ 6 = 14 days. So the backlog first reaches zero after 14 working days. Option A comes from ignoring arrivals (84 ÷ 15 = 5.6, rounded to 6) — the most tempting error, because 15 is the number that describes the team's effort. Options C and D have no valid derivation. The lesson transfers directly to operations planning: a team that "resolves 15 a day" is not clearing 15 a day if 9 keep arriving. When you hear your team discuss backlog forecasts, this net-rate thinking is what is happening underneath.

**Question 10 (hard) — Percentage points versus percentages**

In June, 88% of incidents were resolved within SLA. In July, the figure was 92%. Which statement is accurate?

- A) Performance improved by 4%.
- B) Performance improved by 4 percentage points, which is an increase of about 4.5% on June's figure.
- C) Performance improved by 4 percentage points, which is the same as a 4% increase.
- D) Performance worsened, because more incidents were resolved late in July.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This distinction trips up experienced managers, so mastering it early is a quiet superpower. The difference between 88% and 92% is 4 percentage points — you subtract: 92 − 88 = 4. But the relative increase is calculated against the starting figure: 4 ÷ 88 = 0.04545…, which is about 4.5%. So the correct statement is B: an improvement of 4 percentage points, equivalent to roughly a 4.5% relative increase. Option A is loose language that assessments (and careful analysts) mark as wrong; option C explicitly claims the two are the same, which they are not; option D contradicts the data. Note also that the question gives no information about total incident numbers, so nothing can be concluded about the count of late incidents — only the proportions. Precise language about percentages keeps your team's reporting honest.

**Question 11 (hard) — Forecasting from a trend**

Timeout errors on the document application have been rising steadily: week 1: 40 errors; week 2: 48; week 3: 56; week 4: 64. If the trend continues in the same pattern, how many timeout errors would you expect in week 6, and what is the most useful action for you to take now?

- A) 72 errors; wait until the number exceeds 100 before mentioning it.
- B) 80 errors; describe the trend and pass the problem to your team now.
- C) 80 errors; fix the application yourself immediately.
- D) 128 errors; raise a severity 1 incident.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** First the arithmetic. The weekly increase is constant: 48 − 40 = 8; 56 − 48 = 8; 64 − 56 = 8. This is a linear trend adding 8 errors per week. Step 1: week 5 = 64 + 8 = 72. Step 2: week 6 = 72 + 8 = 80. Option D assumes doubling (a very different, exponential pattern) with no evidence. Now the judgement part. A steady four-week rise is a problem signal, and your **Problem management** skill says you investigate enough to describe it, then contribute by passing it to your team — not sitting on it until an arbitrary threshold (option A), not attempting a solo fix beyond your current level and direction (option C), and not declaring a severity 1 emergency when the service is still functioning (option D). The best answer combines correct extrapolation with proportionate action: describe the pattern — "rising by 8 per week for four weeks" — and hand it over promptly.

**Question 12 (hard) — Multi-step SLA calculation**

Your team's monthly report needs one combined figure: the percentage of all incidents resolved within SLA across two applications. Case Tracker had 60 incidents, of which 54 were resolved within SLA. Document Store had 40 incidents, of which 30 were resolved within SLA. A colleague suggests averaging the two percentages. What is the correct combined figure, and how does it compare with the colleague's method?

- A) 84%, which is higher than the average-of-percentages method gives.
- B) 82.5%, and both methods agree.
- C) 84%, and both methods agree.
- D) 82.5%, which is lower than the average-of-percentages method gives.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Work both methods to see the difference. Correct method — combine the raw numbers first. Step 1: total resolved within SLA = 54 + 30 = 84. Step 2: total incidents = 60 + 40 = 100. Step 3: combined percentage = 84 ÷ 100 = 84%. Colleague's method — average the percentages: Case Tracker: 54 ÷ 60 = 90%; Document Store: 30 ÷ 40 = 75%; average = (90 + 75) ÷ 2 = 82.5%. The two disagree because the applications had different numbers of incidents: the simple average treats a 40-incident application as equal in weight to a 60-incident one. The true combined figure is 84%, which is higher than the colleague's 82.5% — option A. Because the total happened to be 100 incidents, the arithmetic is friendly, but the principle holds for any totals: combine counts, then divide. Knowing this lets you politely query a report figure — a small contribution that protects the accuracy your whole team is judged on.

### Preparation tips

- **Rebuild your percentage basics.** Practise three moves until they are automatic: part ÷ whole × 100; finding 5% or 10% of a number quickly; and percentage change = difference ÷ original × 100. Almost every operations number uses one of these.
- **Say the denominator out loud.** Before dividing, ask "out of what?" Most table-reading errors are denominator errors — using a subtotal when the question wants the total, or vice versa.
- **Sense-check every answer.** Averages must sit between the smallest and largest values. Weighted averages sit closer to the bigger group. Percentages of a part cannot exceed 100. Two seconds of checking catches most slips.
- **Practise with your team's real reports.** Ask a colleague to show you the monthly service report and try recalculating one or two figures yourself. It builds both numeracy and job knowledge at once.
- **Use rough paper and a calculator methodically.** Write each step; do not chain calculations in your head under time pressure.
- **Learn the percentage-points distinction.** It appears in assessments and in real dashboards, and getting it right marks you out as careful.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Dividing by the wrong base.** Percentage change divides by the original value. "From 150 to 180" is 30 ÷ 150, never 30 ÷ 180.
- **Averaging percentages from different-sized groups.** Combine the raw counts first, then calculate one percentage.
- **Ignoring net rates.** A backlog falls by (resolved − arrived) per day, not by the resolution rate alone.
- **Misreading tables under pressure.** Row and column headers matter more than the numbers. Put your finger (or cursor) on the exact cell before you use it.
- **Confusing hours, minutes and days.** Convert everything to one unit before comparing. 1 hour 45 minutes is 105 minutes; a 30-day month is 720 hours.
- **Spending too long on one question.** If a question stalls you, make your best elimination-based choice, flag it if the platform allows, and move on. One hard question is never worth three easy ones.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately you read, understand, and draw conclusions from written material of the kind your job produces: incident management procedures, service emails, knowledge base articles, policy extracts, and user stories. The most common format presents a short passage followed by statements you must judge as **True** (the passage supports it), **False** (the passage contradicts it), or **Cannot say** (the passage gives too little information to decide either way). Other question types ask you to pick the best summary of a passage, identify what can properly be inferred, or choose the clearest piece of writing.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 15 to 25 minutes with 20 to 30 statements spread across several passages. The golden rule is that you must answer using only the passage — not your own knowledge or experience. Even if you know from your own team that something works differently, the question is about what this passage says. That rule feels strange at first and becomes comfortable quickly with practice.

Why do employers use verbal reasoning assessments for application operations roles? Because your work runs on precise reading. You follow runbooks where one word — "before", "unless", "all" — changes what you should do. You read incident descriptions and must separate what the user actually reported from what you assume. You read handover notes and must notice what has been checked and what has not. And your **User focus** skill depends on reading user stories and understanding exactly what need they express. Misreading costs time and sometimes causes new incidents; careful reading prevents them.

At associate level, the passages are short and practical, and the skill being tested is care rather than vocabulary. The questions below use the documents you genuinely meet: a procedure extract, a service desk email, a knowledge base article, and a user story. Take each passage slowly, and treat every word as deliberate.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Comprehension of procedures** maps to your **Incident management** skill. Identifying and registering incidents, gathering the required information, and allocating to the appropriate channel all follow written procedures. The assessment's true/false/cannot-say discipline mirrors the discipline of following a procedure exactly as written.
- **Inference and restraint** map to your **Problem management** skill. When investigating problems, you must distinguish what the evidence states from what you are tempted to conclude. "Cannot say" is the verbal reasoning version of "we need more information before we decide" — a phrase every good problem investigator uses.
- **Reading user language** maps to your **User focus** skill. Understanding the purpose of user stories and the focus on user needs starts with reading them accurately: who is the user, what do they want, and why?
- **Clear-writing judgement** maps to your **Ownership and topic** skill. When you pass problems on to your team, the handover is written words. Choosing clear, complete, unambiguous wording is what makes a handover useful rather than confusing.

### Practice questions

**Passage 1 — Incident logging procedure extract**

*"All incidents reported by telephone must be logged in the ticketing system before the end of the call, except when the system itself is unavailable, in which case the incident must be recorded on the paper form and entered into the system within four hours of the system becoming available again. Incidents reported by email are logged automatically. The engineer who logs an incident is responsible for confirming the reporting user's contact details. Severity is assigned at the point of logging and may be changed later only by the duty manager."*

**Question 1 (easy) — True, false, or cannot say**

Statement: "Incidents reported by email must be logged manually before the end of the call."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, but only when the system is unavailable

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Find the sentence about email: "Incidents reported by email are logged automatically." The statement claims email incidents must be logged manually, which directly contradicts the passage — and the phrase "before the end of the call" belongs to the telephone rule, not the email rule, so the statement mixes up two different parts of the procedure. When a statement contradicts the passage, the answer is False, not Cannot say. A useful technique: locate the exact sentence in the passage that deals with the statement's topic, and compare them word by word. Do not let the familiar rhythm of the telephone rule pull you into applying it to email.

**Question 2 (easy) — True, false, or cannot say**

Statement: "If the ticketing system is unavailable during a telephone report, the incident must be recorded on the paper form."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) False, because incidents can wait until the system returns

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The passage says telephone incidents must be logged before the end of the call, "except when the system itself is unavailable, in which case the incident must be recorded on the paper form". The statement restates this exception accurately, so it is True. Option D describes what happens after the paper form — entry into the system within four hours of availability returning — but the paper form itself is still required first, so D misreads the sequence. Notice how the word "must" appears in both passage and statement: the strength of the wording matches, which is one of the things to check. If the passage had said "may be recorded on the paper form", a statement saying "must" would not be supported.

**Question 3 (easy) — True, false, or cannot say**

Statement: "The duty manager assigns the initial severity of each incident."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True for telephone incidents only

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage says: "Severity is assigned at the point of logging and may be changed later only by the duty manager." So the initial severity is assigned when the incident is logged — by whoever logs it — and the duty manager's special role is changing severity later, not assigning it initially. The statement swaps those two responsibilities, contradicting the passage, so it is False. This is a classic verbal reasoning pattern: the statement reuses the passage's own words ("duty manager", "severity", "assigned") but rearranges the relationship between them. Reading for relationships — who does what, when — protects you both in tests and in real procedures, where assuming the wrong person owns a step can leave the step undone.

**Question 4 (moderate) — True, false, or cannot say**

Statement: "Most incidents are reported by telephone."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) False, because email logging is automatic

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Search the passage for any information about how many incidents arrive by each route. There is none: the passage describes the procedure for telephone reports and the procedure for email reports, but it never compares their volumes. Without volume information, you cannot judge "most" either way, so the answer is Cannot say. Option D is tempting because it sounds analytical, but automatic logging tells you nothing about how many emails arrive. This is the restraint skill: the passage's silence is an answer in itself. In your role the same restraint sounds like: "The procedure covers both routes, but I do not have data on which is more common" — a much stronger position than guessing.

**Passage 2 — Email from the service desk**

*"Hi team — heads-up for this afternoon. The supplier will apply a security patch to the document store between 14:00 and 15:00. Users may experience slower document opening during this window, but uploads should not be affected. Please do not restart the document store service during the window, even if monitoring shows raised response times, because a restart during patching can corrupt the update. If any user reports being completely unable to open documents, treat it as severity 2 and phone the supplier's hotline rather than using the online portal. Normal service is expected from 15:00. — Service Desk"*

**Question 5 (moderate) — True, false, or cannot say**

Statement: "During the patching window, raised response times on monitoring are expected and are not, by themselves, a reason to restart the service."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, but only after 15:00

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Two parts of the email support this. First: "Users may experience slower document opening during this window" — so slower responses are anticipated. Second: "Please do not restart the document store service during the window, even if monitoring shows raised response times" — so raised response times are explicitly not a reason to restart. The statement combines both accurately, so it is True. Option D inverts the timing: the restriction applies during the window, not after it. Emails like this are genuinely how operations teams communicate temporary rule changes, and the assessment skill — extracting exactly what is expected, what is forbidden, and when — is the job skill. Notice especially the phrase "even if": it exists precisely to stop a well-meaning engineer doing the normally-sensible thing at the wrong time.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Inference from the email**

Which one of the following can properly be inferred from the email?

- A) The supplier's online portal is broken this afternoon.
- B) Complete inability to open documents during the window is being treated as more serious than slowness.
- C) Uploads will definitely work normally throughout the window.
- D) The patch will finish exactly at 15:00.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Test each option against the text. A: the email prefers the hotline "rather than using the online portal", but gives no reason — the portal may simply be slower for urgent issues; "broken" is not supported. C: the email says uploads "should not be affected" — "should" expresses expectation, not certainty, so "definitely" overreaches. D: "Normal service is expected from 15:00" — again expectation, not an exact guarantee. B: slowness is described as something users "may experience" with no special action, while complete inability to open documents gets a severity 2 classification and an urgent phone route — a clearly more serious treatment. B is the only inference the wording genuinely supports. The general technique: hedged words ("should", "expected", "may") never support absolute claims ("definitely", "exactly"), and differences in prescribed action reveal differences in seriousness.

**Question 7 (moderate) — True, false, or cannot say**

Statement: "If a user reports being completely unable to open documents at 14:30, the correct action is to log it as severity 2 and contact the supplier through the online portal."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, because the portal is the standard contact route

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The statement gets the severity right but the contact route wrong. The email says: "treat it as severity 2 and phone the supplier's hotline rather than using the online portal." Because the statement's second half contradicts the email, the whole statement is False — in true/false/cannot-say questions, a statement must be fully supported to be True; one wrong element makes it False when that element is contradicted. Option D appeals to what might be standard practice normally, but the passage explicitly overrides it for this situation. This mirrors a real operational trap: half-remembering an instruction ("severity 2, contact the supplier") and defaulting to the usual channel. When instructions change temporarily, the details are the instruction.

**Passage 3 — Knowledge base article and user story**

*"Article KB-2114: Clearing a stuck print queue in the case application. Symptoms: documents sent to print remain in 'queued' status for more than ten minutes and no error is shown. Resolution: first, confirm with the user that they are printing to the office printer rather than the PDF printer, because PDF jobs are processed by a different service and this article does not apply to them. Second, ask the user to log out and back in; this clears the queue in most cases. If the queue remains stuck, escalate to second-line support with the user's login name and the time of the first stuck job. Do not delete jobs from the queue manually, as manual deletion can leave orphaned records that cause the same user further printing failures later."*

*"Related user story: As a caseworker, I need my documents to print promptly, so that I can include them in case files that are collected for court each afternoon."*

**Question 8 (moderate) — Comprehension of conditions**

According to the article, when does the article's resolution NOT apply?

- A) When the user has waited fewer than ten minutes
- B) When the user is printing to the PDF printer
- C) Both A and B describe situations outside the article's scope
- D) When the user has already logged out and back in

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Check the article's scope conditions. The symptom definition says jobs remain queued "for more than ten minutes" — so a shorter wait does not yet match the symptoms, and the article's resolution is not yet triggered (option A is out of scope). The first resolution step says PDF jobs "are processed by a different service and this article does not apply to them" (option B is explicitly excluded). Both A and B therefore describe situations the article does not cover, making C correct. Option D is wrong because a user who has logged out and back in without success is exactly who the article's escalation step is for — still within scope. Scope-checking is a real support skill: applying the right knowledge base article to the wrong situation wastes the user's time and can make things worse.

**Question 9 (hard) — True, false, or cannot say**

Statement: "Manually deleting stuck jobs resolves the immediate problem but is forbidden because it can cause repeat failures for the same user."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) False, because manual deletion never clears a queue

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** This one requires very careful reading. The article says: "Do not delete jobs from the queue manually, as manual deletion can leave orphaned records that cause the same user further printing failures later." The prohibition is real, and the reason — possible repeat failures — is real. But the statement also claims manual deletion "resolves the immediate problem", and the article never says that: it neither confirms nor denies that manual deletion would clear the queue in the short term. Because part of the statement is unsupported (rather than contradicted), and the rest is supported, the statement as a whole cannot be confirmed — so Cannot say. Option D makes the opposite error, asserting "never clears", which the article also does not say. The discipline here is surgical: separate each claim inside a statement and check each against the text. Statements that bundle a supported claim with an unsupported one are the hardest and most common trap at this level.

**Question 10 (hard) — Understanding the user story**

The user story reads: "As a caseworker, I need my documents to print promptly, so that I can include them in case files that are collected for court each afternoon." Which option best explains why the "so that" part matters to your team's handling of print incidents?

- A) It identifies the printer model the caseworker uses.
- B) It shows the user need has a hard daily deadline, which helps the team understand the real urgency of print incidents for caseworkers.
- C) It proves all print incidents should be severity 1.
- D) It is decorative and can be ignored, because the technical symptom is the same either way.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A user story has three parts: who (a caseworker), what (documents printing promptly), and why (case files collected for court each afternoon). The "so that" clause expresses the underlying need — and here it reveals a hard, externally imposed daily deadline: court collection each afternoon. That context transforms how a support team should think about a "printer is slow" ticket at 15:30 versus 09:00. Option A is simply not in the story. Option C overcorrects: context informs urgency, it does not automatically maximise severity — classification still follows your severity guide. Option D is the mindset your **User focus** skill exists to prevent: the technical symptom may be identical, but the user impact is not, and understanding the purpose of user stories means using the "so that" to see the need behind the ticket. This question rehearses exactly what "explain the purpose of user stories and the focus on user needs" looks like in practice.

**Question 11 (hard) — Choosing the clearest handover**

You are ending your shift and must hand over an unresolved issue. Which handover note is clearest and most useful for the next engineer?

- A) "Printing thing still broken for that user from earlier, tried the usual, might need escalating maybe."
- B) "Print queue issue ongoing. See ticket."
- C) "INC-26-00891: caseworker j.patel reports print jobs stuck in 'queued' since 13:40. Confirmed office printer (not PDF). Log out/in tried at 14:05 — queue still stuck. Per KB-2114, needs escalation to second-line with login name and first stuck job time (13:40). Not yet escalated."
- D) "Escalated j.patel's printing to second line, all sorted, nothing to do."

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A good handover answers: which ticket, which user, what symptoms, what has been tried, what happened, what remains to be done. Option C covers all six: the reference, the user, the symptom and its start time, the scope check from the knowledge base article (office printer, not PDF), the attempted fix and its result, the next required step, and — crucially — the honest status "Not yet escalated". Option A is vague on every element ("that user", "the usual", "maybe"). Option B forces the next engineer to reconstruct everything from the ticket, wasting handover's whole purpose. Option D is worse than vague: it claims an action was taken when the scenario says the issue is unresolved — an inaccurate handover destroys trust and can leave a caseworker without documents for the afternoon court collection. Clear-writing questions reward completeness, specificity, and honesty; so does your team.

**Question 12 (hard) — Best summary of a passage**

Re-read Passage 1 (the incident logging procedure). Which option best summarises it?

- A) All incidents must be logged on paper forms and entered into the system within four hours.
- B) Telephone incidents are logged in the system during the call (or on paper if the system is down, then entered within four hours of its return); email incidents log automatically; the logging engineer confirms contact details; severity is set at logging and only the duty manager may change it later.
- C) The duty manager logs all incidents and assigns all severities.
- D) Incidents may be logged whenever convenient, provided contact details are confirmed.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A strong summary preserves every rule, keeps the conditions attached to the right rules, and adds nothing. Walk through option B against the passage: telephone logging before the end of the call — present; the system-unavailable exception with the paper form and four-hour entry rule — present and attached to the right situation; automatic email logging — present; contact detail confirmation by the logging engineer — present; severity at logging with duty-manager-only changes — present. Nothing added, nothing lost. Option A promotes the exception (paper forms) into the general rule. Option C misassigns both logging and severity to the duty manager. Option D contradicts the timing requirements entirely. Summary questions are really compression tests: can you shrink a passage without breaking any of its conditional links? That is also the skill of summarising an incident accurately when you register it — compressing a user's five-minute phone call into a record that loses nothing that matters.

### Preparation tips

- **Use only the passage.** The single most important habit. Your own team's procedures may differ from the passage — answer from the text in front of you, every time.
- **Locate before you judge.** For each statement, find the exact sentence(s) in the passage that address its topic, then compare wording. Never answer from memory of the passage.
- **Respect hedge words.** "Should", "may", "expected", "usually" express possibility or expectation. They never support statements containing "definitely", "always", "never", or "must" — and vice versa.
- **Split bundled statements.** When a statement makes two claims, check each separately. Supported + contradicted = False. Supported + silent = Cannot say.
- **Practise on real documents.** Read one knowledge base article or procedure a day and ask yourself: what exactly is required, permitted, and forbidden — and under which conditions?
- **Read the question stem carefully.** "Which can be inferred?" is different from "Which is stated?" — inference allows one careful step beyond the text; stating allows none.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Using job knowledge instead of the passage.** You may know a better procedure; the test is about this passage. Answer accordingly.
- **Treating "Cannot say" as a weak answer.** It is a precise answer meaning "the passage gives no basis to decide". Confident use of Cannot say is a sign of strong verbal reasoning, not indecision.
- **Confusing False with Cannot say.** False requires contradiction by the passage. Silence is Cannot say, not False.
- **Letting familiar phrasing lull you.** Statements often reuse the passage's words with the relationships rearranged — who does what, when, to what. Check relationships, not just vocabulary.
- **Skimming exception clauses.** Words like "except", "unless", "only", and "even if" carry the meaning of operational documents. Slow down whenever you meet one.
- **Rushing long passages.** One careful read plus targeted re-reading beats three anxious skims. Budget your time per passage, not per statement.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment (often called an SJT) presents realistic workplace scenarios and asks you to judge possible responses. Unlike the other assessments in this guide, there is no arithmetic and no passage analysis — the material is human: a confused user, a busy colleague, a mistake, a deadline, an awkward moment. Typical question formats include choosing the most effective response, choosing the least effective response, ranking all responses from best to worst, or rating each response on a scale.

The typical format is an online, untimed or generously timed test of 10 to 20 scenarios, each with four possible responses. There is usually no single "trick" — the responses are all things a real person might do, and your task is to judge which serves the situation best. Scoring compares your judgements with those of experienced professionals and with the behaviours the organisation values: user focus, collaboration, honesty, taking appropriate ownership, escalating at the right moment, and treating everyone with respect.

Why do employers use SJTs for associate-level operations roles? Because at your level, judgement about when to act and when to ask matters more than deep technical knowledge. You work under direction in an established team. The most valuable associate behaviours are: communicating early, being honest about mistakes and uncertainty, keeping the user's experience in view, following process while staying alert, and passing problems on rather than letting them silently grow. None of these require years of experience — they require good instincts, and instincts improve with reflection and practice.

A reassuring note: SJTs are not personality tests trying to catch you out, and they are not asking what a heroic expert would do. They ask what a sensible, honest, user-focused trainee should do. Usually the strongest answers share a shape: they deal with the immediate risk, are honest, involve the right people, and protect the user. The weakest answers usually hide something, guess beyond the person's level, ignore the user, or dump a problem on someone else without information. Keep that shape in mind as you practise.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Escalation and asking for help** map to your **Ownership and topic** skill: showing awareness of problem resolution processes and passing problems on to your team is literally the behaviour many SJT scenarios test. Good associates neither sit on problems nor throw them over the wall — they pass them on with useful information.
- **Careful process behaviour** maps to your **Incident management** skill: scenarios about logging, categorising, and routing test whether you follow the process even when you are busy, and whether you gather the required information before allocating.
- **Investigation instincts** map to your **Problem management** skill: scenarios about recurring issues test whether you notice patterns, contribute observations, and support remedies and preventative measures rather than treating every recurrence as brand new.
- **Empathy and service** map to your **User focus** skill: scenarios involving frustrated or confused users test whether you keep the user's need in view — remembering there is a person behind every ticket, often trying to deliver a public service to citizens.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Unsure how to categorise an incident**

You are registering a telephone incident. The user's description does not clearly fit any of the categories in your system, and the senior engineer who usually helps you is at lunch. The service desk asks you to hurry because calls are queuing. What is the most effective response?

- A) Pick a category at random so the queue keeps moving; someone will fix it later.
- B) Choose the closest-fitting category, add a clear note explaining the uncertainty and what the user actually said, and ask your senior colleague to check it when they return.
- C) Leave the incident unregistered until the senior engineer returns from lunch.
- D) Tell the user to email instead, because email incidents are categorised automatically.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B does four good things at once: it keeps the process moving (the incident is registered promptly), it preserves the truth (the user's actual words are captured), it is honest about uncertainty (the note flags the categorisation as provisional), and it uses your team correctly (a light-touch check, not an interruption). Option A moves fast but plants a hidden error — miscategorised incidents get routed to the wrong channel and lost time is invisible until someone complains. Option C protects accuracy but abandons timeliness and the queuing users. Option D pushes the problem onto the user, which fails your **User focus** skill — users should never have to work around your internal processes. The associate-level lesson: when perfect is not available, choose "good, honest, and flagged for review" over "fast and silent" or "slow and safe".

**Question 2 (easy) — You make a mistake**

While closing tickets at the end of the day, you realise you accidentally closed the wrong ticket an hour ago — an unresolved incident from a user who is still waiting for help. Nobody has noticed. What is the most effective response?

- A) Reopen the ticket quietly and say nothing; no harm done.
- B) Leave it closed — the user will call back if it still matters.
- C) Reopen the ticket immediately, tell your senior colleague what happened, and check whether the user has lost time or needs an update.
- D) Wait until tomorrow to see if anyone mentions it.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Three duties are in play: fix the error, be transparent, and protect the user. Option C does all three — the ticket is reopened at once (limiting the damage), your team is told (so they can catch any knock-on effects, and so trust is maintained), and the user's position is checked (they may have been waiting silently for an hour). Option A fixes the record but hides the event; hiding even harmless-seeming mistakes corrodes team trust and prevents the team spotting patterns — perhaps the closing screen is confusing and others make the same slip. Option B transfers the cost of your mistake to the user, the opposite of user focus. Option D adds delay to concealment. SJTs consistently reward early, matter-of-fact honesty about mistakes — and so do real teams: a trainee who says "I got this wrong, I've fixed it, here's what happened" is demonstrating exactly the reliability that earns more responsibility.

**Question 3 (easy) — A frustrated user on the phone**

A user calls, audibly frustrated: "This is the third time this month the case system has lost my work! You people never fix anything!" You can see two previous incidents from this user in the system. What is the most effective first response?

- A) Explain that the previous incidents were closed as resolved, so this must be a new issue.
- B) Acknowledge their frustration, take the details of what has just happened carefully, and note the two previous incidents in the new record so the pattern is visible to your team.
- C) Transfer the call to your senior colleague immediately, since the user is angry.
- D) Point out that "you people" is unfair, because you personally have never handled their tickets.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The user is giving you two valuable things wrapped in frustration: a new incident and evidence of a possible recurring problem. Option B captures both — it treats the person with empathy (acknowledgement costs nothing and lowers the temperature), gathers the required information properly, and connects the three incidents so your team can see a pattern worth investigating. That last step is your **Problem management** awareness in action: three losses of work in a month is a problem signal, not just a third incident. Option A leads with a defence of the record rather than the user's need, and "closed as resolved" is an internal status, not proof the user's experience was resolved. Option C escalates a routine (if uncomfortable) call that is within your role — handling it builds your skills, and transferring angry users reflexively teaches them that anger gets escalation. Option D wins an argument and loses the user. Empathy first, accuracy second, pattern-spotting third — in that order, and B delivers all three.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Colleague suggests skipping the process**

It is a very busy afternoon. A colleague on your team says: "Don't bother logging the small stuff today — just fix the quick ones and move on, we'll never clear the queue otherwise." What is the most effective response?

- A) Follow their suggestion — they are more experienced than you.
- B) Refuse loudly and tell the rest of the team what they said.
- C) Keep logging everything but say nothing, and let them do it their way.
- D) Say you would rather keep logging because unlogged incidents hide patterns and skew the team's figures, and suggest asking the team lead whether there is an approved short-form logging option for busy periods.

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** The colleague's goal (clear the queue) is legitimate; the method (skip logging) is not, because unlogged incidents make problems invisible — if ten users hit the same "small" issue today and none are logged, the pattern that your problem management process depends on never appears, and the team's workload figures underreport reality, which affects future resourcing. Option D holds the line on process while staying collegiate: it explains the reason rather than just citing the rule, and it routes the genuine pressure problem to someone who can actually solve it (perhaps there is a lightweight logging path for exactly these days). Option A follows experience into a bad practice — experience does not make skipped logging harmless. Option C protects your own conduct but leaves the team's data quietly corrupted and your colleague unsupported. Option B turns a process disagreement into a public confrontation, damaging the relationship without fixing the pressure. The strongest answers in SJTs often pair "do the right thing" with "surface the underlying problem to the right person" — both halves matter.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Noticing a recurring pattern**

While registering incidents over two weeks, you notice that the expenses application produces a burst of login failures every Monday morning between 09:00 and 09:30, which then stop by themselves. Each burst generates a handful of tickets that resolve without action. Nobody has mentioned it. What is the most effective response?

- A) Do nothing — the incidents resolve themselves, so there is no impact.
- B) Gather the evidence (dates, times, ticket references, number of users affected), and raise it with your team as a possible underlying problem worth investigating.
- C) Investigate the application's authentication service yourself on Monday morning to find the cause.
- D) Warn users informally to avoid logging in before 09:30 on Mondays.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This scenario is your **Problem management** skill in miniature: incidents are individual events; a problem is the underlying cause behind recurring events. A weekly, time-boxed burst of login failures is a textbook problem signature (perhaps a Monday-morning batch job, a certificate refresh, or a load spike as everyone logs in). Option B does exactly what an associate should: investigate to the point of describing the pattern clearly, then pass the problem to the team with evidence attached — dates, ticket references, and scale turn "I've noticed a thing" into an actionable investigation. Option A mistakes "self-resolving" for "no impact": users are locked out for up to half an hour every week, and tickets consume team time. Option C exceeds your remit — you work under direction, and poking at an authentication service unsupervised could cause a real outage. Option D institutionalises the fault by training users around it, hiding the demand signal and failing user focus. Noticing what nobody mentioned is one of the most valuable things a fresh pair of eyes can do; passing it on well is what makes the noticing count.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Asked to do something beyond your training**

A severity 2 incident is running, and the engineer leading it is deep in investigation. They call over: "Run the database cleanup script on the live server, would you? It's in the tools folder." You have never run this script, you have not been trained on it, and you know the incident is urgent. What is the most effective response?

- A) Run the script immediately — the incident is urgent and they told you to.
- B) Say you have not run it before, and ask them to either talk you through it as you go or confirm the exact command and expected outcome before you run anything on the live server.
- C) Quietly find the script and work it out yourself from the file name, to avoid interrupting them.
- D) Refuse, saying live servers are not your responsibility as a trainee.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Working "under direction" cuts both ways: you act on your team's instructions, and you are entitled to enough direction to act safely. Option B is honest about your experience level, takes only seconds of the lead engineer's attention, and converts a risky instruction into a supervised task — which is exactly how trainees are supposed to touch live systems. During an incident is the worst time for an untrained person to improvise on production (option C): a wrong flag on a cleanup script could convert a severity 2 into a severity 1. Option A treats urgency as a licence for risk; urgency is precisely when mistakes are most costly. Option D over-refuses: flatly declining leaves the engineer without help and misstates your role — trainees do work on live systems, with direction. The professional formula is "yes, with the direction I need": willing, honest about limits, and safe. SJTs at associate level frequently test whether you can decline the risky version of a task while accepting the safe version — that is not weakness; it is exactly the judgement your team wants.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Conflicting instructions**

Your senior engineer asked you this morning to spend today updating the runbook screenshots. At midday, an engineer from another team appears at your desk: "Drop that — I need you to test something for me this afternoon, it's important." What is the most effective response?

- A) Do what the other engineer asks — they seem senior and it sounds important.
- B) Keep working on the screenshots and pretend you did not understand the request.
- C) Explain that your senior engineer has set your work for today, offer to check with them whether you can be released to help, and let the two of them agree the priority.
- D) Try to do both tasks at once, half-finishing each.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** You have received two incompatible instructions from people in different teams, and as a trainee you do not own the authority to arbitrate between them — but you do own the responsibility to surface the conflict quickly and honestly. Option C does that: it is respectful to the requester, transparent with your senior engineer, and it places the prioritisation decision where it belongs, with the people accountable for your time and their work. Perhaps the test really is more important than screenshots — but that is your senior engineer's call to make with full information, not yours to guess. Option A lets assertiveness override your actual reporting line, and "seems senior" is not a work allocation process. Option B is dishonest and leaves the other team's need unaddressed. Option D is the classic multitasking trap: two half-done tasks help nobody, and the incident-prone afternoon version of you is worse at both. The transferable pattern: when instructions conflict, escalate the conflict, not your stress.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Least effective response: the quiet security worry**

While gathering information for a routine ticket, a user mentions in passing: "Oh, and we all share the team login for the reporting application — saves time." You know shared credentials are against your department's security policy. The user's actual ticket is about something unrelated. Which response is LEAST effective?

- A) Handle the user's ticket as normal, then report the shared-login practice to your senior engineer or the appropriate security contact.
- B) Decide it is not your business — you are a trainee and the ticket is about something else — and mention it to no one.
- C) Note the security concern factually in the appropriate channel without blaming the user, and continue helping them with their ticket in a friendly way.
- D) Ask the user a little more about the practice (how many people, which team) so your report is useful, while making clear you are not telling them off.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Read the stem carefully: this question asks for the LEAST effective response — SJTs regularly switch polarity mid-test, and answering the "most effective" question you expected is a common lost mark. Options A, C and D are all reasonable variations of the same good behaviour: keep serving the user, avoid blame, and route a genuine security concern to people who can address it (with D adding useful fact-gathering). Option B is the failure: shared credentials undermine accountability and access control, and "not my business" is exactly how known risks persist until they become breaches. At associate level nobody expects you to fix security policy violations — but everybody expects you to pass them on, which is your **Ownership and topic** skill applied to risk: you do not have to own the solution; you must not orphan the problem. Note also what the good options share: none of them scold the user. Users who mention risky practices openly are giving you information; punishing openness teaches people to hide things.

**Question 9 (hard) — Pressure to close tickets early**

It is the last day of the month. A colleague mentions that the team is close to its monthly resolution target and says: "That ticket of yours — the user hasn't replied for two days. Close it as resolved, it'll count for this month." Your team's process says tickets await user confirmation for five working days before closure. What is the most effective response?

- A) Close it as resolved — the user has probably forgotten about it, and the team benefits.
- B) Keep the ticket open per the five-day process, send the user a friendly follow-up message, and if the pressure to close early continues, mention it to your senior engineer.
- C) Close it as resolved, but reopen it quietly next month if the user comes back.
- D) Keep it open but say nothing to anyone, and avoid that colleague in future.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This is an integrity scenario dressed in everyday clothes. Closing an unconfirmed ticket to hit a monthly figure misrepresents the service's performance — the target exists to measure user outcomes, and gaming it converts a measurement into a fiction. Option B follows the written process, actively serves the user (a follow-up may genuinely resolve the ticket honestly, today), and handles the interpersonal side proportionately: one comment from a colleague warrants a light response, but a continuing pattern of pressure is something your senior engineer should know about. Option A prioritises the metric over the truth. Option C is A with a concealment plan attached — the reopen-quietly step shows you know the closure is wrong. Option D gets the ticket right but ducks both the relationship and the systemic issue; avoidance is not a strategy. Notice the calibration in B: it neither ignores the pressure nor detonates over a single remark. SJTs reward proportionate escalation — matching the size of your response to the size of the problem, while never trading accuracy for appearances.

**Question 10 (hard) — The unhelpful handover**

You arrive for your shift and find a handover note from the outgoing engineer: "Document store playing up again, keep an eye on it." Ten minutes later, a severity 3 ticket arrives from a user who cannot open documents. The outgoing engineer has gone home and is not answering messages. What is the most effective response?

- A) Wait until the outgoing engineer replies, since they know the history.
- B) Treat the new ticket as unrelated to the note, because the note is too vague to use.
- C) Register and work the new ticket properly; check recent tickets, alerts and logs for the document store to reconstruct what "playing up" meant; link anything you find to the new ticket; and tell your senior engineer what you know and do not know.
- D) Close the new ticket, since the team already knows the document store is having issues.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** You have inherited a poor handover — vague notes happen, and the assessment (like real life) tests whether you can recover from one without either freezing or ignoring it. Option C works the problem with what is available: the new ticket gets proper registration and attention (the user's need does not wait for perfect information); the system itself becomes your handover — recent tickets, monitoring alerts, and logs can reconstruct the history the note failed to convey; linking related records rebuilds the pattern; and briefing your senior engineer with an honest "known and unknown" summary invites direction, which is exactly right for your level. Option A leaves a user unserved for an unknown duration on the hope of a reply. Option B wastes real evidence — a vague warning about the same application is still a signal worth checking. Option D is the serious failure: "the team knows" is not a resolution, and closing an open user issue abandons your **User focus** entirely. Bonus lesson: notice how this scenario is also teaching you what your own handovers should look like — refer back to the verbal section's Question 11 for the model.

**Question 11 (hard) — Balancing learning against delivery**

Your team lead has encouraged you to spend two hours each Friday on structured learning, and today is Friday. This morning, a burst of incidents arrived after a supplier update, and the queue is double its normal size. Your learning module on incident categorisation is booked for 14:00. The team has not asked you to cancel it. What is the most effective response?

- A) Take the learning time as planned without telling anyone — it is agreed and protected.
- B) Cancel the learning quietly and work the queue — real work always beats training.
- C) Tell your team lead the queue is double normal size, offer to move your learning slot, and follow their decision.
- D) Compress the two-hour module into a distracted 30 minutes at your desk while watching the queue.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Both commitments here are genuine: protected learning time exists because trainees who skip development stay junior longer, and today's queue is a genuine operational pressure. The key insight is that resolving the trade-off is your team lead's decision to make, and your job is to give them the information and the offer. Option C does exactly that — and accepts either outcome gracefully. Note that C is not "cancel your learning"; many leads will say "the queue is covered, go learn", and protected time stays protected precisely because people ask rather than silently erode it. Option A defends your entitlement while withholding information your lead needs; if the queue melts down at 14:30, "it was in my calendar" will feel thin. Option B silently sacrifices your development and — importantly — hides the decision from the person accountable for both your growth and the service; a pattern of quiet self-cancellation is how trainees stall. Option D delivers the worst of both: ineffective learning and distracted queue-watching. The mature associate behaviour is to surface trade-offs early and let the accountable person choose — the same pattern as Question 7, applied to your own development.

**Question 12 (hard) — Ranking responses: a user asks you to bypass the queue**

A caseworker you helped last week calls your direct line: "You were so helpful before — can you just fix my login now? I know if I go through the service desk it'll take ages, and I'm in court this afternoon." Rank these responses from most to least effective:

1. Log the incident properly yourself right now, note the court deadline as impact information, tell her what will happen next, and flag the urgency to the team working the queue.
2. Fix her login immediately without logging anything — she is in a hurry and you know how.
3. Tell her you cannot help and she must hang up and call the service desk number like everyone else.
4. Log the incident properly and note the deadline, but tell her it will be handled in strict queue order regardless of her court commitment.

- A) 1, 4, 3, 2
- B) 1, 4, 2, 3
- C) 2, 1, 4, 3
- D) 4, 1, 2, 3

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Work through the ranking. Response 1 is clearly best: the incident enters the system (so nothing is lost and the team's picture stays accurate), the genuine deadline becomes recorded impact information that legitimately informs prioritisation, and the user gets clarity — service and process together. Response 4 is second: everything is logged correctly, but refusing to even flag a hard court deadline mistakes rigidity for fairness — impact-based prioritisation is part of the process, not a violation of it. Third versus fourth is the subtle judgement: response 3 is unhelpful and cold (and forcing a repeat call wastes the user's scarce time), but the incident does ultimately enter the proper channel; response 2 feels kind but is worst — an unlogged fix on a live account leaves no record for security, no data for patterns (what if logins are failing for many caseworkers today?), and teaches users that personal contacts beat the queue, which unfairly disadvantages every user without a contact. So the order is 1, 4, 3, 2 — option A. The deep lesson: process exists to make help fair and repeatable; the best answer bends effort, not process, toward the user.

### Preparation tips

- **Learn the shape of strong answers.** Deal with the immediate risk, be honest, involve the right people, protect the user. Before choosing, check each option against those four elements.
- **Read the stem's polarity twice.** "Most effective" and "least effective" alternate without warning. Underline (mentally or on paper) which one this question wants.
- **Judge actions, not intentions.** An option that "feels kind" (like an unlogged favour) can cause real harm; an option that feels strict (like keeping a ticket open) can be the honest one. Evaluate consequences.
- **Think about your level.** The best response for a trainee often includes asking for direction or passing something on — that is not a weakness in SJT scoring; for your role it is precisely the desired behaviour.
- **Reflect on real moments.** After a tricky day, ask yourself: what were my options, which did I choose, what would the best version have been? Five minutes of reflection turns experience into judgement.
- **Talk scenarios through with your team.** Ask a senior colleague "what would you have done?" about anonymised situations. Their reasoning — especially about when to escalate — is exactly what SJTs measure.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Choosing the heroic answer.** Solo fixes on live systems, working through protected time, absorbing pressure silently — heroics score poorly because they create risk and hide information.
- **Choosing pure rule-quoting.** Responses that only cite the rule, without helping the person or surfacing the underlying problem, are usually second-best. The strongest answers pair the rule with service.
- **Punishing honesty.** Options that scold users or colleagues for revealing problems teach people to hide things. Warmth and accuracy are not in conflict.
- **Escalating everything or nothing.** Reflexive escalation clogs your team; silent absorption starves it. Match escalation to genuine risk and to your level's remit.
- **Letting metrics outrank truth.** Any option that improves a number by misrepresenting reality — early closures, skipped logging — is a trap, however team-spirited it sounds.
- **Forgetting the user exists.** Re-read each scenario asking "where is the user in this option?" If an option leaves a user waiting, uninformed, or working around your process, it is rarely the best one.
