## Introduction

Psychometric assessments are standardised exercises that measure the cognitive capabilities and behavioural tendencies predictive of performance in a role. At lead level, the versions worth your time are job-specific: they draw on the materials you genuinely own — portfolio roadmaps, programme dependencies, business cases, cross-team capacity plans, stakeholder strategies, community and coaching commitments — and the decisions you genuinely make: shaping strategy across a product portfolio or end-to-end service, directing stakeholder relationships, predicting and mitigating life cycle risks before they land, and providing product leadership and direction across multiple teams as a member of your product area's management team.

Why does this practice matter at your level? Three reasons. First, consequence: your reasoning operates at portfolio scale, where a misread trend, a mis-audited benefit figure, or an imprecisely read governance condition propagates across teams and quarters rather than sprints. The disciplines these assessments train — valid deduction, denominator vigilance, claim-strength fidelity, proportionate judgement — are the same disciplines that keep portfolio decisions sound. Second, leadership: you coach and manage product managers, contribute to assessing other teams, and are involved in recruiting at all levels. That means you read other people's reasoning for a living — their business cases, their syntheses, their judgement calls — and you interpret assessment evidence about candidates. Practising these instruments sharpens both the reading and the interpreting. Third, example: the reflective practice this guide demands — commit to an answer, test it against a worked rationale, notice where your instinct diverged — is precisely the practice you advocate to your teams. Leaders who visibly sharpen their own fundamentals earn the standing to ask it of others.

This document is organised into four assessment sections — cognitive, numeric reasoning, verbal reasoning, and situational judgement — each following the same pattern: an overview of what the assessment measures and why it matters at lead level; a mapping of its dimensions to the named skills in your role summary; a substantial set of practice questions progressing from easy to challenging, each with a complete worked explanation that ties the technique back to portfolio-level product work; then preparation tips and common pitfalls.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive ability assessment measures the mental processes your role demands, using realistic material rather than abstract puzzles. At lead level that material is portfolio-shaped: multi-team programme plans with interacting constraints, portfolio risk registers, high-level roadmaps under competing pressures, assessment reports from other teams you are guiding, and strategy artefacts whose internal consistency you are accountable for.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 15 to 30 minutes with 15 to 30 questions covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving, scored objectively — at this level usually against a managerial or senior professional norm group — with adaptive difficulty and separate speed and accuracy reporting common.

The assessment's relevance at lead level lies in three shifts from earlier roles. Scale: you maintain the high-level roadmap and plan across multiple teams, so constraint propagation, feasibility testing, and dependency reasoning operate over more moving parts with longer feedback loops — errors compound before they surface. Prediction: your life cycle skill explicitly includes predicting, preventing, and mitigating risks before they materialise, which is pattern recognition pointed at the future: reading weak signals across teams, distinguishing structural drift from local noise, and acting before the trend line forces you to. Guidance: you contribute to the assessment of other teams and coach product managers, which means much of your cognitive work is second-order — checking others' reasoning, spotting the flaw in a case you did not write, and articulating why an inference fails clearly enough that its author improves. The questions below exercise all three shifts, and the explanations are written to be coachable: each names the technique, so you can pass it on.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The assessment dimensions map directly to the named skills in your role summary:

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **Life cycle management** and **Managing product outcomes**: predicting, preventing, and mitigating risks at different phases requires reading weak signals across a portfolio — recurring delivery symptoms, converging metric drifts — before they become incidents.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Strategic ownership** and **Life cycle management**: ensuring strategic alignment across the organisation, and guiding teams through phase decisions, both demand valid inference from conditions, standards, and evidence — with unknowns marked as unknowns.
- **Error checking** maps to your duties to **contribute to the assessment of other teams** and **ensure teams comply with appropriate standards**: reviewing others' artefacts — cases, roadmaps, measurement designs — for internal inconsistency is a defining lead activity.
- **Prioritisation** maps to **Product management** and **Creating value for money**: maintaining the high-level roadmap, planning across teams, and helping others make value-based decisions are portfolio ranking problems under interacting constraints.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Product leadership** and **Agile and Lean practices**: decomposing cross-team, politically loaded problems into testable parts — and tailoring the delivery approach itself when the standard approach is failing — is the lead craft.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Weak signals across a portfolio**

Across your five teams this month you note: Team One's cycle time rose 15%; Team Two lost its user researcher to a leave of absence with no cover; Team Three's last two retrospectives were cancelled; Team Four shipped on schedule; Team Five's product manager escalated twice for decisions they would normally take themselves. Which reading best serves a lead responsible for predicting and preventing risk?

- A) Only Team One's metric matters — the rest is soft information.
- B) Four of five teams show early-warning signals of different kinds — a capability gap, eroding reflective practice, rising cycle time, and decision confidence dropping — each cheap to probe now and expensive to discover later; schedule brief check-ins with those four this week.
- C) Team Four is fine, so the portfolio is fundamentally healthy.
- D) Wait a month to see whether the signals persist before doing anything.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Lead-level risk prediction runs on weak signals — indicators individually explicable and collectively cheap to check. Each of the four flagged items is a known precursor: rising cycle time often precedes missed commitments; an uncovered researcher gap quietly converts a user-centred team into an assumption-driven one; cancelled retrospectives remove the team's own early-warning system; and a capable product manager escalating routine decisions signals lost confidence, unclear authority, or something they are not saying. Option B's response is calibrated — brief check-ins, not interventions: probing weak signals cheaply is the whole economics of prevention, since four conversations this week cost less than one recovered incident next quarter. Option A restricts evidence to the quantified — but "soft" information is where most portfolio risk lives first. Option C commits the healthy-instance fallacy: one team shipping on schedule says nothing about four different risk patterns elsewhere. Option D converts early warning into late confirmation, spending the exact advantage weak signals exist to give; persistence-checking is right for ambiguous metrics, wrong when the check itself is nearly free. The named skill is precise here: predict, prevent, mitigate and manage potential risks — in that order, because each verb is cheaper than its successor.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction from an assessment standard**

You are supporting another team ahead of their service assessment. The standard states: "A service must demonstrate iteration in response to user research findings. Demonstrating iteration requires showing at least one substantive change made because of research, with the evidence trail from finding to change." The team has conducted extensive research and can show many findings, but every design decision predated the research, which was then used to confirm the choices. Which conclusion follows?

- A) The team meets the standard — they have research and they have changes.
- B) The team does not currently meet the standard as stated: they can show findings but no change made because of a finding, so the required evidence trail cannot exist.
- C) The team meets the standard if the research was high quality.
- D) The standard is unreasonable and the assessors will waive it.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The standard defines its terms: iteration means at least one substantive change made because of research, with the trail from finding to change. The team's situation — decisions first, research after, used confirmatorily — means no change was caused by a finding; whatever the volume and quality of research (option C's red herring), the causal direction runs the wrong way, and an evidence trail from finding to change cannot be constructed for changes that predate their findings. Option B follows necessarily. Option A pattern-matches on the ingredients (research ✓, changes ✓) while missing the relation the standard actually requires between them — the most common way teams fail standards they believe they meet, and precisely what your guidance role exists to catch before the assessment does. Option D is hope, not inference. The lead-level coaching content: confirmatory research is itself a finding worth surfacing to the team — research that only ever validates existing decisions usually signals that research is being commissioned too late in their cycle, which is the correctable root cause. Contributing to the assessment of other teams, per your **Life cycle management** skill, means reading standards with this precision and teaching teams to read the causal requirements, not just the ingredient lists.

**Question 3 (easy) — Error checking a high-level roadmap**

Your portfolio's high-level roadmap, going to the management team tomorrow, contains these entries: (1) "Q2: Retire legacy booking system." (2) "Q3: Migrate remaining booking users to new platform." (3) "Q2–Q3: New platform accessibility audit and remediation." (4) "Q4: Decommission legacy data store (depends on booking retirement complete)." Which inconsistency most needs fixing before the meeting?

- A) Entry 4 should be in Q3.
- B) Entries 1 and 2 are out of order: the roadmap retires the legacy system a quarter before migrating its remaining users off it.
- C) Entry 3 spans two quarters, which roadmaps should not allow.
- D) There is no inconsistency.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Read the entries as a dependency structure, not a list. Retiring the legacy booking system in Q2 while its remaining users migrate in Q3 leaves those users a quarter with neither system — the sequence must invert (migrate, then retire). Option B. The other options miss or invent: entry 4's Q4 slot is consistent with its stated dependency once retirement moves to Q3 (and even as written, later-than-necessary is slack, not contradiction); multi-quarter entries (option C) are normal at portfolio altitude. Worth noticing as a reviewer: the error is invisible line-by-line — each entry is individually plausible — and appears only when you ask of every pair, "can these both be true in this order?" That pairwise-consistency pass is the fastest high-yield review technique for roadmaps, and it is exactly what maintaining the high-level roadmap means beyond custodianship: the lead's version of the document is the one whose entries can all be true simultaneously. A secondary flag a thorough review would add: entry 3's accessibility remediation runs to end-Q3 while migration completes in Q3 — migrating users onto a platform whose remediation may still be in progress deserves a sequencing conversation, even if it is a risk rather than a contradiction.

**Question 4 (easy) — Prioritisation across portfolio demands**

Monday morning at lead level. Four demands compete:

1. The programme board meets Wednesday and expects your portfolio's quarterly reprioritisation paper, which requires half a day's work.
2. A product manager you coach has a service assessment Friday and has asked you to review their evidence pack — two hours, and they are anxious.
3. The head of product asks for your input to a cross-government community session you are co-leading next month.
4. A team's live service has degraded overnight — error rates tripled; the team is investigating and has not asked for help.

Which sequencing is most defensible?

- A) 4 (confirm incident ownership and escalation route, ~30 minutes), 1, 2, 3
- B) 1, 2, 4, 3 — governance papers outrank operational noise
- C) 2, 1, 4, 3 — people you coach come first
- D) 4 (personally lead the incident until resolved), then whatever remains

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The discriminating judgement is item 4's handling, and both extremes fail. Option B ranks a live user-facing degradation below paperwork; option D personally commandeers an incident a competent team is already investigating — the lead anti-pattern of subtracting the team's ownership while adding little, and at the cost of everything else in the week. Option A calibrates: a brief check that the incident has an owner, a communication path, and a clear trigger for escalating to you — then trust the team, remaining interruptible. That is what "ensure effective support for the product or service and its users" means at lead altitude: assurance of the response, not performance of it. Thereafter, impact-weighted urgency: the Wednesday board paper (half a day, hard deadline, portfolio consequence) precedes the Friday assessment review (two hours, and scheduling it today with the anxious product manager — "tomorrow morning, protected" — delivers most of the reassurance immediately at zero cost); the community input has a month of runway. Option C's instinct is honourable but mis-timed: coaching-first is a value, not a sequence — the product manager needs your review before Friday, not before Wednesday's board. The reusable pattern: for every demand, separate the two-minute triage response (confirm ownership, schedule the slot, acknowledge receipt) from the work itself; leads who sequence the triage first buy calm for the whole week.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Constraint reasoning across a programme**

Your portfolio contributes three deliverables to a programme milestone at the end of month 6: the payments capability (P), the notifications capability (N), and the reporting layer (R). Constraints: (i) P requires a security assessment that can only be booked for month 3 or month 5, and takes the full month, and build must be complete before it; (ii) P's build takes 2 months; (iii) N depends on P's assessed build and takes 2 months; (iv) R can be built any time but its final month must overlap N's final month for joint testing; (v) R takes 2 months. If everything is sequenced as early as possible, in which months does N run, and is the milestone met?

- A) N runs months 4–5; milestone met with a month to spare
- B) N runs months 4–5; milestone missed
- C) N runs months 5–6; milestone met exactly
- D) The milestone cannot be met

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Propagate forward. P's build takes months 1–2 (complete before assessment). The assessment slots are month 3 or month 5; earliest is month 3, occupying it fully. N depends on P's assessed build, so N starts month 4 and runs months 4–5. R (2 months) must have its final month overlap N's final month (month 5), so R runs months 4–5. All three complete by end of month 5 — the milestone at end of month 6 is met with a month to spare: option A. Option C mistakenly starts N after a month-5 assessment slot (using the later slot for no reason); option B computes N correctly but forgets the milestone sits at month 6, not month 5. The lead-level content is the slack analysis after the arithmetic: the month of slack exists only because the month-3 assessment slot was secured — miss it, and the next slot (month 5) pushes N to months 6–7 and the milestone fails outright. That converts a scheduling fact into a management action: the security assessment booking is the plan's single point of fragility, so book it now, confirm it twice, and hold P's build scope fiercely against anything that would slip it past month 2. Predicting and preventing life cycle risk means finding the constraint whose failure collapses the plan — the binding constraint — and defending it disproportionately. In the assessment, compute cleanly; in the job, read the computed plan for where it is brittle.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Error checking another team's outcome claims**

Reviewing a product team's quarterly report as part of your assessment-support role, you find: "Our onboarding improvements succeeded: activation rose from 55% to 61%. We define activation as completing setup within 14 days. Note: this quarter we also began excluding accounts created by internal testers, who previously depressed the figure." What is the most important review finding?

- A) The improvement is confirmed: 61% exceeds 55%.
- B) The comparison is confounded: the definition's population changed in the same quarter (excluding testers, which the team itself notes previously depressed the figure), so an unknown share of the 6-point rise is definitional; the team should restate last quarter's figure on the new basis before claiming any effect.
- C) Activation is the wrong metric and the report should use satisfaction.
- D) The exclusion of testers is illegitimate and should be reversed.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The report contains its own confession, gently worded: the exclusion "previously depressed the figure", so removing testers mechanically raises measured activation independent of any real improvement. With the population changing in the measurement quarter, 55% (old basis) versus 61% (new basis) compares different things; the rise could be mostly real, mostly definitional, or any mixture — indeterminable as presented. The remedy option B names is the standard one and cheap: restate the prior quarter on the new basis (the team has the data — testers are identifiable, that is how they excluded them), yielding a clean like-for-like comparison. Option D overcorrects: excluding internal testers is usually the right measurement choice — the flaw is the unbridged transition, not the destination; option C changes the subject. Two lead-level notes. First, the reviewing heuristic: whenever a metric improves in the same period its definition, population, or instrumentation changed, the burden of proof sits with the claimant, and "restate the baseline" is the request that resolves it — teach your product managers this phrase. Second, the tone: this team disclosed the change (in a note, but disclosed) — the coaching response distinguishes the honest-but-methodologically-naive report from the engineered one, and builds the skill without punishing the disclosure. That distinction is how leading teams to create effective measurement methods stays a culture rather than a police action.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Choosing the delivery approach, not just the plan**

A team in your portfolio is struggling: they build a data-integration product with hard external dependencies (three other departments' release schedules), their two-week sprints keep "failing" because dependency slips invalidate sprint plans mid-cycle, morale is sagging, and their delivery manager proposes doubling sprint length to four weeks. Applying your Agile and Lean expertise, what is the strongest response?

- A) Approve four-week sprints — longer cycles absorb slips better.
- B) Insist on standard two-week Scrum — consistency across the portfolio matters more than local fit.
- C) Diagnose the mismatch first: the team's work is dependency-driven flow, not batch-plannable scope, so trial a flow-based approach — pull-based work intake, work-in-progress limits, explicit dependency-wait states made visible, cadenced (not scope-committed) reviews — evaluate against cycle time and team health after six weeks, and share what is learned across the portfolio.
- D) Escalate the three departments' slips to programme governance and keep the process unchanged.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The presenting proposal treats the symptom: four-week sprints (option A) halve the frequency of plan invalidation without addressing its cause, while doubling batch size and slowing feedback — the slips will simply invalidate bigger plans less often. The diagnosis option C performs is the named lead skill — create and tailor the right approach for a team, challenging, evaluating and iterating it: sprint commitment assumes the team controls enough of its inputs to plan a fixed scope; this team's inputs are other departments' release schedules, making their work flow-shaped (arrive, wait, process) rather than batch-shaped. Flow methods fit: pull-based intake stops promising what dependencies won't permit; WIP limits and explicit wait states make the dependency cost visible and measurable (evidence for exactly the escalation option D jumps to); cadenced reviews keep stakeholder rhythm without scope commitments the team cannot honour. Note C's remaining structure: trial, evaluation criteria (cycle time and team health), a review date — tailoring is an experiment, not a decree, which is also what makes it coachable — and sharing the learning portfolio-wide, per "experiment with new and innovative ways of working to improve delivery across the organisation". Option B mistakes uniformity for discipline; portfolio consistency in ceremonies is worth far less than fit between work-shape and method. Option D's escalation is a legitimate component (and C's visibility data feeds it) but as a whole answer it outsources the fixable half of the problem. The reusable diagnostic: when a team persistently "fails" its process, suspect the process's fit before the team's execution — recognised experts change the approach, not just the tempo.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Reading converging metrics across products**

Three products in your portfolio serve adjacent user groups. Over four months: Product A's completion rate drifted 84→82→80→79; Product B's support contacts per 1,000 transactions rose 61→64→70→75; Product C's satisfaction is stable but its comment themes shifted from feature requests toward "takes longer than it used to". All three products share one platform service — the identity component — which released a "hardening upgrade" four months ago. What is the strongest reading?

- A) Three unrelated product-level issues; assign each team to investigate its own metric.
- B) The three signals are consistent with a common cause degrading a shared dependency — the identity component, whose hardening upgrade coincides with all three drifts; commission a joint investigation measuring identity-step timing and failure rates across all three products before team-level fixes.
- C) Product A's decline is the priority since completion is the most important metric.
- D) Satisfaction is stable on C, so nothing systemic is occurring.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Portfolio position is precisely what makes this diagnosis available: each team, seeing only its own signal, would investigate locally (option A) — plausible-looking work that cannot find a cause outside its own product, while three teams separately fail to converge on the shared component. The lead sees the cross-product pattern: three different metrics (completion, contact rate, duration complaints) drifting in the same direction over the same window, across products sharing one dependency whose change coincides with onset. That is not proof — coincidence in timing plus shared architecture is a hypothesis — which is why option B's action is measurement (identity-step timing and failure rates, per product) rather than rollback: confirm the mechanism, then fix at the component. Option C ranks symptoms instead of causes and sends the other two teams home; option D misreads C's stability — satisfaction is a lagging, blunt instrument, and the theme shift is the leading edge showing through before the score moves. Two named skills operate together here: identify opportunities to share and connect product data (the diagnosis was only possible because you look across products — portfolios that never join their metrics structurally cannot see shared-dependency degradation), and predict, prevent, mitigate risks (platform components serving multiple products deserve standing cross-product telemetry, which is the preventive generalisation of this incident). The reusable rule: when several products drift together, interrogate what they share before what they don't.

**Question 9 (moderate) — The business case that proves too much**

A product manager brings you a case for expanding their document-checking feature: "The feature saved 12,000 caseworker hours last year. Rolling it out to the remaining six casework teams, which are collectively four times our size, will therefore save 48,000 hours annually." You know the originating team was selected for the pilot because its work was the most document-heavy. What is the most important challenge to raise?

- A) The arithmetic: 12,000 × 4 is 48,000, so the case is sound.
- B) Selection effect: the pilot team was chosen for maximal document-intensity, so per-team savings elsewhere will likely be materially lower; the case needs the remaining teams' actual document volumes, with the saving re-estimated per team from their workloads rather than scaled from the best case.
- C) The feature should be rebuilt before any expansion.
- D) Hours saved is not a real benefit unless posts are cut.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The multiplication is clean and the flaw is upstream of it: scaling from a pilot selected for favourable conditions projects the maximum observed effect onto populations chosen for having less of the driving characteristic. Document-checking savings scale with document volume; the pilot team was picked as most document-heavy; the six remaining teams are, by construction, less so — 4× size does not deliver 4× savings when the per-unit intensity falls. Option B names both the effect and the remedy: estimate from each team's actual document workload — data the organisation has — turning an extrapolation into a calculation. The result may still justify expansion (even half the projected saving might), which is why this is a challenge, not a rejection. Option A audits only the visible step — the assessments-and-boards failure mode this question exists to train against: correct arithmetic on unsound premises is the most persuasive form of wrong. Option D contains a legitimate benefits-realisation question (cash-releasing versus capacity-releasing savings — worth one line in the case, distinguishing them) but overclaims in dismissing capacity benefits entirely; redeployed caseworker hours reducing backlogs are real value even when no post closes. The coaching layer: this product manager did what pilots invite — generalised from their best evidence — and the lesson to transfer is the selection question itself: "how was the pilot chosen, and does the choosing variable drive the benefit?" Asked routinely, it immunises your portfolio's cases before they reach the boards where **Creating value for money** — determine the benefit and persuade others — gets tested by people paid to find exactly this flaw.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Feasibility under interacting people and programme constraints**

You must staff three concurrent workstreams for the next quarter from your product management group: the flagship migration (needs a senior-capable PM full-time), the discovery for a ministerial priority (needs a PM half-time, must be someone strong with senior stakeholders), and business-as-usual coverage for two products (needs one PM full-time equivalent, splittable). Available: PM Anna (senior-capable, strong with stakeholders, full-time); PM Ben (mid-level, full-time, developing stakeholder skills, has asked for a stretch opportunity); PM Chloe (senior-capable, strong with stakeholders, but committed half-time to a cross-government community role you sponsor). Which allocation is feasible and best serves both delivery and development?

- A) Anna: migration full-time; Chloe: discovery with her free half; Ben: BAU full-time — meets every constraint, but gives Ben no stretch this quarter unless deliberately compensated.
- B) Anna: discovery half-time plus BAU half-time; Ben: migration full-time; Chloe: BAU with her free half.
- C) Ben: discovery half-time (his stretch) plus BAU half-time; Anna: migration; Chloe: unassigned beyond community work.
- D) Anna: migration; Ben: discovery half-time plus BAU half-time; Chloe: BAU with her free half — with Chloe mentoring Ben on the discovery's stakeholder work.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Test feasibility first, ruthlessly. The migration needs senior-capable full-time: Anna or Chloe — but Chloe has only half her time, so the migration takes Anna (full-time, consuming her entirely). The discovery needs strong-with-stakeholders at half-time: with Anna consumed, only Chloe qualifies, and her free half fits exactly. BAU (1.0 FTE, splittable) then falls to Ben — full-time, covered. Option A is the unique allocation meeting every hard constraint; check the others: B puts mid-level Ben on a senior-requiring migration and halves Anna across roles the constraints price at full-time-plus-half; C staffs the ministerial discovery with a PM whose stakeholder skills are still developing (the constraint says "strong", and a ministerial-priority discovery is a high-cost place to discover the gap) and leaves BAU half-covered while Chloe idles; D fails arithmetic quietly — Ben at half discovery plus half BAU leaves BAU needing another half, which Chloe's free half covers, but the discovery still has Ben where "strong with stakeholders" is required; the mentoring flourish mitigates without satisfying. The lead-level content is in option A's honest tail: the feasible answer has a development cost — Ben's stretch request goes unmet — and naming that cost is what separates resource arithmetic from people leadership. The compensations are real: Ben shadows the discovery's stakeholder sessions alongside BAU, takes a defined slice of migration governance, and gets the first call on next quarter's stretch, explicitly. Managing people and managing products, per your role summary, meet exactly here: constraints decide the quarter; leaders decide what the quarter costs each person, and pay it back deliberately.

**Question 11 (challenging) — The flaw in a portfolio-level argument**

At the management team meeting, a peer argues: "Across government, the products that survived spending reviews all had quantified benefits cases. Our portfolio should therefore require every product to produce a quantified benefits case, because that is what makes products survive reviews." Which is the sharpest critique?

- A) The argument is sound: the correlation is strong and the recommendation is prudent.
- B) The argument infers causation from survivor data without the comparison class: products that died may also have had quantified cases (we are not told), and survivors may share other traits — political salience, delivery track record — that drive both survival and the resources to produce polished cases; requiring cases universally may still be right, but on different grounds, and believing quantification causes survival invites gaming the number rather than the value.
- C) The argument is wrong because spending reviews are unpredictable and preparation is pointless.
- D) The argument is irrelevant because your portfolio faces no review.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Two inferential failures stack. First, survivorship framing: "products that survived all had X" is uninformative about X's effect without the base rate among non-survivors — if quantified cases were near-universal, the trait discriminates nothing. Second, confounding: capability and salience plausibly cause both survival and case quality; the polished case may be a marker of the traits that save products rather than the mechanism of salvation. Option B also does what lead-level critique must: it separates the inference (broken) from the recommendation (possibly still sound — quantified cases aid internal prioritisation and honest governance regardless of their survival magic), because critiques that demolish the reasoning and the practice together overreach and lose the room; "right conclusion, wrong argument, and the wrongness matters" is the precision that changes minds. Why the wrongness matters is B's final clause: if the organisation believes the number is a talisman, it will optimise the number — inflated benefits, gamed baselines — which is the measurement-corruption your outcome-leadership exists to prevent; if it understands cases as decision tools, it will optimise their honesty. Options C and D refuse the question rather than answering it. The coaching transfer: survivorship arguments are endemic in government product discourse ("successful services all did X") — equip your product managers with the two questions that dissolve them: what did the failures do, and what else do the survivors share? Supporting strategic decision making, per your **Strategic ownership** skill, is substantially the craft of catching these patterns in the management-team room, kindly, before they become policy.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Strategic alignment with contradictory mandates**

Your portfolio operates under three current mandates: the departmental digital strategy ("consolidate onto shared platforms; no new bespoke transactional builds"), a ministerial commitment ("a dedicated application service for the new resettlement scheme, live within six months"), and the accessibility regulations (non-negotiable compliance). Your platform team's assessment: the shared platform can host the new scheme's application journey, but its current form-builder cannot meet two of the scheme's accessibility requirements without an estimated four months of component work; a bespoke build could meet everything in five months; the six-month clock is running. What best serves strategic alignment?

- A) Build bespoke — the ministerial deadline and accessibility both bind, and the strategy must yield; proceed quietly since the conflict resolves itself.
- B) Refuse the bespoke route entirely — the digital strategy prohibits it, so the platform gap defines the timeline and the ministerial commitment must move.
- C) Take the conflict, with the platform team's evidence and a costed recommendation, to the strategy's governance: propose funding the platform's four-month accessibility component work now (serving the scheme at month five via the platform, strengthening the platform for every future service, and preserving all three mandates), with the bespoke route priced as the explicit fallback if component work slips past a named checkpoint.
- D) Deliver on the platform as-is by month six and schedule the accessibility gaps for remediation after launch.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Enumerate what actually binds. Accessibility regulations: non-negotiable — option D, launching a statutory scheme's application service with known accessibility failures, is unlawful exclusion of exactly the users a resettlement scheme is likeliest to serve, and fails immediately. The ministerial commitment: politically binding, six months. The digital strategy: organisationally binding but governed — it has an owner and an amendment process, which is the fact options A and B both ignore in opposite directions. A resolves the conflict privately and buries the precedent: a quiet bespoke build teaches the portfolio that strategies bend to whoever declares an exception, and forfeits the platform improvement that would have served every subsequent scheme. B treats the strategy as the one immovable mandate — inverting the actual hierarchy (regulations > ministerial commitment > internal strategy) and volunteering a political collision the evidence doesn't require. Option C is strategic alignment as your framework means it — ensure strategic alignment across the organisation — executed through governance rather than around it: the arithmetic works (four months of component work < five-month bespoke < six-month deadline, with a month's contingency), the platform investment converts a scheme-specific cost into portfolio-wide capability (the strategy's own logic, turned to fund its exception-avoidance), all three mandates survive, and the named-checkpoint fallback converts residual delivery risk into a governed decision rather than a month-five panic. Note the general shape, because it recurs: when mandates collide, rank them by their real bindingness, then design the option that satisfies the rankable ones and take it to the governance that owns the flexible one — leads who resolve mandate conflicts in the open, with costed options, are how organisations keep strategies credible and ministers served simultaneously.

### Preparation tips

- Practise on your portfolio's own artefacts: run the pairwise-consistency pass over your current high-level roadmap; list this month's weak signals across teams and what each would cost to probe; find the binding constraint in your live programme plan and check it is being defended proportionately.
- For deduction questions, extract the required relation, not just the required ingredients — standards and gates usually demand a causal or sequential connection ("change because of finding", "assessed before dependent build") that ingredient-checking misses.
- Write out multi-constraint problems: fix the anchored items, propagate, then feasibility-test each option in full — including the arithmetic of half-allocations, which is where plausible staffing options quietly fail. Watch for the option that is feasible but carries a named cost; at lead level it is often the keyed answer.
- When a metric and its definition change in the same window, the burden of proof reverses. "Restate the baseline on the new basis" resolves most such disputes — in the test, choose the option that says so; in the job, make it a portfolio norm.
- Interrogate pilots and survivor stories with the two dissolving questions: how was the pilot chosen (and does the choosing variable drive the benefit)? What did the non-survivors do? These cover a remarkable share of hard applied-reasoning items — and of management-team debate.
- Bank accuracy on the early items, time-box the constraint puzzles, and keep two minutes for flagged returns. You coach others to work sustainably; model it under test conditions.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- Restricting evidence to the quantified. Cancelled retrospectives, unusual escalations, and shifted comment themes are data; the portfolio risks that hurt most announce themselves softly first.
- Reviewing line-by-line when the defect is pairwise. Roadmap and plan errors live between entries — always ask whether adjacent claims can be simultaneously true in the stated order.
- Auditing the arithmetic and waving through the premise. Selection effects, changed denominators, and survivor framing make correct multiplication the delivery vehicle for wrong conclusions.
- Uniformity mistaken for discipline. Insisting every team run the same method regardless of work-shape fails the tailoring your Agile and Lean expertise names; fit beats consistency.
- Resolving mandate conflicts privately — in either direction. Quiet exceptions corrode strategies; reflexive refusals collide with commitments. Rank bindingness, design the reconciling option, use the governance.
- Solving the staffing puzzle and ignoring its people cost. Feasible allocations that silently spend someone's development, promised stretch, or community commitment are half-answers; the lead-level response names the cost and schedules the repayment.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures accuracy and judgement with the numbers your role genuinely handles — at lead level, portfolio numbers: multi-product KPI tables, programme budgets and burn rates, benefit profiles across several cases, capacity arithmetic spanning teams, and the derived figures (unit costs, weighted rates, variances, projections) whose correct construction you are accountable for and whose incorrect construction you are expected to catch in others' work.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 20 to 40 minutes with 15 to 25 questions, calculator permitted, objectively and often adaptively scored against a senior professional norm group, with speed and accuracy reported separately.

The lead-level case for numeric fluency is a governance case. You help others make value-based decisions and lead teams to create effective measurement methods, so you are the quality gate through which your portfolio's numbers pass on their way to boards — the last reviewer positioned to catch the unweighted blend, the shifted denominator, the pilot-inflated projection. And you are involved in programme management activities, where budgets, forecasts, and benefit tracking are conducted in a numeric register that assumes fluency: the lead who must recompute slowly in the meeting has already lost the meeting. The reassurance remains what it was at every level: the mathematics is percentages, ratios, averages, rates, and trends — a bounded toolkit, shown step by step below, applied to progressively more adversarial material. Mastery here is not computational speed; it is knowing, instantly, which computation settles the question and which base it must stand on.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Portfolio spend against profile**

Five months into the financial year, your portfolio has spent £1.35m against an annual budget of £3.6m, which is profiled evenly across the year. What is the position against profile?

- A) £150,000 underspent against profile
- B) £150,000 overspent against profile
- C) Exactly on profile
- D) £450,000 underspent against profile

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Even profiling means the year-to-date profile is 5/12 of £3.6m. Step 1: £3.6m ÷ 12 = £300,000 per month. Step 2: × 5 months = £1.5m profiled. Step 3: actual £1.35m − profile £1.5m = −£150,000: underspent by £150,000, option A. Option B inverts the sign — under time pressure, anchor the subtraction as actual minus profile and read negative as under. The management reading matters more than the arithmetic: a 10% underspend at month five is not automatically good news — in government financial years it usually signals delayed starts or recruitment lag, and the question it forces is whether the remaining £2.25m can be usefully and properly spent in seven months, or whether an early, honest conversation about reprofiling or returning funds beats a quarter-four scramble. Leads involved in programme management earn credibility precisely by raising underspend as a delivery signal in month five rather than explaining it in month eleven.

**Question 2 (easy) — Weighted completion across a portfolio**

Your portfolio's three transactional products recorded last month: Product A: 52,000 transactions at 81% completion; Product B: 13,000 at 69%; Product C: 35,000 at 90%. What is the portfolio completion rate?

- A) 80.0%
- B) 82.6%
- C) 83.3%
- D) 84.2%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Weight by transactions. Completions: A: 52,000 × 0.81 = 42,120. B: 13,000 × 0.69 = 8,970. C: 35,000 × 0.90 = 31,500. Total completions: 42,120 + 8,970 + 31,500 = 82,590. Total transactions: 52,000 + 13,000 + 35,000 = 100,000. Portfolio rate: 82,590 ÷ 100,000 = 82.59% ≈ 82.6%, option B. Option A is the unweighted mean ((81 + 69 + 90) ÷ 3 = 80%), which underweights the two large products. The convenient round total (100,000) is an assessment kindness; the method — sum numerators, sum denominators, divide once — is invariant. The reporting judgement to attach: 82.6% is a fair headline, but Product B at 69% is twelve points adrift of the blend, and a portfolio report that shows the blend with the spread ("82.6%, with B at 69% under investigation") informs, while the blend alone anaesthetises. At lead level you are usually the person who decides which version reaches the management team; decide like someone who will later be asked why B's decline was invisible.

**Question 3 (easy) — Unit cost with shared platform costs**

Product X handled 40,000 transactions with £48,000 of direct costs. It also bears an allocated share of platform costs: the platform costs £90,000 and serves three products equally. What is Product X's fully loaded cost per transaction?

- A) £1.20
- B) £1.95
- C) £2.45
- D) £3.45

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Allocated platform share: £90,000 ÷ 3 = £30,000. Fully loaded cost: £48,000 + £30,000 = £78,000. Per transaction: £78,000 ÷ 40,000 = £1.95, option B. Option A uses direct costs only — the figure teams prefer to quote; option D adds the whole platform cost, triple-counting across the portfolio. Two lead-level annotations. First, the equal-thirds allocation is a modelling choice, not a fact: if X drives half the platform's load, usage-based allocation would load it with £45,000 (unit cost £2.325) — allocation methods change unit costs materially, so when products' unit costs are compared for investment or retirement decisions, the lead must ensure one method applies consistently and is stated; a product can be made to look efficient or expensive by allocation alone. Second, this is a live portfolio duty: identifying opportunities to share and connect — platforms, components, data — is your skill set's direction of travel, and honest shared-cost arithmetic is what keeps sharing decisions evidence-based rather than territorial.

**Question 4 (easy) — Benefit profile variance**

A business case in your portfolio profiled benefits of £75,000 per quarter. Actuals: Q1: £60,000; Q2: £81,000; Q3: £66,000. What is the cumulative variance at the end of Q3?

- A) −£18,000
- B) −£9,000
- C) +£6,000
- D) −£27,000

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Profile to date: £75,000 × 3 = £225,000. Actual: 60,000 + 81,000 + 66,000 = £207,000. Variance: 207,000 − 225,000 = −£18,000, option A. The quarterly pattern deserves the second look the single figure invites you to skip: −15k, +6k, −9k is oscillation, not trend — unlike a steady widening, this profile suggests lumpy realisation (benefits arriving late or early across quarter boundaries) rather than structural shortfall, and the honest governance line is "8% behind profile cumulatively, variance oscillating, no trend evidence yet; next quarter discriminates". That is a different — and better-evidenced — message than either "on track" (false: cumulative is negative) or "benefits failing" (unproven: no direction). Reading variance shape, not just variance size, is the difference between benefit tracking as ritual and as management information; it is also the coaching point for whichever product manager wrote "broadly on track" in the draft you are about to review.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Capacity arithmetic with ring-fenced work**

Your four teams have next-quarter delivery capacities of 30, 24, 36, and 30 team-weeks respectively (120 total). Standing obligations consume capacity first: live-service support takes 20% of each team's capacity, and one team (the 36-week team) must also ring-fence 6 further weeks for a security remediation. Committed new delivery across the portfolio is 74 team-weeks. What is the position?

- A) 96 team-weeks free; 22 weeks of headroom
- B) 90 team-weeks free; 16 weeks of headroom
- C) 90 team-weeks free; 16 weeks over-committed
- D) 84 team-weeks free; 10 weeks of headroom

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Support takes 20% of 120 = 24 team-weeks, leaving 96. The ring-fence takes 6 more from the third team: 96 − 6 = 90 team-weeks genuinely available for new delivery. Against 74 committed: 90 − 74 = 16 weeks of headroom, option B. Option A forgets the ring-fence; option D double-counts it (taking 20% and 6 from the total as if the 6 were portfolio-wide, or misapplying 25% to the third team's gross — either route lands near 84). The distribution check is the lead-level step the headline hides: the third team's own position is 36 − 7.2 (support) − 6 (remediation) = 22.8 weeks — if its committed share exceeds that, the portfolio's comfortable 16-week headroom coexists with one overloaded team, and team-weeks are not fungible across products without onboarding cost. Portfolio capacity truths come in two layers — the aggregate and the allocation — and planning across multiple teams while minimising risk means checking both before telling the programme board the quarter fits. Ensure effective support for the product and its users, meanwhile, is why the 20% comes off the top rather than being squeezed when delivery pressure rises: support capacity raided for features is an outage on layaway.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Comparing business cases with different shapes**

Two cases compete for the same £200,000 of portfolio investment. Case One: £200,000 cost, benefits £90,000 per year for 4 years, delivery confidence 85%. Case Two: £200,000 cost, benefits £70,000 per year for 8 years, delivery confidence 70% — and Case Two's benefits additionally depend on a policy environment reviewed at year 4: if the review goes against the scheme (the team judges this 30% likely), benefits stop at the end of year 4. Using expected values over each case's stated horizon, what is the comparison?

- A) Case One EV ≈ +£106k; Case Two EV ≈ +£192k; Two leads strongly
- B) Case One EV ≈ +£106k; Case Two EV ≈ +£133k; Two leads modestly on expectation, but its edge is back-loaded and exposed to a policy risk it does not control
- C) Case One EV ≈ +£306k; Case Two EV ≈ +£333k
- D) The cases cannot be compared because their horizons differ

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Build each expectation explicitly. Case One: gross benefit £90k × 4 = £360k; apply delivery confidence: × 0.85 = £306k; net of cost: − £200k = +£106k. Case Two has two independent risk layers, so draw the tree. Policy fork first: with 70% probability the year-4 review passes and benefits run the full 8 years (£70k × 8 = £560k); with 30% probability they stop at year 4 (£70k × 4 = £280k). Expected gross benefit given successful delivery: 0.7 × 560 + 0.3 × 280 = 392 + 84 = £476k. Apply delivery confidence: × 0.70 = £333.2k. Net of cost: − £200k = +£133.2k. So One ≈ +£106k, Two ≈ +£133k: option B. Now check what each distractor got wrong, because each is a real reviewing failure: option A prices the policy fork at zero (0.70 × 560 − 200 = £192k) — the most common error with conditional benefits, treating a 30% cliff as if disclosure were mitigation; option C computes both expectations correctly and forgets to subtract the cost — gross presented as net, the oldest trick in a benefits table; option D refuses a comparison that expected-value methods exist precisely to enable. The lead-level content is option B's second clause: Two's £27k edge on expectation is built from stacked round-number probabilities (70%, 70%, 30%) whose error bars dwarf the gap, and its value profile is worse on every dimension expectation ignores — back-loaded (years 5–8 carry the upside), exposed to an unowned policy risk, and slower to payback. When EVs sit this close, the decision migrates from the expectation to the distribution: time-to-value, reversibility, and whose hands hold the risk. Fund the case whose failure you can afford — and in your own papers, show the fork arithmetic explicitly so reviewers never have to reconstruct it.

**Question 7 (moderate) — The programme dashboard's improving average**

Your programme dashboard shows portfolio "average days from idea to live" improving from 120 to 96 days over two quarters. The decomposition behind it: the portfolio shipped 10 items in the earlier quarter (8 standard items averaging 100 days; 2 complex items averaging 200 days) and 12 items in the recent quarter (11 standard items averaging 98 days; 1 complex item at 274 days). Does the improvement reflect faster delivery?

- A) Yes — 96 is better than 120, and both item types improved.
- B) Mostly mix, not speed: standard items barely moved (100 → 98) and the complex item got dramatically slower (200 → 274); the average "improved" because the mix shifted from 20% complex to 8% complex. Verify: earlier: (8×100 + 2×200) ÷ 10 = 120 ✓; lately: (11×98 + 1×274) ÷ 12 = (1,078 + 274) ÷ 12 = 1,352 ÷ 12 ≈ 112.7 — which does not equal 96, so the dashboard's 96 is also inconsistent with its own decomposition and needs auditing before any narrative is reported.
- C) Yes, and the complex-item slowdown is within normal variation.
- D) The dashboard should report medians, which would resolve everything.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Run both checks option B models. Mix check: within-type performance is flat-to-worse (standard 100 → 98 is noise; complex 200 → 274 is a 37% deterioration on the hardest work), so any genuine average improvement could only come from composition — fewer complex items in the mix (20% → 8%). Reconciliation check: recompute the average from the decomposition — earlier: (800 + 400) ÷ 10 = 120, matches; lately: (1,078 + 274) ÷ 12 ≈ 112.7, which contradicts the dashboard's 96. So the headline is doubly wrong: directionally misleading (mix-driven) and arithmetically irreproducible (96 ≠ 112.7 — a data error, a changed definition, or silent exclusions). The audit precedes the narrative: option B is the only response that refuses to interpret a number its own components cannot reproduce. Option A reads the headline; option C waves off a 37% deterioration unexamined; option D names a genuinely better statistic for skewed durations but as a non-sequitur — switching to medians neither explains the irreproducible 96 nor addresses the complex-item slowdown, and "change the metric" is how broken dashboards escape audits. Two portable lead disciplines: always recompute the aggregate from any decomposition you are shown (thirty seconds; catches an embarrassing share of dashboard errors), and when an average improves, ask mix-or-speed before ask why — Simpson's-pattern shifts are the standard way portfolio dashboards flatter themselves. Advocating for data-driven decision making, per your skills, includes being the person data cannot flatter.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Recruitment funnel arithmetic**

You are recruiting product managers. The last campaign: 240 applications; 25% passed sift; of those, 70% attended interview; of attendees, 40% were appointable; you filled 12 of the 17 appointable candidates into posts. For the next campaign you need 20 hires. Assuming the same conversion rates and the same fill rate among appointables, roughly how many applications does the funnel require?

- A) About 405
- B) About 480
- C) About 290
- D) About 680

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Verify the historical chain first, because the stem lets you: 240 × 0.25 = 60 passed sift; × 0.70 = 42 interviewed; × 0.40 = 16.8 ≈ 17 appointable ✓ (matches the stated 17); fill rate among appointables: 12 ÷ 17 ≈ 0.706. Overall application-to-hire conversion: 0.25 × 0.70 × 0.40 × 0.706 = 0.0494 — just under 5%. Applications required for 20 hires: 20 ÷ 0.0494 ≈ 405, option A. The distractors encode the standard slips: option C (≈290) assumes perfect fill — 20 ÷ 0.07 ≈ 286 — forgetting that historically five appointable candidates were lost between "appointable" and "in post"; option D (≈680) applies an extra conversion stage that does not exist in the chain; option B splits the difference plausibly enough to catch guessers. The management content sits behind the arithmetic: a 5% end-to-end yield prices each hire at roughly 20 applications, so a 20-hire campaign is a major attraction exercise — and the leverage analysis writes itself from the chain. The fill rate (0.706) is usually the cheapest stage to improve: appointable candidates lost after assessment are speed-of-offer and candidate-experience problems, not assessment problems, and recovering fill to 0.9 cuts the required applications to about 317 — a third off the attraction budget for the price of faster offers. Being involved in recruiting at all levels, per your role summary, includes exactly this: reading your own funnel's arithmetic to find which stage to fix before scaling the top.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Savings claims that must be reconciled**

Two teams in your portfolio claim savings from the same contact centre budget (£1.8m annually). Team One's case: "our status tracker deflects 30% of progress-chasing calls, which are 40% of all calls — saving 12% of contact costs (£216,000)." Team Two's case: "our redesigned guidance deflects 25% of confusion-driven calls, which are 35% of all calls — saving 8.75% of contact costs (£157,500)." A finance reviewer asks whether the portfolio can book £373,500. What is the correct caution?

- A) Yes — the calculations are each internally correct, so the savings add.
- B) Each calculation is internally correct, but the claims may overlap: a progress-chasing call can also be confusion-driven, and both teams may be deflecting some of the same calls; the portfolio should measure actual call volumes against a shared baseline, book the observed reduction, and attribute by evaluation rather than by adding independent models.
- C) Neither saving is real until posts are removed.
- D) Book the larger claim only and discard the smaller.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Verify internal arithmetic: Team One: 0.30 × 0.40 = 0.12 → £1.8m × 0.12 = £216,000 ✓. Team Two: 0.25 × 0.35 = 0.0875 → £157,500 ✓. The addition is where the error would live: summing to £373,500 (20.75% of all calls) assumes the two call categories — and the two interventions' effects — are disjoint, which nothing establishes; call taxonomies overlap (a caller confused by guidance about their application's progress is in both categories), and if the categories share members, both models claim credit for deflecting some of the same calls. Double-counted benefits across a portfolio is among the most common findings against programme benefit claims, and option B states the remedy in the auditable order: shared baseline, observed total reduction (the contact centre's actual volumes cannot double-count), attribution by evaluation (which intervention moved which segment) rather than by model addition. Option A audits each case and never the sum — but the portfolio books the sum, and the lead is the person positioned to see both cases at once; that positional duty is the question's point. Option C re-litigates capacity-versus-cash-releasing benefits (a classification question, not a validity one — worth a line in the case, not a veto). Option D resolves double-counting by arbitrary deletion, punishing whichever team submitted second. The portable rule: benefits models add only when their populations provably don't — otherwise measure the total and divide it, never the reverse. Determining benefits and persuading others, at portfolio altitude, means your name stands behind the sum, not just the summands.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Projection with a capacity ceiling**

Digital uptake of your end-to-end service has grown: 40%, 46%, 52%, 58% over four quarters. The board paper projects "76% by Q8 on current trend". Your assisted-channel analysis shows: the remaining non-digital population (42% of 500,000 annual users = 210,000) includes 90,000 users your research classifies as "digital with support" (convertible with investment) and 60,000 classified as "unlikely digital" (long-term assisted-channel users); the remainder are "digital-capable, not yet converted". What ceiling does this imply, and how should the Q8 projection be treated?

- A) The linear trend is sound: 58% + 3 quarters × 6 points = 76% by Q8.
- B) The classification implies a practical ceiling near 88% (100% minus the 12% "unlikely digital"), so 76% remains arithmetically below the ceiling — but growth must slow as the easy conversions exhaust: the remaining "capable, not yet converted" pool is 60,000 users (12% of the base), sustaining at most two more quarters of 6-point growth before further gains require converting the "with support" group, which needs the investment the linear projection silently assumes; the honest paper shows the trend, the ceiling, the pool arithmetic, and the investment dependency.
- C) The ceiling is 58% and growth has already stopped.
- D) Project 100% eventually, since all populations convert given time.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Do the pool arithmetic the linear trend skips. Non-digital: 42% of 500,000 = 210,000 users, decomposed as 90,000 "digital with support" (18% of base), 60,000 "unlikely digital" (12%), leaving 210,000 − 150,000 = 60,000 "capable, not yet converted" (12%). Each 6-point quarter converts 6% of 500,000 = 30,000 users. The free-conversion pool (capable-not-yet) sustains exactly 60,000 ÷ 30,000 = two more quarters at trend — to 70% by Q6 — after which the frontier hits the "with support" population, whose conversion is not free: it needs the assisted-digital investment, at whatever slower rate that machinery achieves. So the 76%-by-Q8 projection is not impossible (ceiling ≈ 88%), but its final points are purchased, not extrapolated, and a paper presenting 76% as "current trend" is booking the purchase without showing the invoice. Option B's required disclosure — trend, ceiling, pool exhaustion, investment dependency — converts a hope into a decision the board can actually make (fund the support conversion or accept ~70% plateau). Option A is straight-line innumeracy about segmented populations; option C mistakes a decomposition for a stop sign (nothing says growth has ceased — two strong quarters remain); option D denies the "unlikely digital" classification, which is not pessimism but service design: 60,000 users with a durable assisted-channel need are a population to serve well, not a conversion backlog — and note the strategic corollary your **Applying user-centred insights** skill would add: the assisted channel is therefore permanent infrastructure to be resourced, not a transition cost to be minimised. The portable technique: any projection over a finite population must be checked against segment pools — divide the projected gain by the pool that supplies it, and the quarter the easy pool empties is the quarter your growth model changes.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Auditing a unit-cost comparison used for a retirement case**

A paper recommends retiring Product K, citing: "K costs £8.40 per transaction against the portfolio average of £3.10." You pull the workings: K handles 15,000 transactions annually with £126,000 total allocated cost — but K's allocation includes £54,000 as a one-third share of a legacy data centre used by three products, allocated equally; the other two products in that data centre handle 90,000 and 45,000 transactions. The portfolio average of £3.10 is computed across products whose platform costs are allocated by usage, not equally. What does a consistent comparison show?

- A) K at £8.40 versus £3.10 stands; retire K.
- B) On usage-based allocation, K's data-centre share becomes 15,000 ÷ 150,000 = 10% of £162,000 = £16,200 (versus £54,000), so K's total falls to £126,000 − £54,000 + £16,200 = £88,200, and unit cost to £88,200 ÷ 15,000 = £5.88 — still above the portfolio's £3.10, but the retirement case's headline overstated K's excess cost by 43%, and the decision should be re-argued on consistent figures (and on K's users' alternatives, which unit cost alone cannot decide).
- C) K's unit cost is actually below portfolio average once corrected.
- D) Allocation methods never change decisions, only presentation.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The inconsistency is methodological: K is costed with equal-shares platform allocation while the comparator average uses usage-based allocation — a comparison across methods, which is meaningless until harmonised. Harmonise to usage (the portfolio's own convention): the data centre's total cost is £54,000 × 3 = £162,000; total transactions across its three tenants: 15,000 + 90,000 + 45,000 = 150,000; K's usage share: 15,000 ÷ 150,000 = 10%, so £16,200. K's corrected total: 126,000 − 54,000 + 16,200 = £88,200; unit cost 88,200 ÷ 15,000 = £5.88. Option B — and check option C's temptation: £5.88 remains 90% above the £3.10 average, so the correction weakens the case's arithmetic without reversing its direction; C overshoots. That calibrated conclusion is the lead-level answer's signature: the paper's headline (£8.40, 171% above average) overstated the true excess (£5.88, 90% above) by inflating K with a legacy facility's fixed costs that K's small volume did not drive — equal-shares allocation systematically punishes low-volume products, which is precisely why retirement cases favour it. Option B's final clause completes the audit: even corrected, unit cost is one input — K's 15,000 users' alternatives, transition costs, and any statutory obligations K discharges belong in a retirement decision, and a lead who has guided products through to retirement — your **Life cycle management** skill in its final phase — knows the unit-cost line is where the argument starts, not where it ends. Option D is refuted by the numbers just computed: allocation moved the headline 30%; methods that move headlines move decisions. The reusable audit: in any cross-product cost comparison, first question every allocated line — "allocated how, and is the method uniform across the comparison?" — because allocation is where portfolio politics does its quietest work.

**Question 12 (challenging) — The quarter's story in five numbers**

Preparing your management-team report, you assemble the quarter's portfolio figures: transactions up 9% (1.10m → 1.20m); total cost up 4% (£2.50m → £2.60m); completion rate down 1.2 points (83.4% → 82.2%); support contacts up 15% (48,000 → 55,200); benefits realised £310,000 against a £280,000 profile. Which single-paragraph synthesis is most accurate and most useful?

- A) "A strong quarter: volumes up, benefits ahead of profile, and unit costs improved from £2.27 to £2.17 per transaction."
- B) "A mixed quarter requiring one investigation: demand grew 9% and unit cost improved 5% (£2.27 → £2.17), with benefits £30k ahead of profile — but completion fell 1.2 points and support contacts rose 15%, outpacing volume growth (contacts per 1,000 transactions up from 43.6 to 46.0), which together suggest a developing user-experience problem that the cost and benefit lines have not yet felt; diagnostic underway, findings by [date]."
- C) "A concerning quarter: completion is falling and contacts are rising, indicating portfolio decline."
- D) "A stable quarter: most figures moved only slightly."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Compute what each option claims. Unit cost: 2.50 ÷ 1.10 = £2.27; 2.60 ÷ 1.20 = £2.17 — improved ~4.6% ✓ (volume grew faster than cost). Contacts per 1,000: 48,000 ÷ 1,100 = 43.6; 55,200 ÷ 1,200 = 46.0 — up 5.4% per transaction, so the 15% contact rise is not explained by the 9% volume rise alone ✓. Benefits: +£30k against profile ✓. Completion: −1.2 points ✓. Option B is the only synthesis that holds all five, normalised where normalisation is the honest view (contacts per transaction, cost per transaction) — and, crucially, that reads the cross-metric structure: completion falling while normalised contacts rise is the classic leading pair for a degrading user experience, which cost and benefit lines lag because they aggregate slowly. Option A is a true-facts-false-story synthesis: everything it says is arithmetically correct and its selection buries the pair of signals that will own next quarter's narrative — the report that ages worst. Option C inverts the error, discarding genuine efficiency and benefit performance to tell a monotone decline story two metrics don't support. Option D calls 15% and 9% moves "slight". The craft option B models — and the reason this question closes the section — is that portfolio numeric fluency terminates in composition: every figure computed correctly, normalised appropriately, and then arranged to say what is actually happening, including the part still unknown, with a date attached to the unknown. That paragraph is the numeric section's entire toolkit — rates, normalisation, variance, leading-versus-lagging structure — compressed into the artefact your role actually ships: the honest quarterly story. Communicate business-value propositions, per your skills, includes communicating the quarter the propositions live in.

### Preparation tips

- Drill the portfolio aggregation invariants until reflexive: sum numerators and denominators before dividing; harmonise allocation methods before comparing unit costs; reconcile every headline against its decomposition (recompute — thirty seconds); and never add benefit models whose populations might overlap.
- For expected-value questions, draw the probability tree — delivery risk and environmental risk as separate branches — and net the cost at the end. When options cluster near parity, expect the keyed answer to be the one discussing risk shape, time-to-value, and reversibility rather than declaring a winner on £3k of expectation.
- Check every projection against its supplying pool: gain per period ÷ pool remaining = periods before the model must change. Segmented populations (uptake, conversion, deflection) all obey this; linear extrapolation past pool exhaustion is the flaw to spot.
- Normalise before narrating: per transaction, per 1,000, against profile, on consistent baselines. In synthesis questions, the best answer is almost always the one whose every clause survives recomputation and whose selection hides nothing that will matter next quarter.
- Practise on your genuine artefacts: this month's programme finance pack (recompute the burn), your portfolio dashboard (reconcile one headline to its parts), the last two benefit claims (check for population overlap). Real-artefact practice both prepares you and occasionally saves real money.
- Keep test discipline: bank the four or five quick items early, show one line of working per step on chained calculations, and when two options are arithmetically close, re-read the stem for the assumption that separates them — at this level the discriminator is usually interpretive, not computational.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- Averaging percentages, or reporting a blend without its spread. The portfolio figure is sum-over-sum; the management information is the outlier the blend conceals.
- Comparing unit costs across inconsistent allocation methods. Equal-shares versus usage-based allocation can move a product's apparent cost by tens of percent — harmonise first, and state the method.
- Adding savings models that may deflect the same units. Measured totals divide; independent models don't add. The portfolio lead is the person positioned to catch the overlap — and accountable when it isn't caught.
- Extrapolating linear trends across segmented populations. Pools exhaust; ceilings bind; conversions stop being free. Show the trend and its supply.
- Accepting aggregates their own decompositions cannot reproduce. If the parts don't recompute to the whole, the audit precedes the narrative — always.
- Selecting true facts into false stories. Arithmetic correctness is necessary, not sufficient: the synthesis that omits the leading indicators is wrong in the way that matters most at your level, because yours is the version the management team believes.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment measures the precision with which you read, interpret, and infer from written material — at lead level, the material through which portfolios are actually governed: programme board papers, strategy documents, assessment reports on other teams, procurement and supplier text, policy instructions, research syntheses, and the recommendation papers your product managers draft for your review and your name.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 15 to 30 minutes: short passages with questions, predominantly true / false / cannot say (True: the passage guarantees it; False: the passage contradicts it; Cannot say: neither), plus best-summary, assumption identification, inference, and clear-writing judgement items. Scoring is objective. The governing rule never changes: answer from the passage alone — and at lead level, where your domain experience makes almost every distractor plausible, that discipline is tested hardest.

Why verbal precision is a lead-level control function: you operate at the altitude where words become commitments. You review and sign papers whose sentences will be quoted in governance for years; you direct the stakeholder relationship strategy, where the difference between what a director said and what your product manager heard becomes your problem at the next board; you contribute to assessments of other teams, where your written findings carry consequence for their funding and their morale; and you coach product managers whose drafting you must improve without rewriting — which requires articulating exactly why a sentence fails, not merely sensing it. You also read upward: ministerial commitments, policy instructions, and legal advice arrive as prose whose modal verbs, scope limiters, and conditions define your portfolio's actual obligations, as distinct from its assumed ones. The questions below train that double fluency — forensic reading and precise writing — and each explanation is written to be usable in your coaching conversations verbatim.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Comprehension of governance, policy, and assessment text** maps to your duties in **programme management activities**, **contributing to the assessment of other teams**, and **ensuring teams comply with appropriate standards**: extracting exactly what documents require, permit, defer, and omit.
- **True/false/cannot-say discipline** maps to **Managing product outcomes** and **Applying user-centred insights**: portfolio claims must match portfolio evidence — no over-claim survives an assessment panel, and no under-claim serves your teams.
- **Assumption and inference analysis** maps to **Creating value for money** and **Strategic ownership**: cases, strategies, and survivor stories are arguments whose unstated premises you are paid to surface — supporting strategic decision making means finding what must be true for the recommendation to hold.
- **Commitment-strength reading** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**: directing the relationship strategy and negotiating with senior stakeholders requires perfect pitch for the difference between endorsed and approved, anticipated and committed, noted and agreed.
- **Clear-writing judgement** maps to **Product leadership** and coaching: your product managers' papers improve at the rate you can diagnose their prose — and your own escalations, findings, and syntheses set the portfolio's writing standard.

### Practice questions

For questions 1 to 6, decide whether the statement is True (definitely follows from the passage), False (definitely contradicted by the passage), or Cannot say (the passage gives insufficient information). A fourth option is included where relevant.

**Question 1 (easy) — A programme instruction's actual scope**

Passage: "All programmes must submit quarterly benefits returns to the portfolio office. Programmes in closedown are exempt from quarterly returns but must submit a final benefits statement within 60 days of closure. The portfolio office may request interim returns from any programme at any time."

Statement: "A programme in closedown has no reporting obligations to the portfolio office."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, until closure completes

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The exemption is precise: closedown programmes are exempt from quarterly returns — one instrument, not all obligations. Two others persist on the passage's face: the final benefits statement (within 60 days of closure) and exposure to interim-return requests, which the office "may request... from any programme at any time" — "any programme" includes those in closedown. "No reporting obligations" is therefore contradicted twice: False. Option D fails on the same grounds — the interim-return power applies during closedown too. The reading pattern is exemption-scoping: an exemption relieves exactly the duty it names, and the reflex to check is "exempt from what, precisely?" — because teams granted a named exemption reliably behave as if granted a general one, and the lead reviewing their compliance position is where that inflation should be caught. The same discipline applies when you negotiate exemptions for your own portfolio: get the relieved duty named in writing, and assume everything unnamed still binds.

**Question 2 (easy) — What an assessment report found**

Passage (service assessment report on a team you support): "The panel was impressed by the team's user research operation and its evidence trail from findings to iterations. However, the panel was not shown evidence that the service has been tested with users of assistive technology, and the team could not describe the assisted digital support route. The service is rated 'not met' on point 5. All other points are rated 'met'. Reassessment of point 5 only is required within three months."

Statement: "The panel found that the service is inaccessible to users of assistive technology."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, which is why point 5 was not met

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Read what the panel actually recorded: it "was not shown evidence" of assistive-technology testing. Absence of evidence of testing is not evidence of inaccessibility — the service may work perfectly with screen readers and simply never have been tested, or may fail badly; the panel's finding is compatible with both worlds, so the statement can be neither confirmed nor contradicted: Cannot say. Option D repackages the conflation attractively — but point 5 failed on the evidence gap and the missing support-route description, not on any demonstrated inaccessibility. The distinction is not pedantry; it drives the remediation. A team that hears "the panel found us inaccessible" may launch a redesign; a team that hears accurately — "we could not evidence testing" — runs the testing, fixes what it finds, and documents the route: a three-month-shaped task, matching the reassessment window. Contributing to the assessment of other teams, per your **Life cycle management** skill, includes translating panel findings for teams without inflation or deflation — the lead who relays findings at their exact strength is the one whose reassurance and whose warnings both get believed.

**Question 3 (easy) — Reading a ministerial commitment precisely**

Passage (from a written ministerial statement): "We will introduce a digital application route for the carers' support scheme by April, and we expect the majority of applications to be made digitally within two years of launch. Paper applications will continue to be accepted for those who need them."

Statement: "The minister has committed to ending paper applications within two years."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, once digital uptake reaches a majority

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Separate the three clauses by strength. "We will introduce... by April" — a commitment, dated. "We expect the majority... within two years" — an expectation about uptake share, not a commitment, and about the majority, not the totality. "Paper applications will continue to be accepted for those who need them" — a commitment in the opposite direction from the statement's claim: the passage promises paper's continuation, unbounded by any date. Ending paper within two years is thus directly contradicted: False. Option D manufactures a trigger the text nowhere contains. The lead-level stakes are concrete: a portfolio that reads this statement as a paper-switch-off mandate will design (and fund) a channel-closure programme the minister never asked for and the commitment actually forbids — while the accurate reading tasks you with a dual-channel service where the assisted route is permanent infrastructure. Ministerial text rewards exactly this clause-by-clause strength audit; the expensive misreadings in government are usually upgrades — expectations read as commitments, majorities read as totalities — performed by people planning in good faith. Direct your teams to plan from the verbs, not the vibe.

**Question 4 (moderate) — A supplier's contract-variation letter**

Passage (supplier letter): "Further to our discussions, we confirm we can accommodate the additional API capacity from 1 September, subject to agreement of the revised charging schedule attached. Absent such agreement by 31 July, we will continue to deliver at current capacity under the existing terms. We note that current capacity may be insufficient for your projected autumn volumes, as discussed, and we cannot accept liability for service degradation arising from volumes exceeding contracted capacity."

Statement: "If the revised charging schedule is not agreed by 31 July, the supplier will be in breach of contract should autumn service degradation occur due to excess volumes."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, because the supplier was warned about the volumes

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Trace the letter's carefully built position. The capacity increase is conditional (agreement by 31 July); failing that, delivery continues "at current capacity under the existing terms" — performance of the contract, not breach of it. The final sentence completes the shield: degradation "arising from volumes exceeding contracted capacity" is excess demand against the contracted level, and the supplier both flags the risk in advance and expressly declines liability for it. A supplier delivering contracted capacity while your volumes exceed it is not in breach — the statement inverts the letter's legal effect: False. Option D reverses the direction of the warning's work: the supplier's "as discussed" notation protects the supplier (it evidences that you knew), not you. Now read the letter as its recipient must: this is a well-drafted commercial squeeze — a dated condition, a priced remedy, and a pre-built defence — and the clock is the mechanism, because every week of non-agreement narrows your alternatives toward autumn. The lead response is to move the decision (agree, negotiate the schedule, or source capacity elsewhere) at the letter's tempo, not your quarterly one, and to ensure your own correspondence never concedes that current capacity is adequate. Influencing and negotiating with senior stakeholders, per your **Stakeholder relationship management** skill, extends to suppliers: the negotiation is happening in the letters, and the side that reads structure — condition, default, liability — wins it.

**Question 5 (moderate) — The strategy sentence and the reorganisation**

Passage (departmental strategy): "Product teams should be organised around end-to-end user journeys rather than internal organisational structures. Where legacy contracts or statutory boundaries make journey-based teams impractical, directorates must document the constraint and the planned path to journey alignment, reviewed annually."

Statement: "A directorate that keeps a team organised around an internal structure, documents that a legacy contract makes journey alignment impractical, and reviews the position annually, is compliant with the strategy as stated — provided it also documents a planned path to journey alignment."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) False, because the strategy prohibits structure-based teams outright

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Parse the two-tier structure. Tier one: a default with "should" force — journey-based organisation. Tier two: a conditional compliance route where the default is impractical, carrying three cumulative duties — document the constraint, document the planned path to alignment, review annually. The statement's directorate invokes a qualifying constraint (legacy contract — one of the two named categories) and discharges all three duties; the proviso in the statement's tail ("provided it also documents a planned path") supplies the third element explicitly. Every requirement of the exception route is met: True. Option D reads tier one as absolute, erasing the exception the passage spends half its length constructing — the mirror image of Question 1's inflation error: there, an exemption was read too broadly; here, a prohibition is. The craft point for your role: strategies with documented-exception routes are governance instruments, and "ensure strategic alignment across the organisation" — your **Strategic ownership** skill — is largely the administration of exactly these routes: distinguishing the directorate that is compliant-via-exception (documented, pathed, reviewed) from the one that is simply non-compliant with paperwork. The annual review is where the two diverge — a path to alignment that never advances across reviews is the tell — and the lead who tracks exception-route reviews holds the strategy's real enforcement mechanism.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Research synthesis at portfolio altitude**

Passage: "Across the portfolio's three products, this quarter's research included 41 sessions. In Product A's sessions (n=18), users consistently struggled to understand eligibility rules. Product B's sessions (n=15) focused on caseworkers, who praised the new queue design but flagged slow document loading. Product C's sessions (n=8, all with existing frequent users) surfaced no significant usability issues."

Statement: "Product C has fewer usability issues than Products A and B."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, since its sessions surfaced none

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The comparative claim requires comparable evidence, and the passage's own sampling notes dismantle that. Product C's eight sessions were conducted entirely with existing frequent users — the population most practised at navigating whatever issues exist, and structurally least likely to surface novice-facing problems; "no significant issues found among experts, n=8" is weak evidence about the product's usability, and no evidence at all for a cross-product ranking against A's findings (n=18, population unstated) and B's (a different user type again — caseworkers). C might genuinely be the cleanest product; it might merely be the least-scrutinised: Cannot say. Option D converts absence-of-findings into absence-of-issues — the same inferential error as Question 2, now driven by sampling rather than by scope, and the pair is worth teaching together: what wasn't tested and who wasn't asked are the two silent qualifiers on every "no issues found". The lead-level action the passage actually licenses is a research-portfolio correction: C's next sessions should recruit new and infrequent users, and your role's named skill — advocate for a range of effective research approaches across the organisation, and for research with all types of users — is precisely the authority to require it. Portfolio research synthesis is where sampling artefacts become strategy errors; the lead who reads the "n=" and the recruitment line before the findings is the control on that conversion.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Best summary of a contested board discussion**

Passage (board paper section you must summarise for your teams): "The board discussed the proposal to consolidate the two casework products. The finance member supported consolidation on cost grounds. The operations member opposed, citing migration risk during the winter peak. The chair concluded that consolidation is agreed in principle, subject to a migration plan that avoids the November–February peak, with the plan to return to the March board for approval before any migration activity begins."

Which relay to your teams is most faithful?

- A) "The board approved consolidation; migration planning starts now for delivery from March."
- B) "Consolidation is agreed in principle. No migration activity is authorised yet: we must produce a migration plan that avoids November–February, and the March board must approve that plan before anything moves. Planning work can and should start now."
- C) "The board was split, so consolidation is on hold until finance and operations agree."
- D) "The board rejected consolidation because of migration risk."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The chair's conclusion is a three-part governance object: agreement in principle (a real decision — the direction is set), a condition on substance (the plan must avoid the winter peak), and a condition on process (March board approval precedes any migration activity). Option B preserves all three at strength and adds the one legitimate operational inference — planning work, as distinct from migration activity, is what the conclusion implicitly commissions, since a plan must exist to be approved in March. Option A commits two upgrades: "approved" (in-principle agreement is not approval — the passage explicitly reserves approval to March) and "delivery from March" (March approves the plan; it does not start migration, whose earliest window the peak-avoidance condition actually shapes). Option C mistakes the discussion for the decision — boards are supposed to contain disagreement, and the chair's conclusion supersedes the members' positions; relaying the split as the outcome teaches your teams to lobby members rather than meet conditions. Option D relays the losing argument as the verdict. The coaching frame this question hands you: a board conclusion decomposes into decision + conditions + reserved approvals, and the relay test is whether a team acting solely on your summary would do exactly what governance authorised — no less (option C and D paralysis), no more (option A's unauthorised migration). Directing stakeholder strategy for your teams includes being their reliable instrument for exactly this translation.

**Question 8 (moderate) — The assumption under a portfolio-level argument**

Passage (from a paper you are reviewing): "We should move all three products onto the shared design system, because it will eliminate the visual inconsistencies users currently experience when moving between our products during a single journey."

Which unstated assumption is most load-bearing?

- A) That users actually move between the three products within single journeys in meaningful numbers — the benefit is defined entirely by cross-product journeys, which the paper asserts exist but never evidences.
- B) That the shared design system is cheaper to maintain than three local ones.
- C) That designers prefer working in shared systems.
- D) That visual consistency is measurable.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Skeletonise: adopt shared system → eliminate cross-product visual inconsistency → benefit to users "moving between our products during a single journey". The entire benefit is scoped to that journey pattern — so the argument stands or falls on whether the pattern is real at meaningful scale, which the paper assumes in a subordinate clause and never evidences. If cross-product journeys are rare, the stated benefit approaches zero regardless of how completely the inconsistencies are eliminated. That is option A, and note the diagnostic that found it: the load-bearing assumption in benefit arguments almost always lives inside the sentence's beneficiary clause — who exactly experiences the claimed gain, and how many of them exist? Options B and C are real considerations for the decision but external to this argument's logic (the paper claims a user-experience benefit, not a maintenance or designer-experience one); option D is a measurement quibble that the argument survives. The review move the question models is the constructive one: the assumption is cheaply testable — analytics can count cross-product journeys within sessions this week — so the reviewing lead converts "unevidenced premise" into "evidence request with a date", which either grounds the case or redirects it toward the benefits shared design systems actually deliver at scale (development speed, accessibility-by-default, maintenance). Helping others make value-based decisions means teaching authors that the strongest version of their case is the one whose premises are checked before the board checks them.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Two documents and a delegated authority**

Passage 1 (scheme of delegation): "Leads may approve product spend up to £50,000 per item within approved programme budgets. Items exceeding £50,000, and any item that commits the organisation beyond the current financial year, require director approval regardless of value."

Passage 2 (email from your product manager): "Great news — the annual licence renewal came in at £48,000, under your £50k threshold, so I've told the supplier you'll sign this week. It's the usual 12-month term starting 1 February."

Statement: "Under the scheme of delegation, this renewal is within your authority to approve."

- A) True — £48,000 is below the £50,000 threshold
- B) False — a 12-month term starting 1 February extends beyond the current financial year, triggering the director-approval requirement regardless of the item's value
- C) Cannot say — it depends on whether the programme budget is approved
- D) True — licence renewals are routine and the delegation is designed for exactly this

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The delegation has two independent triggers for director approval, joined by "and any" — value above £50,000, or commitment beyond the current financial year "regardless of value". The renewal passes the value test (£48,000 < £50,000) and fails the duration test: a 12-month term from 1 February runs ten months into the next financial year (UK financial years ending 31 March), committing the organisation beyond the current one. The second trigger fires; director approval is required; the statement is False — as is your product manager's cheerful conclusion, which audited the visible threshold and missed the temporal one. Option C invents a dependency the passage resolves (the delegation presupposes "within approved programme budgets" as a scope condition — but nothing in the stem questions the budget's status, and the duration trigger fires regardless); option D substitutes custom for text, the exact instinct delegation schemes exist to check. Three lead-level notes. First, the reading pattern: multi-trigger authorities must be checked trigger by trigger — the visible test (value) is usually the decoy; the structural one (duration, novelty, category) is where breaches happen in good faith. Second, the situational overlay: the supplier has been told you will sign this week — your product manager has created a commitment expectation outside the authority chain, so your response manages both the correction (route to the director, with the timeline honestly reset with the supplier) and the coaching (the two-trigger lesson, delivered as method, not reproach). Third, the systemic fix: if licence renewals routinely straddle financial years, the standing answer is either scheme amendment or early annual planning — leads who spot the recurring near-breach redesign the pipeline, not just the instance.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Clear-writing judgement: the assessment finding**

You are drafting the key finding in your contribution to another team's service assessment. The evidence: the team deployed weekly to a staging environment but only quarterly to production, because a manual security review board meets quarterly; users therefore waited up to three months for fixes to known issues, including two accessibility fixes. Which drafting serves the assessment's purpose best?

- A) "The team's deployment practices fall below expected standards and require significant improvement going forward."
- B) "Deployment cadence is constrained by governance outside the team's control: weekly staging deployments demonstrate strong engineering practice, but production releases occur only quarterly because the manual security review board meets quarterly. The user impact is concrete — fixes for known issues, including two accessibility fixes, waited up to three months. Recommendation: the department should enable continuous security assurance (automated checks plus risk-based review) so production cadence can match the team's demonstrated capability; the team should meanwhile agree an expedited-release route for accessibility and security fixes with the review board."
- C) "The security review board is the problem and should be disbanded."
- D) "Deployment happens weekly to staging and quarterly to production. The security review board meets quarterly. Two accessibility fixes were delayed. Further detail is available in the technical annex."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** An assessment finding exists to change something, and its quality is measured by whether the right actor can act on it accurately. Option B does the four things the genre requires. It locates causation correctly — the constraint is departmental governance, not team capability, and it says so explicitly, which matters because option A's drafting ("the team's practices fall below standards") would misdirect the remediation at the actor who cannot fix it while demoralising a team whose staging cadence shows the capability is already there; findings that mis-assign cause produce action plans that miss. It evidences impact concretely (three-month waits, accessibility fixes named — the detail that converts a process observation into a user-harm finding with the priority that carries). It recommends at two levels with the right addressees — the department for the structural fix (continuous assurance), the team for the interim mitigation (expedited route for accessibility and security) — mirroring the causal analysis, so each actor receives an action within their power. And its tone permits adoption: option C is causally aligned but written to be rejected — "disband the board" attacks the security function rather than its cadence, guaranteeing a defensive response and no change; assurance is the requirement, the quarterly manual bottleneck is the flaw. Option D is the fact-sheet failure: accurate, complete, and inert — no causation, no weight, no recommendation; it delegates the actual finding to whoever reads the annex, which is nobody. The transferable template for your assessment contributions and your coaching of others': capability observed, constraint located, impact evidenced, recommendation addressed to the actor who owns each part. Findings drafted to that template are the ones panels adopt verbatim — which is the quiet measure of assessment writing done well.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Inference across an evaluation and a decision memo**

Passage 1 (evaluation extract): "The proactive-renewal trial automatically renewed licences for users who had opted in, unless their details had changed. Auto-renewed users showed 98% retention against 84% for the manual-renewal comparison group. However, opt-in required an existing online account, and opt-in users were disproportionately long-tenured, high-engagement licensees."

Passage 2 (decision memo, drafted by your product manager): "The evaluation shows auto-renewal increases retention by 14 points. Extending auto-renewal to all licensees will therefore lift overall retention toward 98%."

Statement: "The memo's projection overstates the likely effect of extending auto-renewal to all licensees."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say — the extension has not been tried
- D) True only if the evaluation's arithmetic is wrong

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The statement asks about the projection's relationship to its own evidence, and that can be assessed from the passages. The trial's 98% was achieved on a self-selected population the evaluation itself flags as unrepresentative: opt-in users needed an online account and skewed long-tenured and high-engagement — characteristics that independently predict high retention. Their 14-point edge over the comparison group therefore bundles the mechanism's effect with the selection effect, in unknown proportions; extending to all licensees means extending to the shorter-tenured, lower-engagement population whose baseline retention is lower and whose response to auto-renewal is unmeasured. "Toward 98%" projects the best-case population's outcome onto everyone — an overstatement follows from the passages' own content, making the statement True, not Cannot say: option C confuses "the precise effect is unknown" (correct) with "whether the memo overstates is unknown" (incorrect — the memo claims the top of a range the evidence cannot support; the direction of error is determinate even though its size is not). That distinction — direction versus magnitude — is the question's teachable core, and it recurs throughout portfolio life: you can often know a claim is biased upward from its method alone, before any new data arrives. Option D misdirects to arithmetic that was never the flaw. The memo's honest redraft, which is your review note to the product manager: report the 14-point finding with its selection caveat, project a range whose lower bound assumes the mechanism adds little beyond selection, and propose a staged extension with retention measured by tenure band — converting an overstatement into a test. Advocating for continuous, honest use of insight — your **Applying user-centred insights** skill at strategic altitude — is mostly this: keeping the gap between what was measured and what is claimed at zero, especially in memos travelling upward under your portfolio's name.

**Question 12 (challenging) — The policy instruction and the three readings**

Passage (policy instruction to your directorate): "In light of the efficiency review, directorates should prioritise work that reduces administrative burden on small businesses. New transactional services for business users must include a burden impact statement. Existing services need not produce statements retrospectively, but any material change to an existing business-facing service must be accompanied by one."

Your portfolio's plan includes: (1) a brand-new licensing application service for small businesses; (2) a major redesign of the existing export-declaration journey; (3) routine bug fixes and content updates to the existing grants portal; (4) a new internal casework tool used only by departmental staff.

Statement: "Under the instruction, items 1 and 2 require burden impact statements; items 3 and 4 do not."

- A) True
- B) False — item 4 also requires a statement, since efficiency applies to staff burden too
- C) Cannot say — "material change" is undefined, so item 2's status is unresolvable
- D) True, provided item 3's changes are genuinely non-material

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Apply the instruction's two triggers to each item. Item 1: a new transactional service for business users — the first trigger applies squarely: statement required. Item 2: a "major redesign" of an existing business-facing service — the second trigger requires a statement for "any material change", and a major redesign is a material change on any reasonable construction: statement required. Item 4: the instruction's scope is burden "on small businesses" and services for "business users"; an internal staff tool sits outside it entirely — option B's expansion imports the efficiency review's spirit to override the instruction's stated scope, the over-reading error. Item 3: routine bug fixes and content updates are presumptively non-material — but here is why D beats A: "material" is a judgement the instruction delegates to its readers, and item 3's exemption holds only while its changes stay genuinely routine; a "content update" that rewrites eligibility guidance could well be material to business users. Option D preserves the statement's conclusion while flagging the one classification that is contingent rather than categorical — which is precisely the reading a lead should produce, because it converts into portfolio practice: a lightweight materiality test for grants-portal changes, so the exemption is verified per change rather than assumed per service. Option C overcorrects into paralysis: undefined terms in policy instructions are delegations of reasonable judgement, not defects that suspend the instruction — "major redesign = material" does not await a definitions annex. The closing lesson of the section sits here deliberately: at lead level, the best reading of an instruction is usually neither the literal minimum nor the expansive maximum but the administrable one — the reading you can operate consistently across a portfolio, defend at review, and teach your product managers to apply without you in the room. Ensuring teams comply with appropriate standards is, in the end, the craft of building exactly those readings.

### Preparation tips

- Run the strength audit on every passage: mark each verb as commitment, expectation, permission, or aspiration before reading the statements. Most lead-level items turn on an upgrade or downgrade between those categories — as do most portfolio misunderstandings.
- Check exemptions and authorities structurally: "exempt from what, precisely?" and "which triggers, checked independently?" The visible test (the named duty, the value threshold) is routinely the decoy; the unnamed duty and the second trigger are where the answer lives.
- For research and evaluation passages, read the sampling line before the findings line: who was recruited, who opted in, what "n=" hides. Then separate direction from magnitude — method alone often determines which way a claim errs, even when it cannot say by how much.
- For summary and relay questions, decompose conclusions into decision + conditions + reserved approvals, and test each candidate against "what would a team do on this summary alone?" The faithful relay authorises exactly what governance authorised.
- Practise findings-drafting against the four-part template — capability observed, constraint located, impact evidenced, recommendation addressed to the owning actor — on a real document this week. It doubles as preparation for your next assessment contribution.
- Coach as you practise: for every question you get right, articulate the rule well enough to teach it. At lead level, reasoning you cannot articulate is reasoning you cannot delegate — and the articulation test catches your own soft spots first.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- Upgrading claim strength while reading — expectations into commitments, in-principle agreements into approvals, majorities into totalities. The expensive misreadings are optimistic upgrades made in good faith by people planning ahead.
- Treating absence of evidence as evidence of absence: untested is not inaccessible; unsurfaced is not absent; "no issues found" carries its sampling frame with it, always.
- Reading exemptions broadly and prohibitions absolutely. Exemptions relieve the named duty only; defaults with documented-exception routes are compliance mechanisms, not loopholes — and not absolutes either.
- Checking only the visible trigger of a multi-trigger authority or instruction. Value thresholds, named duties, and headline rules are decoys; duration clauses, scope limiters, and "regardless of value" tails decide the hard cases.
- Confusing unknown magnitude with unknown direction. A selection-biased projection overstates even when the true effect is unmeasurable; say so, and require the range.
- Producing — or accepting — fact-complete, judgement-free prose. Findings without located causation, relays without preserved conditions, and memos without addressed recommendations transfer the real work to readers who will not do it. At your level, the unfinished paragraph is the one with your name on it.
