Psychometric assessments are structured, standardised exercises that measure the mental capabilities and behavioural tendencies that predict success in a role. For a business relationship manager — sometimes known as a business partner — that means far more than abstract puzzles. The assessments described here are job-specific: they use the kinds of artefacts you genuinely handle — opportunity pipelines, engagement logs, business cases, stakeholder maps, satisfaction survey results, service review packs, and user needs evidence — and the kinds of decisions you genuinely make, such as deciding which new idea to document and champion first, judging when to escalate a strained relationship, and turning conversations with teams across your organisation into a coherent pipeline of opportunities that adds value.

Numerical reasoning shows up when you interpret satisfaction scores, compare investment options, or read a demand pipeline table under time pressure. Situational judgement shows up dozens of times a week, every time you choose how to respond to a frustrated colleague, a competing priority, or a blocker that will not move.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive ability assessment measures the exact mental processes your role demands, using practical workplace scenarios rather than abstract puzzles. Unlike a generic intelligence test, it presents you with the everyday materials of your job — in your case, opportunity pipeline entries, engagement records, service catalogue extracts, stakeholder maps, meeting notes, and improvement logs — and asks you to reason quickly and accurately about them.

Typical format: an online, timed test lasting 15 to 30 minutes, with questions covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective, comparing your responses against a norm group, and modern platforms often adapt question difficulty based on your previous answers — get a question right and the next may be slightly harder, get one wrong and the platform recalibrates. Employers usually receive a breakdown of speed versus accuracy rather than a single score, so working both quickly and carefully matters. You will normally be offered short, ungraded practice questions before the real test begins, so you can get comfortable with the interface without pressure.

For a business relationship manager, cognitive ability assessment is particularly relevant because your professional value lies in bringing structure to a stream of unstructured conversations. Teams across your organisation share perspectives, challenges and ambitions with you; your job is to turn that into a well-maintained view of activity, a documented pipeline of opportunities, and clear cases for support. That requires holding many moving parts in mind at once: which team raised which need, which opportunities depend on which decisions, where two records contradict each other, and what genuinely deserves your attention first on a busy Monday morning. A well-designed cognitive assessment simulates precisely those demands in miniature, which is why employers use it as a fair, evidence-based predictor of performance in engagement-heavy roles like yours.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The assessment dimensions map directly to the named skills in your role summary:

- **Pattern recognition** maps to your **Business analysis (IT operations)** skill: investigating problems and opportunities in existing processes means noticing recurring themes — the same complaint surfacing from three different teams, the same bottleneck appearing in different guises — across large and messy sets of engagement notes.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Strategic thinking** and **Service management framework knowledge**: working within a strategic context, and applying a Level 3 service management framework, both require you to reason validly from stated rules — strategy commitments, service level definitions, process preconditions — to their consequences.
- **Error checking** maps to your role-level duty to **use relevant systems to maintain a view of activity and business-related knowledge**: a pipeline record that contradicts a meeting note, or a stakeholder map that lists a colleague who moved on months ago, quietly destroys the trust others place in your data.
- **Prioritisation** maps to **Ownership and topic** and **Stakeholder relationship management (IT operations)**: owning an issue until a new owner is found or the problem is resolved, while managing many relationships at once, depends on rapidly ranking what matters most by impact and urgency.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Business analysis (IT operations)**, **Service focus** and **User focus**: stakeholders bring you tangled situations — a process that frustrates users, an opportunity with unclear benefits — and you must take inputs, establish coherent frameworks that work, and contribute to recommending solutions grounded in user needs evidence.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern recognition in an opportunity pipeline**

Your team records opportunities in a pipeline system using a fixed reference format: a two-letter directorate code, a hyphen, the four-digit year, a hyphen, then a three-digit sequence number (e.g. "FN-2025-014" for the fourteenth Finance opportunity logged in 2025). While tidying the pipeline, you see these entries:

1. FN-2025-014
2. HR-2025-007
3. OP-2025-21
4. FN-2025-015
5. DG-2025-102

Which entry breaks the reference format?

- A) Entry 2
- B) Entry 3
- C) Entry 4
- D) Entry 5

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The format requires a three-digit sequence number, so single- and double-digit sequences must be zero-padded: entry 3 should read "OP-2025-021", not "OP-2025-21". Entries 1, 2, 4 and 5 all follow the two-letter, four-digit, three-digit pattern (entry 5's "102" is a genuine three-digit number, so no padding is needed). This looks like a trivial clerical point, but it is exactly the kind of consistency check your role depends on: you use relevant systems to maintain a view of activity, and other people sort, filter and report on your references. A malformed reference silently drops out of searches and reports, which means an opportunity can disappear from a governance pack without anyone noticing. In an assessment, scan structured data field by field — letters, digits, separators — rather than reading entries as whole words; the eye forgives what the parser will not.

**Question 2 (easy) — Logical deduction from an engagement rule**

Your directorate's engagement standard states: "Every new digital investment idea must have a completed opportunity canvas before it can be presented at the monthly demand board, unless the sponsoring director requests an expedited slot." A colleague tells you: "The records team's scanning idea was presented at this month's demand board, and it did not have a completed opportunity canvas." Which one of the following conclusions must be true?

- A) The engagement standard was breached.
- B) The sponsoring director requested an expedited slot.
- C) Either the sponsoring director requested an expedited slot, or the standard was breached.
- D) The records team did not know about the opportunity canvas requirement.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The standard is a conditional rule with exactly one permitted exception. From the facts given you cannot tell whether the exception applies: perhaps the director expedited the slot (making B true and A false), or perhaps nobody followed the process (making A true and B false). Neither A nor B must be true on its own — each is merely possible. D invents a motive that appears nowhere in the facts. The only conclusion guaranteed in every scenario consistent with the evidence is the either/or statement in C. Assessments reward this discipline: deduce only what the information guarantees. The same discipline serves you in the workplace — before telling a stakeholder their team skipped the process, you would check whether an expedited slot was granted. Jumping to accusation A without checking would damage exactly the relationships you are employed to build.

**Question 3 (easy) — Error checking a stakeholder map**

You maintain a stakeholder map for the corporate services portfolio. Your rule set says: (i) every stakeholder entry must name one primary contact; (ii) every "High influence / High interest" stakeholder must have a named relationship owner from your team; (iii) contact details must be current within six months. During a review you find an entry for the Estates team dated fourteen months ago, marked "High influence / High interest", with a primary contact named and no relationship owner recorded. How many of your three rules does this entry break?

- A) 0
- B) 1
- C) 2
- D) 3

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Test each rule separately against the entry. Rule (i): a primary contact is named, so this rule is satisfied. Rule (ii): the entry is High/High but has no relationship owner, so this rule is broken. Rule (iii): fourteen months exceeds the six-month currency requirement, so this rule is broken too. Two rules are broken, so the answer is C. The technique — checking each rule independently rather than forming an overall impression of "this entry looks bad" — matters in assessments because partially compliant records are the norm, and it matters at work because your remediation actions differ per rule: rule (ii) needs an ownership decision within your team, while rule (iii) needs a refresh conversation with Estates. Ensuring consistency in engagement across the business starts with knowing precisely which part of the record is inconsistent.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Prioritisation across competing engagement demands**

It is Monday morning. Four items compete for your attention:

1. The monthly demand board meets Wednesday and needs the documentation pack for two new opportunities, which you have drafted but not yet circulated for sponsor sign-off.
2. A team leader in Operations has emailed overnight, unhappy that an issue you own — intermittent failures in a shared reporting tool — has recurred and is disrupting month-end work due Friday.
3. Your continuous improvement action from last month's retrospective — documenting the engagement playbook — is due to be shared with the team at Friday's meeting.
4. A colleague in Digital asks if you can "have a quick chat sometime this week" about an idea they want to raise.

Which sequencing is most defensible?

- A) 2, 1, 3, 4
- B) 1, 2, 3, 4
- C) 2, 1, 4, 3
- D) 4, 1, 2, 3

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Weigh impact and urgency together. Item 2 combines both: an issue you own is actively disrupting live month-end work, and the "Ownership and topic" expectation of your role is that you hold an issue until it is mitigated, resolved, or a new owner is found — so re-engaging with it (checking status, chasing the fix, updating the affected team) comes first. Item 1 has a hard Wednesday deadline and a dependency on other people's sign-off, so it must start today: circulate the pack this morning so sponsors have time to respond. Items 3 and 4 both point at Friday or "sometime this week"; the playbook (3) has a fixed commitment to the team, while the chat (4) can be scheduled in the gaps — booking it takes two minutes and can happen any time. Option B ignores the live disruption; C demotes a team commitment below an undated chat; D confuses responsiveness with priority. Note the pattern the assessment rewards: sequence by impact-weighted urgency, and start dependency-laden work early even when its deadline is later.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Pattern recognition across engagement records**

Reviewing six months of engagement notes, you tally how often each theme was raised by the four business units you partner with:

| Theme | Unit A | Unit B | Unit C | Unit D |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Slow onboarding of new starters to systems | 4 | 5 | 4 | 0 |
| Duplicate data entry across tools | 2 | 1 | 2 | 2 |
| Reporting tool reliability | 1 | 0 | 1 | 6 |

The four units are comparable in size and workload. As a business relationship manager shaping a pipeline of opportunities, what is the most reasonable first interpretation of these figures?

- A) Slow onboarding is a cross-organisation theme worth a shared opportunity; Unit D's reporting spike warrants its own investigation.
- B) Unit D has the most problems and should be your top engagement priority.
- C) The onboarding theme can be ignored because Unit D never raised it.
- D) The tallies are too small to support any interpretation.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Two distinct patterns sit in this table, and the strong answer names both. First, "slow onboarding" clusters at 4–5 mentions across Units A, B and C — a broad, consistent signal that suggests a shared underlying process problem, exactly the kind of shared need that justifies a single cross-organisation opportunity rather than three local fixes. Second, "reporting tool reliability" is near-zero everywhere except Unit D, where it spikes to 6 — a local anomaly that needs investigation (a unit-specific configuration? a different usage pattern? one vocal team?) before you conclude anything. Option B misreads the table: Unit D raised seven issues total, roughly comparable to others, and volume alone does not set priority. Option C treats one unit's silence as a veto — but Unit D's zero might mean they onboard few starters, not that the problem is unreal. Option D is over-cautious: small tallies limit certainty but still direct inquiry. This mirrors your real work of exploring the perspectives and challenges of teams: aggregate to find shared needs, and isolate anomalies for follow-up.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Logical deduction about approval sequencing**

Your organisation's investment process has these rules: (i) an opportunity cannot receive funding approval until it has passed the demand board; (ii) the demand board will not consider an opportunity until a sponsor is confirmed and an opportunity canvas is complete; (iii) security review can run in parallel with anything, but funding cannot be released until security review is complete. The records digitisation opportunity has a complete canvas, no confirmed sponsor, and a completed security review. Which one of the following must be true?

- A) The opportunity can be considered at the next demand board.
- B) Funding can be released as soon as the demand board passes it.
- C) The opportunity cannot currently be considered by the demand board.
- D) The security review will need to be repeated once a sponsor is confirmed.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Apply rule (ii): demand board consideration requires both a confirmed sponsor and a complete canvas. The canvas is complete but the sponsor is not confirmed, so the board cannot yet consider it — C must be true and A must be false. B is tempting because security review is already complete, and indeed once the board passes the opportunity, funding could follow — but B says "as soon as", which assumes the board will pass it; passing the board is a possibility, not a guarantee, and rule (i) makes board passage a precondition, not a promise of funding. D invents a repetition requirement found nowhere in the rules; rule (iii) explicitly allows security review to run in parallel with anything, so its early completion is valid. Process-rule questions like this reflect a real duty of your role: when you prepare documentation and obtain support to progress new ideas, you must know exactly which precondition is missing so that your next action — here, securing a sponsor — is the one that actually unblocks progress.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Error checking between systems**

Your pipeline system and this month's demand board pack should agree. The pipeline shows: opportunity OP-2025-031 "Field tablet refresh", status "Awaiting sponsor", estimated benefit £40,000 per year. The board pack shows: OP-2025-031 "Field tablet refresh", status "Sponsored — ready for review", estimated benefit £40,000 per year. The board meets tomorrow. Your first action should be to:

- A) Correct the pipeline to "Sponsored — ready for review", since the board pack is the more formal document.
- B) Correct the board pack to "Awaiting sponsor", since the pipeline is the system of record.
- C) Check with the opportunity's contact which status is true, then correct whichever record is wrong before the board meets.
- D) Flag the discrepancy verbally at the board meeting and let the board decide.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The two records conflict, and nothing in the evidence tells you which is correct: sponsorship may genuinely have been confirmed after the pipeline was last updated, or the pack may contain an optimistic error. Options A and B both resolve the conflict by assumption — picking whichever document feels more authoritative — and each has a fifty percent chance of writing a falsehood into your records the day before a governance decision. Option D outsources a data quality problem to a senior meeting, wasting board time on something you could resolve with one message, and risks the board reviewing an unsponsored opportunity in breach of its own rules. Option C is the disciplined move: verify against the source (the person who would know), then fix the wrong record — and, ideally, note why the two diverged so your continuous improvement work can stop it recurring. Assessments frequently test whether you resolve contradictions by evidence or by assumption; your role, which depends on others trusting the view of activity you maintain, rewards exactly the same habit.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Applied problem solving with user needs evidence**

Three teams have asked you to champion investment in a new shared file transfer tool. You gather evidence: Team 1 says transfers fail "constantly"; the service desk logs show 11 failed-transfer tickets from Team 1 in the last quarter, 2 from Team 2, and 1 from Team 3. A survey you ran shows all three teams rate the current tool 2 out of 5 for ease of use, and free-text comments across all teams focus on the confusing upload interface rather than failures. What is the most defensible reading of the evidence?

- A) The evidence supports replacing the tool because everyone rates it poorly.
- B) The main shared problem is usability of the interface; the failure problem is concentrated in Team 1 and needs separate diagnosis.
- C) Team 1 is exaggerating, since only 11 tickets were logged.
- D) The survey should be discounted because satisfaction scores are subjective.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Separate the two problem signals. The quantitative ticket data shows failures cluster heavily in Team 1 (11 versus 2 and 1) — that is a localised pattern, perhaps a network path, a file-type mix, or a local configuration, and it needs diagnosis rather than a blanket tool replacement. The survey data shows a genuinely shared problem, but it is usability, not reliability: consistent 2/5 scores plus free-text comments converging on the upload interface across all teams. Option A overreaches — poor ratings support an interface problem, not necessarily replacement, and the strongest business case might be an interface improvement or training rather than a new tool. Option C confuses "fewer tickets than the word 'constantly' implies" with exaggeration; users often stop logging tickets for recurring faults, so 11 tickets may understate the pain. Option D throws away valid data: qualitative and quantitative evidence about users are exactly what your **User focus** skill asks you to combine to turn user focus into outcomes. The assessment habit — and the workplace habit — is to let each evidence stream answer the question it is actually capable of answering.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Multi-constraint scheduling**

You must arrange three meetings this week — a sponsor briefing (S), an opportunity workshop (W), and a follow-up with the service owner (F) — across Monday to Friday, at most one of these meetings per day. Constraints: (i) S must happen before W, because the sponsor's steer shapes the workshop; (ii) the sponsor is available only Tuesday and Thursday; (iii) preparing the workshop materials takes one full working day after S, so W cannot occur on the day immediately after S; (iv) F must take place the day immediately after W. On which day must the workshop W take place?

- A) Tuesday
- B) Wednesday
- C) Thursday
- D) Friday

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Work through the constraints systematically. S needs the sponsor, so S is Tuesday or Thursday. Test S on Thursday: constraint (iii) pushes W to Saturday at the earliest (Friday is the day immediately after, which is barred), and (iv) would push F beyond the week — impossible. So S must be Tuesday. With S on Tuesday, W cannot be Wednesday (the immediately-following day, barred by (iii)), so W is Thursday or Friday. Test W on Friday: F would need Saturday — impossible by (iv). Therefore W must be Thursday, with F on Friday. Check the full solution: S Tuesday, W Thursday, F Friday satisfies every constraint. The method — eliminate impossible placements rather than hunting directly for the right one — is the reliable way to handle multi-constraint questions under time pressure, and it is the same reasoning you use when sequencing engagement activities around the diaries of busy stakeholders and the preparation time that quality documentation genuinely needs.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Deduction from strategy commitments**

Your digital strategy states: (i) all new investment proposals must demonstrate alignment with at least one of the three strategic priorities: improving user experience, reducing running costs, or strengthening security; (ii) proposals aligned only with reducing running costs must include a statement confirming no negative impact on user experience; (iii) any proposal touching citizen data must strengthen security or hold a formal risk acceptance. A proposal reaches you: it touches citizen data, claims alignment only with reducing running costs, includes the no-negative-impact statement, and holds no risk acceptance. Which is the correct assessment?

- A) The proposal is compliant: it aligns with a strategic priority and includes the required statement.
- B) The proposal is non-compliant: because it touches citizen data without strengthening security or holding a risk acceptance.
- C) The proposal is non-compliant: cost-only proposals are not allowed to touch citizen data.
- D) Compliance cannot be determined without knowing the size of the investment.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Check each rule in turn. Rule (i): the proposal aligns with reducing running costs, one of the three priorities — satisfied. Rule (ii): it is cost-only and includes the no-negative-impact statement — satisfied. Rule (iii): it touches citizen data, so it must either strengthen security (it does not — it claims cost alignment only) or hold a formal risk acceptance (it does not). Rule (iii) is breached, so the proposal is non-compliant and B is correct. A stops checking after two rules — the classic error of partial verification. C invents a prohibition the strategy does not contain: cost-only proposals may touch citizen data provided they hold a risk acceptance, so the fix is obtaining one, not abandoning the proposal. D imports an irrelevant consideration; nothing in the rules mentions investment size. In your role, contributing to strategy and preparing documentation to obtain support means running exactly this kind of completeness check before a proposal reaches a board — catching the missing risk acceptance in advance is the difference between a smooth approval and a public rejection that costs your stakeholder credibility.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Problem decomposition across a process chain**

A monthly management report is assembled through four steps: Team A extracts data from the finance system (takes 1 day), Team B cleanses and merges it with operational data (2 days), Team C drafts commentary (1 day), and Team D formats and publishes (1 day). Steps must run in that order, except that Team C can start drafting commentary using the previous month's data while Team B works, needing only half a day at the end to update figures. The report is due 4 working days after the finance system closes. Recently the report has been late, and each team blames the next. Which single change most directly addresses the structural cause of lateness?

- A) Ask every team to work faster.
- B) Have Team C start commentary in parallel with Team B, as the process already allows, cutting the critical path from 5 days to 4.5 — then examine the remaining half-day gap.
- C) Move the deadline to 5 days after close.
- D) Merge Teams C and D to reduce handovers.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** First quantify the structural problem. Run sequentially, the chain takes 1 + 2 + 1 + 1 = 5 days against a 4-day deadline — the process is late by design, not by anyone's slowness, which is why inter-team blame is misplaced. The stem tells you an overlap already exists in principle: Team C can draft against last month's data during Team B's 2 days, needing only 0.5 days afterwards. Exploiting it gives 1 + 2 + 0.5 + 1 = 4.5 days — a real improvement that uses no extra resource, though still 0.5 days over, which is why B honestly says "then examine the remaining half-day gap" (perhaps Team D can prepare templates in parallel too). Option A demands effort without changing structure — the deficit returns next month. Option C solves the lateness by redefining it, which may occasionally be right but abandons improvement before trying it. Option D restructures teams on no evidence that handover friction, rather than sequence arithmetic, causes the delay. This is your **Business analysis (IT operations)** skill in action: investigate the process, find the structural cause, and contribute a recommendation that works — a core move in the continuous improvement activities your role level asks you to support.

### Preparation tips

- **Practise with your own artefacts.** Ten minutes with your real pipeline, stakeholder map, or engagement log — actively hunting for format breaks, stale entries, and contradictions — is cognitive training with a day-job payoff. You will start seeing the question types everywhere.
- **Learn the conditional-rule pattern.** Many workplace rules have the shape "X must happen unless Y". Practise stating exactly what follows when you observe X missing: not "the rule was broken" but "either the exception applied or the rule was broken". This single pattern appears constantly in both assessments and governance conversations.
- **Time-box your practice.** Cognitive tests reward steady pace. Practise answering in 60–90 seconds per question, and practise moving on: one stubborn question is never worth three easy ones.
- **Check rules one at a time.** When a question gives you multiple rules or constraints, number them and test each independently. Impressionistic reading ("this entry looks wrong") is the main source of avoidable errors.
- **Do the eliminations.** In multi-constraint puzzles, disprove placements rather than searching for the answer directly. Elimination is faster, and it gives you certainty rather than a hopeful guess.
- **Warm up on the day.** Before a real test, do two or three practice questions to get your pattern-spotting eye working, find a quiet space, close your email, and have rough paper or a notes window ready for sequencing questions.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Reading records as prose.** Structured data — references, dates, statuses — needs field-by-field scanning. Your reading brain autocorrects "OP-2025-21" into validity; your checking brain must not.
- **Concluding more than the evidence allows.** The most common wrong answers in deduction questions are over-strong: "the standard was breached", "the team is exaggerating". Train yourself to ask, "is there any scenario consistent with the facts where this is false?" If yes, it is not a valid conclusion.
- **Prioritising by loudness or seniority alone.** Assessment prioritisation questions punish sequencing that follows whoever shouted loudest or ranks highest. Impact and urgency together, with early starts for work that depends on other people, is the defensible pattern.
- **Resolving contradictions by assumption.** When two records disagree, the tempting shortcut is to trust the "more official" one. The correct move — in tests and in your systems — is to verify against the source.
- **Losing the second constraint.** In multi-rule questions, most candidates check the first rule or two and stop. Discipline yourself to check every rule; the breach usually hides in the last one.
- **Letting time pressure abolish method.** Under the clock, people abandon systematic checking and start pattern-guessing. A steady method run slightly fast beats intuition run at full speed — in the test, and in the week before a demand board.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures your ability to interpret, manipulate and draw sound conclusions from numerical information of the kind your role genuinely handles. It is not a mental arithmetic contest: calculators are usually permitted, and the skill being tested is choosing the right calculation, executing it accurately, and interpreting the result sensibly in context.

Typical format: an online, timed test of 20 to 40 minutes, presenting tables, charts and short data-rich passages, each followed by two to four multiple-choice questions. Common topics include percentages and percentage change, ratios and proportions, averages, rates, and reading values accurately from tables and charts. Many platforms are adaptive, and most report accuracy and speed separately. As with the cognitive test, brief ungraded practice items usually precede the scored questions.

For a business relationship manager, numeric reasoning matters because numbers are the currency of the cases you prepare and the improvement work you support. When you prepare documentation to progress a new idea, opportunity or investment, the persuasive core is usually numeric: an estimated benefit, a cost comparison, a usage trend, a satisfaction score. When you maintain a view of activity across the business, that view is full of counts, percentages and trends — engagement coverage, pipeline conversion, survey results, service performance against targets. When you support continuous improvement, you need before-and-after measures to show the improvement is real. Stakeholders will quote numbers at you, sometimes selectively; your credibility rests on being able to check them quickly, spot the flattering denominator or the misleading average, and restate the figure that actually matters. Employers test numeric reasoning for this role because a relationship manager who cannot interrogate numbers can be led anywhere by whoever supplies them.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Percentages and percentage change** map to your **Business analysis (IT operations)** skill: quantifying how big a problem is, and how much better a recommended solution would make it, almost always means percentage comparisons — ticket reductions, adoption increases, cost changes.
- **Ratios and proportions** map to **Service focus** and **Stakeholder relationship management (IT operations)**: establishing coherent frameworks means comparing like with like — engagements per team, issues per hundred users, benefits per pound invested — so that your view across many stakeholders is fair.
- **Averages and distributions** map to **User focus**: using quantitative data about users to turn user focus into outcomes requires knowing when a mean is distorted by outliers and when a satisfaction average hides a divided user base.
- **Rates and trends** map to **Strategic thinking**: communicating how activities meet strategic goals often means projecting a trend — demand growth, cost trajectory, adoption rate — and knowing how far a projection can honestly be pushed.
- **Reading tables and charts accurately** maps to your role-level duty to **use relevant systems to maintain a view of activity**: dashboards, pipeline reports and survey exports are only useful if you extract exactly what they say, no more and no less.
- **Benefit and cost calculations** map to your duty to **prepare documentation and obtain support to progress new ideas, opportunities and investments**: sponsors approve numbers, and the numbers must be right.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Percentage of engagements delivered**

Your team's engagement plan committed to 40 stakeholder engagement sessions this quarter. You delivered 34. What percentage of the planned sessions did you deliver?

- A) 80%
- B) 85%
- C) 88%
- D) 90%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Percentage delivered = delivered ÷ planned × 100. Step by step: 34 ÷ 40 = 0.85; 0.85 × 100 = 85%. So the answer is B. A quick sanity check: 90% of 40 would be 36 sessions and 80% would be 32, so 34 sits between them, nearer 36 — consistent with 85%. In assessments, always confirm which number is the denominator: the question asks for a percentage *of the plan*, so the plan (40) goes on the bottom. Reporting delivery against plan is bread-and-butter for the view of activity you maintain, and getting the denominator right is what separates "85% of plan" from accidentally reporting the meaningless "34% delivered".

**Question 2 (easy) — Reading a satisfaction survey table**

Your annual partner satisfaction survey returns these results for the question "The digital team understands our business needs":

| Response | Count |
|---|---|
| Strongly agree | 18 |
| Agree | 42 |
| Neither | 25 |
| Disagree | 10 |
| Strongly disagree | 5 |

What percentage of respondents agreed or strongly agreed?

- A) 55%
- B) 58%
- C) 60%
- D) 65%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** First find the total number of respondents: 18 + 42 + 25 + 10 + 5 = 100. Then sum the favourable responses: 18 + 42 = 60. Percentage favourable = 60 ÷ 100 × 100 = 60%, so C is correct. The total conveniently equals 100 here, but never assume it does — always add the column, because real survey exports rarely oblige. Notice also what the number does not say: 60% favourable leaves 25% sitting on the fence and 15% unfavourable, and a good relationship manager reads the fence-sitters as an engagement opportunity, not as passive support. Assessments often follow a question like this with "what proportion of those who expressed an opinion agreed?" — which would be 60 ÷ 75 = 80% — so keep alert to exactly which group the question defines.

**Question 3 (easy) — Ratio of opportunities to conversions**

Last year your directorate logged 48 opportunities in the pipeline, of which 12 progressed to funded investments. What is the ratio of logged opportunities to funded investments, in simplest form?

- A) 3:1
- B) 4:1
- C) 12:48
- D) 6:2

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The ratio of logged to funded is 48:12. Simplify by dividing both sides by their greatest common divisor, 12: 48 ÷ 12 = 4 and 12 ÷ 12 = 1, giving 4:1. So for every four opportunities logged, one was funded. Option A miscalculates; option C states the ratio unreduced and reversed; option D (6:2) equals 3:1 and is neither simplest form nor correct. Order matters in ratios — "logged to funded" puts logged first — and assessments test that reading discipline deliberately. In your work, this conversion ratio is a genuinely useful management figure: if next year 60 opportunities are logged, a stable 4:1 ratio predicts about 15 funded investments, which helps you set realistic expectations with the teams whose ideas you champion — and a changing ratio tells you something about either the quality of the pipeline or the appetite of the demand board.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Percentage change in service desk demand**

A shared reporting tool you look after generated 250 support tickets in the first quarter and 290 in the second quarter. What is the percentage increase in tickets from the first to the second quarter?

- A) 14%
- B) 16%
- C) 40%
- D) 86%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Percentage change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100. Step by step: 290 − 250 = 40; 40 ÷ 250 = 0.16; 0.16 × 100 = 16%. The answer is B. Option C is the raw difference (40) mistaken for a percentage; option A results from dividing by the new value (40 ÷ 290 ≈ 13.8%) — the single most common percentage-change error, and one that always flatters an increase; option D is 250 ÷ 290 dressed up as an answer. Anchor the rule: the *old* value is the base, because change is measured relative to where you started. In your role this calculation earns its keep when you brief a service owner: "tickets are up 16% quarter on quarter" is an actionable engagement signal, and if a supplier tells you the rise is "only 13.8%", you will know exactly which denominator they chose and why.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Weighted average satisfaction across units**

You partner with three business units. Their satisfaction scores (out of 5) and respondent counts this quarter are: Unit A scored 4.2 from 50 respondents, Unit B scored 3.0 from 150 respondents, and Unit C scored 4.0 from 100 respondents. What is the overall average satisfaction score across all respondents, to one decimal place?

- A) 3.5
- B) 3.7
- C) 3.6
- D) 3.4

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** A simple average of 4.2, 3.0 and 4.0 would be 11.2 ÷ 3 ≈ 3.7 — but that wrongly gives tiny Unit A the same weight as Unit B, which has three times the respondents. Weight each score by its respondent count. Step by step: Unit A contributes 4.2 × 50 = 210 score points; Unit B contributes 3.0 × 150 = 450; Unit C contributes 4.0 × 100 = 400. Total score points = 210 + 450 + 400 = 1,060. Total respondents = 50 + 150 + 100 = 300. Weighted average = 1,060 ÷ 300 = 3.53, which to one decimal place is 3.5 — option A. Verify by reverse multiplication: 300 × 3.5 = 1,050, close to 1,060 with the 0.03 remainder accounted for, so the figure holds. The unweighted 3.7 (option B) is the trap, and it is not a harmless one: quoting 3.7 in a report would overstate satisfaction precisely because your largest partner unit is your least satisfied — the one insight a relationship manager most needs to surface. Whenever groups differ in size, weight by the count behind each score; assessments plant the unweighted mean among the options every time.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Cost comparison for an investment case**

You are documenting an opportunity to replace a legacy meeting-room booking tool. The current tool costs £18,000 per year in licences plus £6,000 per year in support. The proposed tool costs £30,000 in one-off implementation, then £15,000 per year in licences with support included. Over three years, how do total costs compare?

- A) The proposed tool costs £3,000 less than the current tool.
- B) The proposed tool costs £3,000 more than the current tool.
- C) The costs are equal.
- D) The proposed tool costs £9,000 more than the current tool.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Compute each three-year total. Current tool: (£18,000 + £6,000) × 3 = £24,000 × 3 = £72,000. Proposed tool: £30,000 one-off + £15,000 × 3 = £30,000 + £45,000 = £75,000. Difference: £75,000 − £72,000 = £3,000 more for the proposed tool, so B is correct. The one-off implementation cost is the item candidates most often mishandle — either forgetting it entirely (which would make the proposed tool look £27,000 cheaper) or multiplying it by three as if it recurred. In your documentation work this matters doubly: a sponsor will ask "what does it cost over the spending review period?", and an honest answer here is "£3,000 more over three years, but £9,000 less per year from year four onwards" — because once implementation is paid, the annual saving is £24,000 − £15,000 = £9,000. Being able to present both the three-year and the steady-state view, accurately, is what obtaining support for an investment actually requires.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Proportion and coverage of engagement**

Your organisation has 32 teams in scope for business partnering. Your engagement log shows you held at least one structured engagement session with 24 of them this year. Your team's target is at least 85% coverage. What was your actual coverage, and how many more teams would you have needed to engage to meet the target?

- A) 75% coverage; 4 more teams needed
- B) 80% coverage; 3 more teams needed
- C) 75% coverage; 3 more teams needed
- D) 85% coverage; target met

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Two calculations, done in order. First, actual coverage: 24 ÷ 32 = 0.75 = 75%. Second, the number of teams the target requires: 85% of 32 = 0.85 × 32 = 27.2 teams. You cannot engage a fifth of a team, and the target says *at least* 85%, so check the whole numbers either side: 27 teams gives 27 ÷ 32 = 84.4% (just under target — fails), while 28 teams gives 28 ÷ 32 = 87.5% (passes). The requirement is therefore 28 teams, and the gap is 28 − 24 = 4 more teams — option A. Option C is the near-miss trap: rounding 27.2 down to 27 conventionally feels natural, but an "at least" threshold must be rounded *up*, because 27 teams genuinely fails the 85% test. The general rule is worth memorising: when a minimum percentage target meets a small whole-number population, convert the percentage to a count, then check the whole numbers either side of it against the threshold rather than trusting the rounding. In the workplace, the honest brief to your team is "24 of 32 teams engaged — 75% against an 85% target, so we need four more teams in the plan", and knowing the exact gap turns a red status into a concrete engagement plan.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Reading a trend for a strategic conversation**

Monthly active users of a self-service HR portal you have championed:

| Month | Jan | Feb | Mar | Apr | May | Jun |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Active users | 1,200 | 1,320 | 1,450 | 1,600 | 1,760 | 1,940 |

Approximately what has the month-on-month growth rate been, and what would July's figure be if the trend continues?

- A) About 10% per month; roughly 2,130 users in July
- B) About 10% per month; roughly 2,000 users in July
- C) About 15% per month; roughly 2,230 users in July
- D) About 5% per month; roughly 2,040 users in July

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Test the growth rate on a few intervals. Feb ÷ Jan = 1,320 ÷ 1,200 = 1.10, a 10% rise. Mar ÷ Feb = 1,450 ÷ 1,320 ≈ 1.098. Jun ÷ May = 1,940 ÷ 1,760 ≈ 1.102. The series grows at close to 10% per month, compounding. July's projection = 1,940 × 1.10 = 2,134, so "roughly 2,130" — option A. Option B pairs the right rate with an arithmetic short-change (adding a flat 60); option D mistakes the *absolute* increases (which grow each month: 120, 130, 150, 160, 180) for a small percentage; option C overshoots. Two habits matter here. First, test the rate at more than one point — a series can start at 10% and decay. Second, present projections honestly: "if the current 10% monthly growth continues, we would expect around 2,100 users in July" is a conditional statement, and your **Strategic thinking** skill — communicating how activities meet strategic goals — is best served by projections that state their assumption, because a director will remember the number you gave long after they have forgotten the caveat you failed to attach.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Benefits arithmetic for an opportunity canvas**

An opportunity canvas you are preparing claims a new digital form will save time for caseworkers. Evidence: 5,200 applications are processed per year; the current paper process takes an average of 22 minutes of caseworker time per application; the digital form is expected to cut this to 14 minutes. Caseworker time is costed at £30 per hour. What is the estimated annual saving, to the nearest £1,000?

- A) £21,000
- B) £26,000
- C) £35,000
- D) £42,000

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Work step by step. Time saved per application = 22 − 14 = 8 minutes. Total minutes saved per year = 5,200 × 8 = 41,600 minutes. Convert to hours: 41,600 ÷ 60 = 693.33 hours. Cost saving = 693.33 × £30 = £20,800, which rounds to £21,000 — option A. Option D (£42,000) comes from forgetting to convert minutes to hours properly (41,600 × £30 ÷ 30, a muddle); option B typically arises from using the full 22 minutes rather than the 8-minute *difference* somewhere in the chain; option C from a units slip. Two checks protect you: keep units visible at every step (minutes → hours → pounds), and sanity-check the scale — 8 minutes is roughly an eighth of an hour, so the saving is about 5,200 × (1/7.5) ≈ 690 hours, matching the detailed figure. When this number goes into documentation that a sponsor will defend at a demand board, remember to label it honestly: it is a *time-released* saving, which only becomes cashable if the released hours are redeployed — a distinction finance colleagues will press you on, and one that separates credible canvases from optimistic ones.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Percentage points versus percentages**

Two years ago, 40% of the teams you partner with rated engagement "good" or better. This year, 50% do. A colleague drafts a slide saying "engagement satisfaction has increased by 10%". As the owner of this data, what is the accurate statement?

- A) The colleague is right: the increase is 10%.
- B) Satisfaction increased by 10 percentage points, which is a 25% relative increase.
- C) Satisfaction increased by 25 percentage points.
- D) Satisfaction increased by 10 percentage points, which is a 20% relative increase.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The gap between 40% and 50% is 10 *percentage points* — the absolute difference between two percentages. The *relative* increase is (50 − 40) ÷ 40 × 100 = 10 ÷ 40 × 100 = 25%. So the precise statement is B: up 10 percentage points, a 25% relative improvement. The colleague's "increased by 10%" is ambiguous at best and understates the relative change; option D divides by the new value (10 ÷ 50 = 20%), the recurring wrong-denominator error; option C confuses the two measures entirely. This distinction is worth mastering because it moves in both directions in your work: a supplier whose service availability fell from 99% to 97% may describe that as "a 2% reduction", when incidents affecting users have actually tripled the *unavailability* (1% → 3%, a 200% relative increase). Choosing the honest framing — and being able to defend it when challenged — is part of communicating clearly and regularly with stakeholders, and assessments test it because slide-ready numbers are exactly where careless phrasing hides.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Interpreting a two-way table under time pressure**

A quarterly report cross-tabulates the 60 opportunities currently in your pipeline:

| | Aligned to strategy | Not yet assessed | Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sponsored | 18 | 6 | 24 |
| Unsponsored | 22 | 14 | 36 |
| Total | 40 | 20 | 60 |

Which statement is supported by the table?

- A) Most sponsored opportunities have not been assessed for strategic alignment.
- B) Exactly 75% of sponsored opportunities are aligned to strategy, a higher alignment rate than unsponsored opportunities show.
- C) Sponsorship causes strategic alignment.
- D) 40% of all opportunities are sponsored and aligned.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Verify each option against the cells. Option A: sponsored opportunities split 18 aligned versus 6 not yet assessed, so most (18 of 24) *have* been assessed and found aligned — A is false. Option B: sponsored alignment rate = 18 ÷ 24 = 0.75 = 75%; unsponsored alignment rate = 22 ÷ 36 ≈ 61%. Both parts of B check out — B is supported. Option C leaps from association to causation: the table shows sponsored opportunities are more often aligned, but perhaps alignment attracts sponsors rather than the reverse, or well-run teams produce both — a table cannot settle direction. Option D: sponsored *and* aligned is the single cell 18, and 18 ÷ 60 = 30%, not 40% — D confuses the cell with the row or column totals. Two-way tables reward one discipline above all: identify precisely which subgroup each percentage should be computed over. That is also the workplace skill — when a board member asks "what proportion of our sponsored pipeline is strategic?", the answer comes from the sponsored row alone, and reaching for the wrong total in a live meeting is how misleading figures enter organisational folklore.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Combining measures for a continuous improvement case**

Your team wants to show that a new triage step, introduced at the start of Q3, improved the handling of partner requests. The data:

| Measure | Q2 (before) | Q3 (after) |
|---|---|---|
| Requests received | 300 | 360 |
| Requests resolved within 5 days | 210 | 288 |
| Average days to resolve | 4.8 | 4.1 |

Which summary most fairly represents the improvement?

- A) Resolutions within 5 days rose from 210 to 288, an increase of 78 requests, proving the triage step worked.
- B) The within-5-day rate rose from 70% to 80% despite a 20% rise in demand, and average resolution time fell — consistent with a genuine improvement, though other Q3 factors cannot be ruled out.
- C) Demand rose 20%, so performance cannot be compared between quarters.
- D) Average days fell by 0.7, a 15% improvement, which is the only figure worth reporting.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Compute the comparable rates first, because raw counts mislead when demand changes. Q2 rate: 210 ÷ 300 = 70%. Q3 rate: 288 ÷ 360 = 80%. Demand change: (360 − 300) ÷ 300 = 20% increase. So the team resolved a *higher proportion* of a *larger workload* faster (4.8 → 4.1 days, a fall of 0.7 days, and 0.7 ÷ 4.8 ≈ 14.6% ≈ 15%). Option B states all of this and adds the honest caveat that a before/after comparison is evidence, not proof — Q3 may also have brought staff changes or an easier request mix. Option A leans on raw counts and the word "proving", both weaknesses: 288 versus 210 partly reflects higher demand, and one uncontrolled comparison proves nothing. Option C surrenders too easily — rates exist precisely to make changed volumes comparable. Option D discards two of three relevant measures. This is the numeric heart of the continuous improvement work your role level asks you to support: normalise for demand, report rates alongside volumes, quantify the time change, and claim exactly as much as the evidence supports. Cases built this way survive scrutiny; cases built on option A's logic get dismantled in their first governance meeting.

### Preparation tips

- **Rehearse the five core moves.** Percentage of a total, percentage change, weighted average, ratio simplification, and rate normalisation cover most workplace numeric questions. Practise each until the *setup* (which number goes where) is automatic, because setup errors — not arithmetic — cause most lost marks.
- **Always name the denominator.** Before calculating any percentage, say to yourself "per cent *of what*". Change is measured against the old value; subgroup rates are measured against the subgroup total.
- **Keep units visible.** Minutes, hours, pounds, teams: write the unit at every step. The £21,000-versus-£42,000 gap in benefits arithmetic is almost always a silent unit slip.
- **Sanity-check by reversing.** Multiply your answer back (if 85% of 40 is 34, then 34 ÷ 40 should be 0.85) or estimate the order of magnitude first, so a wild result rings an alarm.
- **Use your own dashboards as practice material.** Take this quarter's survey export or pipeline report and set yourself three questions: what changed, by what percentage, and normalised for what? You will simultaneously prepare for tests and arrive better-armed at your next service review.
- **Practise with a calculator you will actually have.** If the test permits an on-screen calculator, practise with one; the friction of an unfamiliar tool costs real seconds per question.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Dividing by the new value.** The single most common percentage-change error, and it always understates increases and overstates decreases. Old value is the base, every time.
- **Confusing percentage points with percentages.** A move from 40% to 50% is 10 points but 25% relative growth. Sloppy phrasing here can misstate your own success — or let a supplier misstate their failure.
- **Averaging averages.** Unit scores of 4.2, 3.0 and 4.0 do not average to 3.7 when the units differ in size. Weight by the count behind each score.
- **Ignoring one-off versus recurring costs.** Implementation costs happen once; licences recur. Muddling them can swing a three-year comparison by tens of thousands of pounds.
- **Comparing raw counts across changed volumes.** More resolutions can coexist with worse performance if demand rose faster. Convert to rates before comparing periods.
- **Over-claiming from projections and associations.** "If the trend continues" and "consistent with" are honest; "will reach" and "proves" are not. Assessments test the calculation; sponsors test the claim — keep both defensible.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately you understand, evaluate and use written information of the kind your role runs on. You are typically shown a short passage — a policy extract, a stakeholder email, a service report, a strategy statement — and asked what follows from it. The classic response format is True / False / Cannot say: "True" means the passage guarantees the statement; "False" means the passage contradicts it; "Cannot say" means the passage provides insufficient information either way. Other question types test inference, identifying the main point, and judging which rewrite of a sentence is clearest.

Typical format: an online, timed test of 15 to 30 minutes, with several passages of 100–250 words, each carrying two to four questions. The discipline the test demands — and the one candidates find hardest — is answering strictly from the passage, setting aside everything you happen to know or believe about the topic. Adaptive scoring and speed/accuracy reporting are common, as with the other assessment types.

For a business relationship manager, verbal reasoning is close to the centre of the job. Your working day is a stream of words: emails from stakeholders that must be read for what they actually commit to rather than what they seem to imply; service management framework definitions where a single word such as "shall" or "should" changes an obligation into a suggestion; strategy documents whose commitments you must represent faithfully to teams; and the documentation you yourself prepare, where imprecise wording can send an opportunity to a board with a claim its sponsor never made. Ensuring consistency in engagement across the business is, in large part, a verbal precision task — everyone you partner with must hear the same accurate message. Employers test verbal reasoning for this role because misreading a document, or writing an ambiguous one, damages relationships and decisions in ways that are expensive to unwind.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Reading comprehension of policy and process documents** maps to your **Service management framework knowledge** skill: a Level 3 service management qualification is largely a test of reading framework definitions precisely — the difference between an incident and a problem, or a service level target and a service level agreement, lives entirely in careful wording.
- **True/False/Cannot-say discipline** maps to **Business analysis (IT operations)**: when you investigate problems and opportunities, the evidence you collect says exactly what it says; concluding more than stakeholder interviews or documents support produces recommendations that collapse under challenge.
- **Inference and reading between the lines — carefully** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management (IT operations)**: influencing decisions and dealing with challenging situations requires you to notice what an email does *not* say — the commitment carefully avoided, the qualifier quietly inserted — without inventing meanings that are not there.
- **Identifying the main point** maps to your role-level duty to **prepare documentation and obtain support**: sponsors and boards read summaries first and sometimes only; finding and leading with the point that matters is how support is obtained.
- **Clear-writing judgement** maps to **Strategic thinking** and **User focus**: communicating how activities meet strategic goals, and representing user needs evidence, both fail if your prose is ambiguous, jargon-heavy, or structured so the reader must work to find the message.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — True/False/Cannot say on a service report**

Passage: "The shared printing service met its availability target in eleven of the twelve months this year. The single miss occurred in March, when a supplier firmware fault caused four days of intermittent outages. The supplier has since replaced the firmware across the printer estate, and no related faults have been recorded in the eight months following the fix."

Statement: "The shared printing service missed its availability target in March because of a supplier firmware fault."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Check each element of the statement against the passage. Missed the target in March? The passage says the single miss "occurred in March" — confirmed. Because of a supplier firmware fault? The passage says the fault "caused four days of intermittent outages" in that month, and the miss is attributed to that event — confirmed. Every component of the statement is directly supported, so the answer is True. Easy verification questions like this train the core habit: match the statement to the passage clause by clause, rather than answering from overall impression. Note what would flip the answer: if the statement said the service "repeatedly missed its target", the passage's "eleven of twelve months" would contradict it (False); if it said the March fault "was the supplier's first failure", the passage would be silent (Cannot say). Reading service reports at this level of precision is exactly what you do before repeating a performance claim to a partner team.

**Question 2 (easy) — Cannot-say discipline on a stakeholder email**

Passage (email from a finance business partner): "Thanks for the opportunity summary. I can see the attraction of the proposal, and the projected savings are certainly material. However, I am not yet in a position to confirm finance's support. We would want to see the assumptions behind the savings figure, and ideally an option that phases the spend across two financial years."

Statement: "Finance has decided to oppose the proposal."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The statement claims a decision to oppose. The passage says finance is "not yet in a position to confirm support" — which is explicitly a *pending* position, not opposition — and then sets out what would help: sight of the assumptions and a phased option. A pending position with stated conditions contradicts "has decided to oppose"; the email's whole structure signals conditional openness. So the answer is False, not Cannot say: the passage does not merely fail to support the statement, it actively conflicts with it. This distinction — between a passage being silent (Cannot say) and a passage pointing the other way (False) — is the most commonly confused boundary in verbal tests. It is also a workplace skill: a relationship manager who reports "finance is blocking us" after this email has misread caution as opposition, and may trigger needless escalation. The accurate reading — "finance is open, pending assumptions and a phasing option" — turns the same email into a to-do list.

**Question 3 (easy) — Identifying the main point**

Passage: "Attendance at the monthly service review has fallen from nine business units to four over the past two quarters. Several invitees have said the meeting repeats information already available on the dashboard. Two units have asked whether their standing agenda slot could be replaced by a short written update. Meanwhile, the issues raised at the review that do generate discussion are almost always about upcoming changes rather than past performance."

Which sentence best states the main point?

- A) The service review meeting is too long.
- B) The service review in its current form is losing relevance, and its content and format need rethinking.
- C) Business units no longer care about service performance.
- D) The dashboard should be decommissioned.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage assembles four converging signals: falling attendance, feedback about duplication with the dashboard, requests to replace slots with written updates, and discussion gravitating to future changes rather than past performance. Together they support one conclusion — the meeting as currently designed is losing its audience and its content emphasis is misaligned — which is what B says. Option A picks a plausible cause never mentioned (length is not raised). Option C over-interprets: units still engage with the dashboard and with future changes, so disengagement from one meeting is not indifference to performance. Option D inverts the evidence — the dashboard is the *substitute* attendees prefer. In main-point questions, the right answer covers the breadth of the evidence without exceeding it. This mirrors a real judgement in your continuous improvement work: reading weak signals across several sources and naming the underlying issue is how meeting formats, and engagement models generally, get improved before they quietly die.

**Question 4 (moderate) — True/False/Cannot say on a strategy extract**

Passage: "The digital strategy commits the department to reducing the number of standalone case management systems from fourteen to no more than six by 2028. Consolidation decisions will be taken by the design authority, informed by business impact assessments. No system will be decommissioned without a supported migration path for its users, and systems supporting statutory functions will be consolidated last."

Statement: "Under the strategy, at least eight standalone case management systems will be decommissioned by 2028."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The arithmetic looks straightforward — from fourteen down to no more than six implies at least eight fewer standalone systems — but test the exact wording. The strategy commits to *reducing the number of standalone systems*; the statement claims the surplus systems will be *decommissioned*. Are those the same? Not necessarily: a system can stop being a standalone case management system by being merged into another platform, absorbed as a module, or re-scoped, without a formal decommissioning; conversely the passage describes decommissioning safeguards but never states that decommissioning is the sole consolidation mechanism. Because the passage guarantees a reduction of at least eight but does not guarantee the *mechanism* the statement names, the statement goes beyond the passage — Cannot say. This is a deliberately fine distinction, and it is the level at which verbal tests operate at the harder end. It is also the level at which strategy communication operates: telling a nervous system owner "the strategy says your system will be decommissioned" when it actually says "consolidated, with safeguards, statutory systems last" is precisely the kind of imprecision that manufactures resistance a relationship manager then spends months unwinding.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Inference from an email exchange**

Passage (two emails). From a service owner to you, Monday: "We can support the pilot going live next month provided the two open security actions are closed by the 15th." From the security lead to you, Thursday: "One of the two actions on the pilot is now closed. The second depends on a supplier patch currently scheduled for the 20th."

Which inference is best supported?

- A) The pilot will not go live next month.
- B) On current plans the service owner's condition will not be met, so the pilot's go-live is at risk unless the patch is accelerated or the condition renegotiated.
- C) The security lead has failed to manage the supplier effectively.
- D) The service owner will withdraw support for the pilot.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Combine the two emails carefully. The condition: both actions closed by the 15th. The current position: one closed; the other depends on a patch scheduled for the 20th — five days after the deadline. On current plans, therefore, the condition fails. But note what the evidence does *not* establish: schedules can move (the patch could be accelerated), and conditions can be renegotiated (the service owner might accept a mitigations-in-place go-live). So A and D overstate — each converts a risk into a certainty, and D additionally assumes the owner's response. C assigns blame with no evidence about the security lead's supplier management; a scheduled patch date is not proof of failure. B states exactly what follows: the condition will not be met on current plans, so go-live is at risk, and it names the two realistic paths — accelerate or renegotiate. This is verbatim the reasoning a relationship manager performs before deciding whether to convene the parties, and the assessment is checking that your inference sits precisely at the strength the evidence supports: neither complacent nor catastrophising.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Framework language: shall, should, may**

Passage (extract from your organisation's engagement standard): "Relationship owners shall record every structured engagement in the partnering system within five working days. Relationship owners should share a written summary with the stakeholder after each engagement. Relationship owners may invite subject matter experts to engagements where specialist topics are expected."

Which statement accurately reflects the standard?

- A) Sharing a written summary with the stakeholder is mandatory.
- B) Recording engagements in the partnering system is recommended but optional.
- C) Recording engagements within five working days is mandatory; sharing summaries is recommended; inviting experts is permitted at the owner's discretion.
- D) Inviting subject matter experts is required for specialist topics.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Standards and service management frameworks use modal verbs as a precise control vocabulary: "shall" denotes a mandatory requirement, "should" a recommendation that ought to be followed absent good reason, and "may" a permission. Map each clause: recording within five days takes "shall" — mandatory; sharing summaries takes "should" — recommended; inviting experts takes "may" — discretionary. That is exactly option C. Option A promotes a "should" to mandatory; B demotes a "shall" to optional; D converts a "may" into a requirement. Candidates with strong general reading skills lose marks here because everyday English blurs these distinctions — but your **Service management framework knowledge** qualification trades on them, and so does compliance conversation in the workplace. When you tell a colleague "you have to send the summary", you have just misrepresented the standard and, if they are audited, your misstatement travels. Precision with modal verbs is one of the cheapest credibility investments available to you: it costs nothing but attention.

**Question 7 (moderate) — True/False/Cannot say on user research findings**

Passage: "Research with 25 caseworkers across three sites found that 19 used the workaround spreadsheet daily. Participants consistently described the official system's search as 'too slow for live calls'. Younger participants were no more likely than older participants to use the official system's advanced features. Site managers, who were not included in the research, are believed by the research team to rely on the official system's reports."

Statement: "Most caseworkers in the organisation use the workaround spreadsheet daily."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage reports that 19 of 25 *research participants* — 76%, drawn from three sites — used the spreadsheet daily. The statement generalises to "most caseworkers in the organisation". Does the sample license that generalisation? The passage gives no information about how many caseworkers or sites the organisation has, nor whether the sample was representative. Nineteen of twenty-five is strong evidence *within the sample*, and a reasonable prompt for wider investigation, but "most caseworkers in the organisation" is neither guaranteed (so not True) nor contradicted (so not False). Cannot say. Notice the companion trap in the final sentence: what managers are "believed by the research team" to do is a belief, not a finding — an assessment statement about managers' actual behaviour would also be Cannot say. This sample-versus-population discipline is central to your **User focus** skill: representing research honestly means saying "19 of the 25 caseworkers we spoke to" rather than "most caseworkers", until the evidence base supports the larger claim. Stakeholders remember the claim, and if it later fails, the research — and your advocacy of it — takes the reputational damage.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Clear-writing judgement**

You are drafting the summary line of an opportunity document for a demand board. Which version is clearest and most useful to the board?

- A) "Leveraging synergies across the estate, this transformational opportunity will optimise stakeholder-facing paradigms going forward."
- B) "This proposal asks the board to approve £45,000 to pilot digital appointment booking in two clinics, aiming to cut missed appointments by a quarter within six months."
- C) "It was determined by the team that the potentiality of appointment-related digitalisation should be examined at this point in time by the board."
- D) "The board is invited to note various considerations relating to appointments, bookings, clinics, budgets, and timescales."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Test each version against what a board needs from a summary: what is being asked, how much, to do what, with what intended outcome, by when. Option B answers all five in twenty-six words — decision (approve), amount (£45,000), action (pilot digital booking in two clinics), outcome (cut missed appointments by a quarter), timescale (six months). Option A is jargon without content: no reader can tell what is proposed or what it costs, and "going forward" adds nothing. Option C smothers a simple request in passive voice ("it was determined") and noun inflation ("potentiality", "digitalisation", "at this point in time"). Option D lists topics without making any request — a board cannot act on "note various considerations". Clear-writing questions reward the version that is specific, active, and structured around the reader's decision. This is your documentation duty in miniature: boards fund proposals they can understand in one reading, and the discipline of B — verb, number, action, outcome, time — is a template worth stealing for every summary you write.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Evaluating an argument**

Passage (from a supplier's renewal proposal): "Our service has achieved 99.2% availability this year, up from 98.9% last year. Customer organisations that renewed for three years last cycle reported higher satisfaction than those that renewed for one year. We therefore recommend a three-year renewal, which will maximise your satisfaction while locking in current pricing against inflation."

Which is the strongest criticism of the argument?

- A) The availability figures are irrelevant to a renewal decision.
- B) The satisfaction comparison assumes renewal length caused satisfaction, when satisfied customers may simply be more likely to choose longer renewals.
- C) Inflation may not occur, so price-locking has no value.
- D) 99.2% availability is too low to justify any renewal.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The proposal's central move is: longer renewers report higher satisfaction, therefore a longer renewal will maximise *your* satisfaction. That reverses a plausible causal arrow — organisations already satisfied with the service are naturally more willing to commit for three years, so the correlation may reflect selection, not benefit. Naming that flaw is option B, and it is the criticism that dismantles the recommendation's logic rather than quibbling its edges. Option A goes too far: availability trends are relevant context for renewal, just not proof of the satisfaction claim. Option C attacks the weakest, most defensible part — price-locking has hedging value even if inflation is uncertain. Option D asserts a standard the passage never sets and most services would dispute. Argument-evaluation questions reward finding the load-bearing flaw: ask "which claim does the conclusion actually rest on, and does the evidence support *that* claim?" As a relationship manager you sit between suppliers and business stakeholders precisely so that persuasive-but-flawed reasoning like this gets caught before it becomes a three-year commitment made for someone else's reasons.

**Question 10 (challenging) — True/False/Cannot say with a qualifier chain**

Passage (governance minute): "The board agreed in principle to the records digitisation opportunity, subject to a revised benefits estimate and confirmation of storage costs. The chair noted that, should the revised estimate fall materially below the current figure, the opportunity would need to return to the board rather than proceed under delegated authority. No date was set for the return of the paper."

Statement: "If the revised benefits estimate falls materially below the current figure, the opportunity can proceed under delegated authority once storage costs are confirmed."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Trace the conditions carefully, because the statement is engineered to sound like a paraphrase while reversing a key clause. The minute says: agreement in principle, subject to two conditions (revised estimate, storage cost confirmation), *and* a specific carve-out — if the revised estimate falls materially below the current figure, the opportunity "would need to return to the board rather than proceed under delegated authority". The statement claims that in exactly that scenario — a materially lower estimate — the opportunity *can* proceed under delegated authority. That directly contradicts the carve-out, so the answer is False. It is not Cannot say: the passage speaks precisely to this scenario and rules the statement out. Minutes, standards and conditions clauses reward this "trace the if" discipline, and so does your governance work: when you prepare documentation and shepherd opportunities through boards, you are often the person who must remember, months later, exactly what a conditional approval permitted. Misremembering this minute in the statement's direction — proceeding under delegation when the board required a return — is the kind of error that costs a team its credibility with governance for a year.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Synthesis across two passages**

Passage 1 (dashboard commentary): "Uptake of the new expenses app reached 62% of eligible staff by end of June, against a target of 60%. Support tickets per hundred users fell for the third consecutive month."

Passage 2 (email from a union representative): "Members in field-based roles tell us the expenses app is effectively unusable on the shared devices they carry, and many have reverted to paper claims, which are now taking longer to process than before the app launched."

Which statement do the two passages, taken together, best support?

- A) The union representative's claims are contradicted by the uptake data.
- B) The app has succeeded overall, so the field-based concerns can be addressed in a future phase.
- C) Aggregate uptake and support figures are healthy, but they may mask a poor experience for field-based staff on shared devices, which merits targeted investigation — especially as the paper fallback is degrading.
- D) The dashboard data must be wrong.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passages are not in logical conflict, and seeing why is the whole question. Passage 1 reports aggregates: 62% uptake and falling tickets per hundred users. Passage 2 reports a subgroup: field-based staff on shared devices, struggling and reverting to paper. Both can be true simultaneously — 62% uptake leaves 38% of staff unaccounted for, and users who abandon an app stop raising tickets about it, so falling ticket rates can even be a symptom of the problem. Option A therefore misreads aggregate success as contradiction of subgroup failure. Option B accepts the subgroup's exclusion too cheaply — and misses the sting in Passage 2's tail: the paper route those staff depend on is now *slower than before launch*, so the status quo is actively worsening for them. Option D abandons one source instead of reconciling both. Option C does what your **User focus** and **Business analysis** skills exist to do: hold the quantitative and qualitative evidence together, notice that averages can hide channels and user groups, and convert the tension into a targeted investigation. Meeting user needs across a variety of channels starts with refusing to let a green dashboard close the conversation.

### Preparation tips

- **Answer from the passage, not from your knowledge.** The test's cardinal rule. You may know how demand boards or ITIL really work; the question is what *this* passage guarantees. Practise consciously setting aside background knowledge.
- **Learn the three-way boundary.** True = guaranteed by the passage. False = contradicted by the passage. Cannot say = the passage is silent or insufficient. Most lost marks sit on the False/Cannot-say border: ask "does the passage actively point the other way, or merely fail to confirm?"
- **Underline quantifiers and modals.** Words like all, most, some, only, at least, shall, should, may, will, would need to — these small words carry the logical load. Train your eye to snag on them in tests, minutes, standards, and stakeholder emails alike.
- **Watch for scope shifts.** "Reduced" is not "decommissioned"; "participants" is not "all staff"; "agreed in principle" is not "approved". Statements that swap a nearby word for the passage's actual word are the test's favourite trap — and everyday workplace misquotation works the same way.
- **Practise on your real inbox.** Take a stakeholder email and write down: what is committed, what is conditional, what is merely implied. This is verbal reasoning practice and relationship management practice at once.
- **Read the question before re-reading the passage.** For multi-question passages, a quick first read for structure, then targeted re-reading per question, beats laborious memorisation — the passage stays on screen.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Importing outside knowledge.** The most common error for experienced professionals: you know what usually happens, so you "read" it into the passage. The test — and accurate stakeholder communication — both punish this.
- **Treating plausible as proven.** An inference can be sensible, likely, and still not supported. If a scenario consistent with the passage would make the statement false, the statement is not True.
- **Confusing silence with contradiction.** A passage that fails to mention finance's support does not say finance opposes. Keep Cannot say and False rigorously apart.
- **Skimming past modal verbs and qualifiers.** "Should" read as "shall", "may" read as "must", "up to 25%" read as "25%" — each skim-error changes an obligation or a claim. Slow down exactly where the words are smallest.
- **Choosing summaries that sound impressive.** In clear-writing questions, the polished-sounding, jargon-rich option is nearly always wrong. Specific, active, and reader-focused wins — in tests and in the papers you send to boards.
- **Letting sympathy set the answer.** In passages with competing voices (a supplier, a union, a dashboard), the supported answer reconciles the evidence; it does not pick the side you instinctively favour. Your value as a relationship manager rests on exactly that even-handedness.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment (SJT) presents realistic workplace scenarios and asks you to judge the effectiveness of possible responses. Unlike the other three assessments, it has no single formula: it measures your practical judgement, your alignment with professional behaviours, and your instinct for what actually works in organisations — with real people, real constraints, and real history. Common formats include choosing the most effective response, choosing the most and least effective from a set, ranking all responses, or rating each response independently on an effectiveness scale.

Typical format: 15 to 25 scenarios, often untimed or generously timed (30 to 45 minutes), scored against a key defined by experienced practitioners and occupational psychologists. There is rarely a perfectly right answer; instead, responses are scored on a gradient, and the difference between candidates shows in consistently preferring active, collaborative, user-focused choices over passive, political, or unilateral ones.

For a business relationship manager, the SJT is arguably the most job-relevant assessment of the four, because judgement in ambiguous interpersonal situations *is* the job. You sit between the digital function and the business, absorbing frustration from both directions while trying to keep relationships warm and work moving. You own issues that other people must fix. You champion ideas that compete for scarce funding. You hear things in corridors that governance has not yet heard. Every one of those situations demands a choice about what to do next, and the professional behaviours the SJT scores — user focus, collaboration, ownership, honest communication, sensible escalation, inclusion — are the same behaviours your role summary describes: managing relationships effectively, dealing with challenging situations, removing blockers, and supporting mutual needs while focusing on user needs. Employers use SJTs for this role because they predict the thing that matters most: how you behave when nobody is telling you what to do.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Ownership scenarios** map to your **Ownership and topic** skill: the SJT will test whether you hold an issue until it is genuinely resolved or a new owner is found — or whether you drop it into someone else's queue and look away.
- **Conflict and negotiation scenarios** map to **Stakeholder relationship management (IT operations)**: influencing decisions, dealing with challenging situations, and removing blockers — the scoring key rewards responses that preserve the relationship while still moving the issue.
- **Prioritisation and demand scenarios** map to **Strategic thinking** and **Business analysis (IT operations)**: when everything is urgent, the effective responses connect choices back to strategic goals and evidence rather than to noise or seniority.
- **User advocacy scenarios** map to **User focus**: several scenarios will offer a politically convenient option that quietly sacrifices users; the key almost never rewards it.
- **Process and standards scenarios** map to **Service focus** and **Service management framework knowledge**: coherent frameworks only work when people follow them under pressure, and the SJT tests whether you uphold, adapt, or abandon process when it becomes inconvenient.
- **Team development scenarios** map to your role-level duty to **support continuous improvement activities that mature the capability of the team**: the key rewards responses that fix the instance *and* improve the system that produced it.

### Practice questions

For each scenario, choose the option indicated (most effective, or most and least effective as stated).

**Question 1 (easy) — The unanswered stakeholder**

You return from a week's leave to find that a head of unit you partner with emailed twice during your absence about a delayed laptop rollout for her team, and received no reply — your out-of-office pointed to a shared mailbox that nobody monitored. She has now copied your line manager, describing the digital function as "impossible to reach". What is the most effective first action?

- A) Reply, copying your line manager, explaining that the shared mailbox failure was not your fault.
- B) Phone the head of unit, apologise for the silence, get the current facts on the rollout, and commit to a specific update by a specific time — then fix the shared mailbox gap so it cannot recur.
- C) Forward her email to the rollout team and ask them to respond directly.
- D) Wait until you have a full rollout status before making any contact.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The relationship damage here is about feeling unheard, so the first repair must be human and fast: a call, an apology without defensiveness, and a concrete commitment ("update by Thursday noon") that starts rebuilding reliability. B also fixes the system — the unmonitored mailbox — which is your continuous improvement duty in miniature and is what stops the same failure damaging the next colleague's leave. Option A optimises for blame allocation rather than repair; explaining fault to an audience including your manager reads as self-protection and will land badly with someone who feels ignored. Option C passes the relationship to a delivery team — but the relationship is precisely what *you* own; the rollout team can supply facts, not partnership. Option D confuses "complete information" with "worthy of contact": silence is the injury, so more silence while you research deepens it. The SJT pattern to internalise: acknowledge fast, commit specifically, fix the instance, then fix the system.

**Question 2 (easy) — Credit for an idea**

In a cross-team workshop you facilitated, a quiet colleague from the operations team suggested a change to the onboarding process that later becomes a funded opportunity. At the demand board, a senior manager congratulates *you* on "your excellent idea". What is the most effective response?

- A) Accept the praise; as the facilitator, the pipeline entry is genuinely your work.
- B) Thank the manager and correct the record then and there: name the operations colleague as the source of the idea, and follow up by letting the colleague and their manager know it was funded.
- C) Say nothing at the board, but privately tell the colleague their idea was funded.
- D) Email the board afterwards with a formal correction and an apology.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Your role exists to set the conditions for a culture of meaningful connections — and nothing sets those conditions faster than teams seeing that ideas they share with you come back as credited, funded work. B corrects the attribution in the moment (cheaply and gracefully: "thank you — the idea came from Priya in operations; we shaped it together"), then completes the loop by telling the colleague and their manager. That closes the credibility circuit that keeps your pipeline fed: people stop offering ideas to a partner who absorbs the credit. Option A takes a small reputational gain today at the cost of the trust your entire function runs on — and quiet colleagues notice most. Option C preserves the colleague's feelings but leaves the board's record wrong and the colleague invisible to decision-makers. Option D treats a light-touch social correction as a governance event, generating awkwardness disproportionate to the error. SJT keys consistently reward generous, immediate, low-drama attribution — because in real organisations it is the behaviour that keeps information flowing toward the people whose job is to hear it.

**Question 3 (easy) — The tempting shortcut**

A team you partner with wants to buy a £4,000 software subscription and asks you to "just put it through as an existing-service renewal so we can skip the new-demand paperwork — it's basically the same tool as the one we already use". You know the demand process requires new subscriptions to be assessed, partly to check security and duplication. What is the most effective response?

- A) Process it as a renewal this once, and tell the team the proper route must be used next time.
- B) Refuse and report the team's request to the governance board as an attempted process breach.
- C) Explain why the assessment exists, decline the workaround, and offer to help them through the new-demand route quickly — including checking first whether the existing tool already meets the need.
- D) Tell the team to submit it however they wish; the process is their responsibility, not yours.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The effective response holds the process *and* the relationship, and C does both: it declines the mislabelling, explains the genuine purpose of assessment (security and duplication checks protect the team too), and converts your role into help — including the sharp-eyed suggestion that the "basically the same tool" claim might mean no purchase is needed at all, which is exactly the duplication the process exists to catch. Option A trades a control away for comfort; "just this once" becomes precedent the moment the team mentions it to a neighbouring team, and if the tool later fails a security check, the mislabelled renewal is your name on a bypass. Option B escalates a request into an accusation: the team asked a question, and treating questions as breaches teaches teams to stop asking — the opposite of the transparency you need. Option D abandons your service focus: coherent frameworks survive because people like you make the compliant route genuinely easier than the workaround. That is the repeatable key: make doing it properly the path of least resistance.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Most and least effective: the stalled issue**

Six weeks ago you took ownership of a recurring failure in the interface between the HR and finance systems, which causes duplicate records that both teams manually correct. The fix sits with a supplier, who has twice missed promised dates. Both teams are losing patience with you personally, since you are the visible owner. Identify the **most** effective and **least** effective responses.

1. Tell both teams the supplier keeps missing dates, so the delay is outside your control, and share the supplier's latest promised date.
2. Convene the supplier and both teams: get a realistic date with intermediate checkpoints from the supplier, agree an interim workaround that reduces the manual correction burden, and send all parties a single shared status you update weekly until resolution.
3. Hand the issue to the service desk queue so it is tracked formally, and move on to your other work.
4. Escalate immediately to the supplier's account director demanding contractual penalties.

- A) Most effective: 2; Least effective: 3
- B) Most effective: 4; Least effective: 1
- C) Most effective: 2; Least effective: 4
- D) Most effective: 1; Least effective: 3

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Response 2 is the model of the **Ownership and topic** behaviour your role names: it attacks all three dimensions of the problem — the unreliable fix date (checkpoints make slippage visible early), the ongoing pain (an interim workaround reduces the manual burden now), and the eroding trust (one shared, regularly updated status replaces two anxious teams chasing you separately). Response 3 is least effective because it is disguised abandonment: the issue gains a ticket number and loses its owner, precisely what your skill definition — own an issue until a new owner is found or the problem is mitigated or resolved — rules out; a queue is not an owner. Ranking the remaining two: response 1 is weak (it relocates blame and re-shares a promise from a source that has twice proven unreliable, doing nothing for the teams' workload) but it is at least honest communication; response 4 is premature as a *first* move yet remains a legitimate escalation tool if checkpoints fail — so neither is the floor. The pair in option A captures the key's logic: the best answer fixes pain, process and trust together; the worst answer makes ownership disappear while appearing procedurally tidy.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Competing sponsors**

Two directors you partner with each want the single available delivery slot next quarter. Director A's project digitises a paper process used by 3,000 staff, with a solid benefits case. Director B's project fixes a clunky journey for 400 external users with accessibility needs, with a weaker financial case but strong user research showing real exclusion. Director B phones you: "You're our business partner — make sure we get the slot." What is the most effective response?

- A) Advocate for Director B as requested; external users with accessibility needs must always come first.
- B) Advocate for Director A; the benefits case is stronger and benefits cases exist to settle exactly this question.
- C) Tell Director B that you will present both cases objectively to the prioritisation forum — including the accessibility evidence, which the financial case understates — and help both directors strengthen their submissions rather than championing one privately.
- D) Suggest both directors escalate to their common superior to decide.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The trap in this scenario is the phrase "you're our business partner" — an invitation to convert a trusted-broker role into a private advocate role. C declines that conversion while genuinely serving Director B: it commits to putting the accessibility evidence squarely in front of the forum (where a purely financial comparison would under-represent it), offers the same strengthening help to both, and keeps the decision where it belongs — with the prioritisation forum, using transparent criteria. This is your **Stakeholder relationship management** skill working as designed: supporting mutual needs and commitments while focusing on user needs, without becoming one stakeholder's lobbyist against another. Option A abandons even-handedness and substitutes a personal rule ("always first") for the organisation's prioritisation process; the sentiment honours inclusion, but the method makes you unusable as a partner to Director A ever again. Option B mirrors the same error in the other direction, and quietly discards user research as evidence — your **User focus** skill says quantitative *and* qualitative data both count. Option D escalates a decision the organisation already has a forum for, teaching directors that lobbying beats process. The durable lesson: a relationship manager's influence comes from being trusted by all sides to make every case well.

**Question 6 (moderate) — The corridor disclosure**

A team leader mentions to you, informally over coffee, that his team has quietly started using a free consumer file-sharing app to send case documents to an external partner organisation "because the official transfer tool keeps timing out". Case documents include personal information. He adds: "Don't make this into a thing — I'm telling you because you're the one person who actually listens." What is the most effective response?

- A) Respect the confidence; report nothing, but encourage him to stop using the app.
- B) Tell him clearly that personal data in a consumer app is a risk you cannot sit on, help him report it to the security team that day — framing it as fixing a broken tool that forced a workaround — and take ownership of getting the timeout problem investigated so the compliant route becomes usable.
- C) Report the team to the security team immediately without telling him, to avoid tipping anyone off.
- D) Advise him to delete the app history and revert to the official tool once the timeouts are fixed.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Two obligations collide: the trust that makes people tell you things, and a live data-protection risk involving personal case information that you cannot un-know. B honours both to the maximum extent possible: it is honest with him about the limits of confidence ("this one I can't sit on"), it brings *him* into the reporting rather than going around him, it frames the report truthfully as a symptom of a failing official tool — which protects his team from being cast as villains and points remediation at the real cause — and it commits your ownership to fixing the timeout, without which the workaround will simply return. Option A makes you complicit: an unreported personal-data exposure continues daily while you hope encouragement works. Option C treats a colleague who trusted you as a suspect; the report may be necessary, but doing it *behind* him converts one disclosure into a permanent lesson — organisation-wide, once he tells the story — that telling you things is dangerous. Option D is the worst of all worlds: concealing evidence compounds a risk into misconduct. The key's logic: candour about your obligations, performed *with* the discloser, preserves more trust than secrecy in either direction — and fixing the broken tool is what stops the next team making the same choice silently.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Most and least effective: the improvement that lands on you**

In a team retrospective, colleagues agree that engagement records are inconsistent — different people record different things, and reports are unreliable. Someone suggests a standard template, and all eyes turn to you: "You're good at this — can you sort it?" Your own workload is already full, and you privately think the team's tooling, not just the template, is the real problem. Identify the **most** and **least** effective responses.

1. Accept the task and quietly add it to your backlog, where it will realistically wait months.
2. Agree to lead a first, small step — drafting a template with two volunteers within a fortnight — while putting your tooling hypothesis to the team as a follow-on question with evidence to be gathered from the next month's records.
3. Decline, explaining your workload is full, and suggest the team lead assigns it to someone else.
4. Accept, then redesign the team's entire recording toolchain to fix the problem properly before releasing any template.

- A) Most effective: 2; Least effective: 1
- B) Most effective: 4; Least effective: 3
- C) Most effective: 2; Least effective: 4
- D) Most effective: 3; Least effective: 1

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Response 2 embodies how continuous improvement actually matures a team's capability: a small, dated, shared first step (template, two volunteers, a fortnight) that creates momentum, plus the honesty to surface your deeper hypothesis — tooling — as a question to be tested with evidence rather than a unilateral diagnosis. It also distributes the work, which matters: improvement owned by one "good at this" person is improvement the team never learns. Response 1 is least effective precisely because it *looks* cooperative: you accept in the meeting, the team relaxes, and the inconsistency continues untouched for months behind the shield of your acceptance — a silent commitment failure that is worse than an honest decline, because it blocks anyone else from picking the task up. Comparing the other pair confirms the ranking: response 3 is suboptimal but honest — the team can re-plan around a clear no; response 4 over-scopes a fortnight's fix into a toolchain programme, delaying all value and betting the team's patience on your private theory, but at least it is moving. The SJT pattern: small committed steps beat both silent acceptance and grand redesigns — and hypotheses about root causes earn their status through evidence, not seniority of voice.

**Question 8 (moderate) — The overheard misinformation**

At a directorate all-hands, a well-liked senior manager tells the audience that "the new case management system is being imposed on us without any user input". You know this is wrong: two rounds of user research included eight staff from this very directorate, and their feedback changed the rollout plan. The manager outranks you, and the meeting is large. What is the most effective response?

- A) Publicly correct the manager from the floor, citing the research rounds and participant numbers.
- B) Say nothing; contradicting a senior manager in public would damage your relationship.
- C) Speak to the manager privately straight after the meeting with the research facts, offer to connect her with the staff who participated, and suggest a joint follow-up message or session so her audience hears the corrected picture from her.
- D) Email the whole directorate afterwards with the correct information, copying the manager.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Weigh what actually needs to happen: the misinformation needs correcting *for that audience*, the manager's standing needs preserving (she is a relationship you will need for years), and the correction sticks best if it comes from the person who made the error. C achieves all three: private, prompt, evidence-based, face-saving — and it upgrades the correction into engagement by offering to connect her with the very staff who participated, turning a critic into a potential advocate. Note that C does not let the record stand: the joint follow-up is the mechanism that reaches the audience. Option A wins the argument and loses the relationship; public correction of a senior colleague in a large meeting humiliates, and humiliated stakeholders do not become partners — dealing with challenging situations, in your skill set, means removing blockers without creating new ones. Option B protects the relationship but abandons the truth: a directorate now believes users were ignored, which poisons adoption of the system and contradicts your duty to champion the user research that actually happened. Option D corrects the record while publicly exposing the manager's error in writing — A's damage at email speed. The SJT key here rewards the pattern: correct privately, equip the person to fix it publicly, and convert the moment into connection.

**Question 9 (challenging) — The pipeline under pressure**

Late on Friday, your head of function asks you to add a senior director's pet idea to next week's demand board pack "with a positive recommendation — it will make the board go smoothly". Your investigation notes from earlier in the month show the idea duplicates roughly 70% of an existing service's capability, and the affected service owner has not been consulted. What is the most effective response?

- A) Add the idea with a positive recommendation as instructed; your head of function owns the pack.
- B) Add the idea to the pack, but with your genuine assessment — the duplication evidence and the missing consultation — and tell your head of function on Friday what the pack will say and why, offering a path that keeps the board smooth: a two-week deferral to consult the service owner and test the overlap.
- C) Refuse to include the idea and tell your head of function the process must be followed.
- D) Include the idea with a positive recommendation, but privately brief the service owner so they can object at the board.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The pressure here is to trade analytical honesty for meeting-room smoothness, and the effective answer refuses the trade while still serving the underlying interests. B keeps your assessment intact — duplication evidence and the consultation gap go in the pack, because a recommendation you do not believe is not yours to sign — and it treats your head of function as a partner, not an obstacle: they hear on Friday, not at the board, and they receive a constructive route (short deferral, consult the service owner, test the 70% overlap) that plausibly serves the senior director better than a positive recommendation that unravels publicly when the duplication surfaces later. That is influencing decisions and dealing with challenging situations, applied upward. Option A makes you the author of advice you know to be wrong; when the duplication is discovered — and service owners discover these things — the pack carries your name and your function's credibility. Option C defends the process but abandons the people: a bare refusal leaves your head of function surprised and cornered, converting a resolvable pressure into a conflict. Option D is the most corrosive: it manufactures an ambush at the board, using the service owner as a proxy for the objection you chose not to make openly — the opposite of the transparent brokering your role stands on. The pattern the key rewards: dissent early, in private, with evidence, and always attached to a workable alternative.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Most and least effective: the failing relationship**

Your relationship with the head of a major business unit has deteriorated. He cancels meetings, answers your emails tersely, and last week told a colleague that "the digital partnering thing adds a layer without adding value". His unit's engagement scores are your lowest, and two of his ideas were rejected by the demand board earlier this year. Identify the **most** and **least** effective responses.

1. Reduce contact to essential items only, and focus your energy on the units that value partnering.
2. Ask his deputy — with whom you get on well — to act as your channel into the unit from now on.
3. Request a short one-to-one with him, open by asking what would make partnering worth his time, listen without defending, revisit what happened to his two rejected ideas — including what could make a future submission succeed — and agree one concrete thing you will deliver for the unit in the next month.
4. Raise the deteriorating relationship with your line manager and ask for advice before doing anything.

- A) Most effective: 3; Least effective: 1
- B) Most effective: 3; Least effective: 2
- C) Most effective: 4; Least effective: 1
- D) Most effective: 2; Least effective: 4

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Diagnose before ranking: the behavioural evidence (cancellations, terseness, "adds a layer without value") plus the history (two rejected ideas) suggests a stakeholder who feels the partnership costs him time and delivers him refusals. Response 3 addresses that diagnosis directly — it asks *his* definition of value, absorbs criticism without defensiveness, reopens the rejections as learning rather than grievance, and stakes credibility on one delivered outcome within a month, which is how trust is actually rebuilt: demonstration, not persuasion. Response 1 is least effective because it ratifies his critique — a partner who withdraws when scores drop is *proving* that partnering adds no value in hard cases — and it abandons the unit's users and pipeline along with its head; your lowest-scoring unit is precisely where the role earns its keep. Ranking the middle pair: response 2 keeps information flowing but institutionalises the breakdown, and a head of unit who discovers he has been managed *around* via his deputy acquires a legitimate grievance to add to his existing ones; response 4 is genuinely reasonable as preparation — seeking advice is not weakness — but as a *complete* response it delays contact and delegates thinking about your core professional challenge. So 3 over 4 over 2 over 1. The key's consistent logic: toward the difficult stakeholder, not around, not away — with listening first and a deliverable attached.

**Question 11 (challenging) — The improvement that threatens a colleague**

While mapping engagement activity, you find that a monthly report a colleague in your own team has produced for years is read by almost nobody: the three units that receive it confirm they stopped using it when the dashboard launched, and its production takes her about two days a month. She is proud of the report and has mentioned it in her performance conversations. Your team is under pressure to free capacity for higher-value partnering work. What is the most effective response?

- A) Present the readership evidence at the next team meeting and recommend the report be discontinued to free two days of monthly capacity.
- B) Say nothing; two days a month is a small price for a colleague's morale, and it is not your report to question.
- C) Share the evidence with her privately first, frame it as the dashboard having overtaken the report rather than the report failing, and explore with her what the freed two days could become — ideally letting her propose and own the change, and carry the improved offer to the three units herself.
- D) Suggest to your team lead, without naming the report, that a general audit of all team outputs be run to find low-value work.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The task wraps a straightforward continuous-improvement finding (two days a month producing an unread report) inside a human problem (the finding lands on a proud colleague's signature work). C sequences it correctly: evidence goes to *her* first, framed accurately and kindly — the dashboard overtook the report, which is a story about organisational change, not personal failure — and the freed capacity is offered as *her* opportunity to shape and own, including the stakeholder conversations, which converts a potential humiliation into a visible improvement she leads. That is what "support continuous improvement activities that mature the capability of the team" looks like when the improvement has a face. Option A is procedurally clean and humanly careless: ambushing a colleague with readership data in front of the team guarantees defensiveness, and teaches everyone present that improvement findings are attacks — after which nobody volunteers information about their own low-value work again. Option B is the comfortable failure: it protects one person's feelings this quarter at the price of twenty-four days a year of capacity your pressured team needs, and it leaves her investing pride in an artefact whose irrelevance she will eventually discover less kindly. Option D launders a specific, known finding through a fake-general process — colleagues see through synthetic audits, and the indirection costs you the candour credit that C earns. The pattern: take the evidence to the person, separate the work's obsolescence from their worth, and hand them the authorship of the change.

**Question 12 (challenging) — The spending review squeeze**

A spending review requires your directorate to model a 15% reduction in digital spend. Your head of function asks you, as the person closest to the business units, to sound out "quietly, without alarming anyone" where cuts would do least harm. Within a day, a unit head you partner with asks you directly: "I hear there's a cuts exercise — is my modernisation project safe?" The project is, in your honest view, one of the more likely candidates for deferral. What is the most effective response?

- A) Deny any knowledge of a cuts exercise, as you were asked to keep it quiet.
- B) Tell him his project is probably going to be deferred, so he can prepare.
- C) Acknowledge that scenario planning for the spending review is happening without confirming specifics, decline honestly to speculate on individual projects before any decision exists, commit to telling him as soon as there is something real to tell, and separately press your head of function to communicate about the exercise before rumour does the communicating.
- D) Refer his question to your head of function and say nothing further.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** You hold two legitimate duties in tension: confidentiality owed to an unfinished planning exercise, and honesty owed to a stakeholder whose trust is your working capital. C threads them precisely. It refuses the lie (the exercise exists and he has already heard so — denial would be discovered and remembered); it refuses the leak (naming his project as a likely cut converts your private analysis into organisational fact, pre-empting a decision no one has made and potentially triggering exactly the alarm and lobbying the quiet phase was meant to avoid); it gives him something true and useful — a commitment to early, direct communication; and, crucially, it acts on the systemic problem by pushing your head of function to get ahead of the rumour, because "quietly" has already failed and unmanaged silence is now the biggest risk to every relationship you hold. Option A spends your integrity on a secret already half-out; when the exercise surfaces, he will recall the denial every time you speak. Option B feels like loyalty to him but is a betrayal of process that also harms *him* — "probably deferred" from the business partner becomes certainty by the third retelling, and if the decision goes the other way you have caused needless damage. Option D is technically safe and relationally empty: a partner who answers hard questions with a referral is teaching stakeholders to route around him. The senior-level lesson inside this mid-level scenario: when confidentiality and candour collide, be honest about the *existence* of uncertainty, refuse speculation, commit to early truth, and fix the communication gap that created the collision.

### Preparation tips

- **Learn the behaviours being scored.** SJT keys for roles like yours consistently reward: active ownership over passivity, going *to* the difficult person over around them, fixing the system as well as the instance, honesty about limits and mistakes, user needs held visible under political pressure, and early private dissent with an alternative attached. Read your role summary before the test — the scoring key was probably written from something very like it.
- **Answer as your best professional self on a normal day** — not an idealised hero and not your most tired Friday. The test asks what is effective, and effectiveness includes being realistic about organisations.
- **Separate the instance from the system.** Many scenarios contain both a problem to fix now and a process that let it happen. The strongest responses usually address both; train yourself to look for the second half.
- **Watch the verbs in the options.** "Wait", "note", "forward", "hope" signal passivity; "convene", "agree a date", "commit", "own" signal the behaviours keys reward. This is not infallible, but it is a strong prior.
- **For most/least formats, find the floor first.** The least effective option is often easier to identify (passive abandonment, public humiliation, concealment, dishonesty) and eliminating it clarifies the top choice.
- **Debrief your real weeks.** After a difficult stakeholder situation, ask: what were my four options, which did I take, and what would the scoring key have said? Your own diary is the best SJT question bank available.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Choosing the answer that avoids all discomfort.** Options built on silence, delay, or routing around people minimise short-term awkwardness and score poorly, because discomfort deferred compounds. Effective responses lean in early while the problem is small.
- **Confusing escalation with abdication.** Escalating *with* evidence, options, and your own recommendation is a professional act; referring a problem upward so it stops being yours is not. Keys reward the first and punish the second.
- **Over-weighting hierarchy.** "The senior person asked, so comply" and "never challenge upward" both misread the keys, which reward respectful, early, private, evidence-based challenge — with an alternative attached.
- **Sacrificing users when they are off-stage.** In scenarios where users are absent and stakeholders are present, the politically smooth option often quietly costs users. Keys for user-focused roles almost never reward it; keep the absent party in your reasoning.
- **Protecting yourself instead of the outcome.** Documentation trails, covering emails, and blame-precision ("not my fault") optimise for blamelessness. Assessments — and the colleagues you partner with — can tell the difference between covering yourself and owning the problem.
- **Treating honesty and kindness as alternatives.** The weakest answers are brutally honest or kindly dishonest. The strongest are both: accurate about the evidence, generous about the person. If an option achieves that combination, it is very likely the key.
