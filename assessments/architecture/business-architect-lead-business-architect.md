## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the exact mental processes your role demands, using practical scenarios and artefacts rather than abstract puzzles. Unlike a generic IQ test, every question is grounded in the kind of material a lead business architect really handles: enterprise capability models, strategic roadmaps, target operating model designs, governance decision logs, and portfolio dependency maps. The assessment is typically delivered online, timed at around 15 to 30 minutes, and scored objectively against a benchmark or norm group. Some platforms adapt question difficulty based on your previous answers, and many employers now receive a breakdown of speed versus accuracy rather than a single score.

For your role, cognitive assessment matters because the core of lead business architecture work is cognitive: you aggregate and disaggregate complex information from across the organisation, spot strategic misalignment, deduce the consequences of design decisions, and check that project-level designs remain consistent with enterprise strategy. When you direct the work of other business architects, your ability to rapidly identify a flaw in a capability map or a contradiction in a roadmap is what allows you to give timely, credible direction. The assessment simulates that under time pressure.

Expect a mix of question styles: logical deduction from a set of architectural rules, pattern recognition across models and matrices, error checking of documents and data, prioritisation of competing demands, and structured problem solving where you must identify the strongest inference from partial information. You will usually get a few ungraded practice questions first so you can get comfortable with the interface.

### What it measures for your role

The dimensions of this assessment map directly onto the skills in your role profile:

- **Business architecture**: recognising patterns and inconsistencies in capability models, frameworks, and standards; simplifying complex frameworks into clear actions; identifying strategic misalignment and reasoning about how to correct it.
- **Strategic design and business change**: deducing the short- and long-term impact of business events and design choices; reasoning about principles, patterns, standards, policies, and roadmaps; keeping the focus on outcomes rather than solutions.
- **Strategic thinking**: evaluating whether current strategies actually meet business requirements, and reasoning from strategy down to delivery detail and back up again.
- **Making and guiding decisions**: logical deduction under conditions of medium-to-high risk and complexity, including judging when a decision can be made and when it should be escalated or recommended instead.
- **Methods and tools**: assessing the effectiveness of different approaches (such as Agile or plan-driven) from evidence, and reasoning about which method fits which problem.
- **Consultancy**: rapidly diagnosing the real problem beneath a stakeholder's stated request - a fundamentally cognitive act of disaggregation.

Aggregating and disaggregating complex information - a duty named explicitly in your role - is essentially what a cognitive assessment tests: can you take a mass of detail, find the structure inside it, and draw the correct conclusion?

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) - Logical deduction from architectural principles**

Your organisation's enterprise architecture principles include the following three rules:

- Rule 1: Every business capability must have exactly one accountable owner at director level.
- Rule 2: Any capability rated "critical" must be reviewed by the architecture board annually.
- Rule 3: A capability may only be rated "critical" if it supports at least one ministerial priority outcome.

The "Grants Administration" capability supports two ministerial priority outcomes and is rated "critical". Which of the following statements must be true?

- A) Grants Administration has more than one accountable owner.
- B) Grants Administration must be reviewed by the architecture board annually.
- C) Grants Administration is the most important capability in the organisation.
- D) Every capability supporting a ministerial priority outcome is rated "critical".

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** Rule 2 states that any capability rated "critical" must be reviewed annually by the architecture board; Grants Administration is rated critical, so B follows necessarily. A contradicts Rule 1, which requires exactly one accountable owner. C introduces a comparative claim ("most important") that appears nowhere in the rules. D reverses Rule 3: supporting a ministerial priority is a necessary condition for a critical rating, not a sufficient one. Watch for this reversal pattern - conflating "only if" with "if" is one of the most common deduction errors, and it mirrors a real architectural mistake: assuming that because a project touches a strategic outcome, it automatically warrants strategic treatment.

**Question 2 (easy) - Error checking a capability register extract**

You are quality-assuring a capability register extract prepared by one of your business architects before it goes to the executive committee. The register states:

| Capability | Owner | Maturity (1-5) | Target maturity | Gap |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Case Management | Director of Operations | 2 | 4 | 2 |
| Identity Verification | Director of Digital | 3 | 4 | 1 |
| Payments Processing | Director of Finance | 4 | 3 | 1 |
| Correspondence Handling | Director of Operations | 2 | 5 | 3 |

Which row contains an anomaly that you should query before publication?

- A) Case Management
- B) Identity Verification
- C) Payments Processing
- D) Correspondence Handling

**Correct answer: C.**

**Explanation:** Payments Processing shows a current maturity of 4 and a target of 3 - the target is below the current state, yet the gap is recorded as a positive 1 with no indication that the organisation intends to deliberately reduce investment in that capability. This may be a data-entry transposition (target and current swapped) or a genuine strategic decision to simplify - either way, it needs a query before an executive audience sees it. The other rows are internally consistent (target minus current equals the stated gap). This is classic lead-architect error checking: the arithmetic is trivial, but spotting that a number is *implausible in context*, not merely miscalculated, is the skill being measured.

**Question 3 (moderate) - Pattern recognition across a roadmap**

A three-year business architecture roadmap shows the following sequencing pattern for capability uplift work:

- Year 1: Discovery for Capability A; Design for nothing; Delivery for nothing.
- Year 2: Discovery for Capabilities B and C; Design for Capability A; Delivery for nothing.
- Year 3: Discovery for Capability D; Design for Capabilities B and C; Delivery for Capability A.

If the pattern continues, in which year would Capability D first enter Delivery, and how many capabilities would be in Design in Year 4?

- A) Year 4; one capability in Design.
- B) Year 5; one capability in Design.
- C) Year 5; two capabilities in Design.
- D) Year 4; two capabilities in Design.

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** The pattern is a three-stage pipeline in which each capability spends one year in Discovery, moves to Design the following year, and reaches Delivery the year after that. Capability D enters Discovery in Year 3, so it moves to Design in Year 4 and reaches Delivery in Year 5. In Year 4, the capabilities in Design are those that were in Discovery in Year 3 - only Capability D - so exactly one capability is in Design. Option D is the trap for candidates who track the delivery date correctly but forget that B and C have already moved out of Design by Year 4. Pipeline reasoning of this kind is exactly what you do when you assure that a roadmap's dependencies and sequencing are coherent.

**Question 4 (moderate) - Strategic misalignment detection**

Your organisation's published strategy commits it to "channel shift: reducing reliance on paper and telephone transactions by consolidating onto a single digital service platform by 2028". You review four programme design summaries. Which one is most clearly misaligned with the strategy?

- A) A programme decommissioning a legacy telephony system and migrating its transactions to the digital platform, completing in 2027.
- B) A programme extending the digital platform to support assisted-digital users who cannot self-serve online.
- C) A programme procuring a new, separate case-handling portal for one business unit because "the central platform's roadmap is too slow for our needs".
- D) A programme digitising inbound paper correspondence so that it enters the digital platform's workflow.

**Correct answer: C.**

**Explanation:** The strategy commits to consolidation onto a single platform. Option C creates a second, separate portal - a direct structural contradiction of the consolidation commitment, however understandable the local motivation. Options A and D actively advance channel shift and consolidation. Option B might superficially look misaligned because it supports non-digital users, but assisted digital provision is a standard and necessary component of inclusive channel shift in UK government services; it extends the single platform rather than fragmenting it. The lesson at your level: misalignment is usually rationalised locally ("too slow for our needs"), and detecting it requires comparing structural consequences against strategic commitments, not judging intentions. Identifying strategic misalignment and recommending improvement is a named behaviour in your business architecture skill.

**Question 5 (moderate) - Prioritisation under constraint**

It is Monday morning. You have the following five items competing for your attention this week, and capacity to fully address only three:

1. The executive committee meets Thursday and has asked you to present the target operating model options paper; the current draft has a known gap in the workforce implications section.
2. A junior business architect in your team has asked for coaching on a capability modelling technique before their first solo stakeholder workshop on Friday.
3. A programme board meeting Wednesday will approve a solution design that you suspect conflicts with an enterprise principle, though you have not yet confirmed the conflict.
4. Your quarterly community of practice session, scheduled for next Monday, needs an agenda circulated by this Friday per your own published standard.
5. A peer in another department has asked for informal comments on their architecture framework "whenever you get a chance".

Which three should you prioritise this week?

- A) 1, 2, and 5
- B) 1, 3, and 4
- C) 1, 2, and 3
- D) 2, 3, and 4

**Correct answer: C.**

**Explanation:** Item 1 is non-negotiable: an executive presentation with a known gap, due Thursday, sits squarely within your accountability for team outputs and your duty to work with executive stakeholders. Item 3 is time-critical and high-consequence: if the Wednesday board approves a design that breaches an enterprise principle, the cost of unwinding it later is far higher than the cost of investigating now - and confirming or dismissing your suspicion before the decision point is exactly the assurance role a lead architect plays. Item 2 has a hard deadline (Friday workshop) and reflects your accountability for developing your team's capability through coaching; sending an unprepared architect into their first solo workshop damages both the individual and the function's credibility. Item 4 matters but is administratively light and could be delegated or trimmed without material harm; item 5 is explicitly non-urgent. Option B is the plausible distractor: it sacrifices your coaching commitment for an internal deadline you set yourself and could flex. The measured skill is distinguishing urgency, consequence, and accountability - not simply doing whatever has the nearest date.

**Question 6 (moderate) - Logical deduction from stakeholder statements**

Preparing for a target operating model decision, you gather three statements:

- The Finance Director says: "If the shared services option is chosen, my directorate will need 18 months to restructure."
- The HR Director says: "No restructure of any directorate can begin until the workforce agreement is renegotiated, which completes in six months."
- The Chief Operating Officer says: "Whichever option is chosen, all restructuring must be complete within 24 months of the decision."

Assuming all three statements are accurate, which conclusion follows?

- A) The shared services option cannot be delivered within the COO's constraint.
- B) The shared services option can meet the COO's constraint only if Finance's restructure starts immediately after the workforce agreement completes and runs to plan.
- C) The workforce agreement renegotiation should be accelerated.
- D) The Finance Director opposes the shared services option.

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** Chain the constraints: restructuring cannot begin for 6 months (HR), Finance then needs 18 months (Finance), giving 6 + 18 = 24 months minimum - exactly equal to the COO's 24-month limit. The option is therefore feasible, but only with zero slack: the restructure must start the moment the workforce agreement completes and must not overrun. A is too strong (24 = 24, not > 24); C is a recommendation, not a deduction - nothing in the statements says acceleration is possible or needed; D infers a position from a factual statement about duration, a classic error when reading stakeholders. In real executive work, recognising a zero-slack plan is a critical insight: you would advise the executive committee that the option is technically feasible but carries schedule risk with no contingency.

**Question 7 (moderate) - Aggregation and disaggregation**

You receive capability assessments from four business units. Each unit assessed its own fraud-checking activity independently:

- Unit W: "We check 100% of high-value cases manually."
- Unit X: "We use an automated rules engine for all cases; no manual checks."
- Unit Y: "We check a 10% random sample manually plus all flagged cases."
- Unit Z: "Fraud checking is performed by Unit W on our behalf."

You are asked whether the organisation has "a consistent enterprise fraud-checking capability". Which is the most defensible conclusion to draw from the evidence?

- A) Yes - all four units perform or receive fraud checking, so the capability exists consistently.
- B) No - the capability exists in all units, but it is delivered through at least three materially different operating approaches, so it is not consistent.
- C) No - Unit Z has no fraud-checking capability at all.
- D) Cannot conclude anything until every unit adopts the automated rules engine.

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** This question tests the aggregation/disaggregation duty in your role profile. Aggregating: all four units are covered by some fraud-checking arrangement, so the capability exists organisation-wide. Disaggregating: the delivery models differ fundamentally - full manual on high-value only (W, and Z by proxy), fully automated (X), and hybrid sampling (Y). "Exists everywhere" and "consistent everywhere" are different claims; a lead business architect must keep them apart. A conflates existence with consistency. C is wrong because sourcing a capability from another unit is a legitimate delivery model - Z has the capability via a shared-service arrangement. D confuses the analytical conclusion with a particular solution, violating the outcome-over-solution principle in your strategic design skill.

**Question 8 (challenging) - Method selection from evidence**

You are assessing which delivery approach to recommend for three initiatives, applying your organisation's method-selection guidance: "Use iterative (Agile) approaches where user needs are uncertain or evolving; use plan-driven approaches where requirements are fixed by legislation or external contract; use hybrid approaches where a fixed regulatory core is surrounded by evolving user-facing services."

- Initiative 1: Implementing a levy calculation engine whose rules are defined precisely in a new Act, with a citizen-facing dashboard whose design will be shaped by user research.
- Initiative 2: Redesigning the internal staff intranet based on continuous user feedback.
- Initiative 3: Producing statutory annual accounts to an externally mandated format and deadline.

Which allocation follows the guidance?

- A) 1: Agile; 2: Agile; 3: hybrid
- B) 1: hybrid; 2: Agile; 3: plan-driven
- C) 1: plan-driven; 2: hybrid; 3: plan-driven
- D) 1: hybrid; 2: plan-driven; 3: Agile

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** Initiative 1 has exactly the hybrid signature described in the guidance: a fixed legislative core (the levy rules) plus an evolving user-facing layer (the dashboard shaped by research). Initiative 2 is the textbook Agile case - user needs uncertain and evolving, no external fixity. Initiative 3 is fixed by external mandate in both format and deadline, so plan-driven. This question rehearses your methods and tools skill: setting direction on methodology means matching the method to the intrinsic characteristics of the work, not to fashion or habit. Note the reasoning discipline: each classification is justified by a specific feature of the initiative mapped to a specific clause of the guidance - the same traceable justification you would expect from an architect you were coaching.

**Question 9 (challenging) - Dependency logic in a transformation portfolio**

Your portfolio dependency map records the following (X → Y means X must complete before Y can start):

- Data Migration → Case System Go-Live
- Workforce Training → Case System Go-Live
- Case System Go-Live → Legacy Decommission
- Process Redesign → Workforce Training
- Data Cleansing → Data Migration

The Process Redesign workstream reports a three-month delay. Data Cleansing reports a one-month delay. All other workstreams remain on plan, and no activity durations change. What is the effect on Legacy Decommission?

- A) Delayed by four months, because delays accumulate along the chain.
- B) Delayed by three months, because the Process Redesign path is the binding constraint.
- C) Delayed by one month, because Data Cleansing sits earliest in the chain.
- D) Cannot be determined without knowing the planned float on each path.

**Correct answer: D.**

**Explanation:** This is the subtlest question so far, and the correct answer is the honest one. Two paths lead into Case System Go-Live: Process Redesign → Workforce Training → Go-Live, and Data Cleansing → Data Migration → Go-Live. A three-month delay on one path and a one-month delay on the other only translate into a Go-Live delay if those paths had no float - and the scenario tells you nothing about float or about which path was originally critical. If Workforce Training was originally scheduled to finish four months before Data Migration, a three-month delay to its predecessor might consume slack without moving Go-Live at all. A commits the accumulation fallacy (delays on parallel paths do not add). B and C each assume, without evidence, that a particular path is critical. Recognising when the given information is insufficient - and saying so - is a hallmark of senior architectural judgement, and assessments at this level deliberately include questions whose correct answer is "cannot be determined". Never let time pressure push you into manufacturing certainty.

**Question 10 (challenging) - Simplifying a complex framework into clear actions**

Your organisation has adopted an industry business architecture framework with 41 practice areas across 6 domains. The executive committee has asked you for "no more than three actions this year". Your maturity assessment shows: 34 practice areas scored at or above target; 4 scored one level below target, all within the "Strategy Traceability" domain; 3 scored two levels below target, spread across three different domains, but all three relate to how change initiatives are prioritised and funded.

Which set of three actions best reflects sound disaggregation of this evidence?

- A) Launch improvement projects for all 7 below-target practice areas, grouped into three tranches by domain.
- B) One action to uplift the Strategy Traceability domain as a coherent whole; one action addressing the cross-domain prioritisation-and-funding weakness as a single systemic issue; one action to sustain the 34 at-target areas through the community of practice.
- C) Focus all three actions on the three practice areas scoring two levels below target, since they are furthest behind.
- D) Commission a deeper assessment of all 41 practice areas before acting.

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** The evidence contains two genuine patterns and the correct response names both. The four one-below-target areas cluster in a single domain - that is one coherent problem (Strategy Traceability) deserving one coherent action. The three two-below-target areas look scattered by domain but share a common causal theme (prioritisation and funding of change) - correct disaggregation groups by cause, not by the framework's own taxonomy. The third action - sustaining what is working through the community of practice - reflects the maturity-development behaviour in your business architecture skill and avoids the common failure of ignoring healthy areas until they decay. A treats 7 areas as 7 problems, scattering effort. C uses depth-below-target as the only criterion and misses the domain cluster entirely. D defers action despite already having a clear, recent evidence base - a passive option that executives asked you specifically to avoid. "Simplify complex business architecture frameworks into clear actions" is a named behaviour in your role; this is what it looks like under assessment conditions.

**Question 11 (challenging) - Error checking a strategic options paper**

A draft options paper prepared by your team for the executive committee contains the following four statements. Which one contains an internal logical flaw, independent of whether its facts are accurate?

- A) "Option 1 consolidates 12 regional processing centres into 3; because each retained centre absorbs the workload of 4, accommodation costs per case are expected to fall."
- B) "Option 2 retains all 12 centres but standardises processes; it therefore delivers the lowest workforce disruption of the three options."
- C) "Option 3 outsources processing entirely; it delivers the highest savings, and since it delivers the highest savings, it is the option that best meets the strategy."
- D) "All options require investment in the first two years before net benefits are realised from year three."

**Correct answer: C.**

**Explanation:** Statement C assumes that "highest savings" and "best meets the strategy" are the same thing - but a strategy is a set of outcomes that typically includes service quality, resilience, user experience, and workforce commitments alongside cost. Deriving strategic fitness from a single financial dimension is a non sequitur, regardless of whether the savings figure is accurate. A states a mechanism (consolidation) and a proportionate expectation (costs per case fall) - arguable, but logically coherent. B draws a bounded comparative conclusion that follows from retaining all sites. D is a plain factual claim with no internal inference to break. Assurance of your team's outputs - a stated accountability at your level - is often precisely this: reading past the arithmetic to test whether each conclusion actually follows from its premises.

**Question 12 (challenging) - Whole-enterprise reasoning**

You are forming a whole-enterprise view for the executive team. Three facts are established:

- Every service the organisation runs depends on the Identity capability or the Payments capability (or both).
- The Identity capability depends on a contract that expires in 14 months and cannot be renewed under current procurement rules.
- Re-procuring an equivalent identity contract takes at least 18 months under those same rules.

Which statement expresses the enterprise-level risk most precisely?

- A) All services will fail in 14 months.
- B) Every service that depends on Identity faces a capability gap of at least 4 months unless procurement rules are varied, an alternative sourcing route is found, or dependency on Identity is removed.
- C) The Payments capability is unaffected, so the organisation is resilient.
- D) Procurement rules must be changed.

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** Work the logic: the contract lapses at month 14; a compliant replacement arrives no earlier than month 18; therefore services dependent on Identity face an exposure window of at least 4 months - unless one of the stated assumptions changes. B captures the affected population precisely (only Identity-dependent services - those relying solely on Payments are outside the exposure), quantifies the gap, and enumerates the escape routes without asserting which to take. A overstates: services depending only on Payments are unaffected, and even Identity-dependent services may have degraded rather than failed modes. C is true but answers the wrong question - it describes what is safe rather than what is at risk. D leaps from analysis to a single mandated solution, foreclosing options that the executive should weigh. Presenting the whole enterprise "in a way that is easily understood by stakeholders" - a duty at your level - means exactly this: precise scope, quantified exposure, open options.

### Preparation tips

- **Practise with your own artefacts.** Take a real capability model, roadmap, or options paper you have worked on and interrogate it: what must be true, what cannot be true, what cannot be determined? This builds the deduction habit in your native material.
- **Rehearse the "cannot say" discipline.** Senior-level cognitive tests include questions whose correct answer is that the information is insufficient. Practise noticing when you are filling gaps with experience rather than evidence - your expertise is an asset in the workplace but a contamination risk in a reasoning test.
- **Time-box ruthlessly.** With 15 to 30 minutes for the whole test, no single question deserves more than about 90 seconds. Flag, skip, and return. You already prioritise portfolios this way; apply the same logic to the test.
- **Do the ungraded practice questions properly.** They exist to familiarise you with the interface so that no cognitive capacity is wasted on navigation during scored items.
- **Warm up beforehand.** Ten minutes of logic puzzles or a quick review of a dependency map immediately before the test measurably sharpens processing speed. Take the test rested, in a quiet space, with any permitted materials ready.
- **Trust your professional maturity.** You direct this kind of analysis daily. The test format is unfamiliar; the thinking is not.

### Common pitfalls

- **Reversing conditional logic.** Confusing "only if" with "if" (as in Question 1) is the most common deduction error and maps directly onto real misjudgements about when governance rules apply.
- **Answering from experience instead of the stimulus.** The test scores reasoning from the material given. Your knowledge of how government "really works" can lead you to a real-world-correct but test-incorrect answer.
- **Accumulating parallel delays.** Adding up delays that sit on parallel paths (Question 9) rather than reasoning about the binding constraint.
- **Time-sink questions.** Spending four minutes proving you can crack the hardest item while three easy ones go unanswered. The score rewards total correct answers, not heroics.
- **Confusing existence with consistency, or savings with strategy.** Category errors (Questions 7 and 11) are what job-specific cognitive tests at lead level are designed to catch - and what your assurance role exists to prevent.
- **Ignoring plausibility checks.** Verifying arithmetic while missing that a number is contextually absurd (Question 2). Always ask "could this be true?" as well as "does this compute?".


## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment evaluates your ability to analyse, interpret, and make sound decisions using numerical data of the kind your role genuinely produces and consumes. It is emphatically not a mathematics exam: the arithmetic rarely goes beyond percentages, ratios, weighted averages, and cost-benefit comparisons. What is tested is interpretation - extracting the right figures from tables, charts, budget lines, and capacity plans, combining them correctly, and knowing what the result does and does not tell you. Most assessments allow or provide an on-screen calculator, precisely because the intent is to measure business numeracy rather than mental arithmetic. Expect strict time limits - often around a minute to ninety seconds per question - which simulate the reality of absorbing a finance pack the morning of a board meeting.

For a lead business architect, numeric fluency underpins credibility at executive level. Target operating model options come with cost profiles and benefits cases; capability investments compete for finite funding; maturity programmes are tracked through scores and trends; your own team's capacity must be planned against portfolio demand. When you present to an executive committee, the Finance Director will probe your numbers, and when you assure your team's outputs, you are the last check before flawed figures reach senior decision-makers. This assessment simulates both situations.

Question inputs typically include: multi-column tables comparing options or business units, budget and benefits profiles across years, percentage-change and ratio calculations, resource and capacity figures, and performance metrics. The skill is always the same three-step loop: locate the right data, apply the right operation, and sanity-check the result against context.

### What it measures for your role

- **Strategic design and business change**: understanding the short- to long-term impact of business decisions frequently means reading multi-year cost and benefit profiles, break-even points, and cumulative positions - all numeric.
- **Making and guiding decisions**: decisions characterised by medium risk and complexity are usually decisions supported by imperfect numbers; the assessment tests whether you extract the decision-relevant figure and understand its limits.
- **Business architecture**: capability maturity scores, gap analyses, and investment allocations are quantitative artefacts; identifying strategic misalignment often starts with a number that does not fit the strategy.
- **Consultancy**: advice and recommendations to stakeholders must survive numeric scrutiny; the assessment rehearses building recommendations from data.
- **Stakeholder relationship management and communicating information**: influencing and negotiating with senior stakeholders means commanding the numbers in the room - translating a table into a message without distorting it.
- **Methods and tools**: assessing the effectiveness of different approaches often requires comparing delivery metrics (throughput, cost per release, benefit realisation rates) across teams using different methods.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) - Percentage change in channel volumes**

Your organisation's channel-shift strategy is tracked through quarterly transaction volumes:

| Channel | Q1 volume | Q2 volume |
|---|---|---|
| Digital | 480,000 | 540,000 |
| Telephone | 210,000 | 189,000 |
| Paper | 110,000 | 99,000 |

What was the percentage increase in digital transactions from Q1 to Q2?

- A) 11.1%
- B) 12.5%
- C) 60%
- D) 6%

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** Increase = 540,000 − 480,000 = 60,000. Percentage increase = 60,000 ÷ 480,000 = 0.125 = 12.5%. The classic error is dividing by the new figure instead of the original: 60,000 ÷ 540,000 ≈ 11.1%, which is option A, deliberately placed as the trap. Option C is the raw change in thousands misread as a percentage. Always divide the change by the *starting* value for percentage change - a habit worth making automatic, because channel-shift reporting is exactly the kind of figure you will quote to an executive committee.

**Question 2 (easy) - Team capacity**

You lead a business architecture team of 6 architects. Each architect has 220 working days per year, of which 15% is reserved for professional development, community of practice activity, and corporate duties. The portfolio pipeline requires 1,080 architect-days of project work next year. What is the team's shortfall or surplus in architect-days?

- A) Surplus of 42 days
- B) Shortfall of 42 days
- C) Surplus of 240 days
- D) Shortfall of 198 days

**Correct answer: A.**

**Explanation:** Available days per architect = 220 × (1 − 0.15) = 220 × 0.85 = 187 days. Team capacity = 6 × 187 = 1,122 days. Demand = 1,080 days. Surplus = 1,122 − 1,080 = 42 days. Option C (240 days) is what you get if you forget the 15% reservation entirely (6 × 220 = 1,320; 1,320 − 1,080 = 240) - a trap that mirrors a real planning error: committing your team's theoretical capacity while forgetting the development time you are accountable for protecting. As the leader responsible for building capability through coaching, mentoring, and training, that 15% is not slack - it is the maturity investment named in your role profile.

**Question 3 (moderate) - Comparing operating model options**

An options paper presents three target operating model options:

| Option | One-off transition cost | Annual running cost | Annual benefit |
|---|---|---|---|
| A: Consolidate | £4.2m | £8.1m | £10.5m |
| B: Standardise in place | £1.8m | £9.3m | £10.2m |
| C: Outsource | £6.0m | £7.2m | £10.8m |

Measured over four years (transition cost incurred once, running costs and benefits accruing in each of the four years), which option delivers the highest net position, and what is it?

- A) Option A: £5.4m
- B) Option B: £1.8m
- C) Option C: £8.4m
- D) Option C: £14.4m

**Correct answer: C.**

**Explanation:** For each option: net = 4 × (annual benefit − annual running cost) − transition cost. Option A: 4 × (10.5 − 8.1) − 4.2 = 4 × 2.4 − 4.2 = 9.6 − 4.2 = £5.4m. Option B: 4 × (10.2 − 9.3) − 1.8 = 3.6 − 1.8 = £1.8m. Option C: 4 × (10.8 − 7.2) − 6.0 = 14.4 − 6.0 = £8.4m. The highest is Option C at £8.4m. Option D is the trap for candidates who compute Option C's gross four-year margin (£14.4m) and forget to subtract the transition cost. Note also the professional caution the numbers invite: on a two-year horizon the gap narrows sharply (A = £0.6m, B = £0.0m, C = £1.2m), and on a one-year horizon Option B would actually lead, because its low transition cost means it loses least (A = −£1.8m, B = −£0.9m, C = −£2.4m). Horizon choice changes rankings; a lead architect always asks "over what period?" before endorsing a comparison.

**Question 4 (moderate) - Maturity score aggregation**

Your annual business architecture maturity assessment scores five practice areas on a 1-5 scale, weighted by strategic importance:

| Practice area | Weight | Score |
|---|---|---|
| Strategy traceability | 30% | 2 |
| Capability modelling | 25% | 4 |
| Roadmapping | 20% | 3 |
| Standards adoption | 15% | 4 |
| Community of practice | 10% | 5 |

What is the weighted average maturity score?

- A) 3.6
- B) 3.3
- C) 3.0
- D) 3.5

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** Weighted average = (0.30 × 2) + (0.25 × 4) + (0.20 × 3) + (0.15 × 4) + (0.10 × 5) = 0.6 + 1.0 + 0.6 + 0.6 + 0.5 = 3.3. Option A (3.6) is the unweighted mean of the five scores ((2+4+3+4+5) ÷ 5) - the standard trap when weights are provided. The business insight matters as much as the arithmetic: the organisation's *most strategically important* practice area (strategy traceability, weighted 30%) has the *lowest* score, dragging the weighted result below the simple mean. When you develop business architecture maturity in your organisation - a named behaviour in your skill profile - the weighted view tells you where improvement buys the most: a one-point uplift in strategy traceability raises the overall score by 0.30, three times the effect of the same uplift in community of practice.

**Question 5 (moderate) - Benefits realisation tracking**

A transformation programme you assure committed to £12.0m of annual benefits once fully implemented. The benefits profile assumed 25% realisation in year 1, 60% in year 2, and 100% from year 3. At the end of year 2, cumulative realised benefits are £7.8m. Cumulative benefits to the end of year 2 were planned to be:

- A) £10.2m, so the programme is £2.4m behind plan
- B) £7.2m, so the programme is £0.6m ahead of plan
- C) £12.0m, so the programme is £4.2m behind plan
- D) £8.5m, so the programme is £0.7m behind plan

**Correct answer: A.**

**Explanation:** Planned year 1 = 25% × £12.0m = £3.0m. Planned year 2 = 60% × £12.0m = £7.2m. Cumulative planned = £3.0m + £7.2m = £10.2m. Realised = £7.8m, so the shortfall is £10.2m − £7.8m = £2.4m behind plan. Option B is the trap for reading the year-2 planned figure (£7.2m) as if it were cumulative - the percentages describe realisation *in* each year, not *by* each year. Misreading in-year versus cumulative figures is one of the most common numeric errors in benefits reporting, and one of the most consequential: a programme can look "ahead" on an in-year view while being materially behind cumulatively. When you report to executive stakeholders on whether business change is meeting strategy, you must be certain which basis your figures use.

**Question 6 (moderate) - Ratio reasoning on architect allocation**

Governance guidance in your organisation recommends business architect effort be allocated across strategic, programme, and operational work in the ratio 3:5:2. Your team of 6 architects delivers 1,122 available architect-days next year. A new ministerial priority requires 90 additional strategic architect-days beyond the ratio-based allocation. How many architect-days remain for programme work if the strategic addition is funded by reducing programme work only?

- A) 561
- B) 471
- C) 426
- D) 336

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** The ratio 3:5:2 has 10 parts; 1,122 ÷ 10 = 112.2 days per part. Programme allocation = 5 × 112.2 = 561 days (option A - the trap for stopping too early). The 90 extra strategic days come entirely from programme work: 561 − 90 = 471 days. Option C subtracts 90 from both programme and operational shares' combined effect incorrectly; option D double-counts. Beyond the arithmetic, notice what the numbers are telling you as a leader: the ministerial priority consumes 16% of your programme capacity (90 ÷ 561), which is the kind of quantified impact statement that turns a vague "we're stretched" into an executive-ready trade-off: "we can do this, and it means roughly one in six programme-support days is withdrawn - here are the programmes affected."

**Question 7 (moderate) - Interpreting a performance dashboard**

Two directorates deliver a similar casework service. A dashboard shows last month's figures:

| Metric | Directorate North | Directorate South |
|---|---|---|
| Cases completed | 8,400 | 12,600 |
| Staff (full-time equivalent) | 70 | 120 |
| Unit cost per case | £38 | £34 |
| Cases reworked due to error | 336 | 630 |

Which directorate has the higher productivity (cases per FTE), and which has the higher error rate?

- A) North has higher productivity; South has higher error rate.
- B) South has higher productivity; North has higher error rate.
- C) North has higher productivity; both have the same error rate.
- D) South has higher productivity; both have the same error rate.

**Correct answer: A.**

**Explanation:** Productivity: North = 8,400 ÷ 70 = 120 cases per FTE; South = 12,600 ÷ 120 = 105 cases per FTE. North is more productive despite completing fewer total cases - a reminder that absolute volumes and rates answer different questions. Error rate: North = 336 ÷ 8,400 = 4.0%; South = 630 ÷ 12,600 = 5.0%. South's error rate is higher. So North wins on both productivity and quality, yet South shows the lower unit cost (£34 versus £38) - possibly through lower-grade staffing or different accommodation costs. This deliberate tension is the point: no single metric crowns a "better" directorate, and when you aggregate cross-organisational information for executives, presenting one metric as the whole story is how strategic misjudgements begin. A defensible whole-enterprise view names the trade-off explicitly.

**Question 8 (challenging) - Budget variance with in-year adjustment**

Your business architecture function's annual budget is £1.44m, profiled evenly across 12 months. At the end of month 8, actual spend is £1.02m. A mid-year decision (effective from month 9) adds £180,000 of new funding for capability development, spread evenly across the remaining 4 months. What is the function's variance against profiled budget at month 8, and what is the revised monthly budget for months 9-12?

- A) £60,000 overspent; £165,000 per month
- B) £60,000 overspent; £150,000 per month
- C) £60,000 underspent; £165,000 per month
- D) £42,000 overspent; £120,000 per month

**Correct answer: A.**

**Explanation:** Monthly profile = £1,440,000 ÷ 12 = £120,000. Profiled budget at month 8 = 8 × £120,000 = £960,000. Actual = £1,020,000, so variance = £1,020,000 − £960,000 = £60,000 *overspent* (actual exceeds profile). Revised months 9-12: remaining original budget = £1,440,000 − £960,000 = £480,000 profiled, plus £180,000 new funding = £660,000 across 4 months = £165,000 per month. Option C flips the variance direction - always anchor on "actual minus profile: positive means overspent" and say the direction out loud before answering. Option B correctly finds the variance but forgets to add the new funding to the remaining profile. Note a real-world subtlety the question sets aside: the £60,000 overspend still has to be recovered or approved; the new funding is ring-fenced for capability development, not for absorbing overspend - a distinction a Finance Director will certainly make even if the arithmetic doesn't.

**Question 9 (challenging) - Cost-benefit of a community of practice**

You propose expanding the cross-government business architecture community of practice you chair. The business case rests on reduced duplication: last year, four departments independently produced capability models for the same shared policy area, at an average cost of 65 architect-days each, with architect-days costed at £520. The community proposal would produce one shared model (110 architect-days) plus per-department adaptation (15 architect-days each for the four departments). Community coordination overhead is £24,000 per year. What is the net annual saving?

- A) £41,600
- B) £46,800
- C) £22,800
- D) £17,680

**Correct answer: C.**

**Explanation:** Work through it in three stages. Current cost of duplication: 4 departments × 65 days = 260 architect-days; 260 × £520 = £135,200. Proposed cost: shared model 110 days + adaptations (4 × 15 = 60 days) = 170 days; 170 × £520 = £88,400; add coordination overhead of £24,000, giving £112,400. Net saving = £135,200 − £112,400 = £22,800. Option B (£46,800) is the gross saving before coordination overhead - the trap for candidates who stop before applying the final cost line. Option A prices only the shared model against the current cost, forgetting the adaptations entirely. The deeper lesson for your role: benefit cases for maturity investments like communities of practice are won or lost on exactly these components - avoided duplication, adaptation costs, and coordination overheads - and you should be able to rebuild the calculation live in front of a sceptical finance stakeholder. Developing business architecture maturity through a community of practice is a named behaviour in your skill profile; being able to quantify its value is what secures its funding.

**Question 10 (challenging) - Weighted scoring of strategic options**

An investment board uses weighted criteria to score two capability investment options on a 1-10 scale:

| Criterion | Weight | Option X score | Option Y score |
|---|---|---|---|
| Strategic alignment | 40% | 9 | 6 |
| Deliverability | 30% | 5 | 8 |
| Cost | 20% | 4 | 7 |
| User impact | 10% | 8 | 4 |

The board asks: "If 10 percentage points of weight moved from strategic alignment to deliverability, would Option Y overtake Option X?" What are the current weighted scores, and does Y overtake X after the shift?

- A) X = 6.7, Y = 6.6; they are effectively tied and remain so
- B) X = 6.7, Y = 6.6; yes, Y overtakes after the shift
- C) X = 6.9, Y = 6.5; no, X stays ahead after the shift
- D) X = 6.3, Y = 6.8; Y is already ahead

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** Current scores. X: (0.4 × 9) + (0.3 × 5) + (0.2 × 4) + (0.1 × 8) = 3.6 + 1.5 + 0.8 + 0.8 = 6.7. Y: (0.4 × 6) + (0.3 × 8) + (0.2 × 7) + (0.1 × 4) = 2.4 + 2.4 + 1.4 + 0.4 = 6.6. X currently leads by a whisker. Now apply the shift (strategic alignment 30%, deliverability 40%). X: (0.3 × 9) + (0.4 × 5) + 0.8 + 0.8 = 2.7 + 2.0 + 1.6 = 6.3. Y: (0.3 × 6) + (0.4 × 8) + 1.4 + 0.4 = 1.8 + 3.2 + 1.8 = 6.8. After the shift Y leads 6.8 to 6.3 - so yes, Y overtakes. The governance lesson is sharper than the arithmetic: when two options sit within 0.1 of each other, the weighting scheme *is* the decision, and a lead business architect should surface that to the board explicitly. A 10-point weight transfer between two criteria flipped a 0.1 lead into a 0.5 deficit. Sensitivity analysis is not decoration; it is the decision made visible - and presenting it is part of influencing and negotiating with senior stakeholders to enable sound progress.

**Question 11 (challenging) - Break-even on standardisation**

A process standardisation initiative you sponsor costs £350,000 to implement and reduces processing cost from £6.20 to £5.55 per transaction. The organisation processes 40,000 transactions per month. After how many complete months does the initiative break even?

- A) 12 months
- B) 14 months
- C) 13 months
- D) 9 months

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** Saving per transaction = £6.20 − £5.55 = £0.65. Monthly saving = 40,000 × £0.65 = £26,000. Break-even = £350,000 ÷ £26,000 = 13.46 months. The question asks for *complete* months at which the cumulative saving first covers the cost: after 13 months, savings = 13 × £26,000 = £338,000 (not yet covered); after 14 months, £364,000 (covered). The answer is 14. Option C (13) is the trap for rounding 13.46 down or reading it as "in the 13th month"; option A comes from rounding £0.65 to £0.70 early - a reminder not to round intermediate values. Break-even framing is one of the most persuasive numeric devices in your consultancy toolkit: "this pays for itself in 14 months" lands with executives far better than an abstract percentage saving.

**Question 12 (challenging) - Disaggregating a headline figure**

The annual report headline says: "Average case-processing time improved 10%, from 20 days to 18 days." You disaggregate by case type:

| Case type | Share of volume (this year) | Avg days (last year) | Avg days (this year) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Standard | 80% | 16 | 16 |
| Complex | 20% | 36 | 26 |

Last year, standard cases were 75% of volume and complex cases 25%. Which statement most accurately describes what happened?

- A) Both case types got faster, driving the improvement.
- B) Standard cases got faster; complex cases were unchanged.
- C) The improvement came from complex cases getting faster and from the volume mix shifting towards standard cases; standard case performance was unchanged.
- D) The headline is wrong: average processing time worsened.

**Correct answer: C.**

**Explanation:** Verify the averages. Last year: (0.75 × 16) + (0.25 × 36) = 12 + 9 = 21 days... the headline says 20; recompute with the stated shares: the question gives last year's mix as 75/25 and this year's as 80/20. This year: (0.80 × 16) + (0.20 × 26) = 12.8 + 5.2 = 18.0 days - matching the headline's 18. Last year's computed 21 versus the stated 20 signals that headline baselines are often rounded or rebased - exactly the kind of footnote-hunting instinct the question rewards. The substantive decomposition stands: standard cases were flat at 16 days (ruling out A and B), complex cases improved from 36 to 26 days, and the mix shifted 5 points towards the faster case type, which mechanically lowers the average even with no operational improvement at all. This is a mix-shift effect (the workplace cousin of Simpson's paradox), and spotting it is precisely the "aggregate and disaggregate complex information" duty in your role profile: a headline improvement can conceal completely flat performance in the majority case type. D is false - the average genuinely did fall - but the *reasons* matter enormously for what you tell the executive committee to do next.

### Preparation tips

- **Rebuild the arithmetic of real papers.** Take a recent options appraisal or benefits report from your own portfolio and recompute its key figures from the raw inputs. This is the highest-fidelity practice available to you, using exactly the artefact types the test mimics.
- **Drill the core four.** Percentage change, weighted average, ratio allocation, and break-even cover the large majority of questions at this level. Ten minutes of drills a day for a week will make them automatic.
- **Say the units and the basis out loud.** In-year versus cumulative, per-case versus total, thousands versus millions, profile-to-date versus full-year. Most numeric errors at senior level are basis errors, not calculation errors.
- **Use the calculator for arithmetic, your head for structure.** Decide what to compute before touching the calculator; candidates who calculate first and think second run out of time.
- **Don't round intermediate values.** Carry full precision through the calculation and round only the final answer (see Question 11).
- **Practise choosing the nearest option.** Occasionally, in a real test, your computed answer will not exactly match any option, usually because of a rounding difference. Recheck the highest-risk step once, then commit to the nearest value rather than burning minutes.

### Common pitfalls

- **Dividing by the wrong base.** Percentage change taken against the new value rather than the original (Question 1) - the single most common numeric reasoning error.
- **Misreading chart and table labels.** Confusing monthly with annual data, in-year with cumulative (Question 5), or ignoring scale qualifiers such as "in thousands"; the spec literature flags this as the top pitfall, and executive packs are full of such qualifiers.
- **Ignoring stated weights.** Computing simple means when weights are given (Question 4).
- **Over-calculating.** Performing precise multi-stage computation when estimation would eliminate three of four options in seconds; estimation first, precision only if two options survive.
- **Time-sink questions.** One stubborn multi-stage problem can consume the time of four easier ones. Flag, skip, return.
- **Stopping one step early.** Finding an intermediate value that happens to appear among the options (Questions 3, 6, and 8 all contain this trap).
- **Accepting headline figures.** Averages conceal mix effects (Question 12); at your level, the assessment - like the boardroom - rewards those who disaggregate before they conclude.


## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment evaluates how well you understand, analyse, and draw correct conclusions from written business information. The passages mirror the exact reading demands of your role: strategy papers, governance policies, architecture standards, board minutes, ministerial correspondence, and stakeholder emails. Two rules define the format. First, you must answer based strictly on the text provided, setting aside your outside knowledge - however expert it is. Second, most questions offer exactly three verdicts: **True** (the statement follows from the passage), **False** (the statement contradicts the passage), or **Cannot Say** (the passage provides insufficient information either way). A smaller number of multiple-choice comprehension questions test your grasp of a passage's main argument, tone, or implication. Timing is tight - typically less than a minute per question - to simulate reading a board pack under deadline.

For a lead business architect, this is arguably the most job-relevant assessment of the four. Your working life is textual: you read strategies and translate them into architectural direction; you read programme documentation and judge its alignment; you draft principles, standards, policies, roadmaps, and vision statements whose precise wording will be quoted back at you in governance forums; you read what executives write and infer what they mean - while being careful not to infer more than they wrote. The distinction between what a document *says*, what it *implies*, and what it *does not address* is the daily discipline of architectural assurance, and it is exactly what True/False/Cannot Say measures.

### What it measures for your role

- **Communicating information**: extracting the precise meaning of high-risk, high-complexity written material is the receiving half of the communication skill; you cannot mediate between stakeholders or represent a community accurately if you misread what was written.
- **Strategic design and business change**: principles, patterns, standards, policies, roadmaps, and vision statements - artefacts you are expected to define - are all exercises in precise language; the assessment tests whether you notice when wording creates or removes obligations.
- **Business architecture**: leading others in best practice includes reading industry standards accurately and knowing exactly what they mandate versus merely recommend.
- **Consultancy**: advice grounded in "significant specialist knowledge" must nonetheless rest on what the evidence documents actually say - the Cannot Say discipline is the antidote to advice that outruns its evidence.
- **Making and guiding decisions**: distinguishing what is established from what is assumed is the foundation of defensible decisions and recommendations.
- **Stakeholder relationship management**: reading stakeholder correspondence precisely - including what was deliberately not said - is core to managing expectations and negotiating at senior levels.

### Practice questions

**Passage A - Extract from a draft enterprise architecture policy**

"All programmes with a whole-life cost exceeding £5 million must submit a business architecture impact assessment to the Design Authority before their outline business case is approved. Programmes below this threshold are encouraged, but not required, to submit an impact assessment. The Design Authority will review submissions within 15 working days. Where a submission identifies a conflict with an enterprise architecture principle, the programme must either amend its design or obtain a formal exemption from the Chief Architect. Exemptions are time-limited and must be reviewed at each subsequent business case stage. The Design Authority may, at its discretion, escalate unresolved conflicts to the Executive Design Board."

**Question 1 (easy) - True / False / Cannot Say**

Statement: "A programme with a whole-life cost of £4 million is required to submit a business architecture impact assessment."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B (False).**

**Explanation:** The passage states that programmes below the £5 million threshold "are encouraged, but not required" to submit. A £4 million programme is below the threshold, so the claim that it is *required* directly contradicts the text. This is False, not Cannot Say: the passage speaks explicitly to the sub-threshold case. Candidates sometimes reach for Cannot Say whenever a statement concerns an edge case; the discipline is to check whether the passage actually addresses it - here it does, in terms.

**Question 2 (easy) - True / False / Cannot Say**

Statement: "The Chief Architect can grant permanent exemptions from enterprise architecture principles."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B (False).**

**Explanation:** The passage says "Exemptions are time-limited and must be reviewed at each subsequent business case stage." A permanent exemption contradicts "time-limited". Precision point: the passage does confirm the Chief Architect grants exemptions - that part is true - but the statement as a whole asserts permanence, which the text rules out. In verbal reasoning, a statement that is half right and half contradicted is False. In your assurance work, the parallel is exact: a programme claiming "we have an exemption" may be correct, while its implicit claim "so this is settled forever" is not.

**Question 3 (moderate) - True / False / Cannot Say**

Statement: "The Executive Design Board resolves most conflicts that the Design Authority escalates to it."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C (Cannot Say).**

**Explanation:** The passage establishes only that the Design Authority *may* escalate unresolved conflicts to the Executive Design Board. It says nothing about what the Board does with escalations, how many it receives, or its resolution rate. "Most" is a quantitative claim with no textual support in either direction - it is neither confirmed nor contradicted, so Cannot Say. Notice how natural it feels to assume a governance board resolves what reaches it; that is your professional experience talking, and the test is measuring whether you can hold it apart from the text.

**Question 4 (moderate) - Multiple choice comprehension**

Which of the following best describes the policy's mechanism for handling principle conflicts?

- A) All conflicts are escalated to the Executive Design Board.
- B) Programmes must resolve conflicts by amending their design; no other route exists.
- C) Programmes facing a conflict have two routes - amend the design or secure a time-limited exemption - with discretionary escalation available for unresolved cases.
- D) The Design Authority grants exemptions within 15 working days.

**Correct answer: C.**

**Explanation:** C reproduces the passage's structure faithfully: two programme-side routes (amend or obtain exemption from the Chief Architect), plus a discretionary escalation route held by the Design Authority. A converts "may, at its discretion, escalate unresolved conflicts" into a universal rule. B erases the exemption route. D misattributes the exemption power to the Design Authority (it belongs to the Chief Architect) and misapplies the 15-day commitment, which governs review of submissions, not exemption decisions. When you lead the definition of guidance for your organisation - a behaviour in your consultancy skill - this is precisely the reading you must perform on others' drafts: who holds which power, under what conditions, with what discretion.

**Passage B - Email from a programme director to the lead business architect**

"Thanks for your team's assessment of our target operating model. We accept the finding that our proposed regional structure diverges from the enterprise blueprint, and we acknowledge the risks you set out. However, our user research indicates that a single national model would materially worsen outcomes for vulnerable service users in rural areas. We are therefore minded to proceed with the regional structure while adopting your recommendations on shared back-office functions in full. We would welcome a conversation about whether a formal exemption or a blueprint change is the better vehicle. Separately, please note that our delivery director role is currently vacant, which may affect the pace at which we can implement any agreed changes."

**Question 5 (moderate) - True / False / Cannot Say**

Statement: "The programme rejects the business architecture team's assessment."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B (False).**

**Explanation:** The email explicitly *accepts* the finding of divergence, *acknowledges* the risks, and adopts the back-office recommendations "in full". What the programme resists is one implication - conforming to the national model - and even there it proposes dialogue about the right governance vehicle. "Rejects the assessment" contradicts the text at multiple points. This distinction is professionally vital: a stakeholder can accept your analysis while contesting your preferred remedy, and reading that accurately - rather than hearing "rejection" - is the difference between a negotiation and a standoff. Mediating between people and mending relationships starts with reading their words as written.

**Question 6 (moderate) - True / False / Cannot Say**

Statement: "The programme's user research shows that the enterprise blueprint is wrong."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C (Cannot Say).**

**Explanation:** The email claims the research indicates a single national model "would materially worsen outcomes for vulnerable service users in rural areas". Whether that makes the blueprint *wrong* is a judgement the text does not make: the blueprint may serve other objectives well, the research may be contested, and "diverges from the blueprint in one respect" is not "the blueprint is wrong". The statement overreaches the text, so Cannot Say. Note the temptation here runs in both directions - architects loyal to the blueprint want to answer False, architects sympathetic to user needs want to answer True. The text supports neither. In a real negotiation you would treat the research as significant evidence deserving examination, which is exactly what "Cannot Say - yet" looks like in practice.

**Question 7 (challenging) - Multiple choice: reading what is signalled**

The final sentence about the vacant delivery director role is best read as:

- A) A formal request for the architecture team to fill the vacancy.
- B) An irrelevant administrative detail.
- C) An early signal that implementation of agreed changes may be slower than planned, offered as context for expectation-setting.
- D) An admission that the programme cannot implement the back-office recommendations.

**Correct answer: C.**

**Explanation:** The sentence says the vacancy "may affect the pace" of implementing "any agreed changes" - a hedged, forward-looking statement about speed, not capability. A invents a request that is nowhere made. B ignores the sender's evident purpose: programme directors do not add "separately, please note" paragraphs without intent - this one is managing expectations in advance. D overreads "may affect the pace" into "cannot implement". Option C captures both the literal content and its evident function. Managing stakeholder expectations is a named behaviour in your communicating information skill, and it works in both directions: recognising when a stakeholder is pre-positioning an expectation with you is as important as setting your own.

**Passage C - Extract from a government digital strategy document**

"Departments should adopt a 'strategy before structure' approach: organisational redesign must follow, not precede, agreement of measurable policy outcomes. Evidence from previous transformation programmes suggests that restructures launched without agreed outcomes are more likely to be reversed within three years. Departments are expected to publish outcome frameworks before initiating structural change, except where machinery of government changes are directed by the centre. Business architecture functions, where they exist, should own the mapping between outcomes and organisational capabilities. Nothing in this guidance prevents departments from making urgent operational changes required to maintain service continuity."

**Question 8 (moderate) - True / False / Cannot Say**

Statement: "According to the document, restructures launched without agreed outcomes are always reversed within three years."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B (False).**

**Explanation:** The passage says such restructures are "more likely to be reversed within three years" - a probabilistic claim. The statement converts it to "always" - an absolute claim. An absolute assertion is contradicted by a text that deliberately states only a tendency, so this is False. This modality trap - swapping "suggests", "more likely", "should" for "proves", "always", "must" - is the single most reliable trick in verbal reasoning tests, and it matters in your work: quoting evidence more strongly than it was written is how strategies lose credibility with the executives who fact-check them.

**Question 9 (challenging) - True / False / Cannot Say**

Statement: "A department directed by the centre to merge with another department is not expected to publish an outcome framework before the structural change begins."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A (True).**

**Explanation:** The passage sets the expectation to publish outcome frameworks before structural change, "except where machinery of government changes are directed by the centre". A centrally directed departmental merger is a machinery of government change directed by the centre, so it falls squarely within the stated exception; the expectation does not apply. This is True by direct application of the exception clause. Candidates often mark exception-based questions Cannot Say because the specific scenario (a merger) is not named in the text - but applying a general clause to a clearly covered instance is deduction, not assumption. Knowing exactly when an exception clause bites is bread-and-butter work when you define principles, standards, and policies for others to follow.

**Question 10 (challenging) - True / False / Cannot Say**

Statement: "The document requires every department to establish a business architecture function."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B (False).**

**Explanation:** The passage assigns a responsibility to "business architecture functions, where they exist". The qualifier "where they exist" explicitly contemplates that some departments do not have one, and the document imposes no requirement to create one. A statement that the document *requires* every department to establish the function is contradicted by the conditional wording. Careful: this is not Cannot Say - the phrase "where they exist" is affirmative textual evidence that existence is optional under this document. Reading qualifiers like "where they exist", "where appropriate", and "as far as practicable" is exactly the skill of reading industry standards accurately, which you exercise when you lead or guide others in best practice.

**Question 11 (challenging) - Multiple choice: identifying the core argument**

Which statement best captures the passage's central argument?

- A) Organisational restructures are usually a mistake and should be avoided.
- B) Structural change should be anchored to agreed, measurable outcomes, with defined exceptions for central direction and operational urgency.
- C) Business architecture functions should control all organisational redesign.
- D) Departments must seek central approval before any restructure.

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** B synthesises the whole passage: the core principle (strategy before structure, outcomes before redesign), plus both stated exceptions (machinery of government changes directed by the centre; urgent operational changes for service continuity). A overstates - the passage disciplines restructures but never argues against them. C inflates "own the mapping between outcomes and organisational capabilities" into "control all organisational redesign" - ownership of a mapping is not control of redesign. D invents an approval requirement that appears nowhere. Summarising a policy's central argument without inflating or deflating any clause is precisely what you do when you communicate complex information about the organisation to stakeholders - and when you brief an executive committee in ninety seconds on a forty-page strategy.

**Passage D - Board minute extract**

"The Board noted the lead business architect's paper on capability investment. The Board agreed the proposed prioritisation approach in principle, subject to the Finance Director's assurance review of the underlying cost model. The Board did not agree funding at this stage. The Chief Executive asked that the next iteration of the paper include an assessment of workforce implications, noting that the trade union consultation calendar may constrain the timing of any announcements. Action: lead business architect to return to the January Board."

**Question 12 (challenging) - True / False / Cannot Say**

Statement: "The Board approved the prioritisation approach."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C (Cannot Say).**

**Explanation:** This is the hardest kind of verbal item because both True and False are defensible at a glance - which is the signal to slow down. The Board "agreed the proposed prioritisation approach in principle, subject to" an assurance review. Is that approval? Agreement in principle with an unresolved condition is neither full approval (the condition is outstanding, so True overstates) nor rejection (agreement in principle was given, so False overstates). The statement "approved" is ambiguous relative to the minute's carefully conditional language, and the honest verdict on the unqualified claim is Cannot Say: whether conditional agreement counts as "approval" depends on information (the governance rulebook's definitions) not in the text. If the statement had read "The Board gave unconditional approval", the answer would be False; had it read "The Board agreed the approach in principle", True. Boards draft minutes this precisely on purpose, and lead architects who report "the Board approved it" when the minute says "agreed in principle, subject to" create exactly the expectation-management failures your role exists to prevent.

**Question 13 (challenging) - True / False / Cannot Say**

Statement: "The trade union consultation calendar will delay the January Board decision."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C (Cannot Say).**

**Explanation:** The minute records the Chief Executive noting that the consultation calendar "may constrain the timing of any announcements". Announcements are not Board decisions - the constraint attaches to communicating decisions, not making them - and "may constrain" is a possibility, not a prediction. The text neither confirms nor denies any effect on the January Board itself. Two separate overreadings must both be resisted: sliding from "announcements" to "decisions", and hardening "may" into "will". Each alone would make the statement unsupported; noticing both is the level of precision expected at lead level, where you routinely parse exactly this kind of minute to determine what your team is actually authorised to do next.

**Passage E - Extract from an industry business architecture standard**

"A capability map shall describe what the organisation does, independent of how, where, or by whom it is done. Capabilities shall be named using noun phrases and shall be mutually exclusive at each level of decomposition. A capability map should be decomposed to no more than three levels for executive communication purposes, although deeper decomposition may be maintained for analytical use. Conformance with this standard is assessed at the whole-map level: a map that violates any 'shall' requirement is non-conformant, while deviations from 'should' guidance require documented justification but do not affect conformance."

**Question 14 (moderate) - True / False / Cannot Say**

Statement: "A capability map decomposed to five levels can still conform to the standard."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A (True).**

**Explanation:** The three-level limit is a "should" ("should be decomposed to no more than three levels for executive communication purposes"), and the passage states explicitly that deviations from "should" guidance "require documented justification but do not affect conformance". Moreover, the standard itself allows that "deeper decomposition may be maintained for analytical use". A five-level map therefore can conform - provided the deviation is justified in documentation and no "shall" requirement is broken. This is True by combining two clauses, which is what makes it moderate rather than easy. The shall/should/may hierarchy is the grammar of every industry standard you will ever guide your team through; tests and real assurance both turn on it.

**Question 15 (challenging) - True / False / Cannot Say**

Statement: "A capability map in which one capability is named 'Managing Grants Efficiently' violates the standard's naming requirement."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C (Cannot Say).**

**Explanation:** The naming requirement is that capabilities "shall be named using noun phrases". Is "Managing Grants Efficiently" a noun phrase? A gerund phrase such as "Managing Grants" functions grammatically as a noun phrase in many analyses, and the standard as quoted does not define what it accepts as a noun phrase, nor does it prohibit adverbs such as "efficiently". Whether this name violates the requirement depends on definitional detail the passage does not supply - so the honest verdict is Cannot Say. (Many practitioners would advise renaming to "Grant Management" for clarity and to strip the how-flavoured "efficiently", which arguably smuggles in the "how" the first sentence excludes - but "arguably" is precisely the point: the text as given does not settle it.) Recognising when a standard is genuinely silent or ambiguous - rather than forcing a verdict - is what allows you, as the leader defining guidance, to know where your organisation's local conventions must fill the gap.

### Preparation tips

- **Read the statement before re-reading the passage.** Identify exactly what the statement claims - especially its verbs and quantifiers ("requires", "always", "most", "will") - then hunt the passage for confirming or contradicting language. This is faster and more accurate than absorbing the whole passage first.
- **Build a modality radar.** Circle (mentally) every "may", "should", "must", "in principle", "subject to", "where they exist", "except where". Tests hide answers in these words because policy documents hide meaning in them - as you know from drafting standards yourself.
- **Rehearse the Cannot Say verdict until it feels comfortable.** Senior professionals are paid to have views, and suppressing the view to report only what the text supports takes deliberate practice. Cannot Say is not a confession of confusion; it is a finding.
- **Use your own artefacts as practice material.** Take a board minute, a strategy extract, or a programme email from your recent work; write five statements about it; classify each as True, False, or Cannot Say against the text alone. Twenty minutes of this is worth hours of generic practice.
- **Watch the clock but do not let it panic you.** At under a minute per question, you have time for one careful read of the statement and one targeted scan of the passage - not for three full re-readings. If a verdict will not settle, choose your best answer and move on.
- **Answer from the passage even when you know better.** If the passage says something your experience contradicts, the passage wins. The test measures textual reasoning, not domain knowledge - and, usefully, so does architectural assurance: the question is always "what does this document actually commit them to?"

### Common pitfalls

- **Importing outside knowledge.** The most common failure for expert candidates: you know how Design Authorities usually work, so you answer from that knowledge rather than the passage. Job-specific tests are deliberately built to punish this.
- **Absolutising hedged language.** Converting "more likely" to "always" (Question 8) or "may constrain" to "will delay" (Question 13). If the statement is stronger than the text, it is not True.
- **Marking edge cases Cannot Say without checking.** When a statement concerns a threshold or exception, verify whether the passage actually addresses it (Questions 1 and 9) before reaching for Cannot Say.
- **Half-true statements.** A statement containing one accurate element and one contradicted element is False (Question 2); do not average the halves into Cannot Say.
- **Missing whose power it is.** Attributing the Chief Architect's exemption power to the Design Authority (Question 4) - governance texts turn on who may do what, and so do these questions.
- **Reading the passage first, slowly, in full.** Under one minute per question, statement-first targeted reading beats passage-first comprehension. Save the leisurely full read for documents you are assuring at work.


## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment evaluates your decision-making, interpersonal skill, and professional conduct through realistic workplace dilemmas. Unlike the cognitive, numeric, and verbal assessments, there is rarely a single objectively right answer: several responses may seem reasonable, and the scoring reflects how well your choices align with the values and behavioural expectations of the organisation and the role. For UK government digital roles, those values are well documented: meeting user needs, transparency, collaboration across boundaries, accessibility and inclusion, evidence-based decision-making, and the Civil Service values of integrity, honesty, objectivity, and impartiality.

Formats vary. The most common asks you to identify the **most effective** and **least effective** responses from a set of options; others ask you to rank all options from best to worst, or to rate each option's effectiveness independently. Timing is usually generous or untimed - the test measures natural judgement, not speed.

For a lead business architect, the dilemmas are distinctive. You sit at the junction of strategy and delivery, holding accountability for your team's outputs while depending on stakeholders you do not control. Your dilemmas involve executive sponsors with conflicting agendas, programme boards under delivery pressure, architects in your team who need development or protection, cross-government peers, and the permanent tension between architectural integrity and delivery pragmatism. The assessment tests whether you can act with authority without arrogance, escalate without abdicating, compromise without capitulating, and develop others while safeguarding quality.

As you work through the practice scenarios below, notice a recurring pattern in the strong answers: they engage directly with the problem rather than deferring it, they preserve relationships while protecting standards, they are transparent about reasoning, and they choose the outcome over the ego. The weak answers tend to be passive (wait, ignore, hope), purely escalatory (pass the problem up without adding value), or unilateral (impose a solution that damages trust you will need later).

### What it measures for your role

- **Stakeholder relationship management**: influencing and negotiating with senior stakeholders to resolve issues and enable progress; directing the relationship strategy for your teams; the dilemmas repeatedly test whether you protect long-term relationships while resolving short-term conflicts.
- **Communicating information**: mediating between people and mending relationships; managing expectations; hosting difficult discussions about high risk and complexity within constrained timescales.
- **Making and guiding decisions**: knowing which decisions to make yourself (medium risk and complexity), which to recommend upwards as risk grows, and how to build consensus between independent stakeholders.
- **Consultancy**: giving advice that stakeholders may not want to hear, grounded in specialist knowledge, without becoming either a pushover or a blocker.
- **Business architecture and strategic design**: defending strategic alignment and architectural principles under delivery pressure, while staying focused on outcomes rather than solutions.
- **Leadership of the function**: your accountability for team outputs and your duty to develop capability through coaching, mentoring, and training surface in scenarios about delegation, quality assurance, and team development.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (moderate) - The executive shortcut**

A director-general sponsoring a major transformation tells you privately: "The architecture board is slowing us down. I want you to sign off our operating model design this week, outside the board process - I'll square it with the Chief Architect later." You believe the design is broadly sound but has one significant unresolved risk around data-sharing arrangements.

Options:

- A) Sign off the design as asked; the sponsor is senior, the design is broadly sound, and the relationship matters.
- B) Refuse outright, stating that you never operate outside governance, and report the request to the Chief Architect.
- C) Explain that you cannot sign off outside the board process, but offer a concrete path: an expedited board review this week, with your team preparing the papers, and flag the data-sharing risk as the one item needing genuine attention.
- D) Agree to review it informally and give a personal opinion, while making no formal commitment either way.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: A.**

**Explanation:** Option C does three things at once: it protects governance (transparency, objectivity), it serves the sponsor's legitimate underlying need - speed - by offering an expedited legitimate route, and it is honest about the one substantive risk rather than hiding behind process. This is textbook influencing and negotiating with senior stakeholders to enable progress: you say no to the method while saying yes to the goal. Option A is least effective: it bypasses governance, buries a known data-sharing risk (a serious matter in government, engaging legal and public-trust obligations), and sets a precedent that governance is negotiable for the sufficiently senior - a precedent that will be quoted back at you for years. Option B protects integrity but is needlessly adversarial: refusing *and* reporting a private request as though it were misconduct converts a negotiable moment into a damaged relationship, and offers the sponsor nothing for their real problem. Option D is the passive trap - an informal opinion without commitment gives the sponsor deniable cover ("the lead architect saw no problem") while resolving nothing.

**Question 2 (moderate) - The struggling architect**

A business architect in your team, six months in post, has produced a capability assessment for a programme board that you review the evening before submission. It contains significant analytical errors: two capabilities conflated, and a maturity score unsupported by the evidence gathered. The architect has worked hard on it and has a development plan you agreed with them focusing on exactly these skills.

Options:

- A) Correct the errors yourself overnight and submit the fixed version, mentioning nothing to avoid denting their confidence.
- B) Submit the work as it stands; the team must learn from real consequences, and the board will spot the issues.
- C) Contact the architect first thing, walk through the two errors together, agree the corrections, and ask the board secretariat for a 48-hour extension if the rework cannot be completed properly in time; note the episode as material for your next coaching session.
- D) Withdraw the paper from the board agenda and reassign future board-facing work to a more experienced architect.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: B.**

**Explanation:** You hold two accountabilities that this scenario deliberately places in tension: you are accountable for the outputs of the business architecture team, and you are responsible for developing capability through coaching and mentoring. Option C honours both - the board receives correct analysis (accountability), and the architect learns by co-correcting their own work (development), with a modest, honest process cost (a possible 48-hour extension) that is transparent rather than concealed. Option B is least effective because it knowingly sends flawed analysis into a decision-making forum: the board may *not* spot the issues, decisions may rest on them, and "learning from consequences" at the expense of a live governance decision is a misuse of other people's time and risk. Option A fails the development duty and embeds a dependency on your heroics - and the architect will eventually discover the silent rewrite, which corrodes trust. Option D overcorrects: it protects this board cycle but publicly signals lost confidence, damages the architect's growth, and solves a coaching problem with a structural punishment.

**Question 3 (moderate) - The misaligned programme with momentum**

During routine assurance, your team confirms that a £30 million programme's solution design duplicates a capability that another directorate's platform already provides. The programme is eight months into delivery, has strong ministerial visibility, and its SRO says: "Raising this now will destabilise the programme for a theoretical saving. Your team should have caught this a year ago."

Options:

- A) Accept the SRO's argument; the moment has passed, and the relationship with a high-profile programme matters more than a duplicated capability.
- B) Quantify the duplication (cost of build, run, and divergence over five years), identify realistic options including a phased convergence that avoids destabilising current delivery, acknowledge candidly the assurance gap on your own side, and present the analysis to the SRO before taking it to the design authority.
- C) Take the finding straight to the design authority and the executive committee without further discussion; the SRO's resistance confirms the programme cannot be trusted to self-correct.
- D) Log the issue in the architecture risk register and revisit it at the programme's next annual review.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D.**

**Explanation:** Option B embodies several behaviours from your profile simultaneously: identifying strategic misalignment and recommending improvement (business architecture), quantifying short- to long-term impact (strategic design and business change), and giving stakeholders difficult advice with candour and evidence (consultancy). Two elements elevate it: the *phased convergence* option respects the SRO's legitimate concern about destabilisation - outcome-focused, not dogmatic - and the open acknowledgement of your own function's late catch models the transparency you ask of others and disarms the SRO's strongest rhetorical card. Option D is least effective: it is the classic passive option that converts a live, compounding problem (duplication costs accrue daily) into a filed document, and it will surface eventually - probably in a spending review - as "the architecture function knew and sat on it." Option C is procedurally available but tactically poor: escalating without first sharing your analysis with the SRO breaks the no-surprises norm that senior relationships run on, and forfeits the chance that the SRO, faced with the numbers, engages constructively. Option A capitulates - and note that its framing ("relationship matters more") misunderstands relationships: an architecture function that folds under pressure has no relationship, only an audience.

**Question 4 (challenging) - The warring directors**

Two directors whose services both depend on a shared customer-data capability have stopped attending the design forum you chair, after a heated disagreement about data ownership. Each has told you separately that the other is "impossible". The target operating model you are accountable for delivering to the executive committee in six weeks requires their joint agreement on the capability's future.

Options:

- A) Meet each director separately to understand their underlying interests (not their stated positions), identify the actual points of genuine conflict versus misunderstanding, then bring them together for a tightly scoped session focused only on the decisions the operating model needs, with the areas of agreement documented in advance.
- B) Report the impasse to the executive committee and ask them to direct the two directors to cooperate.
- C) Proceed with the operating model using your own best judgement on the contested questions, noting the directors' non-participation in the submission.
- D) Postpone the operating model submission until the directors resolve their differences.

**Most effective: A. Least effective: C.**

**Explanation:** Option A is almost a job description for the communicating information skill at your level: "mediate between people and mend relationships" and "host or moderate discussions about high risk and complexity, even within constrained timescales". The technique matters: separate interest-based conversations first (positions harden in joint sessions; interests surface in private), then a joint session deliberately narrowed to the operating model's genuine decision needs, with pre-documented agreement shrinking the contested space. This is how consensus between independent stakeholders is actually built. Option C is least effective: unilaterally deciding contested ownership questions and flagging the directors' absence reads as both a design imposed without consent and a public blame note - it guarantees both directors arrive at the executive committee opposed to your model, and the model fails. Option B escalates prematurely: asking executives to order cooperation produces attendance, not agreement, and spends your credibility as someone who resolves rather than refers. Option D surrenders the timetable to the conflict, rewarding non-engagement - and the executive committee deadline is your accountability, not the directors'.

**Question 5 (challenging) - The community of practice defection**

You lead the cross-departmental business architecture community of practice. A talented lead architect from another department announces in the community's open channel that their department is abandoning the community's shared capability reference model because "it's too centrally driven and doesn't reflect operational reality", and invites others to join a breakaway group. Two smaller departments express interest within hours.

Options:

- A) Respond publicly in the channel, rebutting the criticism point by point and reminding members that the shared model was collectively agreed.
- B) Contact the departing architect privately within the day: listen to the substance of the criticism, acknowledge what is valid, and explore whether a "local extensions" mechanism in the shared model would meet their operational needs; then bring the proposal openly to the next community session, crediting them for surfacing the gap.
- C) Let the breakaway group proceed; communities are voluntary, and competition between models will reveal the better approach.
- D) Ask the departing architect's director to instruct them to remain in the community.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D.**

**Explanation:** Your role profile is unusually explicit here: you "speak on behalf of and represent the community", you develop "business architecture maturity... such as through a community of practice", and you "mediate between people and mend relationships". Option B does all three: it takes the criticism seriously as signal rather than insubordination (the complaint about central drive may be substantively right), it converts a defection into a design improvement (local extensions - a standard federation pattern), and it re-legitimises the community by crediting the critic publicly. Option D is least effective: using hierarchical pressure to compel participation in a voluntary community destroys the community's legitimacy in one move - membership becomes compliance, and the underlying criticism ("too centrally driven") is proven correct in the most visible way possible. Option A wins the argument and loses the community: public point-by-point rebuttal of a respected peer hardens positions and forces spectators to choose sides. Option C sounds liberal but abandons your representative duty: fragmentation into competing reference models is precisely the duplication and inconsistency the community exists to prevent, and "let competition decide" costs every department the reconciliation overhead in the meantime.

**Question 6 (challenging) - The strategy that no longer fits**

Eighteen months ago you defined the business architecture roadmap supporting your organisation's five-year strategy. New evidence - a budget reduction, user research contradicting a core assumption, and a machinery of government change - now suggests that two of the roadmap's four pillars are no longer viable as designed. The executive committee reviews the strategy next month, and your roadmap is cited in it as a flagship commitment.

Options:

- A) Present the evidence to the executive committee next month, recommend revising the two affected pillars, and bring options for what a revised roadmap protects, defers, and stops, framed around the strategic outcomes rather than the original activities.
- B) Defend the roadmap as published; revising a flagship commitment after eighteen months would undermine confidence in the architecture function.
- C) Quietly re-plan the two pillars within your own team and reflect the changes gradually through routine updates, avoiding a formal revision discussion.
- D) Recommend pausing the whole roadmap pending a full strategy refresh.

**Most effective: A. Least effective: C.**

**Explanation:** Your strategic design and business change skill says it directly: "develop, maintain or update strategy in response to feedback and findings" and "effectively focus on outcomes rather than solutions and activities". Option A is that behaviour under pressure: evidence first, revision framed around protected outcomes, and explicit protect/defer/stop options that give executives a real decision rather than a fait accompli. Presenting it at the scheduled strategy review is well-timed transparency, not confession. Option C is least effective and the most tempting: it avoids an awkward conversation, but it means the executive committee reaffirms a strategy citing a roadmap you privately know is partly unviable - a transparency failure that, when discovered (and budget variances will reveal it), damages the function's integrity far more than an honest revision would have. Option B confuses consistency with credibility: defending a design against the evidence is the opposite of the evidence-based practice the profession stands for, and executives forgive revised plans far more readily than defended fictions. Option D overcorrects - two pillars are affected, not four, and pausing everything sacrifices viable delivery and looks like abdication.

**Question 7 (moderate) - The vendor-shaped methodology**

A major consultancy engaged by your Chief Operating Officer recommends that the organisation adopt the consultancy's proprietary operating model methodology - and its associated tooling - across all transformation work. The COO is enthusiastic and asks you to "make it happen across the architecture community". Your assessment is that the methodology is competent but largely duplicates open frameworks your team already uses well, and the tooling would create a licence dependency costing £400,000 per year.

Options:

- A) Implement the COO's request; they have the authority and the consultancy has strong credentials.
- B) Prepare a short comparative assessment - capability coverage, switching costs, licence exposure, and exit terms - of the proprietary methodology against your current open frameworks; share it with the COO with a recommendation, and propose criteria that any methodology decision should meet regardless of vendor.
- C) Tell the architecture community to continue with current methods while formally appearing to adopt the new methodology.
- D) Raise a procurement objection about consultancy influence over methodology selection.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C.**

**Explanation:** Your methods and tools skill sets the standard: "set direction and give others the means and authority to advise on the best methodologies and tools" and "assess the effectiveness of different approaches... and recommend tools". Option B does exactly that - it neither obstructs the COO nor obeys unexamined, but converts an enthusiasm into a decision with criteria, evidence, and a visible recommendation. Proposing vendor-neutral selection criteria is the strategic move: it protects this decision and every future one. Option C is least effective by a distance: instructing a community to fake adoption is dishonesty as management strategy - it will be discovered, it corrodes the community's trust in you, and it places every architect in the position of dissembling to the COO. No plausible benefit survives that cost. Option A abdicates your consultancy duty - giving advice grounded in specialist knowledge precisely when it is unwelcome is what the organisation pays your judgement for; £400,000 per year of avoidable licence dependency is material. Option D may eventually be warranted, but as an opening move it converts a resolvable methodology question into a procedural conflict with the COO, skipping the evidence-based conversation that would probably succeed.

**Question 8 (challenging) - The accessibility trade-off**

A programme within your assurance remit proposes to launch a redesigned grant-application service. Launch is committed publicly for the end of the month. In final assurance, your team finds the service's document-upload journey fails accessibility standards for screen-reader users - roughly 3% of applicants - with a fix estimated at five weeks. The programme director proposes launching on time with a phone-based workaround for affected users and fixing the journey "in the next release".

Options:

- A) Insist the launch be delayed five weeks; accessibility standards are non-negotiable and no service should launch in breach.
- B) Accept the workaround as proposed; 3% of users have an alternative route, and the public commitment matters.
- C) Test the workaround's adequacy with affected users before agreeing anything: if the phone route genuinely provides an equivalent, dignified service, support a time-boxed launch with the fix contractually scheduled, an accessibility statement published honestly describing the interim arrangement, and escalation to the SRO if the fix slips; if the workaround fails user testing, recommend delay.
- D) Approve the launch but record your team's objection formally in the assurance log.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: D.**

**Explanation:** This dilemma tests whether you apply values as judgement or as slogans. Option C is most effective because it makes the decision turn on evidence about real users - the profession's first value - rather than on either the deadline or the standard in the abstract. Its safeguards matter: user-tested adequacy (inclusion verified, not assumed), a published accessibility statement (transparency), a time-boxed fix with named escalation (accountability), and a genuine willingness to recommend delay if the evidence is adverse. Option A has integrity but treats a five-week delay as costless and forecloses the possibility - which only user testing can confirm or refute - that the interim route serves affected users adequately; rigid rule-application without evidence is not the same as inclusion. Option B waves through a degraded experience for disabled users on an untested assumption, which is precisely how services breach both public sector equality duties and public trust. Option D is least effective: it approves the launch *while* privately documenting disagreement - protecting your function's audit position while doing nothing for the affected users or the decision. Objection-on-file without action is the purest form of passive option, and assessors score it accordingly.

**Question 9 (moderate) - The overloaded team**

Demand on your six-architect team has grown 40% in a year. Three architects tell you privately they are close to burnout; one has begun making uncharacteristic errors. The portfolio office has just asked you to support two additional programmes, both genuinely strategic. Your director says: "I can't get you more headcount this year."

Options:

- A) Accept the two programmes and ask the team to prioritise harder; strategic work cannot be turned away.
- B) Rank all demand transparently against strategic value with the portfolio office, agree with your director which existing engagements will be reduced or stopped to create capacity for the two new programmes, communicate the changes to affected stakeholders yourself, and address the workload of the three at-risk architects directly, including redistributing the error-prone architect's assurance-critical work.
- C) Decline the two new programmes, explaining the team is at capacity.
- D) Accept the programmes and quietly deprioritise the existing lower-profile engagements without telling their stakeholders, to avoid difficult conversations.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D.**

**Explanation:** The scenario is a systems problem disguised as a courage problem, and Option B treats it as such: demand made visible and ranked (transparent prioritisation), trade-offs decided with the people who own them (director and portfolio office), consequences communicated by you rather than discovered by stakeholders (directing the stakeholder relationship strategy for your teams, and taking the difficult conversations yourself), and the human signals acted on - because a burnt-out team is a quality risk to outputs you are accountable for, and an error-prone architect on assurance-critical work is an incident in waiting. Option D is least effective: silent deprioritisation means stakeholders discover reduced service by experiencing it, which converts a defensible capacity decision into a trust breach - and it leaves the burnout unaddressed. Option A treats "prioritise harder" as capacity, which it is not; it accepts predictable quality failure and staff harm to avoid one negotiation. Option C is more honest than A or D but forfeits the strategic work without testing whether existing lower-value engagements could yield capacity - a lead architect's first move is to re-rank the whole portfolio, not to defend its current shape.

**Question 10 (challenging) - Representing the profession under fire**

You are representing the cross-government business architecture community on a conference panel, with civil servants and industry delegates present. A well-known industry commentator on the panel says: "Government business architecture is a paper factory - your capability maps and operating models have never shipped a single service. The taxpayer would be better off if the whole function were disbanded." The moderator turns to you.

Options:

- A) Respond in kind, pointing out the commentator's firm sells competing services and has a commercial interest in the criticism.
- B) Acknowledge the legitimate kernel - architecture that never connects to delivery is waste - then give two or three concrete, non-sensitive examples where architectural work changed delivery outcomes (duplication avoided, services converged, investment redirected), and openly name what the profession is still improving.
- C) Decline to engage with a hostile framing and redirect to your prepared remarks.
- D) Agree diplomatically that the criticism has merit and move on, avoiding confrontation in a public setting.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A.**

**Explanation:** Speaking "on behalf of and represent[ing] the community to large audiences inside and outside of government" is a named behaviour in your communicating information skill, and Option B is what that looks like under hostile questioning: concede the valid kernel (which earns credibility and is, in fact, true - unconnected architecture *is* waste), rebut with concrete evidence rather than assertion, and volunteer the profession's genuine improvement areas (transparency, again). This answers the audience, not the attacker. Option A is least effective: attacking the commentator's motives rather than their argument is an ad hominem that reads as having no substantive answer, escalates a public conflict while you are representing thousands of colleagues, and hands the commentator the headline. Option C looks composed but ducks the question everyone heard, leaving the "paper factory" charge standing unanswered. Option D concedes the community's case away wholesale - diplomatic tone is right, but capitulation on substance fails the people you represent; there is a difference between acknowledging a kernel of truth (B) and agreeing with the conclusion (D).

### Preparation tips

- **Anchor in the published values.** Re-read the Civil Service values (integrity, honesty, objectivity, impartiality) and the government digital profession's principles (user needs first, transparency, accessibility and inclusion, collaboration, evidence over opinion) shortly before the assessment. Strong options almost always express these; weak options almost always sacrifice one for convenience.
- **Answer as the lead architect you are asked to be, not as a diplomat or a hero.** The role holds real authority (accountability for outputs, direction-setting) and real limits (you influence programmes you do not command). Options that abdicate the authority or ignore the limits both score poorly.
- **Look for the option that engages directly, adds value, and preserves the relationship.** In nearly every scenario above, the strongest option talks to the person first, brings evidence, offers a constructive route, and keeps governance honest. That pattern is not a coincidence; it is the behavioural profile the assessment encodes.
- **Beware your personal default under pressure.** Most senior professionals have one: some over-escalate, some over-accommodate, some go quiet and work around people. Review the scenarios where your instinct diverged from the keyed answer and ask which default was showing.
- **Ratings are about the option, not your cleverness.** When asked to rate every option independently, resist inventing circumstances that would redeem a weak option ("but if the SRO had already agreed..."). Judge each option on the scenario as written.
- **Take the time offered.** These assessments are usually untimed or generously timed. Read every option fully before judging any of them - the most effective and least effective options are often deliberately written to look similar at first glance.

### Common pitfalls

- **Choosing the idealistic answer over the effective one.** "Refuse and report" or "insist on the standard, whatever the cost" can feel principled but often scores below options that achieve the principled outcome while preserving relationships and momentum (Questions 1 and 8).
- **Selecting passive options.** Logging, filing, postponing, monitoring, and "raising at the next review" are the classic low-scoring choices; they defer the problem without adding value (Questions 3 and 8). The spec literature identifies this as the most common SJT failure, and at lead level the penalty is sharper because inaction by the accountable leader is itself a decision.
- **Escalating without first adding value.** Passing a conflict upward before you have analysed it, quantified it, or attempted resolution spends credibility and burdens executives with work that is yours (Questions 3 and 4).
- **Unilateral action that wins the battle and loses the war.** Deciding contested questions alone, rebutting critics publicly, or compelling participation through hierarchy - each resolves the immediate scene while destroying the trust the role runs on (Questions 4, 5, and 10).
- **Concealment dressed as pragmatism.** Quiet re-planning, silent deprioritisation, fake adoption - any option whose success depends on people not finding out is scored as what it is (Questions 6, 7, and 9).
- **Forgetting whose outcomes matter.** In UK government scenarios, the user - especially the vulnerable or excluded user - is the tiebreaker the scoring key respects (Question 8). When two options seem close, choose the one that treats user needs as evidence to be tested, not overhead to be managed.
