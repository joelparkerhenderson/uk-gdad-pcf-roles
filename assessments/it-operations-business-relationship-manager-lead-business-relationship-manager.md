## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive ability assessment measures the mental processes your role demands, using realistic materials rather than abstract puzzles. At leadership level the emphasis falls on reasoning about systems and about other people's reasoning: evaluating analysis you did not perform, choosing the simplest adequate approach among several defensible ones, deducing consequences through chains of organisational rules, and prioritising a portfolio where every item arrives pre-labelled "urgent" by someone senior.

Typical format: an online, timed test of 15 to 30 minutes covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation and applied problem solving, scored objectively against a norm group — for lead roles, usually a senior-managerial norm group, so the comparison population is quick and careful. Adaptive difficulty and separate speed/accuracy reporting are standard, and ungraded warm-up items usually precede the scored questions.

For a lead business relationship manager, cognitive assessment matters because your errors now propagate through other people. When you direct business analysis, your framing of the question determines what your team finds; a badly framed investigation wastes weeks of other people's work. When you take the bigger-picture view of groups of services, the patterns you see — or miss — become the organisation's consolidation and investment decisions. When you identify "the simplest of a variety of approaches", as your business analysis skill explicitly requires, you are exercising judgement about sufficiency: the costliest analytical failure at your level is not the wrong answer but the needlessly elaborate one, commissioned because complexity feels rigorous. And when escalations arrive, they arrive stripped of easy structure — the structured versions were resolved below you. A well-designed cognitive assessment samples exactly these demands, and employers use it at this level because the leverage of clear thinking, positive and negative, is at its maximum where decisions direct other people's months.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The assessment dimensions map directly to the named skills in your role summary:

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **Service focus** at your level: seeing the bigger picture by taking groups of services and investigating how to get the best of underlying services — pattern recognition performed on a portfolio, where the recurring structure across services is the finding.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Strategic thinking**: defining strategies and policies means reasoning from commitments to consequences before events do it for you — including the consequences of your own rules when they meet real behaviour.
- **Error checking** maps to **directing business analysis**: you now review more analysis than you produce, and ensuring that recommendations fit with strategic business objectives means finding the flaw in work you did not build — a distinct and testable skill.
- **Choosing the simplest adequate approach** maps to the line in your **Business analysis (IT operations)** skill that names it: identify the simplest of a variety of approaches — sufficiency judgement, the leadership form of problem solving.
- **Prioritisation** maps to **Ownership and topic** and your escalation duties: as the point of escalation, what reaches you is by definition contested; ranking by organisational impact rather than by escalation volume or seniority of the complainant is the daily discipline.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to your duty to **facilitate discussions across high risk and complex areas or projects under constrained timelines**: decomposing a deadlocked, deadline-bound situation into the parts that can move is problem solving with people in the variables.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern recognition across a service portfolio**

You review the annual figures for five internal services your function partners across:

| Service | Users | Annual cost (£k) | Satisfaction | Support tickets/year |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Expenses | 8,000 | 240 | 3.9/5 | 1,600 |
| Room booking | 7,500 | 90 | 4.1/5 | 300 |
| HR self-service | 8,200 | 310 | 3.8/5 | 1,700 |
| Travel booking | 3,000 | 220 | 2.6/5 | 1,900 |
| Print management | 6,000 | 85 | 4.0/5 | 250 |

Which service most clearly warrants your attention first, and on what pattern?

- A) HR self-service — it has the highest cost.
- B) Travel booking — it combines the fewest users with near-highest cost and tickets and the lowest satisfaction, giving it the worst per-user profile on every dimension at once.
- C) Expenses — it has the most users, so problems there affect the most people.
- D) Print management — its low ticket count suggests under-reporting.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Lead-level pattern recognition is multi-dimensional and per-unit. Normalise roughly by users: travel booking costs 220 ÷ 3 ≈ £73 per user (versus £30 for expenses, £38 for HR, £12 for room booking, £14 for print) and generates 1,900 ÷ 3,000 ≈ 0.63 tickets per user — triple the expenses rate (0.2) and more than fifteen times room booking's (0.04) — while sitting a full point below the next-worst satisfaction score. One service is an outlier in the same direction on *every* normalised dimension; that convergence is the pattern, and it is invisible to anyone reading the columns raw. Option A reads a single absolute column; HR's £310k serves 8,200 users at unremarkable per-user cost. Option C uses reach as a proxy for priority, but expenses' per-user profile is mid-pack. Option D invents a hypothesis (under-reporting) against the simpler reading that a 4.0-satisfaction service generates few tickets because it works. Taking groups of services and getting the best from the underlying services — your **Service focus** skill — begins exactly here: normalise, then look for the service that is an outlier on every axis at once, because that is where investigation pays.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction through delegation rules**

Your engagement framework states: (i) business relationship managers may commit the function to engagement activities costing up to £5,000 without approval; (ii) commitments between £5,000 and £25,000 require lead BRM approval; (iii) commitments above £25,000 require director approval, and the lead BRM must be consulted before the request reaches the director. A BRM on your team has agreed with a unit to run a discovery exercise costing £18,000, and work has started. Which statement must be true?

- A) The framework has been breached unless you approved the commitment.
- B) The BRM should have sought director approval.
- C) The commitment was within the BRM's own delegated authority.
- D) You must now stop the discovery exercise.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Place £18,000 in the framework's bands: above £5,000, below £25,000 — squarely in band (ii), which requires lead BRM approval, i.e. yours. Work has started, so either you approved it (framework satisfied) or you did not (breach). The disjunction collapses to A's conditional: breached *unless* you approved. Option B applies band (iii) to a band (ii) amount. Option C applies band (i). Option D states a remedy no rule requires — a breach of approval process does not automatically mean the work is wrong; the proportionate response is to review the commitment now, approve or amend it, and address the process miss with the BRM. Notice the leadership texture the correct reading carries: delegation frameworks are how you scale yourself, and they only work if the bands are applied precisely — both by your team (who must know where their authority ends) and by you (who must not treat every band-(ii) event as a crisis, or your team will stop telling you things). Reasoning correctly about your own rules is the precondition for enforcing them proportionately.

**Question 3 (easy) — Error checking a commissioned analysis**

A BRM on your team submits a shared-needs analysis concluding: "All four units want case tracking functionality, so a single shared case tracking platform is justified. Unit interviews confirmed the need: Unit W (12 interviews), Unit X (10), Unit Y (2), Unit Z (11). Combined demand is 35 confirmed user needs." As the director of this analysis, which is the most important weakness to send back?

- A) The interview counts should be equal across units.
- B) Unit Y's conclusion rests on 2 interviews — far too thin to "confirm" a unit-level need — so the all-four-units claim, and with it the shared-platform justification, currently overstates the evidence.
- C) 12 + 10 + 2 + 11 = 35 is correct, so the analysis is sound.
- D) Interviews are the wrong method entirely; only quantitative data can confirm needs.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Directing analysis means finding the inference that outruns its evidence — not checking the arithmetic (option C confirms a sum that was never the issue: 35 interviews is not "35 confirmed user needs", a conflation worth a second margin note) and not imposing methodological absolutism (option D discards a legitimate method; option A demands symmetry that unit sizes rarely justify). The load-bearing weakness is Unit Y: the analysis's headline — *all four* units share the need — and its recommendation — a single shared platform — both depend on a unit whose evidence is two conversations. Two interviews can signal, but cannot confirm, a unit-level need; if Unit Y drops out, the case becomes "three of four units", which may still justify sharing but on different economics and different politics. The correction you send back is precise and cheap: strengthen Unit Y's evidence before the claim ships, or re-scope the claim to match the evidence held. This is the review skill your role formalises — ensuring recommendations fit the evidence *and* the strategic objectives — and its habit is transferable to every paper that crosses your desk: find the conclusion's weakest load-bearing point and test whether it holds the weight the recommendation places on it.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Prioritisation at the point of escalation**

Monday, 08:30. Four escalations sit in your queue, all marked urgent by their senders:

1. A director complains that her unit's favoured investment was ranked below the cut line by the prioritisation forum on Friday and demands the ranking be "corrected" before it is published on Wednesday.
2. One of your BRMs reports that a unit head shouted at her in a meeting and she does not feel able to return to that unit alone; the unit's quarterly planning session — which she was due to facilitate — is Thursday.
3. An external delivery partner emails that, without a signed data-sharing renewal by Friday, they must legally suspend a data feed that three services consume.
4. Your director asks for "thoughts by tomorrow" on a draft restructure of the BRM function that would move two of your team to another division.

Which ordering of first attention is most defensible for Monday morning?

- A) 1, 3, 2, 4 — the director outranks the others and the publication deadline is nearest.
- B) 2, 3, 1, 4 — duty of care to your BRM first and it shapes Thursday's plan; then the legal deadline with lead time for signatures; then the ranking complaint; then the restructure response.
- C) 3, 4, 1, 2 — legal risk, then your director, then the director's complaint, then the team issue.
- D) 4, 1, 2, 3 — your director's request governs your own team's future and must come first.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Lead prioritisation weighs duty of care, legal exposure, governance integrity and hierarchy — in roughly that order when they genuinely collide. Item 2 comes first for two compounding reasons: a member of your team has been subjected to behaviour no process should normalise, and support delayed is support diminished — the first conversation tells her the function has her back, and it also determines Thursday's facilitation plan (you attending with her, or re-assigning, or addressing the unit head first), so it is time-critical as well as human-critical. Item 3 is a hard legal deadline with a dependency chain — data-sharing renewals need legal review and signatures, which consume days, so Monday morning is when the chase must start; its *first attention* can be an hour of mobilisation. Item 1, despite the seniority and the Wednesday date, is not a "correction" at all: a forum outcome a director dislikes is a governance result to be defended or formally appealed, not edited on demand — your Monday action is a measured reply explaining the appeal route, which protects the forum's integrity precisely by not being rushed. Item 4 has a Tuesday deadline and deserves reflective quality, not first-thing reactivity — and its outcome matters most of all in the long run, which is exactly why it gets protected time later in the day rather than a 08:30 hot take. Option A and D over-weight hierarchy; option C treats the shouting incident as the residual item, which — beyond the human failure — signals to your whole team what escalations to you are worth. The pattern: people first, then legal clocks, then governance integrity, then reflective strategy — with "first attention" sized to what each genuinely needs on Monday.

**Question 5 (moderate) — The simplest adequate approach**

A director asks whether her directorate's five annual conferences should consolidate onto one events-management platform. Available approaches: (a) a six-week discovery with user research across all five conference teams; (b) a two-day desk review of what each conference currently uses, costs, and complains about, followed by a one-hour workshop with the five organisers; (c) a market scan and procurement options analysis of events platforms; (d) piloting the leading platform on the next conference and evaluating afterwards. Which approach should you direct first, and why?

- A) Approach (a) — user research should always precede consolidation decisions.
- B) Approach (b) — the cheapest sufficient test of whether a shared need exists at all; the other approaches each assume an answer this one is designed to establish.
- C) Approach (c) — knowing the market is the precondition for any decision.
- D) Approach (d) — real usage evidence beats any analysis.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Your business analysis skill at lead level names this judgement exactly: *identify the simplest of a variety of approaches*. Sequence the logic: the decision-relevant question is not "which platform?" but "is there a shared need worth consolidating?" — five conference teams may have genuinely divergent requirements (scale, security, external attendees) or genuinely common ones, and until that is known, everything else is premature. Approach (b) answers precisely that question for two days' effort: current tools, costs and complaints on the desk, then the five organisers in one room — where divergence or commonality will surface within the hour. Approach (a) spends six weeks researching before establishing there is a decision that needs research-grade evidence; user research earns its cost when the *use* is subtle, not when the question is "do five teams buy similar things?" Approach (c) analyses a market before knowing whether the organisation is a coherent buyer. Approach (d) commits a real conference to a platform nobody has established a shared need for — piloting is a fine *second* step and an expensive first one. The general form, which the assessment is really testing: match the weight of the method to the weight of the present decision, and choose the cheapest approach that can change your next action. Note what B is not: it is not "skip the research" — if the workshop reveals a real shared need with subtle user requirements, approach (a) becomes the right next commission, now aimed at a question worth its cost.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Deduction across a services strategy commitment**

The services strategy commits: (i) by next April, every citizen-facing service must offer an assisted-digital route; (ii) services with fewer than 1,000 annual transactions may share an assisted-digital route with another service, provided a shared-route agreement is signed; (iii) no service may claim compliance through a shared route whose signed agreement it cannot produce. An audit of three services in your portfolio finds: Service P (12,000 transactions) has its own route. Service Q (600 transactions) uses Service R's route and produces a signed agreement. Service R (30,000 transactions) has its own route, which is the one Q shares. Which statement is correct?

- A) All three services comply with the strategy as audited.
- B) Q is non-compliant because it lacks its own route.
- C) R is non-compliant because it shares its route with Q.
- D) Q's compliance cannot be assessed without knowing R's transaction volume.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Walk each service through the rules. P: citizen-facing, has its own route — rule (i) satisfied directly. Q: 600 transactions is below 1,000, so rule (ii) permits sharing, conditional on a signed agreement — which the audit says Q produces; rule (iii)'s documentation test is met. R: has its own route — rule (i) satisfied; nothing in any rule prohibits a compliant service from *hosting* a shared route (rule (ii) constrains who may *rely on* sharing, not who may provide it), so option C invents a restriction the strategy does not contain. Option B ignores the explicit small-service exception — the strategy's own flexibility, which exists precisely so that a 600-transaction service is not forced to build standalone assisted-digital provision. Option D imports an irrelevant condition: the transaction threshold applies to the *sharing* service (Q), not the host; R's 30,000 transactions affect R's own obligations, already met. All three comply — option A — and the leadership point is worth pausing on: audits at your level frequently "find" non-compliance by misreading exactly these structures, either missing an exception (B's error) or inventing a prohibition (C's error). As the person who ensures services strategy commitments are met *and appropriately communicated*, you are the last reader who can catch a compliance misreading before it becomes a remediation demand on a unit that did everything right — a mistake that costs relationship capital out of all proportion to its origin.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Error checking a portfolio dashboard**

Your quarterly portfolio dashboard, compiled by your team for the executive committee, includes:

- "Engagement coverage: 28 of 30 in-scope units engaged this quarter (93%)."
- "Pipeline conversion: 15 of the 60 opportunities logged this year have been funded (25%), up from 20% last year."
- "Escalations: 9 raised this quarter, 9 resolved — resolution rate 100%."
- "Satisfaction: mean 4.1/5 across units responding (12 of 30 units)."

Which line most needs correction or caveat before the dashboard is submitted?

- A) The engagement coverage line — 28 of 30 is 93.3%, so it should say 93.3%.
- B) The satisfaction line — a 4.1 mean drawn from a 40% response rate is presented as portfolio-wide satisfaction, and the non-responding 18 units may differ systematically; the figure needs its base stated prominently and a reweighted or coverage-caveated reading.
- C) The escalations line — 9 raised and 9 resolved does not establish that the *same* nine were resolved, so "resolution rate 100%" may mix cohorts (this-quarter raises versus resolutions of older cases) and should state cohort explicitly.
- D) The pipeline line — 15 of 60 is 25%, which is correctly computed, so no change is needed anywhere.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Two lines carry genuine problems — the skill is ranking them. Option B's critique is real: a 4.1 mean from 12 of 30 units deserves its base stated and a caution about non-response; but the line as written does disclose "12 of 30 units", so the reader has the material for scepticism — the fix is prominence, not substance. Option C identifies a subtler and more misleading defect: "9 raised, 9 resolved, 100%" reads as *this quarter's escalations were all resolved*, but nothing establishes the resolved nine are the raised nine — a quarter that resolved nine old cases while its own nine new ones all remain open would produce identical figures and an opposite reality. Cohort-mixing of exactly this kind is how flow metrics flatter: the ratio of *any* period's completions to *any* period's arrivals approximates 100% whenever volumes are stable, regardless of how long anything takes or whether this quarter's cases progress at all. The correction is one clause — "9 raised this quarter, of which 6 resolved; 3 carried forward; 3 prior-quarter cases also closed" — and it changes the committee's understanding from "escalations are instantly handled" to the truth. Options A and D are cosmetic or empty. The leadership habit: before any dashboard ships upward under your name, interrogate each ratio for its cohort — numerator and denominator drawn from the same population and period — because executive committees remember your dashboards' claims long after your team remembers their caveats.

**Question 8 (challenging) — Multi-constraint facilitation under a deadline**

A cross-government data-sharing initiative you facilitate must reach signed agreement by Friday, or funding lapses. Five parties must sign: departments A, B, C, a regulator R, and an external delivery partner E. Constraints: (i) R will not sign until both A and B have signed, and R's signatures are executed only at its authorisation board, which meets on Thursdays; (ii) E will not sign until R has signed; (iii) C's legal team requires 48 hours between receiving the final text and C signing; (iv) A and B will each sign one working day after the final text is agreed, and the final text will be agreed at Monday's drafting session; (v) apart from R's board rule, each party signs one working day after its preconditions are met, at the earliest. Assuming the text is agreed Monday, which is the earliest day all five signatures can complete, and through which chain does the critical path run?

- A) Thursday; the critical path runs through C.
- B) Friday; the critical path runs through E, via A/B then R's Thursday board.
- C) Wednesday; all parties can sign in parallel once the text is agreed.
- D) Friday; the critical path runs through C's 48-hour legal review.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Build the dependency graph and walk the calendar. Text agreed Monday. A and B: sign Tuesday (one working day after agreement). R: its precondition — both A and B signed — completes Tuesday, but R executes signatures only at its Thursday board, so R signs Thursday; the Wednesday that rule (v) alone would have allowed is unavailable, and this calendar constraint, not the dependency logic, is what sets R's date. E: precondition (R signed) completes Thursday; one working day later is Friday — E signs Friday, with zero margin against the funding deadline. Meanwhile C: receives the final text Monday, the 48-hour legal review completes Wednesday, and C signs Thursday. All five signatures therefore complete on Friday, and the critical path is the chain that lands last: A/B → R's board → E — option B. Option A finds C's chain, which finishes a full day inside the deadline; option D backs the same wrong chain with the right day; option C ignores every stated dependency. Notice what the walk-through hands you as facilitator: the critical path runs through a *calendar* constraint (R's board) that no amount of goodwill from A, B or E can compress — so Monday's drafting session must end with A's and B's signatories diarised for Tuesday morning, R's board secretariat warned that the item must make Thursday's agenda, and E's signatory available Friday, because a single missed step anywhere on the chain converts Friday from deadline into breach. Facilitating high-risk work under constrained timelines — the capability your skill set names — is critical-path management performed on people: find the longest chain, find its least compressible link, and spend your influence exactly there.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Evaluating a consolidation pattern across services**

Reviewing your portfolio's underlying services, you notice: four business-facing services (grants, licensing, inspections, appeals) each separately contract for document storage, identity verification, and payment processing — twelve contracts in all, with three different suppliers per capability across the four services. Renewal dates are scattered across the next 30 months. A colleague proposes: "Consolidate everything now — twelve contracts into three shared capabilities." Which considered response best reflects lead-level service thinking?

- A) Agree: twelve contracts into three is obviously simpler, and simplicity is the goal.
- B) Disagree: the services own their contracts, and interference would damage relationships.
- C) Agree with the destination but sequence by the constraint: confirm the capabilities genuinely are common (identity assurance levels and payment types may differ by service), then consolidate each capability opportunistically at its renewal windows over the 30 months — earliest renewals first as pathfinders — rather than breaking contracts now for a big-bang migration.
- D) Commission a full enterprise architecture review before any position is taken.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Your **Service focus** skill at lead level — see the bigger picture by taking groups of services and investigating how to get the best of underlying services — is precisely this question. The pattern is real: three shared capabilities duplicated four ways is the signature of services procured in isolation, and the steady-state case for consolidation (leverage, consistency, fewer supplier relationships) is strong. But option A's "now" ignores what the scattered renewal dates actually mean: consolidating mid-contract means exit penalties, parallel running, and forced migration timelines — the costs that turn good consolidations into cautionary tales. C sequences by the real constraints in the right order: *first* verify commonality at the level that matters (identity verification at different assurance levels, or payments with different instrument types, may be less common than their labels suggest — the same under/over-abstraction test that governs shared-needs analysis), *then* ride the renewal calendar, converting each expiry from a routine re-procurement into a consolidation step, with the earliest renewals serving as pathfinders that de-risk the later, larger moves. Thirty months is not slowness; it is the shape of the opportunity. Option B converts relationship stewardship into estate paralysis — services own their contracts, but you own the cross-service view, and surfacing duplication *is* the relationship work. Option D commissions the heavyweight generic study where a targeted two-question analysis (are the capabilities common? when do the windows open?) suffices — failing the simplest-adequate-approach test from the opposite direction to A. The lead pattern: endorse the destination, verify the commonality, and let the constraint calendar set the route.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Reasoning about a rule you are asked to waive**

Your engagement framework requires that any investment proposal reaching the prioritisation forum must carry a business-unit sponsor at deputy-director level or above — a rule you introduced after unsponsored proposals repeatedly collapsed post-funding for lack of business ownership. A well-evidenced proposal from a frontline team now reaches the forum's deadline sponsorless: their deputy director retired last month and the replacement starts in three weeks; the unit's director general "supports the idea in principle" but will not formally sponsor ("that's what deputies are for"). The team asks you to waive the rule this once. Which reasoning best serves the situation?

- A) Waive the rule: the proposal is well-evidenced, and rules must serve outcomes, not block them.
- B) Refuse the waiver: rules applied inconsistently are not rules, and the next unsponsored proposal will cite this one.
- C) Neither waive nor refuse mechanically: the rule's *purpose* is secured business ownership post-funding, so test whether that purpose can be met by other means within the deadline — e.g. the DG formally naming an interim sponsor, or conditional forum entry with sponsorship confirmed before funds release; if no purpose-equivalent exists, hold the rule and give the team a fast track into the next cycle.
- D) Let the forum itself vote on whether to waive the rule, keeping yourself neutral.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The question tests whether you reason about rules as mechanisms or as totems. You wrote this rule, and you know its purpose: proposals without secured business ownership fail *after* funding — the sponsor requirement is a proxy for that ownership. C reasons from the purpose: the retirement gap is a proxy failure, not necessarily an ownership failure, so the move is to test for purpose-equivalents — a DG-named interim sponsor gives the forum exactly the secured ownership the rule exists to guarantee (and note the DG's "that's what deputies are for" is an invitation to ask for precisely this smaller, grantable thing); conditional entry with sponsorship-before-release preserves the safeguard at the point where it actually bites. If no equivalent can be built by the deadline, C holds the rule — because option B's core insight is genuinely true: a waiver granted on evidence-quality grounds (option A's reasoning) becomes the citation for every future sponsorless proposal, and "well-evidenced" is a judgement every proposer makes about their own work. A's error is treating the rule's *cost* as the reason to suspend it while ignoring that its *benefit* arrives later and invisibly — the collapsed projects that don't happen. D abdicates: the forum applies the framework; its integrity as an even-handed process depends on the framework's guardian not converting hard cases into votes. The leadership pattern, applicable far beyond this scenario: when asked to waive a rule you own, reason from its purpose — satisfy the purpose another way, or hold the rule and fix the hardship through the process — and never create the precedent that quality of advocacy substitutes for the thing the rule protects.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Directing analysis under conflicting executive narratives**

Two executive narratives circulate about your organisation's flagship internal platform. The COO's office says: "Adoption has plateaued at 60% because the remaining 40% of teams are laggards who need mandation." The service owner says: "Adoption has plateaued because the platform lacks the specialist features the remaining teams need — we need investment, not mandation." Both cite the same adoption curve. You are asked to direct a two-week analysis to inform the executive decision between mandation and investment. Which analytical design most directly serves the decision?

- A) Survey all teams on whether they prefer mandation or investment.
- B) Segment the non-adopting 40% by workload type and blocker: sample teams from each segment, establish for each whether the platform *can* serve their work today (blocker = willingness) or *cannot* (blocker = capability), and size the segments — because mandation is the answer only for the first kind and investment only for the second, and the mix is the decision.
- C) Benchmark adoption rates for comparable platforms in other departments to see whether 60% is actually a plateau.
- D) Interview the COO's office and the service owner to reconcile their narratives.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Directing analysis means designing the investigation backwards from the decision — and the decision here has a precise structure that B exposes: mandation and investment are not rival philosophies but *segment-specific remedies*. For a team whose work the platform can already serve, non-adoption is willingness (habit, switching costs, local tools) and mandation-style levers work; for a team whose work the platform cannot serve, mandation manufactures workarounds and resentment while investment genuinely unlocks adoption. Both executive narratives commit the same fallacy — treating the 40% as homogeneous — and the analysis that serves the decision is the one that measures the heterogeneity: segment, sample, classify each segment's binding blocker as can't-versus-won't, and size the segments. The output lands as decision-ready arithmetic: "roughly 25 points of the 40 are capability-blocked across three feature clusters; 15 are willingness-blocked" prescribes a sequenced mix — invest in the clusters, mandate where capability exists — and dissolves the false binary the executives are deadlocked on. Option A asks the affected population to vote on the conclusion, measuring preference where the question is diagnosis. Option C answers an interesting *different* question — whether 60% is even a problem — worth a background slide but incapable of choosing between the remedies. Option D analyses the narratives instead of the world; reconciling executives is your facilitation duty, but the *analysis* must arbitrate with evidence neither side authored. The lead pattern: when two seniors disagree about causes, the analysis that serves everyone is the one designed to make their shared question empirically decidable — and two weeks is enough when the design is aimed at the decision rather than the debate.

### Preparation tips

- **Practise reviewing, not just solving.** Take a recent analysis your team produced and find its weakest load-bearing inference in ten minutes. Review-mode reading — hunting the point where the conclusion outruns the evidence — is the lead-level cognitive skill, and it is directly what the harder test items sample.
- **Normalise before comparing.** Per-user, per-transaction, per-pound: make normalisation your reflex first move on any table. Raw-column reading is the most common error in portfolio questions and portfolio meetings alike.
- **Interrogate every ratio for its cohort.** Numerator and denominator from the same population and period — check it on dashboards you read, enforce it on dashboards you sign.
- **Rehearse purpose-based rule reasoning.** For each rule you own, write one sentence: what failure does this prevent? The exercise speeds both test questions about rules and real decisions about waivers.
- **Draw the graph under time pressure.** For any multi-party sequencing question, sketch dependencies and walk the calendar — two minutes of drawing beats five minutes of mental juggling, in tests and in Friday-deadline facilitation.
- **Practise the sufficiency question.** Before commissioning or computing anything, ask: what is the cheapest approach that could change my next action? Write it down. The simplest-adequate-approach judgement improves measurably with deliberate practice.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Reading absolute columns.** The biggest number is rarely the finding; the worst *normalised profile* is. Slow down and divide.
- **Confirming arithmetic while missing inference.** Sums that check can still carry claims that don't. Review the reasoning chain, not just the numbers — "35 interviews" is not "35 confirmed needs".
- **Treating every band and threshold impressionistically.** Delegation limits, transaction thresholds, tolerance percentages: place the value in its band precisely before concluding anything. Most rule-question errors are banding errors.
- **Over-weighting seniority in prioritisation.** Directors' complaints and directors' requests are real, but duty of care and legal clocks outrank them — and the scoring keys know it.
- **Defaulting to the heavyweight method.** Six-week discoveries and full architecture reviews feel rigorous; commissioned before the decision needs them, they are expensive procrastination. The tested — and professional — skill is matching method weight to decision weight.
- **Ignoring joint criticality.** In dependency questions, finding *a* critical path is not enough; check whether another chain lands the same day. Real deadlines are usually missed on the path nobody was watching.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures your ability to interpret numerical information, execute the right calculations accurately, and judge what a computed figure legitimately supports. Calculators are usually allowed; what is tested is the choice and interpretation of calculation, not arithmetic memory.

Typical format: an online, timed test of 20 to 40 minutes around tables, charts and data-rich passages, with two to four multiple-choice questions per exhibit. Leadership-normed versions concentrate on the skills your desk actually exercises: multi-year cost and benefit structures, weighted and normalised comparisons, compound trends against capacity and budgets, threshold and tolerance regimes, and — most distinctively — questions where every option is *a* correct calculation and the mark goes to the one that answers the decision at hand.

For a lead business relationship manager, the relationship with numbers is supervisory and adversarial at once. Supervisory: your team produces the dashboards, benefits models and options papers, and your signature converts their arithmetic into the function's official claims — so you must be the reviewer who catches the cohort-mixed ratio, the unweighted mean, and the cashability double-count before an executive committee does. Adversarial in the constructive sense: suppliers, units bidding for investment, and functions defending their budgets all present numbers chosen to flatter their case, and your role — ensuring spending review and services strategy commitments are met and *appropriately communicated* — requires re-deriving what the numbers actually support. And there is a third register unique to leadership: the numbers you set become incentives. A target, a threshold, or a reported metric chosen carelessly will be gamed into meaninglessness by rational people; numeric judgement at your level includes anticipating what a measure will do to behaviour. Employers test numeric reasoning for lead roles because the arithmetic errors that survive to this altitude are precisely the subtle, structural ones — and they move real money.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Multi-year cost, benefit and phasing structures** map to your duty to **ensure strategic demands, such as spending reviews and services strategy commitments, are met**: settlements, tranches, tolerances and phasing are the arithmetic of the commitments you are accountable for.
- **Portfolio-level normalisation and weighting** map to **Service focus**: getting the best of underlying services across a group starts with per-unit comparisons that are fair — weighted, normalised, cohort-consistent.
- **Threshold, tolerance and regime arithmetic** map to **Stakeholder relationship management (IT operations)** with external partners: service credits, review triggers and contractual tolerances are numeric regimes, and arbitrating escalations about them requires computing them exactly.
- **Trend and capacity reasoning** map to **Strategic thinking**: defining strategies means testing them against compounding demand, and evaluating current strategies means knowing when the trend line breaks the plan's assumptions.
- **Metric design and incentive effects** map to your duty to **set conditions that enable a culture of positive impact**: the measures you choose for value, user experience and efficiency shape what your teams and partners optimise — numeric judgement applied to behaviour.
- **Reviewing others' models** maps to **directing business analysis**: the benefits case your BRM built is yours once signed; finding its structural flaw is a numeric skill distinct from building it.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Portfolio engagement coverage**

Your function partners 45 in-scope business units through a team of five BRMs. This quarter, structured engagements were held with 36 units. What percentage coverage is that, and how many additional units would have been needed to reach 90%?

- A) 80% coverage; 5 more units needed
- B) 80% coverage; 4 more units needed
- C) 75% coverage; 6 more units needed
- D) 84% coverage; 3 more units needed

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Coverage: 36 ÷ 45 = 0.80 = 80%. The 90% requirement: 0.9 × 45 = 40.5 units — and since you cannot engage half a unit and the target is a minimum, round up to 41 (40 units would give 40 ÷ 45 = 88.9%, just short). Additional units needed: 41 − 36 = 5, so option A. Option B is the conventional-rounding trap (40.5 → 40 → gap of 4): check it — 40 ÷ 45 = 88.9% fails a 90% floor, so the requirement is genuinely 41. The leadership use of this arithmetic is distribution, not totals: 80% portfolio coverage across five BRMs could be five people each at 80%, or three at 100% and two at 50% — and the second pattern is a workload or capability signal the portfolio figure hides. Whenever you report a team-level rate, glance at its spread across the people who produced it; the executive committee needs the 80%, but you need the spread.

**Question 2 (easy) — Reading a spending review settlement table**

Your directorate's settlement for digital across three years:

| | Year 1 (£m) | Year 2 (£m) | Year 3 (£m) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Core delivery | 12.0 | 11.4 | 10.8 |
| Transformation | 4.0 | 5.0 | 6.0 |
| Cyber (ring-fenced) | 2.0 | 2.0 | 2.5 |

By what percentage does total annual funding change from Year 1 to Year 3?

- A) Up 7.2%
- B) Up 12.8%
- C) Down 4.5%
- D) Up 4.5%

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Total each year first. Year 1: 12.0 + 4.0 + 2.0 = £18.0m. Year 3: 10.8 + 6.0 + 2.5 = £19.3m. Change: (19.3 − 18.0) ÷ 18.0 = 1.3 ÷ 18.0 = 0.0722 = 7.2% increase — option A. The plausible distractor is option C via the core delivery line alone: 12.0 → 10.8 is a 10% cut, and a hurried reader anchoring on the top line concludes the settlement shrinks. The composition is the real story for your communication duty: the total grows 7.2%, but core delivery falls 10% while transformation rises 50% and the cyber ring-fence grows and remains untouchable — meaning business units experiencing "less money for keeping the lights on" and an executive hearing "budget up 7%" are both reading the same true table. Appropriately communicating a settlement — your role-level phrase — means presenting total *and* composition together, because each audience will otherwise quote the row that suits its grievance.

**Question 3 (easy) — Escalation load across a team**

Your five BRMs handled the following escalation counts this year, against portfolios of different sizes:

| BRM | Escalations | Units in portfolio |
|---|---|---|
| P | 18 | 12 |
| Q | 8 | 4 |
| R | 12 | 10 |
| S | 6 | 6 |
| T | 16 | 13 |

Which BRM's portfolio has the highest escalation rate per unit?

- A) P
- B) Q
- C) T
- D) S

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Compute per-unit rates: P 18 ÷ 12 = 1.5; Q 8 ÷ 4 = 2.0; R 12 ÷ 10 = 1.2; S 6 ÷ 6 = 1.0; T 16 ÷ 13 ≈ 1.23. Q's rate of 2.0 escalations per unit is highest — option B — even though P and T carry the largest raw counts, which is what options A and C reward a reader for noticing instead. The supervisory reading goes one step further than the arithmetic: a high per-unit escalation rate is a *signal*, not a verdict, and it points in at least three directions — Q's four units may be genuinely the hardest (which is why they were grouped into a small portfolio), Q may be escalating too readily (a coaching conversation about resolution before escalation), or Q may be the only BRM honestly recording escalations (a data-culture finding that reflects *well* on Q). As the team's leader you hold the context to distinguish these; the assessment habit — normalise first, then interpret against context — is the professional habit in miniature.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Compound growth versus a flat budget**

Cloud hosting costs across your portfolio are £1.6m this year and have grown 12% annually, compounding, for three years. The spending review settlement holds hosting budgets flat at £1.6m for the next three years. If growth continues, what cumulative shortfall accrues over the three settlement years?

- A) About £1.25m
- B) About £1.15m
- C) About £0.65m
- D) About £0.58m

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Project each year at compound growth, then subtract the flat budget. Year 1: 1.6 × 1.12 = £1.792m; shortfall 0.192. Year 2: 1.792 × 1.12 = £2.007m; shortfall 0.407. Year 3: 2.007 × 1.12 = £2.248m; shortfall 0.648. Cumulative shortfall: 0.192 + 0.407 + 0.648 = £1.247m ≈ £1.25m — option A. Option B (£1.15m) is the *linear* projection: treating growth as a flat £192k per year gives shortfalls of 0.192 + 0.384 + 0.576 = £1.152m, understating the true gap by about £100k — and the understatement widens every further year, because compounding grows the growth. Option C is the year 3 shortfall alone mistaken for the cumulative figure; option D is a similar single-year misread. The professional translation of the correct arithmetic: a 12% compounding cost pressed against a flat settlement is short £1.25m over the period — roughly 78% of one year's entire budget — and the honest responses are demand management, efficiency work (rightsizing, reserved-capacity pricing), or an explicit pressures case at the next fiscal event, declared early. The leadership habit the question drills: never brief a compounding pressure from a linear estimate, because the linear number is always the comfortable one, and the comfort is borrowed from exactly the later years when the money will not be there.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Weighted satisfaction with a composition change**

Last year your portfolio's satisfaction survey: Unit group 1 (heavy users, 200 respondents) scored 3.6; group 2 (light users, 600 respondents) scored 4.2. This year: group 1 (400 respondents) scored 3.8; group 2 (400 respondents) scored 4.2. A team member drafts: "Weighted satisfaction fell from 4.05 to 4.0 — our performance declined." What is the accurate reading?

- A) The draft is right: 4.05 to 4.0 is a decline in performance.
- B) Weighted scores fell, but no group's experience worsened: group 1 improved (3.6→3.8) and group 2 held (4.2) — the fall is composition: heavy users doubled their share of respondents. Performance improved or held everywhere; the mix changed.
- C) The figures must be miscalculated, since no group fell but the average did.
- D) The survey should be discarded because respondent numbers changed.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Verify the weighted means. Last year: (3.6 × 200 + 4.2 × 600) ÷ 800 = (720 + 2,520) ÷ 800 = 3,240 ÷ 800 = 4.05 ✓. This year: (3.8 × 400 + 4.2 × 400) ÷ 800 = (1,520 + 1,680) ÷ 800 = 3,200 ÷ 800 = 4.0 ✓. Both computations are right, and option C's instinct — "no group fell but the average did, something's wrong" — is exactly backwards: this is Simpson's-paradox-adjacent composition shift, entirely lawful arithmetic. Heavy users score lower (3.8 versus 4.2), and their share of respondents doubled from 25% to 50%, dragging the blended mean down even as every group's own score improved or held. B is the accurate reading, and the drafting consequence matters at your level: "performance declined" is the sentence an executive will remember, and it is *false* — the true sentences are "heavy users' satisfaction improved from 3.6 to 3.8; light users held at 4.2; the blended average fell because heavy users — our toughest audience — now make up half of respondents". Note the leadership question hiding underneath: *why* did heavy-user respondents double? More heavy users, better survey reach into them, or light users disengaging from the survey? Each has different implications. The pattern to internalise: whenever a weighted average moves, decompose into rate change versus mix change before allowing anyone to narrate it — averages move for reasons, and the reasons are the report.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Service credit arbitration**

An external partner's contract specifies: monthly availability below 99.5% earns a 5% service credit on that month's £40,000 charge; below 99.0%, a 10% credit; below 98.0%, a 15% credit and a formal improvement plan. The quarter's figures: Month 1: 99.6%; Month 2: 98.9%; Month 3: 97.8%. The partner has offered "a goodwill 5% credit on the quarter (£6,000) in full and final settlement". As arbiter of the escalated dispute, what does the contract actually yield, and how does the offer compare?

- A) The contract yields £10,000 plus an improvement plan; the £6,000 offer is £4,000 short and waives the plan.
- B) The contract yields £6,000, so the offer is exactly right.
- C) The contract yields £12,000; the offer is half.
- D) The contract yields £10,000, and the offer is £4,000 short but the improvement plan is negotiable.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Apply each month to its band. Month 1: 99.6% is above 99.5% — no credit. Month 2: 98.9% is below 99.0% (and above 98.0%) — 10% of £40,000 = £4,000. Month 3: 97.8% is below 98.0% — 15% of £40,000 = £6,000 *and* the formal improvement plan triggers. Contractual total: £4,000 + £6,000 = £10,000, plus the plan. The partner's "goodwill 5% on the quarter" — £6,000 — is therefore £4,000 light *and*, via "full and final settlement", quietly extinguishes the improvement plan, which for a service that has deteriorated across the quarter (99.6 → 98.9 → 97.8, a worsening trend the offer's flat framing obscures) is the more valuable entitlement: credits compensate the past; the plan changes the future. Option D concedes the plan as "negotiable" — but the contract makes it an entitlement, and entitlements are negotiated *from*, not negotiated *away* as an opening position. Option B accepts the partner's aggregation (a quarterly average conveniently blending one good month against two bad ones — the same trick as annualising past a quarterly regime). The arbitration posture the arithmetic supports: claim the £10,000 and the plan as the contractual baseline, then discuss goodwill — because a lead who arbitrates escalations must first compute what the words already grant, or every "goodwill" offer becomes an anchor below entitlement.

**Question 7 (moderate) — The metric that will be gamed**

You are choosing a headline efficiency metric for your function's new performance framework. Candidates: (a) number of engagements held per quarter; (b) percentage of demand items given a decision within 10 working days; (c) stakeholder-reported value score (annual survey); (d) percentage of funded investments still meeting their benefits profile at 12 months. Which pairing best balances early behaviour signals against outcome truth, and why?

- A) (a) alone — it is the most controllable by the team.
- B) (b) and (d) — a fast leading indicator that is hard to game without genuinely deciding faster, paired with a lagging outcome measure that catches whether fast decisions were also good ones.
- C) (a) and (c) — activity plus perception covers both bases.
- D) (d) alone — outcomes are all that matters.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Metric design is numeric reasoning applied to incentives, and the question rewards thinking one move ahead of each measure. Metric (a) counts activity: it is trivially gameable (more, shorter, emptier engagements) and — worse — it *invites* the gaming by rewarding motion over outcome; option A's "most controllable" is precisely the disqualification. Metric (c) measures perception annually: valuable context, but slow, coarse, and influenced by factors (settlement outcomes, one bad incident) outside the behaviour you want to steer. Metric (b) is a strong leading indicator: decision speed is what units actually experience, the 10-day threshold creates a clear operational target, and the honest way to improve it — triage, delegation bands, better-prepared submissions — is the behaviour you want anyway; its gaming risk (rushing bad decisions to beat the clock) is real, which is exactly why B pairs it with (d): benefits-at-12-months is the lagging truth-teller that surfaces whether fast decisions were also sound ones. A pair with one fast, behaviour-shaping measure and one slow, gaming-resistant outcome measure is the standard architecture; option D's outcome-only purism leaves the team steering by a light twelve months behind them. The check to run on any metric before adopting it — in tests and in frameworks — is Goodhart's question: *what is the cheapest way to improve this number without improving anything real?* If the cheapest way is also the desired behaviour, the metric is well-chosen; setting conditions for a culture of positive impact, as your role puts it, is done substantially through exactly these choices.

**Question 8 (challenging) — Benefits at portfolio level: the double-count hunt**

Three funded initiatives in your portfolio each claim benefits, and your team has consolidated them for the executive committee:

| Initiative | Claimed annual benefit | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Digital forms programme | £400k | 20,000 staff-hours saved at £20/hour |
| Contact-centre channel shift | £350k | 25% call reduction × 56,000 calls × £25/call |
| Shared CRM consolidation | £300k | Retiring 3 legacy systems (£180k licences) + 6,000 staff-hours saved at £20/hour |
| **Portfolio total** | **£1,050k** | |

Investigation shows the digital forms programme's hour savings include 8,000 hours from the same processes the CRM consolidation also claims its 6,000 hours from, and the channel-shift call reduction assumes the digital forms are live. Which statement best assesses the portfolio claim?

- A) The total is sound: each initiative's arithmetic checks out individually.
- B) The total overstates: up to £120k of staff-hour savings may be claimed twice across forms and CRM (the overlapping processes cannot yield both claims in full), and the channel-shift benefit is *dependent* on forms delivery, so £350k of the total carries forms-programme risk — the honest portfolio range is roughly £930k–£1,050k with a stated dependency.
- C) The total understates, since benefits usually compound.
- D) The CRM claim should be deleted entirely because it overlaps with forms.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Portfolio consolidation is where individually sound cases become collectively unsound, and the two standard defects are both planted here. First, the double-count: forms claims 20,000 hours, CRM claims 6,000, but 8,000 of the forms hours arise on the same processes as CRM's 6,000 — the same hour of the same person's time cannot be saved twice, so the overlap's maximum double-claim is the smaller figure, 6,000 hours × £20 = £120k; the defensible consolidated position deducts the overlap from one claimant (or apportions it), giving a floor of £1,050k − £120k = £930k. Second, the dependency: channel shift's £350k *assumes forms are live* — not a double-count but a correlation, meaning the portfolio's benefit lines are not independent; if forms slips, £350k slips with it, which the committee must know when it treats the total as bankable. Option A audits each row and never the joins — the precise failure mode of team-consolidated packs, and the reason the consolidation review is *your* job. Option D over-corrects: CRM's £180k licence saving is untouched by the overlap and £60k of its hours may be genuinely distinct; deleting £300k to fix a £120k problem is arithmetic vandalism. Option C asserts a generic optimism no evidence supports. The lead-level habits: benefits consolidate by *deduplication, not addition*; every portfolio total needs an overlap review across its claims' bases; and dependencies between benefit lines are risk information the honest pack states — "£930k–£1,050k, of which £350k contingent on forms delivery" is a weaker headline and a far stronger position than a clean £1,050k that dissolves under one committee question.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Capacity arithmetic for a team you lead**

Your five BRMs each have roughly 1,600 productive hours per year. Portfolio demand next year: 45 units requiring on average 100 hours/year of core partnering; the spending review adds a fixed 900 hours of work; two strategic programmes need a combined 1,650 hours of dedicated partnering; and experience shows 15% of total capacity must be held back for unplanned escalations. Can next year's demand be met, and what is the approximate surplus or shortfall?

- A) Shortfall of about 250 hours — roughly 0.16 of a full-time BRM.
- B) Surplus of about 950 hours.
- C) Shortfall of about 1,450 hours.
- D) Exact balance: demand equals capacity.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Work the two sides separately. Capacity: 5 × 1,600 = 8,000 hours; the escalation reserve removes 15% of 8,000 = 1,200 hours from plannable time, leaving 6,800 hours available for planned work. Demand: units 45 × 100 = 4,500; spending review 900; programmes 1,650; total = 4,500 + 900 + 1,650 = 7,050 hours. Position: 6,800 − 7,050 = −250 — a shortfall of about 250 hours, roughly 250 ÷ 1,600 ≈ 0.16 of a full-time BRM, option A. Option B (surplus of 950) is the answer you get by forgetting the reserve entirely (8,000 − 7,050) — the single most common capacity-planning error, and the one that produces teams that "fit on paper" and drown in practice, because escalation work is not optional: history says it *will* consume its 1,200 hours whether you planned for it or not. Option C typically results from double-counting the reserve (subtracting it from capacity *and* adding it to demand). Two leadership notes complete the picture. First, a 250-hour shortfall is small enough to manage but only through explicit choices — trimming average unit hours from 100 to 95 (saving 225) nearly closes it, as would descoping 250 hours of spending review support — and the value of the model is that it forces those trade-offs into the open *before* the year starts, rather than letting the shortfall land silently on whichever BRM absorbs it as unpaid overwork. Second, the model's honesty depends on its two soft assumptions — the 15% reserve (audit last year's actual escalation hours) and the 1,600 productive hours (check it against real leave, training and management overhead) — so present the result as a range across defensible values of both, and the capacity conversation with your director is about real variables instead of a false point estimate.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Trend, seasonality and an external partner's claim**

An external delivery partner, seeking contract expansion, presents: "Demand for our managed service has grown 35% in six months (March: 2,000 transactions; September: 2,700). At this rate, volumes will double within a year — expand capacity now or face failure." Your own records show last year's monthlies: March 2,100 → June 1,750 → September 2,650 → December 2,050. What is the strongest assessment?

- A) The partner is right: 35% in six months is a compounding trend that must be met with expanded capacity.
- B) Last year shows the same March-to-September rise (2,100 → 2,650, +26%) followed by a December fall — the "growth" is substantially seasonal pattern presented as trend; the like-for-like signal is September-on-September (2,650 → 2,700, +1.9%), which supports roughly flat demand, not doubling.
- C) Demand is actually declining, since March this year (2,000) is below March last year (2,100).
- D) The two data sets conflict, so no assessment is possible without a third year.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The partner's arithmetic is correct — 2,700 ÷ 2,000 = 1.35, a 35% rise — and the inference is the flaw: extrapolating a March-to-September movement as a *trend* when your own records show the identical seasonal shape last year: up 26% March-to-September, then back down by December. Seasonal series must be compared like-for-like, and the year-on-year comparisons say: March 2,100 → 2,000 (−4.8%), September 2,650 → 2,700 (+1.9%) — demand is essentially flat, with ordinary noise, and "volumes will double within a year" is a seasonal upswing dressed as compound growth. Option C makes the mirror-image error, reading the March-on-March dip alone as decline — one negative comparison is no more a trend than one positive. Option D overstates the ambiguity: two years of monthly shape is exactly enough to identify seasonality this pronounced, and the like-for-like figures are computable now. B also frames the *commercial* posture the analysis supports: the expansion case fails on the partner's own evidence once seasonally adjusted, and your counter is capacity flexibility across the seasonal peak rather than permanent expansion priced on the peak. This is the adversarial-numbers skill of your role in pure form: counterparties will present arithmetically true, inferentially false claims, and the defence is always the same — find the comparison the data structure actually permits (here, year-on-year), compute it, and let the honest number negotiate.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Choosing what the number supports: investment arbitration**

Two directors escalate a deadlock for your arbitration. £500k of discretionary investment remains. Director A's bid: automate document processing — model shows £150k/year savings from year 1, high confidence (the process is stable, the technology proven). Director B's bid: modernise the field-worker mobile platform — model shows £80k/year efficiency plus "unquantified but substantial" user experience and retention benefits for 900 field staff whose attrition (18%, versus 11% organisation-wide) exit interviews link to "unworkable tools". Both bids are competently costed at £500k. Which analysis most defensibly supports an arbitration?

- A) Fund A: £150k/year beats £80k/year, and unquantified benefits are inadmissible.
- B) Fund B: user experience always outweighs cash savings.
- C) Test whether B's unquantified benefit can be bounded before deciding: each point of excess attrition among 900 staff is 9 leavers/year, and at a conservative £15k full cost per replacement, closing even half the 7-point attrition gap is worth 900 × 0.035 × £15k ≈ £470k/year — an order-of-magnitude that, if the tools-attrition link survives scrutiny, makes B's true return several times A's; commission that scrutiny within a fortnight and arbitrate on the bounded number.
- D) Split the funding £250k each, since both cases have merit.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The deadlock exists because the two bids are expressed in different currencies — A in bankable pounds, B in pounds plus an unquantified human factor — and arbitration by comparing £150k with £80k (option A) simply awards the decision to whichever director quantified more, an incentive with corrosive long-term effects on every future bid you receive. The lead-level move is C's: *bound the unquantified*. The data to do so is already in the stem: 900 field staff, attrition 18% versus 11% — a 7-point excess is 63 leavers a year above the organisational rate; replacement costs (recruitment, onboarding, lost productivity) conservatively £15k each; even attributing only *half* the excess to tools yields 31.5 × £15k ≈ £470k/year of addressable cost, nearly six times B's quantified line and three times A's. That number is rough — which is precisely why C commissions a two-week scrutiny of the attribution (do exit interviews really implicate tools? what does the research say?) rather than funding on it directly. But rough-and-bounded beats precise-and-partial: the plausible range for B's return (£80k certain + £0–470k attrition-linked) dominates A's £150k under most defensible attributions, and the arbitration becomes evidence-shaped rather than advocacy-shaped. Option B's "always" is A's error inverted — a value asserted instead of a value computed. Option D is arbitration's failure mode: £250k likely delivers *neither* bid (both are costed at £500k), converting a hard decision into two funded disappointments — splitting is only defensible when bids scale, which nothing in the stem supports. The pattern, which reaches well beyond this question: when a decision is deadlocked between a quantified and an unquantified good, the leader's job is to make the unquantified *estimable* — bound it conservatively, test its attribution, and let the decision be argued in one currency. That is promoting user-centred investment with a financier's grammar — and it is how user experience stops losing arbitrations it deserved to win.

### Preparation tips

- **Rehearse decomposition of moving averages.** Whenever a blended figure shifts, practise splitting rate change from mix change before narrating it. One decomposition a week on your own dashboards builds the reflex the harder questions — and executive briefings — demand.
- **Compute regimes at their own period.** Credits, tolerances and thresholds: always apply the contract's banding month by month (or quarter by quarter) before considering any aggregate a counterparty offers.
- **Bound the unquantified.** Practise conservative order-of-magnitude estimates for "unquantifiable" benefits — attrition costs, rework, waiting time. The skill of bounding is tested at lead level and priceless in arbitration.
- **Stress your capacity models.** For any plan, identify the two most sensitive assumptions (usually a percentage base and a utilisation factor) and compute the plan at both ends of each. Present ranges, not points.
- **De-duplicate before you consolidate.** For any portfolio total, list each claim's basis and hunt overlaps and dependencies across rows. The joins, not the rows, are where consolidated packs fail.
- **Run Goodhart's check on every metric.** Before adopting or accepting a measure, name the cheapest way to improve it without improving anything real. If that answer alarms you, redesign the metric — or pair it with the lagging measure that catches the gaming.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Linear projection of compounding costs.** The shortfall is always bigger than the straight-line estimate, and it grows fastest exactly where budgets are flat. Compound forward properly.
- **Narrating a mix change as a performance change.** "The average fell" is not "we got worse" — decompose first, and stop false narratives before they ship under your signature.
- **Accepting a counterparty's aggregation or comparison frame.** Quarterly goodwill offers against monthly regimes, six-month "trends" across a seasonal peak: recompute at the regime's period and the data's like-for-like structure, every time.
- **Adding portfolio benefits without an overlap review.** Individually sound cases double-count shared processes and hide cross-dependencies. Consolidation is deduplication.
- **Presenting point estimates from assumption-sensitive models.** Capacity plans and benefit totals that omit their sensitivity are noise wearing precision. State the assumptions; show the range.
- **Letting quantification asymmetry decide contests.** The better-quantified bid is not necessarily the better bid. Bound the unquantified side before comparing — or accept that your decisions systematically penalise the benefits hardest to count, including user experience.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment measures how precisely you extract, evaluate and deploy meaning from written material. Passages of 100–250 words — policy extracts, contractual clauses, ministerial correspondence, research summaries, escalation chains — are followed by True/False/Cannot-say judgements, inference questions, argument evaluations, and clear-writing choices. The governing discipline is strict: answer from the passage alone, however much your experience suggests about what "usually" happens.

Typical format: an online, timed test of 15 to 30 minutes with several passages and two to four questions each, adaptive difficulty, and speed/accuracy reported separately against a senior norm group. Leadership-normed versions distinguish themselves through the *stakes* written into the passages — commitments, conditions, delegations, disclosures — and through wrong options that are one modal verb, one scope term, or one substituted concept away from right.

For a lead business relationship manager, verbal precision operates at the altitude where words function as instruments of governance. Your role summary is explicit: complex needs and challenges must be *understood, prioritised and communicated to relevant audiences* — and at your level, the audiences include executive committees, external partners, and occasionally the centre of government; the communications include commitments that bind your function and clarifications that unbind it from things it never promised. You also read on behalf of the organisation: the services strategy sentence a unit invokes to resist change, the contract clause a partner reads generously in its own favour, the escalation email whose careful drafting conceals what its sender chose not to say. And you set the drafting standard your team learns from — every paper you let ship teaches your BRMs what "good enough" means. Employers test verbal reasoning at lead level because the reading and writing errors that survive to this height are precisely the consequential ones: a misread tolerance moves money; an over-promised commitment becomes a grievance; an ambiguous policy you authored becomes a year of interpretive disputes that all escalate to you.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **True/False/Cannot-say discipline** maps to **directing business analysis**: the boundary between what evidence establishes and what it merely suggests is exactly the boundary your reviews must police in your team's findings — and in your own briefings upward.
- **Reading commitments, conditions and delegations precisely** maps to your duty to ensure **spending reviews and services strategy commitments are met and appropriately communicated**: the commitment's exact scope is the compliance obligation; communicate more and you over-bind your organisation, less and you mislead it.
- **Argument evaluation** maps to **influencing important senior stakeholders and arbitrating when blockers are escalated**: arbitration begins with locating the load-bearing claim in each side's account and testing whether its evidence holds its weight.
- **Reading external and contractual text** maps to your leadership of engagement with **internal and external stakeholders**: partner assurances, contract clauses and joint communiqués are drafted by parties with interests; reading them is a defensive skill.
- **Clear-writing judgement** maps to your duty to ensure complex needs are **communicated to relevant audiences** and to your culture-setting role: the standard you enforce in your function's writing *is* a working condition you create for everyone who reads it.
- **Inference calibration** maps to **Strategic thinking** and long-term relationship stewardship: hearing what a senior stakeholder's carefully drafted message signals — without converting signals into commitments — is the daily verbal work of the strategic approach you direct.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — True/False/Cannot say on a services strategy clause**

Passage: "The services strategy commits each directorate to publishing a service performance dashboard by December. Dashboards must include availability, user satisfaction and cost per transaction. Directorates unable to compute cost per transaction may publish with the other two measures, provided a plan to add cost data within six months is included. The digital function will provide a dashboard template, though its use is optional."

Statement: "A directorate that publishes a December dashboard showing only availability and user satisfaction, with no cost data and no plan to add it, has met the strategy's commitment."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Track the conditional structure. The default requirement is three measures. The concession for directorates unable to compute cost per transaction permits publishing with two — *provided* a plan to add cost data within six months is included. The statement's directorate publishes two measures with no plan, so it fails the concession's condition, and with it the commitment: the passage contradicts the statement — False, not Cannot say. The classic mistake is reading the concession as unconditional ("two measures is allowed") and stopping before the proviso; assessments plant exactly this partial reading, and so does real compliance conversation — a directorate will quote the concession sentence at you without its proviso, and the lead who has read the whole clause is the one who can hold the line kindly: "two measures is fine; the plan is the missing piece". Note also the last sentence's trap-in-waiting: the *template* is optional; the *dashboard* is not — a distractor statement about optionality would test whether you attach the right noun to "optional". Precision about which obligation each qualifier modifies is the whole game, in tests and in strategy stewardship.

**Question 2 (easy) — False versus Cannot say on an escalation email**

Passage (from a unit head, escalating): "I am escalating the CRM data quality issue to you directly. We first raised this with your team in March. Since then we have had two workshops and a promised fix date of August, which has now passed. I do not doubt your team's effort or goodwill, but the issue remains unresolved, and my director is asking me weekly for a date I cannot give."

Statement: "The unit head believes the lead BRM's team has not made an effort on the issue."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage states the opposite in terms: "I do not doubt your team's effort or goodwill". The statement attributes to the unit head a belief the passage explicitly disclaims, so the passage contradicts it — False. It is not Cannot say, because the passage is not silent on the point; it speaks to it directly. The question looks elementary, and its workplace analogue is anything but: escalations are routinely mis-summarised in exactly this direction — "the unit is attacking my team" — because the *fact* of escalation feels like an accusation, and defensive reading imports hostility the text explicitly disavows. Read this email accurately and it is close to a model escalation: a timeline (March, two workshops, a lapsed August date), a disclaimer of blame, and a precise statement of the sender's own pressure (a weekly question they cannot answer). The accurate reading tells you what resolution requires — a *date they can give their director* — which is different from, and cheaper than, what a defensive reading would have you prepare: a justification of your team's effort that nobody asked for. As the named point of escalation in your skill set, reading escalations for what they request rather than what they seem to accuse is a daily act of professional reading.

**Question 3 (easy) — Main point of a partner communication**

Passage (from an external delivery partner's quarterly letter): "We remain committed to the partnership and to the joint roadmap agreed in January. We should flag that the exchange-rate movements of recent months have materially increased our licence input costs, which under clause 14 we absorb until the annual review. We are not seeking any in-year change. We would, however, welcome early discussion of the cost model ahead of the annual review, and we attach an options paper for that conversation."

Which sentence best states the letter's main point?

- A) The partner is invoking clause 14 to raise prices immediately.
- B) The partner is signalling that it will seek cost model changes at the annual review, and is asking to begin that negotiation early — while explicitly honouring current terms in the meantime.
- C) The partner is threatening to exit the partnership over exchange rates.
- D) The letter is a routine reaffirmation of commitment with no operative content.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Read the letter as a sequence of moves: reaffirm commitment (relationship maintenance), flag a cost pressure and cite the clause that currently makes it *their* problem (establishing facts for later), disclaim any in-year demand (removing alarm), request early discussion and attach options (the operative ask). B captures the structure: this is the opening of a negotiation, courteously conducted, with the annual review as its destination. Option A reverses the clause's effect — clause 14 is cited precisely to show they are absorbing costs, not passing them through. Option C hears a threat no sentence carries. Option D mistakes courtesy for emptiness: a letter with an attached options paper and a requested conversation has operative content by definition — "no operative content" is what the letter's *tone* suggests and its *structure* refutes, and the gap between tone and structure is exactly what senior professional reading detects. The practical response the correct reading produces: acknowledge, agree the early discussion on your timetable, and commission your own analysis of the cost model *before* reading theirs — because whoever frames the annual review first frames it best. Directing the strategic approach to an external relationship starts with reading its correspondence as strategy, not weather.

**Question 4 (moderate) — True/False/Cannot say on a delegation letter**

Passage (from the director general's delegation letter): "The lead business relationship manager may approve engagement commitments up to £25,000, and may sub-delegate approval up to £5,000 to named business relationship managers. Sub-delegations must be recorded in the delegations register. Commitments involving external partners require director approval regardless of value. This delegation is personal to the postholder and lapses on a change of postholder until renewed."

Statement: "A named BRM with a recorded sub-delegation may approve a £4,000 engagement commitment involving an external partner."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Layer the rules in the order the letter states them. The sub-delegation route permits named, registered BRMs to approve up to £5,000 — so far, £4,000 qualifies. But the external-partner rule is expressed as an override: "director approval *regardless of value*" — a carve-out that removes external-partner commitments from every delegated band, including yours, and therefore from anything you could sub-delegate (you cannot pass down authority you do not hold). The statement is contradicted: False. The tempting error is sequential reading without integration — each sentence is understood alone, and the £5,000 rule is applied without checking whether a later sentence removes the case from its scope. "Regardless of value", "notwithstanding", "except as provided" — override language is where delegation instruments concentrate their meaning, and misreading it in the permissive direction produces the worst kind of governance failure: a junior colleague acting in good faith on authority their lead misdescribed to them. The final sentence rewards equal care for a different occasion: the delegation is *personal* and lapses with the postholder — meaning that on your promotion or departure, your team's entire approval chain suspends until renewal, a transition detail the departing lead is precisely the person responsible for flagging. Reading instruments of authority exactly is not pedantry at your level; it is the protection you owe the people acting on your account.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Inference from a ministerial commitment**

Passage (extract from a minister's written answer to Parliament): "The department is committed to ensuring that no citizen is left behind by the transition to digital services. Assisted digital support will be available for every major service by next April. We expect the great majority of transactions to be digital by 2028, and the department keeps the balance of provision under continuous review."

Which inference is best supported?

- A) The department has committed to keeping non-digital channels open until 2028.
- B) By next April, assisted digital support is committed for every major service; beyond that, the passage commits to review, not to any particular channel provision.
- C) After 2028, non-digital channels will close.
- D) The commitment to assisted digital support extends to every service the department runs.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Ministerial language is drafted to be quotable as more than it commits, and the question tests whether you can separate the two. The hard commitment: assisted digital support for *every major service* by next April — dated, scoped, checkable. Everything else is soft: "no citizen left behind" is aspiration without mechanism; "we expect the great majority… by 2028" is a forecast, not a promise; "keeps under continuous review" commits to reviewing, which is compatible with any outcome. Option A converts the 2028 *expectation* into a channel guarantee no sentence makes. Option C converts the same expectation into a closure plan — reading the forecast's shadow as its content. Option D drops the scope word: *major* services, not all services — one adjective, and the difference between the commitment made and a commitment considerably more expensive. B is precisely calibrated, and its workplace value is direct: when a unit head asks "so are we obliged to keep the phone line?", the accurate answer — "the binding commitment is assisted digital for major services by April; channel provision beyond that is under review, so the obligation may change" — is the one that neither over-binds your organisation nor over-promises to its users. Ensuring commitments are *appropriately communicated*, your role-level phrase, means transmitting exactly their scope: the aspiration as aspiration, the forecast as forecast, and the commitment as the only thing you call one.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Argument evaluation in an escalated dispute**

Passage (from a service owner, disputing a unit's escalation): "The unit claims our release process is 'blocking their business change'. In fact, our process approved 94% of their release requests this year. The three rejections all failed security checks — the same checks every unit's releases face. The unit's real problem is that it submits requests late and understaffed for remediation: its average remediation time after a failed check is 6 weeks, against a departmental average of 2. Our process is not the blocker; their resourcing is."

Which is the strongest evaluation of the service owner's argument?

- A) The argument is conclusive: 94% approval proves the process is not a blocker.
- B) The argument has real force — high approval rate, uniform checks, slow unit remediation — but its conclusion overreaches: approval *rate* says nothing about approval *speed*, and if the unit's complaint is about elapsed time in the process rather than rejection frequency, the cited evidence does not touch it. The arbitration needs cycle-time data before either account is accepted.
- C) The argument fails because it is self-interested; the service owner would say this.
- D) The argument fails because 94% means 6% of releases were blocked, which confirms the unit's claim.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** As arbiter, your first task is to identify what each side's evidence *can* establish. The service owner's data genuinely damages one version of the unit's claim: rejections are rare (94% approval), applied uniformly (same checks for everyone), and the unit's own remediation is three times slower than average — a real finding about the unit's resourcing. But examine what "blocking" most plausibly means in a business-change complaint: *time*, not refusal. A process can approve 94% of requests and still take six weeks to do it; nothing in the passage speaks to elapsed time from submission to approval — the one measure that would settle the actual dispute. B names both the force and the gap, and specifies the missing evidence: cycle-time data, requested from both sides, before arbitration. Option A accepts a rate as proof about a duration — the argument's own category error, ratified. Option C is the genetic fallacy: self-interest is a reason for scrutiny, never a refutation; the 94% figure is checkable regardless of who cites it. Option D reads 6% rejection as vindication when three security-failed releases in a year, remediated slowly, is closer to evidence *against* the unit. The arbitration pattern this trains — and your skill set names arbitration explicitly: each party will bring true evidence for the version of the dispute they can win; the arbiter's craft is defining the question precisely enough (blocked *how*? measured *by what*?) that the evidence which decides it can be identified, demanded, and read without allegiance.

**Question 7 (moderate) — True/False/Cannot say with survey evidence**

Passage (from your team's annual report draft): "The stakeholder survey achieved 210 responses from a distribution of 300 (70%). Of respondents, 78% agreed that 'the BRM function understands our business priorities', up from 71% last year. Respondents from units with a dedicated BRM agreed at 85%; respondents from units sharing a BRM agreed at 62%. Verbatim comments in shared-BRM units most frequently referenced 'not knowing who to contact'."

Statement: "Giving every unit a dedicated BRM would raise agreement in the currently shared units to about 85%."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage establishes an association: dedicated-BRM units agree at 85%, shared-BRM units at 62%, and the verbatims suggest a mechanism (contact ambiguity) consistent with the sharing arrangement causing some of the gap. But the statement makes a *causal prediction with a magnitude*: convert the shared units and their agreement rises "to about 85%". Three inferential steps separate the data from that claim, and the passage supports none of them fully: allocation is not random (units given dedicated BRMs may differ — larger, more strategically central, easier to serve); the mechanism evidence is suggestive, not quantified; and even a true causal effect need not equal the observed cross-sectional gap. Not True. But not False either — nothing in the passage contradicts the prediction; it may even be roughly right. Cannot say, and the discipline of saying so is the point. Note the drafting hazard for your own report: placing the 85%/62% split beside the verbatims *invites* every reader to draw the statement's conclusion without your ever asserting it — which is either a manipulation or an accident, and a lead signs off neither knowingly nor negligently. The honest construction names the association, offers the mechanism as hypothesis, and proposes the test (pilot dedicated coverage in two shared units; measure the change). Directing analysis includes directing what its write-up *implies*, because senior readers act on implications as readily as on claims.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Clear-writing judgement: the escalation response**

A director has escalated to you in writing, copying your director, claiming your function "sat on" her unit's investment proposal for two months. Records show: her team submitted on 3 June missing the mandatory benefits section; your BRM requested it on 5 June and twice since; the completed submission arrived 28 July; the next available forum is 14 August. Which reply is best?

- A) "Thank you for your email. There are always two sides to these situations, and I think it would be unhelpful to get into the details of who did what when. Let's put this behind us and focus on the future."
- B) "Your team submitted an incomplete proposal on 3 June and ignored three requests for the benefits section until 28 July. The delay is entirely of your unit's making, as the attached email trail proves. I would appreciate a correction to your characterisation, given who was copied."
- C) "Thank you for raising this — the timeline matters, so here it is: initial submission 3 June without the mandatory benefits section; our requests of 5 June, 19 June and 10 July; completed submission 28 July; booked for the next forum, 14 August. Two things from us: we should have escalated to you sooner when the section didn't arrive — we'll do that in future. And to keep momentum, my team can pre-review the completed pack this week so 14 August is a decision, not a discussion. Happy to talk today if useful."
- D) "I have asked my team for a full report on the handling of your proposal and will respond substantively once I have it, which I expect within ten working days."

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Judge each reply by what it must do simultaneously: correct a false record (a "sat on it" claim is now in front of your director), preserve a senior relationship, and move the actual work. C does all three, in a deliberate order: gratitude without concession; the timeline as bare dated facts — which refutes "sat on" completely without one adversarial adjective, because facts sequenced are more devastating and more diplomatic than accusations; then the disarming move that distinguishes leadership writing — owning the one thing your side could have done better (earlier escalation), which costs nothing, converts the exchange from blame to improvement, and makes the correction almost impossible to resent; then forward value (pre-review; a decision on the 14th); then an offer of voice contact, the de-escalation channel writing cannot be. Option A abandons the record — with your director copied, silence ratifies "sat on it" as history, and it will be quoted at the next escalation. Option B wins the argument and loses everything else: "ignored", "entirely of your unit's making", "given who was copied" — each phrase is accurate enough and relationship-terminal, teaching every observer that escalating to you triggers retaliation-by-email-trail. Option D converts a two-minute records check into a ten-day process, leaving the false claim standing all the while — proceduralism as avoidance. The template C instantiates is worth keeping: *facts dated, one thing owned, value forward, voice offered* — the four-part reply that corrects the record while enlarging the relationship, which is the entire verbal craft of being a point of escalation.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Reading a joint commitment with a buried asymmetry**

Passage (from a draft memorandum of understanding with another department): "Both departments commit to sharing service performance data quarterly. Department X will provide its data in the agreed common format. Department Y will provide its data in the agreed common format where its systems permit, and otherwise in its native format with a mapping document. Either department may pause sharing for security reasons, giving notice where practicable. This memorandum does not create legally binding obligations."

You lead engagement for Department X. Which reading should most inform your advice on signing?

- A) The MoU is balanced: both departments commit to quarterly sharing in the common format.
- B) The MoU is asymmetric in two places — Y's format obligation is qualified by "where its systems permit" while X's is absolute, and the security pause requires notice only "where practicable" — and the whole instrument is non-binding; advice should weigh whether X is content to be firmly committed in practice while Y retains discretion at both the format and continuity level.
- C) The MoU is unacceptable and should be rejected because it is not legally binding.
- D) The asymmetries are irrelevant because MoUs are never enforced anyway.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Read the two parties' obligations side by side, clause by clause — the comparative reading that drafts like this quietly discourage by describing the parties in separate sentences. X's format obligation: absolute ("will provide… in the agreed common format"). Y's: self-qualified ("where its systems permit, and otherwise… native format with a mapping document") — a qualification whose trigger *Y's own systems* control, making Y's obligation, in practice, optional at Y's discretion. The pause clause: available to both, but its notice requirement is softened to "where practicable" — again a self-judged standard. And the final sentence removes legal force from the whole. B assembles the asymmetries into the advice that matters: this is an instrument under which X is reputationally committed to full performance while Y retains graceful exits at two levels — which may still be worth signing (non-binding data-sharing MoUs often are; the relationship, not the enforcement, is the mechanism), but only with eyes open, and ideally after negotiating a review clause or a sunset on Y's format qualification. Option A is the single-pass reading the drafting invites. Option C over-corrects: non-binding status is standard for inter-departmental MoUs, not a defect — rejecting on that ground alone mistakes the genre. Option D is cynicism dressed as sophistication: unenforced instruments still allocate *expectations*, and expectations are what your function will be held to at the first quarterly exchange Y misses. The lead-level reading habit: for any bilateral text, build the two-column table of who-must-do-what, and read every qualifier for *who controls its trigger* — because the party that writes its own escape conditions has, in the ways that matter, not committed.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Synthesis across three sources in an escalated conflict**

Source 1 (your BRM's escalation note): "Unit F's head refuses to attend the shared-platform onboarding sessions and told me the platform 'will never work for casework'. I've tried three times. Recommend we escalate to his director."
Source 2 (email from Unit F's head to you, same week): "Your colleague is enthusiastic, but each session she has offered clashes with our statutory reporting deadlines — the 1st and 15th of each month are impossible for my team, as I've said each time. On the platform: our casework requires field-level audit trails, and nobody has yet shown me the platform has them. Show me that, and we'll engage."
Source 3 (platform team's feature list): "Field-level audit logging: available, enabled on request per unit; not included in standard onboarding demo."

Which synthesis best serves your next action?

- A) Source 1 is right: three refusals constitute obstruction, and director-level escalation is warranted.
- B) Source 2 reframes everything: the "refusals" have a stated scheduling cause (statutory deadlines colliding with offered dates) and the "never work" position has a stated technical condition (field-level audit trails) — which Source 3 shows the platform meets but does not demonstrate by default. The blocker is two information gaps, not a resistant stakeholder: schedule around the 1st/15th, and have the platform team demo audit logging to Unit F. Coach your BRM on surfacing conditions behind refusals before recommending escalation.
- C) The sources conflict irreconcilably; convene all parties to establish whose account is true.
- D) Source 3 settles it: the feature exists, so Unit F's objection was baseless and the escalation should note this.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Lay the three accounts side by side and notice that they do not actually conflict — they interlock. The BRM reports refusals (true: three sessions declined); the unit head supplies the causes the BRM's note omits (dates colliding with statutory deadlines he says he stated each time; a concrete technical condition — field-level audit trails — that nobody has addressed); the feature list resolves the technical condition in the platform's favour, with the telling detail that the capability is *not in the standard demo* — explaining how three onboarding attempts could fail to surface the one fact that would unlock engagement. B converts the synthesis into the two cheap actions that dissolve the "conflict": schedule off the 1st/15th, and demo the audit logging. Option A escalates a scheduling clash and an unanswered question to a director — spending your escalation credibility on a problem two emails can fix, and branding as obstructive a stakeholder whose stated condition was reasonable and, it turns out, satisfiable. Option D weaponises Source 3 — "the feature exists so the objection was baseless" — when the objection was "nobody has *shown* me", which remains true and remains the platform team's omission to repair; arriving with vindication where a demo was requested converts a winnable stakeholder into a justified critic. Option C convenes a truth commission where no facts are disputed. The final element of B is the one your role makes distinctive: the coaching. Your BRM's note recorded outcomes ("refuses") without conditions ("impossible dates", "show me audit trails") — the exact information that turns refusals into requirements — and the development conversation ("before recommending escalation, write down what the stakeholder said would change their answer") upgrades the whole team's escalation quality. Enabling others to improve relationship maturity, as your role summary puts it, happens in precisely these margins between an escalation note and what actually occurred.

**Question 11 (challenging) — What your own drafting commits you to**

You are finalising your function's commitment in the directorate's published engagement charter. Legal and practical review will hold you to whatever ships. Which drafting is best?

- A) "The BRM function will resolve all stakeholder issues within 10 working days."
- B) "The BRM function will always be available to support any business need, whatever it takes."
- C) "The BRM function will acknowledge stakeholder issues within 2 working days, provide a named owner and a first substantive response within 10, and where resolution requires other teams, will coordinate actively and keep the stakeholder informed at least fortnightly until resolution."
- D) "The BRM function will endeavour, where reasonably practicable and subject to competing priorities, to respond to stakeholder issues in a timely manner appropriate to the circumstances."

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A published charter commitment is a text you will be *measured against*, so the drafting question is: which promises are entirely within your function's control, and which mortgage you to circumstances or other teams? Audit each option on that axis. Option A promises *resolution* in 10 days — but resolution routinely depends on suppliers, other teams, and problem complexity; the first data-quality issue that takes six weeks makes the charter a public record of your failure, however well your function performed its own part. Option B is generosity as drafting error: "always", "any", "whatever it takes" are unbounded words that convert enthusiasm into unlimited liability, and unbounded commitments are discounted by sophisticated readers precisely because they cannot be true. Option D is the opposite failure: so upholstered with escape language ("endeavour", "where reasonably practicable", "subject to", "appropriate to the circumstances") that it commits to nothing measurable — and stakeholders read such drafting accurately, as a function protecting itself from its own charter; it damages trust at publication, before any issue is even raised. Option C commits only what your function controls — acknowledgement (2 days), named ownership, first substantive response (10 days), coordination behaviour and communication cadence when others hold the fix — and each element is checkable, achievable, and honest about the dependency structure of real resolution. This is the drafting version of a principle running through your whole role: ambitious about what you own, explicit about what you don't, and never promising other people's performance. The charter your team can proudly meet builds more relationship capital than the charter that impresses on publication day and indicts by March.

### Preparation tips

- **Build the two-column table for any bilateral text.** Who must do what, under which qualifier, triggered by whose judgement. Five minutes of tabulation exposes asymmetries that five readings of flowing prose will miss.
- **Attach every qualifier to its noun.** "Optional", "major", "where practicable", "regardless of value" — each modifies something specific. Most senior-level errors are attachment errors: the right qualifier applied to the wrong obligation.
- **Separate the commitment from its packaging.** In ministerial, executive and partner text, isolate the dated, scoped, checkable promises from the aspirations and forecasts around them. Practise on published written answers — they are the ideal training corpus.
- **Read escalations for the request, not the accusation.** Ask of any escalation text: what does the sender actually need to be able to do or say? Answer that, and most of the heat evaporates.
- **Draft to the four-part template under pressure.** Facts dated, one thing owned, value forward, voice offered. Rehearse it until it is your reflexive shape for difficult replies.
- **Audit your own function's texts.** Take your current charter, dashboard commentary, or delegation note and test each sentence: is this checkable? Do we control it? Which reader will quote it against us, and when? The exercise is verbal-reasoning practice with compound interest.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Partial-clause reading.** Concessions come with provisos, delegations with overrides, commitments with scope words. Stopping at the sentence that helps your reading — or your stakeholder's — is the most common and most expensive error at this level.
- **Ratifying a category error.** An approval *rate* offered as proof about *speed*, an annual average against a quarterly regime, a feature's existence against a demand to be shown it: when evidence answers a different question than the dispute asks, name the mismatch — do not adjudicate on it.
- **Importing hostility or reassurance the text doesn't carry.** Escalations read angrier than they are; partner letters read friendlier than they operate. Read the moves, not the music.
- **Converting signals into commitments — in either direction.** "We expect", "we would welcome", "in principle" are real information and no promise. Brief them as what they are, and draft your own signals with the same discipline, knowing others will try to harden them.
- **Winning the record and losing the room.** In writing-choice questions and real replies, the option that proves the other party wrong most forcefully is almost never the effective one. Facts sequenced beat adjectives deployed.
- **Signing drafting you would not want quoted.** Every charter line, MoU clause and dashboard sentence you approve will eventually be read back to you by someone with an interest. The test — and the job — reward the reader who hears that future quotation before signing.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment (SJT) presents realistic workplace dilemmas and asks you to judge the effectiveness of possible responses — most effective, most and least effective, rankings, or independent ratings against an effectiveness scale. Scoring keys are built with experienced practitioners and occupational psychologists; there is rarely a flawless answer, and the differentiation comes from consistently preferring responses that are active, honest, developmental and systemic over those that are passive, political, self-protective or merely fast.

Typical format: 15 to 25 scenarios in 30 to 45 minutes, often untimed. Leadership-normed SJTs are distinguished by three features. The dilemmas are second-order: not "what do I do?" but "what do I do, given that my team, my peers, my seniors and external partners will all learn from what I do?" The goods in tension are all legitimate: candour and confidentiality, pace and inclusion, an individual's growth and a stakeholder's experience, this quarter's outcome and the culture that produces every future quarter's outcomes. And the strongest options usually operate at two levels at once — resolving the instance while adjusting the system, the person, or the precedent that generated it.

For a lead business relationship manager, the SJT is effectively a simulation of the role's hardest hours. Your role summary is a catalogue of judgement work: leading engagement with internal and external stakeholders; acting as the point of escalation when relationships break down or become too challenging for junior team members; influencing important senior stakeholders and arbitrating when blockers are escalated; facilitating discussions across high-risk areas under constrained timelines; setting conditions for a culture of positive impact; enabling others to improve relationship maturity. None of these can be performed by procedure; all of them are performed in moments of choice under ambiguity, which is precisely what an SJT samples. Employers use it at this level because leadership failure is rarely technical — it is judgemental, relational and cultural, and those qualities are visible in how a candidate ranks four defensible responses to an impossible morning.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Escalation and arbitration scenarios** map to **Stakeholder relationship management (IT operations)** at your level: acting as the point of escalation, influencing senior stakeholders, arbitrating blockers — the key rewards even-handed process, evidence-demanding arbitration, and resolutions that leave both parties working with you again.
- **Team development scenarios** map to your duty to **enable others to implement activities that improve relationship maturity** and to leading a team of BRMs: the key rewards responses that grow judgement in others — even at the cost of speed — over responses that substitute your own competence.
- **Culture-setting scenarios** map to **setting conditions that enable a culture of positive impact**: your visible choices under pressure *are* the conditions; the key rewards choices you would be content to see copied.
- **Strategic commitment scenarios** map to ensuring **spending reviews and services strategy commitments are met and appropriately communicated**: the key rewards protecting commitments and the honesty of what is communicated about them, especially when the two collide.
- **External partner scenarios** map to leading engagement with **external stakeholders**: the key rewards firmness on entitlements and evidence, paired with investment in the long-term relationship — neither capture nor combat.
- **Ownership scenarios** map to **Ownership and topic**: taking accountability for issues that occur — including inherited, ambiguous and cross-boundary ones — and proactively hunting problems, at a scale where "owning" usually means building the ownership structure rather than doing the work.

### Practice questions

For each scenario, choose the option indicated (most effective, or most and least effective as stated).

**Question 1 (easy) — The escalation your BRM should have kept**

A BRM on your team forwards you an email chain with the note: "Unit K's head is refusing to prioritise the data cleansing work — can you take this one?" Reading the chain, you find a routine disagreement about sequencing, politely expressed, that the BRM has not yet discussed with the unit head by phone or in person — only email. What is the most effective response?

- A) Take the escalation; unit heads respond faster to leads, and the work matters.
- B) Return it with coaching: talk through with the BRM what an actual conversation (not email) might surface about the unit head's constraints, help them plan it, and make clear you are available if a real impasse remains after that conversation.
- C) Decline the escalation and tell the BRM that escalations require a completed escalation form documenting attempts at resolution.
- D) Email the unit head yourself, copying the BRM, asking him to engage with the BRM's sequencing proposal.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Your skill set makes you the point of escalation for relationships that "break down or become challenging" — and the leadership judgement is recognising that this one is neither: it is a sequencing disagreement, politely held, that has not yet met a synchronous conversation. Taking it (option A) would be faster today and corrosive by quarter's end: each absorbed escalation teaches your team that difficulty is transferable, migrates their judgement to your desk, and — the quieter cost — tells unit heads that the person worth talking to is always you, hollowing out your BRMs' standing in their own portfolios. B is the developmental move: the coaching question ("what might a conversation surface that email hasn't?") upgrades this BRM's next hundred disagreements, the planning help makes success likely, and the explicit backstop ("if a real impasse remains, I'm in") keeps the safety net that makes stretch feel safe. That is enabling others, in your role summary's exact sense. Option C reaches the right outcome through the wrong instrument — process as a wall; the form teaches compliance, the conversation teaches craft, and a team led by forms escalates *less honestly*, not less often. Option D is escalation-by-stealth: it takes the issue over while appearing not to, and the unit head now knows the BRM's emails carry an implicit cc to power. The pattern the key rewards at lead level: the first question about any escalation is not "how do I resolve this?" but "whose growth does resolving this belong to?"

**Question 2 (easy) — The culture moment in the team meeting**

In your team meeting, a BRM describes "winning" a dispute with a difficult unit: she quietly gathered examples of the unit's process failures over a month, then presented the dossier at a governance board, where the unit head was "finally shown up". Two junior team members look admiring. The dispute's substantive outcome was correct — the unit's process genuinely needed the change. What is the most effective response?

- A) Congratulate her on the outcome; the method is secondary to being right.
- B) Say nothing in the meeting but raise the method privately with her later.
- C) Acknowledge the outcome genuinely, then open up the method in the meeting: ask what the unit head's working relationship with us looks like now, contrast the dossier-ambush with early sharing of concerns, and set the norm explicitly — we surface problems with partners before we surface them at boards; then reinforce it privately with her.
- D) Publicly rebuke the approach so the two admiring juniors are left in no doubt.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** This is a culture-setting scenario in its purest form — your role summary's "set conditions that enable a culture of positive impact" made concrete in ninety seconds of meeting time. The complicating fact is that the outcome was *right*, which is exactly how ambush methods propagate: success launders technique, and two juniors are currently converting this story into their model of how disputes are won. C works at every required level: the genuine acknowledgement keeps the BRM (whose diagnosis was correct and whose effort was real) inside the conversation rather than defending herself; the opened question — "what does the relationship look like now?" — lets the team *reason* to the cost (a unit head publicly blindsided will now share nothing early, with any of them, for years) instead of receiving a ruling; the explicit norm ("problems go to partners before boards") converts the moment into standing culture; and the private follow-up gives the individual coaching its proper channel. Option A endorses the method the moment it worked — and your silence in front of admirers is endorsement. Option B protects the individual but abandons the audience: the two juniors leave the meeting with the lesson intact, and culture is what the audience takes away. Option D buys the norm at the price of the person — public rebuke teaches the team that meetings are dangerous, which suppresses exactly the honest storytelling that let you catch this. The lead pattern: when a good outcome arrives by a bad method, honour the outcome, unpack the method with the audience that witnessed it, and set the norm while the example is warm.

**Question 3 (easy) — The commitment slipping quietly**

Your directorate committed in the services strategy to onboarding all 20 major services to the new support model by December. It is September; 11 are onboarded; the onboarding team privately tells you the realistic year-end number is 15–16, but their programme board reporting still shows "Green — on track for 20", because "the gap might still close". Your role includes ensuring services strategy commitments are met and appropriately communicated. What is the most effective response?

- A) Leave the reporting to the onboarding team; the commitment is theirs to manage and December has not arrived.
- B) Press the team to work faster, since the commitment must be met as stated.
- C) Get the honest trajectory onto the table now: work with the onboarding lead to re-baseline the report (15–16 by December, 20 by a stated date, with causes), brief the affected service owners and the strategy's executive sponsor before the next board, and turn the remaining gap into a resourced recovery plan rather than a December surprise.
- D) Report the discrepancy to the programme board yourself, so the record is corrected by someone outside the team.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Two commitments are in play, and your role phrase — "met *and appropriately communicated*" — holds both: the delivery commitment (20 services) and the truthfulness of what the organisation is told about it. The team's "might still close" Green is how December surprises are manufactured: every reporting cycle that carries known-optimistic status forward converts an honest slippage into a concealed one, and the concealment — not the shortfall — is what destroys credibility when it lands. C acts on both commitments in the right order: the re-baseline makes the communication honest *with* the team that owns the delivery (option D's correction *around* them would be a relationship-breaking move held in reserve for refusal, not a first move); the pre-board briefings mean the sponsor and service owners hear the truth from the function that partners them, with causes and a plan, rather than discovering it — and stakeholders forgive slippage far more readily than surprise; and the recovery plan converts the residual gap from an embarrassment into a resourcing decision the executive can actually take. Option A outsources your named accountability to the team least positioned to declare its own bad news. Option B mistakes pressure for management: if 15–16 is the realistic ceiling, "work faster" produces either corner-cutting on the onboarding quality or a deeper concealment — and addresses nothing about the false Green. The pattern: bad trajectory plus honest early communication is a manageable event; good-looking reports plus December is a crisis. Leads are paid to choose the first while it is still available.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Most and least effective: the poached commitment**

At a cross-directorate board, a peer director announces that "the BRM function has agreed" his directorate's new system will be prioritised in next quarter's delivery slate. No such agreement exists: one of your BRMs had an exploratory conversation with his team, which he has now converted into a public commitment. Contradicting him flatly at the board will embarrass him; staying silent creates a phantom commitment your function cannot honour and displaces genuinely prioritised work. Identify the **most** and **least** effective responses.

1. Stay silent at the board, then have the record corrected by emailing the secretariat a clarification afterwards.
2. In the meeting, without heat: "Let me add context — our teams have had a useful early conversation, and I want to be accurate about where it stands: exploration, not yet agreement. The prioritisation route is [the forum]; I'll make sure this proposal gets a fast, fair passage there, and [director] and I can align this week on what it needs."
3. Contradict directly: "That is not correct — no agreement exists, and this is not how prioritisation works."
4. Let the claim stand and work afterwards to make it true, reshuffling next quarter's slate to accommodate the system.

- A) Most effective: 2; Least effective: 4
- B) Most effective: 2; Least effective: 3
- C) Most effective: 1; Least effective: 4
- D) Most effective: 4; Least effective: 3

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The phantom commitment must die in the room where it was born — boards act on what stands unchallenged in boards, and a correction emailed to a secretariat (response 1) arrives after the impression has hardened and reads, to everyone who was present, as the version you lacked the standing to say aloud; worse, several attendees may never see it. Response 2 kills the claim while building three things: accuracy ("exploration, not yet agreement" — the precise status, stated as precision rather than accusation, giving the director a face-saving ladder: he can agree that's where it stands); process integrity (the prioritisation forum named as the route, protecting every unit whose work would have been silently displaced); and the relationship (a fast, fair passage plus a this-week alignment offer converts the director from cornered to courted). Response 4 is least effective — beneath its accommodating surface it is the worst outcome on every axis: your function's actual prioritisation (presumably evidence-based) is overturned by whoever claims most boldly in public; the director learns that announcing agreements *manufactures* them, a lesson he will reuse; the displaced units' trust in the forum evaporates when they discover how the slate really moved; and your BRM never learns what their "exploratory conversation" became. Ranking the middle pair: response 3 achieves accuracy at maximum relational cost — flat public contradiction humiliates, and humiliated directors litigate forever — but it at least leaves truth and process standing; response 1 leaves neither reliably standing. So 2 over 3 over 1 over 4. The lead pattern: correct public inaccuracies publicly, gently, and with a path forward attached — and afterwards, coach the BRM on the difference between exploring and agreeing, because the phantom was born in that gap.

**Question 5 (moderate) — The two seniors and the constrained timeline**

You are facilitating a high-risk discussion: the finance director and the operations director must jointly agree, by Thursday's submission deadline, which of two incompatible options goes into the spending review — Option 1 (finance's preference: consolidate regional processing into two centres, larger savings, significant staff impact) or Option 2 (operations' preference: modernise in place, smaller savings, minimal disruption). It is Tuesday. Both directors have entrenched publicly; each has separately told you the other "doesn't understand the real constraints". A third session together seems likely to re-run the deadlock. What is the most effective facilitation move?

- A) Run the third joint session anyway, but open it by presenting your own recommendation between the two options, to give the discussion a concrete anchor.
- B) Meet each director separately today with one task each: not "argue your option" but "state the conditions under which you could live with the other's" — then build Wednesday's joint session around the two conditions lists, which usually reveal a composite or sequenced option (e.g. modernise-in-place now with a costed consolidation trigger at defined volume thresholds) that neither pure option allowed.
- C) Escalate on Tuesday to the director general who owns both directors, presenting both options and asking for a decision by Wednesday.
- D) Draft the submission with both options included and a note that agreement could not be reached, protecting the deadline.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** "Facilitate discussions across high risk and complex areas or projects under constrained timelines" — your skill set's sentence, and this scenario is its examination. The diagnostic fact is that positional argument has already failed twice; a third re-run (option A's frame) re-runs it, and A's specific addition — the facilitator tabling their own recommendation — spends your neutrality, the one asset that lets both directors keep talking through you; whichever director your recommendation disfavours now has two opponents. B changes the *question*, which is what unlocks entrenched negotiations: "conditions under which you could live with theirs" converts advocates into specifiers — finance's conditions will reveal that the real requirement is the savings trajectory (not the consolidation per se); operations' will reveal that the real constraint is workforce transition risk (not modernisation per se) — and conditions lists compose in ways positions cannot: the sequenced option B sketches (modernise now, consolidation triggered at volume thresholds, transition provisions pre-agreed) gives each director the thing their conditions actually protect. Wednesday's session then negotiates specifics, not identities. Option C may eventually be needed, but on Tuesday it is premature surrender of your function's core value — and a DG handed a binary by their two directors will often bounce it back ("you two agree"), consuming the buffer day. Option D meets the deadline and fails the assignment: an unagreed pair of options invites the spending review team to choose, transferring the decision from the people who understand the constraints to the people who understand the spreadsheet. The facilitation pattern worth keeping: when positions deadlock, stop collecting arguments and start collecting *conditions* — the composite option is usually already present, distributed across the two lists, waiting for someone neutral to assemble it.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Most and least effective: the partner's overreach on your data**

Your external delivery partner's account director sends you their draft "Partnership Success Report", which they intend to publish on their website and circulate to other government departments. It uses your organisation's satisfaction data (shared for joint service improvement), describes results with claims like "transformed the department's operations", and includes a supportive quote attributed to your director — who, on checking, was never asked. Identify the **most** and **least** effective responses.

1. Reply formally that the data was shared for improvement purposes only, that publication is not authorised, that the attributed quote must be removed, and that the partnership's data-sharing terms are now under review.
2. Call the account director: state plainly that the draft cannot proceed — unauthorised data use and an unapproved quote are serious — and that you want to understand how it happened; then offer the legitimate path: a jointly drafted case study, approved through both organisations' processes, with claims the evidence supports; and log the incident with your commercial team so the pattern is visible if it repeats.
3. Approve the report with light edits; partner marketing that praises the work reflects well on your directorate too, and the relationship benefits from generosity.
4. Say nothing to the partner, but warn your director about the fabricated quote so they are not blindsided.

- A) Most effective: 2; Least effective: 3
- B) Most effective: 1; Least effective: 4
- C) Most effective: 2; Least effective: 4
- D) Most effective: 1; Least effective: 3

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Rank by what the situation actually contains: a genuine boundary violation (data repurposed beyond its sharing basis; a quote invented for a named director) inside a relationship you are charged with leading long-term. Response 2 holds the boundary at full strength — "cannot proceed", named seriousness, commercial logging so a pattern becomes visible — while doing the three things that distinguish leading an external relationship from merely policing one: the phone call (voice, not letterhead, for a first serious conversation — it permits the account director to explain, and explanations matter: an overeager marketing team is a different problem from a deliberate strategy); the diagnosis question ("how did this happen?"), which tells you whether you are correcting a process or a partner; and the legitimate path (a joint, approved, evidence-bounded case study), which redirects the partner's real and reasonable interest — showcasing good work — into a channel that serves you both. That conversion of a violation into a governed opportunity is the relationship-leadership move. Response 3 is least effective: approving normalises unauthorised data use, endorses a fabricated quote (a integrity line no marketing benefit repays), and teaches this partner — and, once the report circulates, every partner who sees it — that your organisation's data and names are available on an ask-forgiveness basis. Ranking the middle: response 1 gets the substance right but leads with maximum formality and a terms-review threat *before* any conversation — escalation as an opening position, which forecloses the innocent explanation and chills the wider relationship; still, it protects the organisation, which response 4 does not: warning your director while the report proceeds to publication is self-protection wearing diligence's clothes — the harm continues, merely pre-excused. So 2 over 1 over 4 over 3. The pattern: with external partners, hold boundaries at full strength, in person first, with the legitimate version of their interest ready to offer — firmness and generosity are not alternatives; sequenced correctly, they are the same move.

**Question 7 (moderate) — The team member ready before the team believes it**

You plan to give the lead role on next quarter's highest-profile engagement — the spending review coordination with the finance directorate — to a BRM who joined from another department eight months ago. She is, in your judgement, the strongest strategic thinker on the team. Two longer-serving BRMs have separately signalled they expected the role; one has muttered about "favouritism towards new people", and you are aware the team is watching the decision as a signal about how advancement works. What is the most effective way to proceed?

- A) Give the role to the most senior long-serving BRM to protect team cohesion, and give the newer BRM a comparable role next cycle when the optics are easier.
- B) Split the role between the newer BRM and the most aggrieved long-serving one, so both are satisfied.
- C) Make the decision on capability and stand by it: give her the role, tell the two long-serving BRMs directly and separately why — including what specifically would position each of them for the next comparable role — and tell the team the criteria you used, so the decision teaches how advancement here actually works.
- D) Ask the team to discuss and agree among themselves who should take the role, so the outcome carries collective legitimacy.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The decision is watched, which means it is not one staffing choice but a published rule: whatever you do becomes the team's understanding of how roles are earned. C publishes the right rule — capability for the work, legibly applied — and pays, in full, the communication cost that makes a merit decision land as merit rather than favouritism: the aggrieved hear the reasons *directly and separately* (respect), each receives a specific development path to the next comparable role (investment, and the difference between "you lost" and "here is how you win"), and the team hears the criteria (transparency, which is what actually protects cohesion — opacity, not disappointment, is what breeds the favouritism narrative). Option A publishes the wrong rule — tenure beats capability, and grievance signalling works — while committing a quiet double injustice: the strongest person is denied earned work for optics, and the senior BRM is handed a role selected for reasons other than believing they were best, which they will eventually sense. Option B decorates avoidance as compromise: split leadership of a single high-stakes coordination confuses the finance directorate (who need one accountable counterpart), sets both BRMs up to negotiate their seam under pressure, and still publishes a rule — "complaint earns a share". Option D abdicates a leader's least delegable duty; teams asked to allocate prestige among themselves reliably produce either seniority (the safe answer) or a wound, and the leader who asked has spent authority to purchase neither legitimacy nor cohesion. The pattern the key rewards: make people decisions on capability, then spend generously on the losers' development and the team's understanding — the decision costs one afternoon of hard conversations; the wrong rule costs a culture.

**Question 8 (challenging) — The inherited commitment that cannot be met**

Two weeks into leading a newly merged BRM function, you discover a written commitment made by your predecessor to a major delivery unit: dedicated BRM support "at least three days per week" for their transformation programme, for eighteen months. Resourcing has since changed; honouring it would consume a quarter of your merged team's capacity and force you to strip partnering from six smaller units — several already fragile relationships. The delivery unit's director has planned around the commitment, mentions it warmly, and is politically influential. The commitment was real, written, and improvident. What is the most effective response?

- A) Honour the commitment in full: your function's word is its currency, and the reputational cost of retreat exceeds the capacity cost of delivery.
- B) Quietly under-deliver: assign the three days nominally but let the actual support settle toward what capacity allows, relying on delivery-phase busyness to blur the difference.
- C) Go to the director early with the full picture: the commitment was made and you take responsibility for it as the function's commitment; the resourcing that made it possible has changed, and honouring it as written would break partnering for six other units; then propose a renegotiated package built on what the programme actually needs now — perhaps two dedicated days plus senior escalation access and named cover for peaks — and offer transition arrangements while the new package beds in.
- D) Write to the director explaining that commitments made by your predecessor under previous resourcing assumptions cannot bind the merged function, and set out the support level the new structure can offer.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The commitment is real and the capacity is not — a collision leads inherit constantly, and the options map the four classic responses: heroic honouring, quiet erosion, honest renegotiation, and repudiation. A fails on second-order arithmetic: the quarter-of-capacity cost is not abstract — it is six smaller units, several fragile, losing partnering; keeping one written promise by silently breaking six implicit ones optimises the *visible* obligation at the expense of the real portfolio, and the invisible breaches will surface as failed relationships with your name on them. B is the worst option wearing the least drama: it makes the function's word *actually* worthless (A at least keeps it merely expensive) while adding deception, and the director — who has planned around three days — will detect the settling within a quarter and hold both the shortfall and the concealment. D is legalism as relationship management: "cannot bind the merged function" may even be organisationally arguable, but the director heard a commitment from your function and watched you repudiate it by letter; every future undertaking you give now carries an implicit asterisk, and the politically influential hold long ledgers. C is the honest renegotiation done in the correct order: responsibility taken *as the function's* (no predecessor-blaming — you inherited the ledger, not just the desk; this is **Ownership and topic** applied to commitments); the constraint shown in full (six units, real numbers — influential directors respect portfolio arithmetic candidly presented far more than unexplained retreat); and the proposal built on the programme's *current needs* rather than the old promise's shape — because "three days a week" was always a proxy for outcomes the programme wanted, and a package of two dedicated days plus senior escalation access and peak cover may serve those outcomes as well or better. Early matters most of all: renegotiation offered before the director feels the shortfall is partnership; offered after, it is damage control. The lead pattern: inherited improvident commitments are renegotiated openly, early, at the level of the need behind the promise — never honoured into portfolio failure, eroded into distrust, or repudiated into permanent grievance.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Most and least effective: the survey you are asked to soften**

Your function's annual stakeholder survey — which you publish to all participating units as a matter of established practice — this year contains a bruising result: units rate "the digital directorate acts on feedback" at 2.4/5, with verbatims naming two live programmes. Your director, whose directorate this is, asks you to "hold the detailed results back this year — just publish the headline scores — while we get the programme issues sorted; publishing the detail now would damage confidence right when the programmes need support." Identify the **most** and **least** effective responses.

1. Publish everything as usual without further discussion; established practice is the shield against any accusation of manipulation.
2. Agree to publish headlines only, and plan to restore full publication next year once scores recover.
3. Take the concern seriously and propose the version that serves it honestly: publish the full results on schedule *with* a response — each bruising finding paired with the action being taken, dated — and offer the director pre-briefings with the units named in the verbatims; make the case that suppressing detail units know they submitted is discovered quickly and converts a performance problem into a trust problem, whereas publishing-with-response converts it into credibility.
4. Tell the director you will publish in full regardless of their view, since survey integrity is not negotiable.

- A) Most effective: 3; Least effective: 2
- B) Most effective: 1; Least effective: 2
- C) Most effective: 3; Least effective: 4
- D) Most effective: 4; Least effective: 2

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Locate the real interests first: the director's stated concern — programme confidence — is legitimate; the proposed instrument — selective suppression — is the error, and it is an error *against the director's own interest*, which is the lever response 3 uses. Units submitted this feedback; they know what they wrote; a publication that omits the detail they supplied is detected in one conversation between two unit heads, and the discovered suppression does what the 2.4 alone never could — it confirms the score's accusation ("doesn't act on feedback" becomes "doesn't even publish it") and converts a recoverable performance finding into a trust breach with the survey instrument itself, poisoning every future year's data. Response 3 declines the instrument, serves the concern: full publication on schedule, every hard finding paired with a dated action (the *only* message that raises confidence: feedback demonstrably acted on), and pre-briefings for the named units (the directors's political exposure managed by preparation rather than concealment). It also does this as a *case made to* the director — persuasion before defiance — which is why it beats response 4: same integrity, but 4 spends the relationship ("regardless of your view") before attempting the argument that would likely make spending it unnecessary; leads who reach for "non-negotiable" first teach their directors to stop consulting them. Response 2 is least effective — narrowly worse than an unforced 1-versus-2 comparison might suggest, because it adds a *plan to restore* publication "once scores recover", which completes the corruption: publication contingent on results is not transparency but advertising, and the survey your units answer next year is now an instrument they have correctly learned tells the truth only when the truth is flattering. Response 1, though it wins on integrity, fails as leadership: publishing a bruising result *without engaging the director's concern, without response actions, without pre-briefing* maximises the damage the director feared and treats established practice as a substitute for judgement — right on the principle, negligent on the landing. So 3 over 4 over 1 over 2. The pattern, which generalises across every "soften the message" request a lead receives: serve the legitimate concern behind the request with an honest instrument — and make suppression's discovery-cost visible to the person requesting it, because that argument, made early and privately, usually wins.

**Question 10 (challenging) — The blocker who is right too late**

A cross-organisational data platform your function has championed reaches its final approval gate. Every unit has signed off except one: the head of the analysis unit — a rigorous, unpopular figure whose objections have twice delayed the programme — now raises, at the gate itself, a concern about the platform's approach to record-level provenance that would cost eight weeks to address. The programme board's mood is "overrule and proceed"; two directors describe the objection as "obstruction as usual". Reading the technical detail overnight, you conclude the concern is substantially valid — provenance as designed would genuinely limit several promised analytical uses. The programme has momentum, a communicated go-live date, and eighteen months of your function's sponsorship behind it. What is the most effective response?

- A) Support proceeding: the objection arrived at the last responsible moment's far side, the process gave the analysis head earlier opportunities he did not use, and eight weeks of delay damages the platform's credibility more than a provenance limitation the roadmap can fix later.
- B) Support the objection on its merits and say so before the board: the concern is valid, the eight weeks buys capability the platform promised, proceeding knowingly limited converts a delay into a broken promise; pair this with naming the process failure — a concern this fundamental surfacing at the gate means earlier reviews failed to draw it out — and commission that fix alongside; then work the room before the vote, because the board must not learn the merits from the meeting's most unpopular voice alone.
- C) Propose proceeding to go-live as scheduled while a parallel workstream addresses provenance, satisfying both the date and the concern.
- D) Abstain from the gate decision, noting your function's role is engagement rather than technical adjudication, and facilitate the board toward its own conclusion.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Strip the scenario to its load-bearing fact: *you have concluded the objection is valid*. Everything else — the objector's unpopularity, the lateness, the momentum, your function's eighteen months of sponsorship — is pressure to weigh a true technical finding by its social packaging, and the question is whether you can keep the merits and the politics in their proper registers. A reasons entirely in the second register: lateness and credibilities are real costs, but "the process gave him opportunities" does not repair record-level provenance, and a platform that goes live *knowingly* unable to support several promised analytical uses converts those promises into discovered breaches on *your* sponsorship — the credibility you are protecting is spent either way, and A spends it on concealment. C is the seductive option and the trap the question is built around: "proceed now, fix provenance in parallel" founders on the technical shape of the problem — provenance is a foundational data-model property; retrofitting it after go-live, under live-service constraints, against accumulating records created without it, is not eight weeks but a multiple of eight weeks, which is precisely why the concern is gate-worthy now. Boards love option C because it defers all pain; leads must recognise when "parallel workstream" is a synonym for "never". D is abdication dressed as humility: your function championed the platform — you are not a neutral facilitator of this gate, and the board is about to make a technically wrong call partly *because* the correct argument is carried by its least credible messenger; withholding your judgement is not neutrality, it is allowing messenger-credibility to decide a merits question. B does the whole job: the merits called correctly and publicly (with your function's credibility transferred to the argument — which is exactly what sponsorship credibility is *for*); the delay honestly priced as buying promised capability; the process failure named and fixed (a valid fundamental concern surfacing only at the gate is an assurance defect, and fixing it is what prevents the next one); and — the senior-craft detail — the room worked beforehand, because a board that first meets a correct argument in the mouth of "obstruction as usual" will vote against the messenger. Note the culture dividend, which is the deepest reason B is right: the organisation watches what happens when the unpopular rigorous person is correct. If he is overruled *while being right*, every future concern from every rigorous voice arrives softer, later, or not at all — and the platform after this one inherits that silence. The lead pattern: judge arguments separately from their messengers, spend your credibility on merits the room cannot yet hear, and treat "right but late" as two problems — act on the rightness, fix the lateness.

**Question 11 (challenging) — The quiet signal about your own function**

At a leadership away-day, a director general you trust mentions, almost in passing: "Between us — the efficiency review is going to look hard at whether the BRM function justifies its cost. Nothing's decided. I'd get your evidence in order." You lead that function. Your team does not know; morale is good; the annual stakeholder survey is about to launch; and two of your BRMs are finalists for roles in other departments. What is the most effective response over the following fortnight?

- A) Tell the team at the next team meeting exactly what you were told, so they hear it from you rather than through rumour, and can contribute to the evidence-gathering.
- B) Say nothing to anyone and begin quietly assembling the function's value evidence yourself, ready for whenever the review formally engages.
- C) Treat the signal as the planning input it is: commission the value-evidence work now through legitimate framing the team will recognise as good practice anyway — tightening the survey's outcome questions, refreshing the benefits attribution for partnered investments, documenting the escalations resolved and duplications prevented — brief your director that you are doing it and why; say nothing speculative to the team, but act on the two flight-risk finalists *now* on their own merits: honest career conversations about their value and their path here, which serve retention whatever the review concludes.
- D) Go back to the DG, ask for specifics about the review's scope and timing, and request that the BRM function be given formal notice and a fair opportunity to make its case before any conclusions form.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Calibrate the signal first: "nothing's decided", passed in confidence, is an *early warning*, not a formal process — and each option mishandles that calibration differently except C. Option A converts a confidence into a team announcement: it breaches the DG's trust (ending such signals permanently — and a lead's early-warning network is among their most valuable assets), and it detonates precisely the harms the fortnight holds — morale spent on a speculative threat, a survey launched into an anxious function (depressing the very scores the review will read), and two finalists handed the final push toward the exits. Honesty with your team is not maximal disclosure of every unformed possibility; leaders metabolise uncertainty so teams can work — the truth-telling obligation crystallises when the review becomes real. Option B gets the discretion right and the leadership wrong twice: evidence assembled alone is slower and thinner than evidence assembled through the team's own systems, and it leaves the two finalists — the function's most immediately losable value — entirely unaddressed. Option D misreads the register: the DG spoke *informally* precisely because formal engagement isn't available yet; marching the confidence back through official channels ("formal notice", "fair opportunity") converts a favour into a procedural demand, embarrasses its giver, and signals that the BRM lead cannot handle soft information — while gathering not one piece of evidence. C matches the signal's register at every point: the evidence work proceeds at full pace through framings that are *true* (outcome-tightened surveys, benefits attribution, escalation and duplication records are good practice a mature function should hold regardless — which is also why their absence is the actual vulnerability the DG flagged); the director briefing keeps your chain informed without speculation spreading; the team's fortnight — survey launch included — proceeds undamaged; and the finalists get what they should have had anyway: career conversations on their merits, which retain people through *this* review and the next one. Note the meta-lesson, which is why this question closes the set: the best response to "get your evidence in order" is to lead a function whose evidence *is* in order — early warnings are most valuable to leads who need them least, and the steady-state disciplines this guide has rehearsed (honest metrics, attributed benefits, documented value) are what convert a threatening review into a showcase. The pattern: match the register of soft intelligence, act through legitimate framings, protect your people's present, and invest in the retention and evidence you should have been building anyway.

### Preparation tips

- **Read your role summary as the scoring key.** Point of escalation, arbitration, facilitation under constrained timelines, culture-setting, enabling others: the lead-level SJT key is a behavioural translation of exactly these phrases. Recognise them inside the options and the ranking usually resolves itself.
- **Apply the copy test.** For each option ask: what happens if everyone who hears about this response copies it? Leadership scenarios are scored on the precedent as much as the outcome — the answer that resolves today while setting a rule you'd regret is rarely the key.
- **Separate the register from the substance.** Many hard scenarios turn on matching the response's formality to the situation's: confidences answered in confidence, public claims corrected in public, first violations met by phone before letterhead. Practise naming the register before choosing the move.
- **Find the least effective option by its victim.** The floor option usually sacrifices someone absent: the six small units, the team watching the decision, next year's survey respondents, the unpopular person who was right. Ask who each option quietly spends.
- **Rehearse renegotiation and correction scripts.** Inherited commitments, phantom agreements, requests to soften findings: the strongest responses share a grammar — responsibility taken, facts dated, the legitimate interest behind the other party's position served by an honest instrument, a path forward attached. Write the script once for each and the scenarios become recognitions, not puzzles.
- **Debrief your real leadership weeks.** After each escalation, arbitration or culture moment, note the four options you had and which you took. Your own calendar is the highest-fidelity SJT practice bank in existence — and the debrief improves the calendar.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Absorbing your team's difficulty.** Taking escalations your BRMs should grow through is the lead-level signature error: fast today, hollowing tomorrow. The keys reward coaching-with-backstop over rescue, almost without exception.
- **Letting outcomes launder methods.** A correct result achieved by ambush, suppression or quiet erosion still teaches the watching team the method. Score options by what they normalise, not only by what they achieve.
- **Correcting in the wrong forum.** Public claims die in public or not at all; confidences returned through formal channels end the flow of confidences. Match the correction's venue to the error's venue.
- **Choosing the deferral dressed as synthesis.** "Proceed now, fix in parallel", "split the role", "publish headlines only for now" — options that promise both goods usually deliver neither, and the keys are calibrated to detect them. Ask what the deferred half actually costs to do later.
- **Weighing messengers instead of merits.** Unpopular rigour, late correctness, favourable-looking newcomers: leadership scenarios repeatedly test whether social packaging moves your judgement of substance. It must not — and the watching organisation learns from every occasion it does.
- **Protecting the visible commitment by breaking invisible ones.** Portfolio thinking is the lead's burden: the written promise, the loud director and the flagship programme are always visible; the six small units, the team's development and next year's data quality are not. The floor options spend the invisible; the keys reward the lead who prices it.
