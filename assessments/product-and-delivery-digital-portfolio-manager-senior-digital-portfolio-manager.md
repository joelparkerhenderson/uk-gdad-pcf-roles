## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the mental operations your role demands — pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation and applied problem solving — through scenarios built from the artefacts of portfolio management: delivery dashboards, dependency networks, governance schedules, risk registers, capacity models and assurance findings. At senior level the questions lean towards integration: holding several constraints simultaneously, detecting inconsistencies across documents rather than within them, and distinguishing what data proves from what it merely suggests.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 15 to 30 minutes with 20 to 30 questions, frequently adaptive, with scoring normed against a professional comparison group and reported as a speed-accuracy profile rather than a single figure. Expect the difficulty to climb if you are performing well — that is the instrument working as designed. Ungraded practice items are usually offered first; take them, if only to calibrate the interface and pacing.

For a senior digital portfolio manager, the relevance is direct. Your role's core transactions — tracking and analysing outcomes, providing delivery confidence, removing blockers, ensuring standards are met — are all exercises in structured reasoning over messy, partial, sometimes contradictory information. The portfolio view exists because no individual project can see cross-cutting patterns: the shared dependency about to bind, the oscillating rating that signals a reporting problem, the aggregate that balances while every period fails. A well-built cognitive assessment reproduces those situations in miniature, and rewards the same disciplines the job does: verify before concluding, quantify before ranking, and separate the defensible inference from the attractive one. There is a second-order relevance too: you guide others' analysis, so your own reasoning habits propagate. The rigour this section drills is rigour your portfolio inherits.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **Communicating analysis and insight**: turning complex data into compelling, clear and actionable stories begins with detecting the structure in the data — trends, oscillations, outliers, and the cross-project recurrences that only the portfolio altitude reveals. The story is only as good as the pattern-reading beneath it.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Governance and assurance**: developing appropriate governance processes, and supporting others in applying them, requires exact reasoning about conditional rules — what a control mandates, permits, and is silent on — and about the downstream consequences when one element of a process changes.
- **Error checking** maps to **Governance and assurance** and **Planning**: ensuring standards are met means finding the inconsistency between register and report, plan and dependency map, narrative and milestone evidence — before a board does. At your level you also design the reconciliation controls that catch what individual checking misses.
- **Prioritisation** maps to **Planning**: understanding the environment and prioritising the most important or high-value tasks — under capacity constraints, across competing projects, with complex internal and external dependencies — is the daily substance of the skill, and of the questions here.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Planning** and your duty to give guidance and deal with issues so teams deliver faster: removing blockers and developing plans for difficult situations is constraint-solving with human and organisational variables included.
- **Interpretive restraint** maps to **Strategic thinking** and **Communicating analysis and insight**: contributing to strategy, and communicating negative as well as positive information credibly, both depend on concluding exactly what the evidence supports — no more, no less — especially when the audience would prefer a stronger or softer message.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern recognition in portfolio-level data**

Twelve projects report monthly. Reviewing six months of submission timestamps, you notice: seven projects submit one to three days early; three submit on the deadline day; two submit one to two days late roughly every other month, and both of those also show the portfolio's highest rates of post-submission corrections. What is the most defensible reading of this pattern?

- A) The two late projects have weaker underlying reporting processes, and the correlation between lateness and corrections suggests a shared root cause worth investigating.
- B) The two late projects are hiding delivery problems.
- C) The deadline should be moved two days later to eliminate lateness.
- D) Ten projects are performing well, so no action is needed.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Read what the data pattern actually licenses. Two projects co-exhibit two symptoms — periodic lateness and high correction rates — and co-occurring symptoms suggest a common cause: plausibly a manual, last-minute compilation process, a data source that arrives late in their month, or a single overloaded person doing the return. That is a hypothesis worth investigating, which is exactly how option A frames it. Option B leaps from process symptoms to a motive accusation the data cannot support — lateness plus corrections indicates friction, not concealment, and treating it as concealment would poison two relationships on no evidence. Option C solves the visible symptom while ignoring the informative one: moving the deadline might convert late submissions into on-time ones and leave the correction rate — the actual quality signal — untouched. Option D discards a genuine finding because the aggregate looks healthy. The senior-level habit being tested: portfolio metadata (timeliness, correction rates, re-baselining frequency) is itself diagnostic data about reporting health, and reporting health determines whether everything else on your dashboard can be believed. Investigate the process before judging the people.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction from an assurance rule**

Your assurance framework states: "A service assessment is required before each phase transition. A project may not enter public beta without passing its beta assessment. A project that fails an assessment may be reassessed after addressing the report's recommendations." Project Tamar is in public beta. Which one of the following must be true?

- A) Tamar passed its beta assessment at the first attempt.
- B) Tamar has passed a beta assessment.
- C) Tamar addressed recommendations from a failed assessment.
- D) Tamar's next assessment will be its live assessment.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Deduce only what the rules and the fact guarantee. Tamar is in public beta; the framework forbids entering public beta without passing a beta assessment; therefore Tamar has, at some point, passed a beta assessment — option B, unconditionally. Option A adds "at the first attempt", which the rules cannot guarantee: the reassessment clause means Tamar might have failed once, addressed recommendations, and passed second time — both histories are consistent with the facts, so neither A nor C must be true; each is merely possible, and they cannot both be required since they describe different histories. Option D imports an assumption about assessment sequencing the passage never states. The discipline — distinguishing must-be-true from could-be-true — is the logical core of assurance work: when you certify to a board that "this project met the standard", you are asserting exactly the must-be-true, and senior portfolio managers who let could-be-true statements harden into certified facts are manufacturing assurance the evidence does not contain. The same discipline governs how you challenge: "the evidence shows X" and "X is consistent with the evidence" are different claims, and boards deserve to know which one you are making.

**Question 3 (moderate) — Error checking across three artefacts**

Three artefacts describe Project Almond's schedule. The dependency map shows Almond's release gated by Project Blythe's API, due 30 June. The portfolio plan shows Almond releasing 15 June. Almond's own highlight report says: "Release on track for 15 June; no external dependencies outstanding." Blythe's report says the API is on track for 30 June. Assuming every artefact is honestly maintained, which single explanation would reconcile all four statements — and what should you do first?

- A) Almond's team is concealing the dependency; escalate to their SRO.
- B) The dependency in the map may be stale — perhaps Almond removed its need for Blythe's API (for example, by building an interim capability) — so verify the dependency's current reality with both teams before touching any plan or report.
- C) Blythe's date is wrong; instruct Blythe to accelerate to 14 June.
- D) The portfolio plan is wrong; move Almond's release to 30 June.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Enumerate the possible worlds before acting in any of them. The four statements conflict only if the dependency is real and current: a live 30 June gate cannot precede a 15 June release. But the stem stipulates honest maintenance, which invites the reconciliation the artefacts themselves suggest: Almond reports "no external dependencies outstanding" — a claim that is true if the dependency has been dissolved (descoped, satisfied early, or worked around) and the map simply has not caught up. Dependency maps are notoriously the stalest artefact in any portfolio, updated at review cadence while reality moves at delivery cadence. Option B both identifies the reconciling hypothesis and prescribes the correct first move: verify with both teams, because the alternative world — the dependency is real and Almond has forgotten it — is also live, and catastrophically different in its implications. Options C and D each pick a world unverified and start issuing instructions in it: C compresses Blythe's schedule to fix what may be a documentation error; D delays Almond's release for a dependency that may no longer exist. Option A converts a data discrepancy into an accusation, on the least charitable reading, against the stem's own stipulation. The senior pattern: when artefacts disagree, the discrepancy is a question, not a finding — and the cheapest, fastest resolution is almost always a direct conversation with the teams, not an artefact-versus-artefact adjudication from your desk.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Prioritisation across the portfolio week**

Monday morning. Five items compete for your week: (1) Wednesday's investment committee needs your delivery confidence assessment for a £2m continuation decision — half a day's work, and two project inputs are still unverified; (2) a delivery manager has asked for help unblocking a cross-department data-sharing agreement stuck for three weeks; (3) your quarterly governance process review — improvements you committed to leadership — is due Friday; (4) an analyst you manage has asked for feedback on their first solo portfolio report before it goes out Thursday; (5) a director has invited you to present the portfolio's dependency approach to another department "sometime soon". Which allocation is most defensible?

- A) 1 Monday–Tuesday with the verification done; 4 Tuesday; 2 started Tuesday with the unblocking conversations scheduled; 3 Thursday–Friday; 5 scheduled for a future week.
- B) 3 first since it is a personal commitment to leadership; then 1, 2, 4; decline 5.
- C) 1 Monday; 5 Tuesday since director requests take precedence; 2, 3, 4 compressed into Thursday–Friday.
- D) 2 all week — unblocking teams is the role's core purpose; fit the rest around it.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Rank by consequence and deadline coupling. Item 1 dominates the early week: an investment committee will allocate £2m partly on your delivery confidence assessment, and unverified inputs are exactly what such an assessment must not contain — this is your "provide delivery confidence" duty at its most literal, and it is immovably dated. Item 4 is deadline-coupled too (Thursday publication) and it is a leverage activity: an hour of feedback develops your analyst and protects the portfolio's output quality — deferring it past the report's deadline converts mentoring into rework. Item 2 is important and already three weeks old, but its resolution mechanism — cross-department conversations — has lead times; the effective move is starting it Tuesday by scheduling the calls, not blocking a full week (option D's error: heroic single-issue focus that sacrifices four commitments to over-serve one). Item 3 has a Friday deadline and belongs to the week's back half. Item 5 is genuinely valuable — sharing practice across government is part of senior communicating-analysis-and-insight — but "sometime soon" is schedulable, not urgent; option C's elevation of it over dated commitments confuses seniority of requester with urgency of request. Option B front-loads the personal commitment ahead of the £2m decision — conscientiousness misallocated. Option A sequences all five by real coupling: A. The senior signature here is item 4's placement: at your level, developing others on their deadlines is not discretionary kindness; it is line-of-duty output protection.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Pattern interpretation: the too-stable metric**

For eleven consecutive months, Project Severn has reported exactly 85% milestone confidence. Every other project's confidence figure moves month to month — typically by three to ten points — as risks open and close. Severn's delivery record over the period has been middling: some milestones hit, two missed. What is the most defensible interpretation?

- A) Severn has unusually stable delivery conditions.
- B) The 85% figure is likely not being genuinely reassessed each month — it has become a ritual number — so the reporting process behind it needs examination.
- C) Severn's confidence methodology should be adopted portfolio-wide, as stability aids planning.
- D) 85% is an acceptable confidence level, so there is no issue.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The signal is not the level; it is the variance — or rather its absence. Genuine confidence assessment responds to events: risks materialise, mitigations land, dependencies slip, and the figure moves, as every other project's does. Severn's figure has not moved in eleven months across a period containing two missed milestones — events that should have dented any honestly reassessed confidence. A metric that ignores its own evidence has stopped measuring: 85% has become a ritual answer, probably copied forward each cycle, possibly chosen originally because it sounds responsibly confident without inviting challenge. Option B names this and — the senior half of the answer — targets the process, not the person: the fix is examining how Severn produces the figure (who assesses, against what evidence, with what challenge) and perhaps how the portfolio's template invites ritual answers generally. Option A is contradicted by the middling delivery record; stable conditions do not miss milestones. Option C rewards the pathology: adopting the least responsive methodology because its output is smooth is optimising for planning convenience over truth. Option D confuses an acceptable level with an acceptable process — the problem is not that 85 is wrong but that nothing could have made it change. The transferable diagnostic, worth teaching your analysts: healthy metrics breathe. Suspicious flatness — in confidence figures, risk counts, or RAG distributions — is a reporting-health finding in itself, and detecting it is exactly the tracking-and-analysing duty your role names.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Deduction through a governance change**

You are redesigning the assurance schedule. Current rules: (i) every project is health-checked every six months; (ii) projects rated red are additionally reviewed monthly until amber or better; (iii) health checks and reviews cannot occur in the same calendar month for the same project. Proposed change: health checks move to every four months. Project Derwent is red, and its last health check was in March. Under the proposed rules, in which month is Derwent's next health check due — and what scheduling conflict does the change create for red projects generally?

- A) July; and none — reviews and health checks are different events.
- B) July; and a recurring conflict — red projects need a monthly review in every month, but rule (iii) forbids a review in any health-check month, so every health check now displaces a monthly review three times a year instead of twice.
- C) September; and no conflict, since rule (iii) resolves it automatically.
- D) July; but only if Derwent returns to amber first.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Two calculations, then a synthesis. First the date: last check March, new cycle four months, so next check July — eliminating C (September is the old six-month cycle) and D (nothing in the rules conditions health checks on rating). Second, the structural consequence — the part that distinguishes a rule-applier from a rule-designer. A red project requires a review every month (rule ii); rule (iii) forbids review and health check sharing a month; therefore every health-check month for a red project is a month its mandatory review cannot occur. That conflict already existed twice a year under the six-month cycle; shortening the cycle to four months makes it three times a year — the proposed improvement mechanically increases how often the assurance regime contradicts itself for exactly the projects needing the most scrutiny. Option B captures both. Option A waves the conflict away by noting the events differ — but rule (iii) exists precisely because they compete, and renaming the collision does not resolve it. The senior lesson: when you develop governance processes, every rule change must be propagated through the other rules' interactions before adoption, and the interaction effects concentrate on edge cases — here, red projects — which is exactly where assurance failures are most expensive. A redesign proposal should arrive with its conflicts pre-identified and a resolution drafted: for instance, amending rule (iii) to let a health check absorb the monthly review's scope in that month.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Error checking a delivery confidence submission**

A programme submits this delivery confidence rationale: "Confidence: High. Rationale: The team has delivered every sprint goal for six sprints. Velocity is stable. The remaining scope fits within four sprints at current velocity, against six sprints of runway before the fixed go-live date. Key dependency: the payments provider's sandbox, promised by 1 October, which we need for the final two sprints of integration testing." Today is 20 September; go-live is 15 December; sprints are fortnightly. What is the flaw in this rationale?

- A) Velocity is the wrong measure of progress.
- B) The arithmetic of sprints is wrong: four sprints of work do not fit in six sprints of runway.
- C) The confidence level ignores the rationale's own single point of failure — the sandbox — whose slippage would consume the schedule buffer sprint for sprint, and which sits outside the team's control with no stated fallback.
- D) Six sprints of delivered goals is too short a record to justify high confidence.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Verify the arithmetic first: from 20 September to 15 December is roughly twelve weeks — six fortnightly sprints, as stated; four sprints of remaining scope inside six of runway gives a two-sprint buffer. The internal numbers hold, eliminating B. The flaw is structural: the rationale itself names a dependency — an external provider's sandbox, needed for the final two sprints — and everything favourable in the submission (streak, velocity, buffer) is within-team evidence, while the named threat is outside the team's control, promised for 1 October with no fallback described. If the sandbox slips, the buffer erodes sprint for sprint, and a two-week provider delay halves the margin; a month consumes it entirely. "High confidence" is thus being carried by the controllable factors while the uncontrollable one — the actual binding constraint — goes unweighted. Option C. This is the single most common defect in delivery confidence submissions: teams assess what they can see (their own execution) and under-assess what they cannot (external dependencies), producing confidence levels that describe effort rather than probability. Options A and D quibble with evidence that is, in fact, reasonable — stable velocity and a six-sprint streak are legitimate inputs; they are just insufficient ones. The senior review question this trains: "what in this rationale is outside the team's control, and how is it weighted?" — asked routinely, it is worth more to your portfolio's delivery confidence than any methodology document.

**Question 8 (hard) — Multi-constraint problem solving: the assurance calendar**

You must schedule four assessments in one five-day week (Monday–Friday), one per day maximum: Discovery peer review (P), Alpha assessment (A), Beta assessment (B), and a spend panel (S). Constraints: (i) the lead assessor for both A and B is available Tuesday, Wednesday and Thursday only, and cannot do two assessments on consecutive days; (ii) S must occur after A, because the panel uses A's outcome; (iii) P must be on Monday or Friday, when the peer reviewers are free; (iv) Friday afternoon is a departmental all-staff event, so Friday can host only a half-day session, and only P is short enough to fit a half day. Which schedule satisfies all constraints?

- A) P Monday, A Tuesday, B Wednesday, S Thursday
- B) P Monday, A Tuesday, S Wednesday, B Thursday
- C) A Tuesday, B Thursday, S Friday, P Monday
- D) P Friday, A Tuesday, B Wednesday, S Thursday

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Apply the constraints in order of restrictiveness. Constraint (iv): Friday fits only P; so if anything occupies Friday it must be P, and S, A, B must all land Monday–Thursday. Constraint (i): A and B need the Tuesday–Thursday window and cannot sit on consecutive days — so A and B occupy either {Tuesday, Thursday} in some order. Constraint (ii): S follows A. Test option A: A Tuesday, B Wednesday — consecutive days for the lead assessor, violating (i). Test option D: same A-Tuesday/B-Wednesday violation. Test option C: A Tuesday, B Thursday satisfies (i); S Friday — but (iv) says Friday fits only P; violation. Option B: P Monday satisfies (iii); A Tuesday and B Thursday are non-consecutive and inside the assessor's window, satisfying (i); S Wednesday is after A's Tuesday, satisfying (ii); nothing needs Friday. All four constraints hold — B. Note the solving order: the most restrictive constraint (Friday's half-day) first collapsed the search space, then the assessor's non-consecutive rule fixed A and B's skeleton, and the remaining pieces slotted deterministically. This is precisely how a senior portfolio manager builds a real assurance calendar — anchor the scarcest resource, respect its recovery constraints, and sequence the dependent events after it. It is also worth noticing what the puzzle quietly teaches about resilience: the feasible schedule is unique, meaning the calendar has zero slack, and any single-day disruption cascades. In real planning you would flag that fragility and hold a contingency week — providing delivery confidence includes knowing when a plan is feasible but brittle.

**Question 9 (hard) — Interpreting a portfolio anomaly under pressure**

Preparing the quarterly leadership pack, you find the portfolio's aggregate benefits forecast has fallen 2%, but decomposition shows this nets two large opposing movements: Programme X has cut its forecast by £1.8m (30% of its total) following a failed pilot, while Programme Y has raised its forecast by £1.6m (25% of its total) based on "revised adoption modelling" — a model revision made by Y's own team the same week X's cut became known, with no new adoption data cited. The pack is due in 48 hours. What is the strongest course of action?

- A) Report the net 2% — aggregation is standard practice and the movements roughly cancel.
- B) Report both gross movements prominently; treat X's evidenced cut as credible; challenge Y's uplift before the pack goes out — what new data supports the revised model? — and if unverified by the deadline, present Y's uplift as unvalidated, with the aggregate shown both with and without it.
- C) Exclude Y's uplift permanently as obviously gamed.
- D) Delay the pack until both movements are fully audited.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Separate the three judgements bundled in this scenario. First, presentation: a net −2% composed of ±30% gross swings is an aggregation that conceals rather than summarises — leadership reading "−2%, broadly stable" would be materially misled about a portfolio containing one large evidenced deterioration and one large unevidenced improvement. Gross movements must be visible; the net alone is false comfort. Second, evidential asymmetry — the senior crux: X's cut follows a failed pilot (new evidence, unfavourable direction, inherently credible — teams rarely cut their own forecasts without cause), while Y's uplift cites a model revision with no new data, made in the same week a portfolio-level shortfall appeared. The timing and the absence of data do not prove gaming, but they establish a verification burden that has not been met. Third, process under deadline: challenge now, and if unresolved, disclose honestly — "uplift under validation" with the aggregate shown both ways — rather than either laundering the number into the total or silently deleting it. Option B executes all three. Option A is the aggregation failure. Option C converts suspicion into verdict without the conversation — perhaps Y's model revision is genuine and merely badly timed; permanent exclusion on pattern-matching poisons a relationship and possibly discards real benefits. Option D sacrifices the leadership cadence to perfectionism when honest caveating fully serves the decision need. The habit this trains: forecasts move on evidence, and your challenge intensity should scale with the movement's size, direction, and evidential backing — communicated with the negative and positive information given equal, honest prominence.

**Question 10 (hard) — Prioritisation with second-order effects**

Budget pressure requires you to recommend pausing one of four initiatives. Analysis: Initiative K — benefits £900k/yr, consumes the portfolio's only two data engineers, 60% complete; Initiative L — benefits £700k/yr, generic skills, 80% complete, contractually committed supplier ending in two months regardless; Initiative M — benefits £1.1m/yr, generic skills, 20% complete; Initiative N — benefits £400k/yr, generic skills, 30% complete, but it builds the data platform that K's remaining work and one committed future initiative both depend on. Purely on these facts, which pause recommendation is most defensible?

- A) N — lowest annual benefits.
- B) M — least sunk progress relative to benefits still distant.
- C) L — its supplier ends in two months anyway, so pausing it forfeits little.
- D) K — highest-cost scarce resource consumption.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Work the second-order effects that a benefits-only ranking misses. Option A pauses N because its £400k headline is smallest — but N is an enabler: K's remaining 40% and a committed future initiative both depend on N's platform, so pausing N strands K's £900k at 60% complete and blocks the future commitment; N's effective value includes what it unlocks, making it the worst pause candidate despite the smallest label. Option C pauses L — but L is 80% complete with a supplier contractually ending in two months: pausing now forfeits nearly-complete benefits and, because the supplier ends regardless, restarting later means re-procuring at unknown cost; L is cheapest to finish and most expensive to abandon. Note C's rhetorical trap — "ends anyway, so forfeits little" inverts the true logic: the closing window is a reason to finish, not to stop. Option D pauses K to free scarce engineers — but K is 60% sunk and its completion is precisely what N's platform serves; pausing K devalues N and strands the most progress. Option M: £1.1m of benefits, but only 20% complete — the benefits are furthest away, the sunk cost smallest, its skills generic (so its team redeploys without stranding a scarce capability), and nothing depends on it. Pausing M loses the least realised-plus-enabled value and preserves every dependency chain: option B. The senior disciplines on display: value flows through dependencies (enablers carry their dependents' value), completion proximity matters (benefits nearly banked outrank larger benefits at a distance, per unit of remaining cost), and scarce-capability continuity is a portfolio asset. A recommendation memo carrying this reasoning — not just the ranking — is what "using data to inform planning" means at your level.

**Question 11 (hard) — Error checking the improvement metric**

Six months after you streamlined the portfolio's stage-gate process, your analyst reports: "Average gate-approval time has fallen from 21 working days to 12. The improvement is working." Checking the underlying data, you find: gate volume fell from 40 approvals in the prior six months to 25; the new process introduced a 'pre-check' step that returns incomplete submissions before the clock starts; and returned submissions — 10 in the period — are excluded from the average. What is the soundest assessment?

- A) The improvement is confirmed: 12 days is unambiguously better than 21.
- B) The headline is unreliable as evidence of improvement: the clock-start redefinition and the exclusion of returned submissions mean the two averages measure different things, so end-to-end elapsed time (including pre-check and returns) must be compared like-for-like before claiming success.
- C) The improvement failed: lower volume proves teams are avoiding the new process.
- D) The metrics are irrelevant; only user satisfaction with the process matters.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The question is whether 12 and 21 measure the same quantity — and they do not, for two compounding reasons. First, the clock-start moved: the new process starts timing at completed pre-check, so any waiting and rework time in pre-check is now outside the measurement window, whereas the old 21 days included the equivalent churn. Second, the sample changed: ten returned submissions — precisely the cases that would have been the slowest under the old regime — are excluded entirely. Both changes bias the new figure downward independently of any real improvement; the process could be unchanged, or slower end-to-end, while the headline improves. This is measurement-boundary gaming's honest cousin: nobody need have intended deception, but a redesign that redefines the metric's boundaries silently breaks its comparability. Option B prescribes the sound test — end-to-end elapsed time from first submission to decision, returns included, compared across regimes — which may well still show genuine improvement (pre-checks often do help) but will show it honestly. Option A accepts the broken comparison. Option C over-reads the volume drop, which the data cannot attribute (fewer gates may reflect portfolio composition, batching, or indeed avoidance — unknown). Option D retreats to a different metric rather than fixing this one; satisfaction matters, but it complements rather than replaces cycle-time evidence. The senior reflex this trains, doubly relevant because the improvement was yours: when a metric moves after a process change, first ask what the change did to the metric's definition — and apply that scepticism most rigorously to evidence flattering your own initiatives. Identifying and recommending improvements to governance processes includes proving them with unbroken measures.

**Question 12 (hard) — Applied problem solving: the cascading slip**

Wednesday: Project Foss confirms its data platform milestone will slip five weeks. Your dependency map shows: Project Gelt needs Foss's platform for performance testing, currently scheduled to start in three weeks; Project Hodder needs Gelt's test results for its security case, due at Hodder's go/no-go board in nine weeks; Hodder's go-live is contractually committed with an external partner twelve weeks out. Gelt's performance testing takes three weeks; producing the security case from test results takes two weeks. If nothing changes, does Hodder's go/no-go date hold — and what is the highest-leverage intervention to examine first?

- A) It holds — twelve weeks is enough; no intervention needed.
- B) It fails by four weeks against the board date; the highest-leverage first move is examining downstream compressibility — whether Gelt's testing can start on partial platform capability, run compressed or in parallel, and whether the security case can be drafted from interim results — since the Foss slip is upstream and fixed.
- C) It fails; Hodder should move its go-live by five weeks as the first response.
- D) It fails; Foss must be instructed to halve its slippage.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Chain the arithmetic forward, baseline first. Pre-slip: Gelt's testing starts week 3, takes three weeks, completing end of week 6; the security case takes two more, ready end of week 8 — one week ahead of Hodder's week-9 board and comfortably ahead of the week-12 contractual go-live. The whole chain, in other words, carried a one-week buffer at the board. Now apply the slip: Foss's platform arrives five weeks late, pushing Gelt's test start from week 3 to week 8; testing completes end of week 11; the security case lands end of week 13. Against the week-9 board that is a four-week miss, and week 13 even overruns the week-12 go-live by one week — the five-week slip has overwhelmed the one-week buffer, with four weeks of net damage at the board. Option A is arithmetically false. Now compare interventions. Option D instructs an upstream fact to improve — Foss's slip is announced and fixed; demanding it halve is not a plan but a wish, and building your response on it repeats the confidence error of Question 7. Option C reaches for the most expensive lever first: moving a contractually committed go-live has partner and possibly financial consequences, and should be the last resort after cheaper options are exhausted, not the first response. Option B names the correct first examination: every compressible segment lies downstream of the fixed slip — test start (can testing begin on partial platform capability?), test duration (parallel test runs?), case production (draft from interim results, finalise on completion?). Finding even two weeks of compression halves the problem before anyone touches a contract; and whatever gap remains after compression is the honestly sized problem to take to Hodder's board and the partner conversation. The senior pattern: when an upstream slip is fixed, recompute the chain yourself, locate the buffer, and work the intervention options in cost order — downstream compression, then scope, then dates. Dealing with issues so teams deliver faster means arriving at Foss's slip meeting with that shortlist already drafted.

### Preparation tips

- **Practise on your portfolio's real artefact set.** Take your live dependency map, last quarter's confidence submissions, and the assurance calendar, and run this section's checks against them: stale dependencies, ritual metrics, boundary-broken measures, zero-slack schedules. The test rehearses the job; the job rehearses the test.
- **Drill must-versus-may deduction.** For any governance rule you touch this month, write down what it mandates, what it permits, and what it is silent on. Senior reasoning errors are overwhelmingly could-be-true statements promoted to must-be-true.
- **Recompute chains by hand.** Dependency and schedule arithmetic — start dates, durations, buffers — should be something you verify rather than trust, in tests and in briefings alike. Practise until a five-item chain takes you under a minute.
- **Interrogate metric boundaries.** Whenever a figure improves after a process change, ask what the change did to the measurement's start point, end point and sample. Write the like-for-like definition before accepting the trend.
- **Time-box anomaly investigation.** In tests, flag-and-return beats rumination; at work, a 48-hour challenge window with honest caveats beats both silent acceptance and indefinite delay. Practise the caveated-disclosure form of words until it is fluent.
- **Rehearse under adaptive pressure.** Do at least one full timed practice set. Senior candidates' commonest test failure is pacing — over-investing in early hard questions — not reasoning. Learn your own tempo.
- **Review errors as a designer.** For each practice question missed, identify not just the right answer but the question's target skill and its trap's construction. You will coach others through these instruments; understanding their architecture doubles the value of your practice.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Promoting possibility to necessity.** "Consistent with the evidence" is not "established by the evidence". Certify only the must-be-true; label the rest as hypothesis.
- **Adjudicating artefact conflicts from your desk.** When map, plan and report disagree, the answer is a conversation with the teams, not a ruling on which document wins. Any artefact can be the stale one.
- **Reading levels and ignoring variance.** A confidence figure's flatness, a metric's suspicious smoothness, an oscillating RAG — the shape of data over time carries diagnostic information that point-in-time reads discard.
- **Trusting netted aggregates.** Portfolio-level sums that conceal large opposing gross movements mislead precisely the audiences that rely on you most. Decompose before you summarise.
- **Weighting controllable evidence over binding constraints.** Team-side execution data is abundant and comforting; external dependencies are sparse and decisive. Confidence assessments fail at the second, not the first.
- **Solving schedules without noticing brittleness.** A uniquely feasible plan is a plan with zero slack. Feasibility and resilience are different findings; report both.
- **Applying scepticism asymmetrically.** Challenge the uplift that rescues your aggregate exactly as hard as the cut that ruins it — and challenge evidence flattering your own improvements hardest of all.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures fluency and accuracy with the quantitative material a senior portfolio manager produces, validates and presents: multi-project spend and forecast tables, weighted portfolio metrics, capacity and demand models, benefits trajectories, trend decompositions, and the confidence arithmetic behind delivery assessments. Question formats present tables, charts or short data narratives followed by calculation and interpretation items — with the senior-level emphasis falling on interpretation: choosing the right measure, spotting the misleading aggregate, and knowing what a figure can and cannot support.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 20 to 35 minutes with 15 to 20 questions, calculator permitted, distractors engineered from predictable errors — wrong bases, averaged percentages, boundary slips, conflated measures. At senior level, expect fewer pure-arithmetic items and more two-step questions where the calculation is easy but selecting the correct calculation is the test.

Employers use numeric reasoning assessments for senior portfolio roles because the role is where project numbers become organisational decisions. You do not merely report figures; you certify them — to investment committees, boards and leadership — and you translate them into recommendations with money and delivery consequences attached. A senior portfolio manager who mistakes a points change for a percentage change, weights a portfolio metric badly, or accepts a forecast whose base has quietly shifted does not make a small error: they miscalibrate a decision. Equally, the role's influence runs on numerical credibility — the stakeholder who catches your arithmetic once discounts your analysis for a year. The questions below drill the calculations your dashboards actually require, each with complete step-by-step working, and each explanation connects the technique to the decision it typically informs.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Weighted aggregation and combined rates** map to **Communicating analysis and insight**: turning complex data into clear, actionable stories requires aggregating unequal projects honestly — budget-weighted confidence, totals-over-totals completion rates — and knowing when an unweighted view would mislead. Presenting complex messages clearly starts with computing them correctly.
- **Forecasting and run-rate arithmetic** map to **Planning** and your duty to provide delivery confidence: burn analysis, straight-line and adjusted forecasts, and buffer arithmetic are the quantitative core of confidence assessment and of using data to inform planning.
- **Capacity and dependency arithmetic** map to **Planning**: managing complex internal and external dependencies and ensuring teams plan appropriately for their capacity are, at bottom, constrained-resource calculations — demand versus monthly capacity, critical-path durations, slack computation.
- **Trend and variance interpretation** map to **Communicating analysis and insight** and **Governance and assurance**: distinguishing signal from noise, percentage from percentage-point, and genuine improvement from measurement artefact protects both your reporting and the standards you are responsible for upholding.
- **Benefits and investment arithmetic** map to **Strategic thinking**: contributing to strategy requires fluency with benefits trajectories, realisation ratios and simple investment comparisons — the numbers with which strategic options are argued.
- **Presentation-of-numbers judgement** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**: influencing stakeholders and resolving issues frequently comes down to showing the right figure, on the right base, with honest caveats — arithmetic in service of trust.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Portfolio delivery rate**

Across your portfolio last quarter, 68 of 80 planned milestones were delivered. What was the portfolio delivery rate?

- A) 68%
- B) 80%
- C) 85%
- D) 88%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Rate = delivered ÷ planned × 100 = 68 ÷ 80. Compute: 68 ÷ 80 = 0.85, so 85% — option C. A fast mental route: 80 × 0.85 = 68 (since 80 × 0.8 = 64 and 80 × 0.05 = 4; 64 + 4 = 68), confirming the division. Distractor A quotes the numerator as a percentage; B quotes the denominator. Sense-check by quarters: three-quarters of 80 is 60, seven-eighths is 70 — 68 sits just below seven-eighths (87.5%), so 85% is plausible and 68% is impossible-looking on inspection. The habit of anchoring against simple fractions takes two seconds and would catch either distractor. At your level this figure rarely stands alone: the follow-up questions — delivered against original plan or re-baselined plan? weighted by milestone significance or counted flat? — are where the real analysis lives, and the later questions in this section work exactly that ground.

**Question 2 (easy) — Percentage change in portfolio spend**

Portfolio monthly spend was £640,000 in January and £560,000 in February. What was the percentage change?

- A) A fall of 14.3%
- B) A fall of 12.5%
- C) A fall of 8.0%
- D) A rise of 12.5%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Percentage change = (new − original) ÷ original × 100. Step 1, the change: £560,000 − £640,000 = −£80,000, a fall. Step 2, divide by the original (January): 80,000 ÷ 640,000 = 0.125. Step 3: a fall of 12.5% — option B. Distractor A (14.3%) is the wrong-base error: 80,000 ÷ 560,000 ≈ 0.143 divides by the new month, and it is placed first in the options because the wrong base is the most common slip in change calculations. The direction check dispatches D instantly — spend went down. A note worth carrying into real reporting: a month-on-month fall of 12.5% in spend is a fact; its meaning — healthy efficiency, invoice timing, or delivery stalling — is an interpretation requiring more data, and a senior portfolio manager's report says which interpretation the evidence supports rather than letting the reader guess. The arithmetic is the easy half of communicating negative and positive information; the framing is the professional half.

**Question 3 (easy) — Reading a capacity table**

Your shared-capability tracker shows next month's committed designer-days by project:

| Project | Committed designer-days |
|---|---|
| Aire | 18 |
| Calder | 12 |
| Douglas | 9 |
| Eden | 15 |

The design team's capacity is 60 designer-days per month. What percentage of capacity is committed?

- A) 80%
- B) 85%
- C) 90%
- D) 95%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Step 1, total demand: 18 + 12 = 30; 30 + 9 = 39; 39 + 15 = 54 designer-days. Step 2, utilisation: 54 ÷ 60 = 0.9 = 90% — option C. The distractors are near-misses from single addition slips (e.g. omitting Douglas gives 45 ÷ 60 = 75%; misadding to 51 gives 85%), which is why summing in written steps beats mental accumulation under time pressure. The planning judgement attached to this number matters more than the number: 90% committed leaves 6 designer-days of slack for a month — roughly one absence, one urgent request, or one estimate overrun, but not two. Teams planned at 90%+ of theoretical capacity have no absorption margin, which is why mature portfolio offices treat 80–85% as the healthy planning ceiling. "Ensure that teams plan appropriately for their capacity" is your named planning skill, and this calculation — with its threshold judgement — is that skill's daily arithmetic.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Budget-weighted portfolio confidence**

Four programmes report delivery confidence, to be aggregated budget-weighted for the leadership dashboard:

| Programme | Budget (£m) | Confidence (%) |
|---|---|---|
| P1 | 10 | 90 |
| P2 | 5 | 70 |
| P3 | 3 | 50 |
| P4 | 2 | 80 |

What is the budget-weighted portfolio confidence?

- A) 72.5%
- B) 75.0%
- C) 78.0%
- D) 80.0%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Weighted average = sum of (budget × confidence) ÷ sum of budgets. Step 1, the products: 10 × 90 = 900; 5 × 70 = 350; 3 × 50 = 150; 2 × 80 = 160. Step 2, sum of products: 900 + 350 = 1,250; 1,250 + 150 = 1,400; 1,400 + 160 = 1,560. Step 3, sum of weights: 10 + 5 + 3 + 2 = 20. Step 4: 1,560 ÷ 20 = 78.0% — option C. Sense-check: the answer must lie within the value range (50 to 90) and lean towards the heavily weighted entries; P1 holds half the money at 90% confidence, so a weighted result well above the middle is exactly right. Distractor A is the unweighted mean — (90 + 70 + 50 + 80) ÷ 4 = 290 ÷ 4 = 72.5% — and the 5.5-point gap between the two views is the substantive story: the biggest budgets carry the higher confidence, so the money-weighted picture is materially stronger than the programme-count picture. Neither view is "the" truth; they answer different questions — "how confident should we be about the portfolio's money?" versus "how are programmes faring on average?" — and on a leadership dashboard you would state the basis explicitly, because a reader who silently assumes the other basis mis-sizes portfolio risk by more than five points. At senior level, choosing and declaring the aggregation basis is part of the analysis, not a formatting afterthought — and when the two views diverge sharply, showing both, with the divergence explained, is usually the most honest presentation of all.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Points versus percent in an assurance trend**

The portfolio's standards-compliance rate rose from 64% to 80% following your assurance process improvements. Which statement describes this change correctly?

- A) Compliance rose 16%, from 64% to 80%.
- B) Compliance rose 16 percentage points — a relative improvement of 25%.
- C) Compliance rose 25 percentage points.
- D) Compliance rose 20%, since 16 of 80 is 20%.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Two distinct measures. Absolute: 80 − 64 = 16 percentage points. Relative: 16 ÷ 64 = 0.25 = 25% — compliance is a quarter higher than it was. Option B states both with correct labels. Option A commits the standard conflation — "rose 16%" formally means 64 × 1.16 = 74.2%, which is not what happened; points and percent are different units. Option D divides the change by the new value — the wrong base again, dressed in a new costume. The reason this precision earns its place in a senior test: improvement claims are advocacy documents, and the gap between "16 points" and "25%" is exactly the space in which improvements get oversold or undersold. When the improvement is your own — as here — stating "sixteen points, a 25% relative gain, measured on an unchanged definition" pre-empts the challenge a sharp finance director would otherwise land; and when the claim is a supplier's or a programme's, converting their chosen framing back to both measures is the fastest honesty check available. Recommending improvements to governance processes carries the duty of measuring them impeccably.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Run-rate with a step change**

Eight months into the year, Programme Quill has spent £1.92m of its £3.3m annual budget. From month nine, a new supplier contract adds a fixed £90,000 per month. Assuming the underlying run rate continues and the contract cost is additional, what is the forecast full-year spend?

- A) £2.88m
- B) £3.24m
- C) £3.30m
- D) £3.42m

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Step 1, underlying run rate: £1.92m ÷ 8 = £0.24m per month. Step 2, remaining four months of underlying spend: £0.24m × 4 = £0.96m. Step 3, contract addition for months nine to twelve: £90,000 × 4 = £0.36m. Step 4, full-year forecast: £1.92m + £0.96m + £0.36m = £3.24m — option B. Against the £3.3m budget this lands £60,000 under: tight but inside, with the caveat that the forecast now has no contingency for any further cost or rate drift (a 2% rise in the underlying rate would breach). Distractor A is the naive straight-line forecast (£0.24m × 12 = £2.88m) ignoring the step change; D applies the £90,000 across six months instead of four. The technique — decompose the forecast into continuing baseline plus known step changes, then recombine — is the honest middle ground between straight-lining (ignores known futures) and guesswork (invents unknown ones), and it is precisely how delivery confidence in financial terms should be built: forecast what you know, state what you have assumed, and quantify the headroom. A senior portfolio manager who reports "£3.24m forecast, £60k headroom, no contingency within it" has given leadership a decision-grade number; one who reports "on budget" has given them a mood.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Combined completion across unequal quarters**

The portfolio's milestone record: Q1 delivered 45 of 60 planned; Q2 delivered 66 of 80. A leadership summary drafts: "Completion improved from 75% to 82.5%, averaging 78.75% for the half-year." Which figure in the sentence is wrong, and what is the correct value?

- A) The Q2 rate; it should be 80%.
- B) The half-year average; combining totals gives 111 ÷ 140 = 79.3%.
- C) The Q1 rate; it should be 72%.
- D) Nothing is wrong; averaging the two rates is valid here.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Verify each figure. Q1: 45 ÷ 60 = 0.75 = 75% ✓. Q2: 66 ÷ 80 = 0.825 = 82.5% ✓. The half-year figure: the draft averages the two rates — (75 + 82.5) ÷ 2 = 78.75% — but the quarters are unequal (60 versus 80 milestones), so the simple average misweights them. Correct method: total delivered ÷ total planned = (45 + 66) ÷ (60 + 80) = 111 ÷ 140. Compute: 111 ÷ 140 = 0.7929, so 79.3% — option B. The error is small here (half a point) because the quarters are only moderately unequal, and that is exactly why it survives review: averaged rates look right when group sizes are close, then mislead badly when they diverge — a portfolio summing a 20-milestone quarter with a 100-milestone one would be more than two points wrong by the same method. The rule admits no exceptions worth memorising: combined rates come from combined totals, always, and the delta between that and the averaged-rates figure is a quick audit you can run on any summary that crosses your desk. Ensuring standards are met includes arithmetic standards in leadership packs — half a point of unearned accuracy costs nothing today and credibility later.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Benefits realisation ratio**

A programme's business case forecast £2.4m annual benefits at full rollout. Rollout is 62.5% complete; measured annualised benefits to date are £1.2m. Assuming benefits scale linearly with rollout, what percentage of expected benefits at this stage is the programme realising?

- A) 50%
- B) 62.5%
- C) 80%
- D) 100%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Step 1, expected benefits at this stage: 62.5% × £2.4m = 0.625 × 2.4 = £1.5m. Step 2, realisation ratio: measured ÷ expected = 1.2 ÷ 1.5 = 0.8 = 80% — option C. Distractor A divides measured by the full-rollout target (1.2 ÷ 2.4 = 50%), which answers a different question — "what share of the eventual target is banked?" — and conflating the two flatters or damns unfairly depending on rollout stage. The projection this ratio supports: if the 80% shortfall pattern persists to full rollout, annual benefits land at 0.8 × £2.4m = £1.92m, a £480,000 annual shortfall against the business case — material, and worth surfacing now, while causes (adoption lag? benefit model optimism? measurement gaps?) can still be diagnosed and addressed. The linearity assumption deserves its caveat in any real report: benefits often lag rollout (users take time to change behaviour), so an 80% ratio mid-rollout is concerning but not damning — the trend of the ratio across successive measurements is the decisive evidence. This is the arithmetic backbone of outcome tracking: expected-at-stage, realised-versus-expected, projected-at-completion, each stated with its assumption. Boards fed all three numbers make better continuation decisions than boards fed any one.

**Question 9 (hard) — Critical path through a dependency network**

Four workstreams feed a portfolio go-live. Durations and dependencies: W1 (data migration) takes 6 weeks and can start now. W2 (integration build) takes 5 weeks and can start now. W3 (assurance testing) takes 4 weeks and cannot start until both W1 and W2 are complete. W4 (training rollout) takes 7 weeks, cannot start until W2 is complete, and can run in parallel with W3. Go-live requires W3 and W4 both complete. What is the earliest go-live, and which workstream is on the critical path's final segment?

- A) Week 10, via W3
- B) Week 12, via W4
- C) Week 13, via W3
- D) Week 12, via W3

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Compute each path's completion. W1 finishes at week 6; W2 at week 5. W3 starts when both W1 and W2 are done — the later of 6 and 5, so week 6 — and takes 4 weeks: complete at week 10. W4 starts when W2 completes (week 5) and takes 7 weeks: complete at week 12. Go-live needs both W3 (week 10) and W4 (week 12): the later governs, so earliest go-live is week 12, arriving via W4 — option B. The critical path is W2 → W4 (5 + 7 = 12), not the intuitively "heavier" W1 → W3 chain (6 + 4 = 10), which carries two weeks of slack. Managerial consequences follow directly, and they are the real content of the question: a one-week slip in W1 or W3 costs nothing (slack absorbs it), while a one-week slip in W2 or W4 moves go-live week for week — so your monitoring attention, your earliest-warning triggers and your compression investment all belong on the W2→W4 chain, and the common instinct to watch the data migration (biggest single block, scariest name) is quantitatively wrong here. Recomputing the critical path after every duration change is the discipline; dependency maps that record links but not durations cannot tell you where delivery confidence actually lives. Managing complex dependencies is, at bottom, knowing at all times which chain owns your go-live date.

**Question 10 (hard) — Interpreting a variance decomposition**

Portfolio spend this quarter was £5.75m against a plan of £5.0m — a £750k overspend. Decomposition: Programme A overspent £900k (unplanned remediation after a security finding); Programme B underspent £400k (recruitment delays on a critical team); Programme C overspent £250k (accelerated delivery, pulling next quarter's work forward). Which reading should anchor your leadership narrative?

- A) The portfolio overspent 15%; spending discipline needs tightening across programmes.
- B) The three variances have three different meanings — A's is a genuine cost increase to fund or offset, B's is delay masquerading as saving, and C's is timing neutral over two quarters — so the honest position is roughly £900k of true cost pressure, partially hidden by £400k of worrying slippage, not a uniform 15% discipline problem.
- C) The net £750k rounds to A's £900k, so report Programme A and omit the others.
- D) B's underspend offsets most of A's overspend, so the portfolio is broadly fine.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The arithmetic is trivial (900 − 400 + 250 = 750 ✓); the test is interpretive taxonomy. Variances carry meanings, not just magnitudes: A's £900k is a real, permanent cost increase with a defensible cause (security remediation is rarely optional) — the question it raises is funding source, not blame. B's £400k "underspend" is the dangerous one: money unspent because a critical team is missing is delivery slipping in financial disguise, and treating it as savings — as option D does — double-counts: you cannot bank the money and still expect the outcomes it was meant to buy. C's £250k is timing: work pulled forward nets to zero across quarters and, if anything, signals delivery health. So the honest narrative is B's: true cost pressure ≈ £900k; apparent offset from B is actually a second problem (delay), not a solution; C washes out. Option A's uniform "discipline" framing would prompt leadership to squeeze all programmes — punishing C's acceleration and doing nothing for A's funding gap or B's recruitment blocker: a wrong diagnosis generating three wrong interventions. Option C fabricates by omission. This decomposition discipline — classify every material variance as cost, timing, or delivery-in-disguise before netting anything — is what makes a portfolio's financial story decision-grade, and it is the numeric face of communicating negative and positive information honestly: the net figure alone is neither.

**Question 11 (hard) — Confidence interval reasoning for delivery forecasting**

Your delivery analytics show a team completes between 18 and 26 story points per sprint (observed range over 12 sprints), with a mean of 22. Remaining backlog for a fixed-date release is 130 points across 5 remaining sprints. Which statement best characterises delivery confidence?

- A) Confident: mean throughput gives 5 × 22 = 110... so the release will miss; report certain failure.
- B) At mean throughput the team delivers 110 of 130 points — a 20-point shortfall — and even at best observed throughput (5 × 26 = 130) success requires every sprint at the historical maximum; so on current scope the release is high-risk-to-implausible, and the conversation should move to scope reduction of roughly 20 points or more.
- C) The range means anything from 90 to 130 points is possible, so the outcome is unknowable; make no forecast.
- D) Confident of success: 130 points is within the observed 5-sprint maximum.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Work the three anchors. Mean case: 5 × 22 = 110 points — 20 short of 130. Worst observed: 5 × 18 = 90 — 40 short. Best observed: 5 × 26 = 130 — exactly enough, but only if the team hits its historical maximum five consecutive times, an outcome the 12-sprint record (mean 22) makes deeply improbable: the best case is not a plan, it is the edge of the distribution. Option B assembles this into the correct posture — quantified shortfall at the central estimate, explicit implausibility of the success path, and the pivot that matters: with throughput evidence this stable, the honest lever is scope (cut ~20+ points, prioritising by value) rather than hope. Option D plans on the maximum — the precise inverse of prudent forecasting. Option C mistakes a range for total ignorance: variability bounds the plausible outcomes and the mean locates their centre; "unknowable" abandons exactly the analysis the data supports. Option A reaches the right arithmetic and the wrong epistemics — "certain failure" overstates as badly as D understates, and forecloses the scope conversation that could still succeed. The senior craft here: delivery confidence is a distribution summarised honestly — central estimate, plausible range, and the decision the numbers point to — and "high risk on current scope; achievable with a 20-point cut" is a forecast a board can act on, where "might be fine" and "will fail" are both malpractice. Providing delivery confidence is your named duty; this is its arithmetic done properly.

**Question 12 (hard) — The re-baselining illusion**

Project Kelvin has re-baselined twice. Original plan: complete 40 milestones by December. First re-baseline (April): scope cut to 32 milestones, December unchanged. Second re-baseline (August): 28 milestones, December unchanged. Kelvin's latest report: "26 of 28 milestones on track — 93% delivery confidence." Measured against the original baseline, what is the on-track proportion, and what does the gap between the two figures represent?

- A) 93% either way; re-baselining does not affect the calculation.
- B) 65% against the original baseline (26 of 40); the 28-point gap between 93% and 65% is the cumulative scope reduction that two re-baselines have moved outside the measure — both figures are true, but they answer different questions and a board should see both.
- C) 70% against the original baseline (28 of 40); the gap is a data error.
- D) 81.25% against the original baseline; the gap is normal delivery variance.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Compute both views. Current-baseline view: 26 ÷ 28 = 0.9286 ≈ 93% ✓ — the report's arithmetic is honest as far as it goes. Original-baseline view: 26 milestones on track against the 40 originally committed = 26 ÷ 40 = 0.65 = 65%. The 28-point gap is neither error nor variance: it is the 12 milestones (40 − 28 = 12, i.e. 30% of original scope) removed across two re-baselines — commitments that have exited the denominator and therefore exited the measure. Option B states this precisely, including the crucial fairness point: both figures are true answers to different questions. "How is Kelvin doing against its current plan?" — 93%, and the team may genuinely be executing well. "How is Kelvin doing against what the organisation originally invested in?" — 65%, and the December date has been protected twice by paying in scope. A governance view showing only the first figure will record two quiet scope cuts as rising confidence — the re-baselining illusion — while showing only the second unfairly erases legitimate, approved re-planning. The portfolio-grade presentation is dual-baseline with annotation: current delivery confidence, original-scope delivery, and the approved change history connecting them. Distractor D (32.5 ÷ 40) manufactures a blend with no meaning; C measures scope rather than on-track work. This question is the numeric twin of a governance principle you will meet again in the situational section: measures must survive re-baselining, or re-baselining becomes the easiest way to hit any target — and designing measures that survive it is exactly the governance-process development your role owns.

### Preparation tips

- **Drill the senior toolkit until mechanical.** Weighted averages (products over weights), combined rates (totals over totals), change on the original base, expected-at-stage benefits, critical-path maxima, run-rate-plus-step forecasts. Six procedures cover ninety percent of portfolio arithmetic; fluency in them frees attention for the interpretive layer where senior tests discriminate.
- **Always compute both views of any contested figure.** Weighted and unweighted; current-baseline and original-baseline; points and percent; net and gross. The gap between the views is usually the analytical story, and knowing both makes you unambushable in committee.
- **Name the base aloud, every time.** "Percent of what?" catches wrong-base errors before they happen — in tests, in your own dashboards, and in figures others present to you. The discipline costs three seconds; base errors cost credibility.
- **Reconstruct one real dashboard monthly.** Take your portfolio's actual pack and recompute five headline figures from source. This is the highest-fidelity practice available, doubles as quality assurance, and periodically finds a live error — the best possible return on preparation time.
- **Classify variances before netting them.** Cost, timing, or delivery-in-disguise: practise the taxonomy on your own quarterly numbers until it is reflexive. Netting before classifying is how portfolios reassure themselves into surprises.
- **Rehearse forecast language.** "Central estimate X, plausible range Y–Z, achievable if..." — write the sentence for three live projects. Numeric fluency that cannot reach the page as decision-grade language is only half the skill your role requires.
- **Practise timed, then review untimed.** Sit a full set against the clock to train pacing, then re-derive every answer slowly. The timed pass builds speed; the untimed review builds the error taxonomy that prevents repeat mistakes.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Wrong-base division.** Change over the new value, spend over the profile, realisation over the end-state target — every variant misleads in a different direction. The original, the plan-at-stage, and the combined total are the bases that serve; name them explicitly.
- **Averaging rates across unequal groups.** Quarters, projects and teams almost never come in equal sizes. Totals over totals, without exception — and audit others' summaries for the averaged-rates signature.
- **Banking underspends without asking why.** Money unspent through delay is a delivery problem wearing a savings costume. Classify before you celebrate — or net.
- **Planning on the best observed case.** Historical maxima are distribution edges, not targets. Central estimates forecast; best cases decorate.
- **Letting re-baselining reset the measure.** Any metric whose denominator shrinks with each re-plan will show improvement while delivery erodes. Dual-baseline reporting is the antidote; insist on it.
- **Trusting the heaviest-looking path.** Critical paths are computed, not intuited — the longest chain, not the largest block, owns the date. Recompute after every duration change.
- **Presenting arithmetic without its assumption.** Straight-line forecasts assume linear burn; benefits ratios assume linearity with rollout; capacity sums assume full availability. The figure with its assumption is analysis; without, it is a hostage to the first person who asks.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment measures precision in reading, inference and written judgement, using material of the kind a senior portfolio manager handles daily: governance frameworks, assurance reports, ministerial and directorate correspondence, spend control policy, delivery narratives, and extracts from standards. The core format presents a passage followed by statements to classify as **True** (supported by the passage), **False** (contradicted), or **Cannot say** (not determinable from the passage). Senior-level variants add inference items (what follows), assumption items (what an argument silently requires), argument-evaluation items (where reasoning is weakest), and clear-writing judgement (which drafting serves the audience).

The typical format is 15 to 25 minutes online, four to six passages, twenty-odd statements, and distractors engineered around modal verbs, scope boundaries, dropped conditions, and plausible-but-unstated claims. The governing rule is strict textual discipline: the passage is the whole universe; professional knowledge stays outside.

Employers use verbal reasoning at senior level because language is the medium in which your role's authority operates. You interpret governance frameworks for teams who act on your interpretation; you certify positions to boards in sentences whose modal verbs carry legal and financial weight; you draft the papers and dashboards through which leadership perceives the portfolio; and you correct — diplomatically — the misreadings of others, from a delivery manager's hopeful reading of a spend control to a director's over-firm summary of a conditional commitment. Precision failures at this altitude are expensive in a characteristic way: they propagate. A misread "may" becomes a team's planning assumption becomes a missed statutory deadline; an over-strong summary sentence becomes a board's recorded position becomes an accountability dispute. The passages below are built from exactly these materials, and the explanations work through both the reading discipline and its workplace consequence.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Textual precision with rules** maps to **Governance and assurance**: developing governance processes and supporting others in applying them requires exact command of what framework language mandates, permits, excepts and omits — the may/must/should/unless architecture on which every control depends.
- **Inference discipline** maps to **Communicating analysis and insight** and your duty to provide stakeholders with information on performance and risk: separating what an update establishes from what it suggests is the difference between reporting and speculating — and communicating negative information credibly requires claims sized exactly to their evidence.
- **Assumption detection** maps to **Planning** and **Strategic thinking**: every delivery commitment and strategy document rests on unstated premises; finding the load-bearing ones is how you test delivery confidence and contribute meaningfully to strategy and policy development.
- **Argument evaluation** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**: influencing stakeholders and resolving issues means engaging with their reasoning — locating the weak link in a proposal, the legitimate kernel in an objection — rather than trading conclusions.
- **Clear-writing judgement** maps to **Communicating analysis and insight**: turning complex data into compelling, clear and actionable stories is finally a drafting skill, and sharing data communication skills with your team and across government makes your own written standards a multiplier.

### Practice questions

**Passage for Questions 1–3:**

"Portfolio assurance framework, section 7: Each programme must undergo an independent assurance review at least once every twelve months. The portfolio office may commission additional reviews where delivery confidence falls below 60% or where spend variance exceeds 15% of profile. Review findings are graded: 'critical' findings require a response plan within ten working days; 'significant' findings require a response plan within twenty working days; 'advisory' findings require no formal response but should be considered at the next planning cycle. A programme's SRO may challenge a finding's grading within five working days of the report; challenges are determined by the head of portfolio, whose decision is final. Programmes may not be granted more than one grading challenge per review."

**Question 1 (easy) — True, false or cannot say**

Statement: "A programme whose delivery confidence is 55% must be given an additional assurance review."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, if spend variance also exceeds 15%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Locate the operative clause: the portfolio office "may commission additional reviews where delivery confidence falls below 60%". Confidence of 55% satisfies the condition — but the verb is "may": the condition creates a discretionary power, not a duty. The statement's "must be given" asserts an obligation the passage does not contain; asserting obligation where the text grants discretion is contradiction, not absence — so False, not Cannot say. Option D compounds the error: the two triggers (confidence below 60%, variance above 15%) are alternatives ("or"), and neither converts "may" into "must" anyway. The distinction is the daily bread of governance interpretation: teams and SROs routinely read "may" clauses as entitlements or as threats, and both misreadings generate friction — a programme demanding the review it is "owed", or resenting the review it "shouldn't" get. Your role's answer, drawn from exact reading: the trigger makes an additional review available; the decision to commission remains the portfolio office's judgement, exercised on the case.

**Question 2 (moderate) — True, false or cannot say**

Statement: "An SRO who misses the five-day challenge window can appeal a critical finding's grading to the head of portfolio."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, because the head of portfolio's jurisdiction is unlimited

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage establishes a challenge route — within five working days, determined by the head of portfolio, decision final, one challenge per review. The statement asks about a case the passage does not regulate: a challenge attempted after the window. Does the framework's silence about late challenges mean they are barred (making the statement False) or that discretion exists to hear them (True)? The text says neither: it defines the in-window route and simply does not address out-of-window requests — no "challenges outside this window will not be considered", no residual-discretion clause. With the case unregulated by the passage, the answer is Cannot say. The instinct to answer False treats a stated window as an implied bar — a natural legal-style inference, but an inference nonetheless, resting on interpretive convention rather than the text; the test rewards noticing the difference. The workplace translation is immediate: when an SRO misses a deadline and asks "can I still challenge?", the exactly correct answer is "the framework doesn't provide for late challenges — it's a discretion question for the head of portfolio", which is both honest and procedurally sound. Senior interpretive craft lies largely in seeing where the rules end and discretion begins — and saying so, rather than manufacturing certainty in either direction.

**Question 3 (moderate) — Applying the framework**

A review of Programme Wye grades one finding 'critical' and two 'advisory'. Wye's SRO believes the critical finding overstates the issue and wants it re-graded; she also wants one advisory finding re-graded upward to 'significant' so it attracts a formal response from a supplier. Based only on the passage, which is correct?

- A) She may challenge both gradings, as they concern different findings.
- B) She faces a constraint: the framework allows her at most one grading challenge for this review, so she must choose which grading matters more — and any challenge must be lodged within five working days.
- C) She may not challenge at all, because critical findings are exempt from challenge.
- D) She may challenge the critical finding now and the advisory finding at the next review.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Assemble the constraints. The challenge right exists ("a programme's SRO may challenge a finding's grading") with a five-working-day window; nothing exempts critical findings, eliminating C. The binding rule is the last sentence: "Programmes may not be granted more than one grading challenge per review." Two desired challenges, one review, a one-challenge ceiling: she must choose — option B, which also correctly carries the deadline. Note what the question quietly tests: the challenge right is not limited by direction — the passage says "challenge a finding's grading", not "appeal against a grading's severity" — so re-grading upward is as challengeable as downward; option B's neutrality on which she should choose respects that. Option D invents a deferral mechanism the passage nowhere provides: the next review is a new review of new findings, not an appeals venue for old ones. The scenario is drawn from life: SROs frequently want multiple gradings revisited, and the portfolio manager who knows the one-challenge rule cold — and communicates it early, before the SRO burns the challenge on the lesser issue — turns textual precision into practical stakeholder service. Supporting others in applying governance processes means knowing the framework's ceilings as well as its rights.

**Passage for Questions 4–6:**

"Extract from an assurance review report: 'The programme's delivery of its core case-management capability is impressive: the team demonstrates strong engineering discipline and the beta service performs well against its published metrics. However, the review team was unable to verify the programme's benefits model. The model's adoption assumptions derive from a 2023 pilot involving 200 users in one region; the business case extrapolates these to a national user base of 1.4 million with materially different demographics. No sensitivity analysis was provided. The programme director advised that a refreshed adoption study is planned. Until its results are available, the review team regards the stated benefits figure of £8.2m per annum as unevidenced, though not necessarily wrong. This concern does not affect our positive assessment of technical delivery.'"

**Question 4 (easy) — True, false or cannot say**

Statement: "The review team found the programme's benefits figure to be wrong."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, because the model was unverifiable

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage's own words settle it: the review team "regards the stated benefits figure... as unevidenced, though not necessarily wrong". The statement claims the team found the figure wrong — but the passage explicitly distinguishes unevidenced from wrong and declines the second judgement. A statement asserting what the text expressly withholds is contradicted: False. Option D repeats the conflation with a causal dress: unverifiable means the evidence chain fails, not that the conclusion is false — the refreshed study could vindicate £8.2m entirely. This distinction — between "not proven" and "disproven" — is among the most consequential in assurance language, and its erosion is a familiar failure pattern: "unevidenced" in a review report becomes "the benefits are wrong" in a hostile summary becomes "the programme's case has collapsed" in a corridor conversation. As the portfolio's information steward, you will often be the person restoring the original claim's exact size — in both directions, since programmes equally convert "not necessarily wrong" into "endorsed". Neither conversion survives contact with the actual sentence, which is why the actual sentence is what you quote.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Identifying the core weakness**

According to the passage, what is the central defect in the programme's benefits model?

- A) The 2023 pilot was too long ago to be relevant.
- B) An extrapolation problem: assumptions from 200 users in one region are scaled to 1.4 million nationally across materially different demographics, with no sensitivity analysis to show how outcomes vary if the assumptions fail to transfer.
- C) The programme has no benefits model at all.
- D) The engineering team lacks discipline in benefits tracking.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The review's reasoning has a precise structure worth reproducing: the model exists (eliminating C) but its adoption assumptions rest on a small, unrepresentative sample — 200 users, one region — extrapolated four orders of magnitude to a national population the passage explicitly notes differs demographically. The missing sensitivity analysis is the aggravating factor: extrapolation from thin evidence can be legitimate when its fragility is quantified ("if adoption runs at half the pilot rate, benefits fall to £X"), and the absence of that quantification is what converts a modelling limitation into an assurance finding. Option B carries the full structure. Option A mislocates the defect in recency — the passage's concern is representativeness and scale, not the date; a 2023 pilot might be perfectly current. Option D transplants a compliment (engineering discipline is praised) into an unrelated criticism. Reading reviews at this resolution matters because your role brokers their consequences: the response plan should target the actual defect — commission the adoption study, add sensitivity analysis — and a programme that responds to option A's misreading (running a newer pilot of the same 200-user shape) would spend money without touching the finding. Extrapolation-without-sensitivity is also, incidentally, among the commonest defects in government business cases; the pattern recognition transfers directly to your investment committee reading.

**Question 6 (hard) — Evaluating a summary's fidelity**

A directorate summary of the review states: "Assurance found the programme technically excellent but its £8.2m benefits claim unsupported; benefits should be treated as at risk pending the refreshed adoption study." Which assessment of this summary is most accurate?

- A) Faithful: it preserves the technical praise, the evidential status of the benefits figure, and the pending remediation.
- B) Unfaithful: the review never called the programme technically excellent.
- C) Unfaithful: "unsupported" is materially harsher than "unevidenced, though not necessarily wrong".
- D) Unfaithful: the summary omits the sensitivity analysis, so it fails.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Audit the summary clause by clause against the source. "Technically excellent" compresses "impressive... strong engineering discipline... performs well against its published metrics" — a fair rendering of an emphatically positive technical assessment; option B's objection is word-matching, not meaning-matching, and summaries are licensed to compress meaning. "£8.2m benefits claim unsupported" against "unevidenced, though not necessarily wrong": "unsupported" and "unevidenced" are near-synonyms for the same evidential status, and the summary's "treated as at risk pending the refreshed study" actually preserves the not-necessarily-wrong nuance — at risk, not rejected — so option C's charge of material harshening fails; the summary keeps the door open exactly as the review did. Option D mistakes compression for infidelity: a summary must preserve the finding's force and status, not every supporting detail; the sensitivity analysis is evidence for the finding, safely recoverable from the source. So the summary is faithful: option A. The senior skill exercised here is bidirectional. You audit others' summaries for the two failure modes questions 4 and 6 bracket — hardening ("unevidenced" → "wrong") and softening ("at risk" → "minor caveat") — and you write your own summaries to survive the same audit. A workable test for any summary sentence: could the original author read it and say "yes, that is what I found"? This one passes; the corridor versions in Question 4's explanation do not.

**Passage for Questions 7–9:**

"Email from a programme director to the senior portfolio manager: 'Following Friday's prioritisation forum, I want to register my concern formally. The forum deferred our scaling investment for the second consecutive quarter, on the basis of the portfolio's capacity model. I understand the model shows the platform team fully committed. But the model counts the platform team at its funded establishment of twelve engineers, when we have been running at nine for five months due to recruitment freezes — so the "full commitment" it reports is calculated on three engineers who do not exist. Meanwhile our service's waiting list has grown 40% in six months, a cost the capacity model does not see at all. I am not asking the portfolio office to bypass prioritisation. I am asking whether decisions of this consequence should rest on a model with known stale inputs and no representation of demand-side cost. If the model cannot be corrected before next quarter's forum, I request that our case be considered alongside the model rather than through it.'"

**Question 7 (moderate) — True, false or cannot say**

Statement: "The programme director is asking the portfolio office to bypass the prioritisation process."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True in effect, whatever his words say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The email states its own position in terms: "I am not asking the portfolio office to bypass prioritisation." His actual requests are two and specific: correct the model's stale inputs before the next forum, or failing that, let the case be argued alongside the model rather than solely through it. Both requests operate within the prioritisation process — they contest the evidential instrument, not the forum's authority. The statement is directly contradicted: False. Option D deserves its own dismantling because it embodies a real and corrosive workplace habit: "true in effect" reasoning attributes to a correspondent the position you suspect behind their words, then holds them to it. A verbal reasoning test bars it because the passage is the universe; professional practice should bar it because it converts a procedurally careful stakeholder — and this email is conspicuously careful — into a rule-breaker by imputation, poisoning exactly the kind of legitimate challenge that improves portfolio instruments. Answering the email he wrote, rather than the email you suspect, is both the test discipline and the relationship discipline. If you have grounds to think the stated position masks another, the professional route is to test that directly in conversation — not to encode the suspicion into your summary for others.

**Question 8 (hard) — Locating the argument's strongest point**

Which element of the programme director's argument, if verified, most seriously undermines the forum's deferral decisions as made?

- A) The waiting list has grown 40% in six months.
- B) The capacity model counts twelve platform engineers while nine exist, so the "fully committed" finding that drove both deferrals was computed on 25% phantom capacity.
- C) The deferral has now happened twice.
- D) The programme director feels strongly enough to write formally.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Distinguish grievance from defect. Option C is procedural history — repetition aggravates but does not invalidate; two deferrals on sound evidence would be sound twice. Option D is intensity, which carries no evidential weight at all. Option A is real cost, and rhetorically powerful, but note its logical position: the model's blindness to demand-side cost is a design limitation arguing for a better instrument — it does not show the capacity finding itself was wrong. Option B is different in kind: if the team runs at nine while the model counts twelve, the model's core output — "fully committed" — was computed on inputs known to be false, and the commitment percentage is wrong in the decision-relevant direction (real utilisation on nine engineers is higher than modelled, meaning the scarcity used to justify deferral is worse than reported, or the allocation baseline is simply fictional). An input error contaminates the specific finding the forum relied on, for both decisions; that is what "undermines the decisions as made" means. The senior craft is triage of critique: when a stakeholder challenges an instrument, separate the fatal claim (verify first, fix fastest — here, reconcile the establishment figure this week) from the design critique (queue for the model's next iteration) from the context (acknowledge, log). The director has handed you the triage in one email; the skill is seeing which clause is which — and Question 9 asks what to do with it.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Choosing the response**

As the senior portfolio manager receiving this email, which reply best serves the portfolio? (Judge by the passage and sound stakeholder practice.)

- A) "The forum's decision followed due process and stands; the next cycle is the appropriate route for re-submission."
- B) "Thank you for the specific challenge. You are right that the model must be accurate to be decisive: we will verify the establishment discrepancy with the platform team this week and correct the model if confirmed. Demand-side cost is a real gap; I will propose it for the model's next iteration. If the correction is not landed before next quarter's forum, your case will be heard alongside the model, as you request — and I will report back on all three points by Friday."
- C) "The model is imperfect, as all models are; the forum weighs many factors and the deferral reflected the whole picture."
- D) "Given the error you have found, I will ask the forum to reverse the deferral and approve your scaling investment."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Map the reply against what the email actually contains: one verifiable factual challenge (establishment versus actual headcount), one design critique (demand-side blindness), and one procedural request (case alongside model if uncorrected) — from a stakeholder who has explicitly stayed inside the process. Option B answers all three, each at its correct speed: verification of the fatal claim this week (facts first — note it commits to verify, not to believe); the design gap acknowledged and routed to the instrument's improvement cycle; the procedural request granted conditionally and exactly as framed; and a dated report-back, which is what converts a gracious reply into an accountable one. That is stakeholder relationship management as your framework defines it — identifying and resolving issues, influencing through evidence, building the long-term relationship — and it also serves the portfolio's own interest: a capacity model with known false inputs mis-prices every allocation, not just this one; the director has done your quality assurance for you. Option A defends process while ignoring that the process's evidence is impugned — proceduralism as a shield, and a relationship-burning one. Option C is the seasoned evader's reply: true in general ("all models are imperfect"), unresponsive in particular, and it would teach every programme director that specific challenge earns vague absolution. Option D overcorrects: the error, if confirmed, invalidates the deferral's basis — it does not validate the investment; the remedy for a corrupted decision input is a re-decision on corrected inputs, not a reflex reversal that would itself bypass the forum. Between defending the instrument and abandoning it lies repairing it; B lives there.

**Passage for Questions 10–12:**

"Extract from draft portfolio strategy: 'Over the next three years the portfolio will shift investment towards services with the highest citizen impact, while maintaining the resilience of critical legacy systems. New investment proposals will be assessed against a citizen-impact weighting from 2027–28. Legacy remediation will be funded at not less than 20% of annual portfolio spend until the legacy risk register shows no systems rated critical for two consecutive years, after which the floor may be reviewed. Nothing in this strategy overrides statutory, security or safety obligations, which take precedence over the citizen-impact weighting in all cases.'"

**Question 10 (moderate) — True, false or cannot say**

Statement: "If no critical-rated legacy systems appear on the register for two consecutive years, the 20% funding floor will be removed."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True from 2027–28 onwards

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Parse the sequence the strategy actually constructs: the floor holds "until" the two-clean-years condition is met, "after which the floor may be reviewed". The condition's satisfaction triggers eligibility for review — a discretionary reconsideration whose outcome is unspecified: the review might retain, lower, or remove the floor. The statement asserts removal as the automatic consequence ("will be removed"), converting may-be-reviewed into will-be-removed — two escalations (discretion to certainty, review to removal) in three words. That contradicts the text's structure: False. This trigger-versus-outcome confusion is endemic in strategy and policy reading — "the clause says the freeze ends after two years" when the clause says the freeze becomes reviewable — and it matters asymmetrically: teams plan spending against the removal they inferred, then meet the review that was actually promised. Option D adds a date from the wrong clause entirely (2027–28 governs the citizen-impact weighting's application to new proposals, not the legacy floor). At your level, the compound conditional — condition, then discretion, then unspecified outcome — is the native grammar of strategy documents, and contributing to the development of strategy, as your framework expects, includes drafting such clauses so they cannot be misread: if the intent were automatic removal, the drafter should write it; if review, say what the review weighs. Precision reading and precision drafting are the same skill facing opposite directions.

**Question 11 (hard) — True, false or cannot say**

Statement: "Under the strategy, a security-critical remediation with low citizen impact could be funded ahead of a high-citizen-impact service improvement."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) False, because citizen impact is the strategy's paramount criterion

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Test the scenario against the exception clause: "Nothing in this strategy overrides statutory, security or safety obligations, which take precedence over the citizen-impact weighting in all cases." A security-critical remediation falls within the protected category; the clause states that such obligations take precedence over the citizen-impact weighting — precisely the priority relationship the statement describes. So the strategy not only permits the low-impact security item to be funded ahead of the high-impact improvement; in a genuine conflict it requires that ordering. "Could be" is comfortably supported: True. Option D reads the headline ("shift investment towards highest citizen impact") and stops before the exception — the classic first-paragraph answer to a last-paragraph question, and the reason the discipline of reading to the end is worth drilling even at senior level: strategy documents state their rule early and their rule's boundaries late, and the boundaries are where the hard cases live. The workplace version arrives predictably: a stakeholder challenges why an invisible security fix outranked their popular service improvement, brandishing the strategy's own headline commitment. Your answer is the exception clause, quoted — and the deeper point that the exception is the strategy: a portfolio that let impact-weighting override security obligations would be optimising a metric into a breach. **Question 12 (hard) — Clear-writing judgement at board level**

The portfolio board needs one paragraph on the strategy's legacy funding position for a mixed audience of executives. Which drafting is best?

- A) "Pursuant to the strategic framework, legacy remediation expenditure shall be sustained at a quantum not inferior to one-fifth of aggregate annual portfolio disbursement, subject to potential recalibration contingent upon the bi-annual absence of criticality-rated register entries."
- B) "Legacy funding is protected: at least 20% of annual portfolio spend goes to legacy remediation. That floor stays until the risk register shows no critical-rated systems for two consecutive years — at which point it becomes reviewable, not abolished. Security, safety and statutory obligations sit above all other priorities throughout."
- C) "We are spending a lot on legacy, as we must, but the strategy sensibly allows this to change as things improve."
- D) "The 20% legacy floor may be reviewed after two clean years on the register."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Assess each against the board-paragraph standard: complete enough to prevent misreading, plain enough to survive a busy read, and precise where precision changes decisions. Option B earns each clause: the floor stated with its number and base; the trigger condition exact (two consecutive clean years); the consequence exactly sized ("reviewable, not abolished" — pre-empting, in three words, precisely the misreading Question 10 tested); and the precedence hierarchy present, because an executive audience deciding trade-offs needs to know what outranks what. Option A is the same content embalmed — "quantum not inferior to one-fifth of aggregate annual disbursement" forces every reader to translate before understanding, and "bi-annual" actively corrupts the meaning (it suggests twice-yearly, where the source says two consecutive years); officialese does not merely slow reading, it breeds errors in transcription. Option C is confident vagueness: no figure, no condition, no hierarchy — a paragraph that feels informative and leaves the board unable to answer a single question about the position. Option D is accurate and radically incomplete: leading with the review clause while omitting the floor's size and the precedence rule, it frames a protection as a sunset — accuracy of clause, distortion of emphasis. The senior drafting standard B exemplifies: every number that governs a decision, every condition that gates a change, every hierarchy that resolves a conflict — and nothing else. Sharing data communication skills across your team, as your framework expects, starts with your own paragraphs being the ones people copy.

### Preparation tips

- **Drill the modal ladder daily.** Must, will, shall, should, may, might — plus their governance modifiers: "not less than", "at least once", "in all cases", "no more than one". Take any framework document you own and tag ten clauses by force. Statement distractors are almost always one rung of the ladder away from the text.
- **Practise trigger-versus-outcome parsing.** For every conditional you read this week — "until X, after which Y may be reviewed" — write down what is automatic and what is discretionary. Strategy misreadings cluster exactly there.
- **Audit summaries against sources.** Take a real assurance summary and its underlying report; check each summary clause for hardening ("unevidenced" → "wrong") and softening ("at risk" → "noted"). Doing this five times builds the ear that Question 6 tests — and that your board-pack review requires weekly.
- **Read to the exception.** Train the habit of asking "where does this document take back or bound what it just granted?" before concluding anything. Headlines state rules; final clauses state the cases that decide arguments.
- **Separate the email written from the email suspected.** In practice items and real correspondence alike, answer the stated position; test suspicions in conversation, never in summaries. This single discipline prevents most stakeholder-communication misfires.
- **Write the plain version.** For one dense paragraph a week — policy, strategy, review finding — draft the three-sentence board version with every governing number and condition intact. Clear-writing judgement is trained by writing, and your drafts are the portfolio's communication ceiling.
- **Time-box per passage.** Four to five minutes each, statements verified against the text one by one, flag and move when stuck. Coverage with discipline beats perfection on one dense passage — in the test and in the Thursday-night board pack alike.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Reading "may" as "must" — in either direction.** Discretionary powers are neither entitlements nor obligations. The commonest single error at every level, and the most expensive at yours, because your interpretations get acted on.
- **Treating silence as regulation.** A stated window does not bar late requests; a stated route does not exclude others — unless the text says so. "The framework doesn't address that" is a finding, and frequently the correct one.
- **Hardening or softening evidential status.** "Unevidenced" is not "wrong"; "at risk" is not "rejected"; "not necessarily wrong" is not "endorsed". Preserve the exact size of every claim you relay — assurance language is calibrated, and recalibrating it is corruption.
- **First-paragraph answers to last-paragraph questions.** Exceptions, precedence clauses and definitions of terms arrive late in documents and decide the hard cases. Never conclude before the final sentence.
- **"True in effect" imputation.** Attributing unstated positions to correspondents fails the test and poisons the relationship. Answer what was written.
- **Compression mistaken for distortion — and vice versa.** Summaries may compress detail; they may not shift force, status or emphasis. Learn to tell the difference in both your auditing and your drafting.
- **Confusing eloquent with clear.** The formal option that sounds authoritative and the vague option that sounds balanced are both traps; the best drafting carries every governing number and condition in plain words. Judge writing by what a busy reader can correctly repeat.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement test (SJT) presents realistic workplace dilemmas and asks you to evaluate candidate responses — most effective, least effective, full rankings, or independent ratings. It measures professional judgement against behavioural benchmarks rather than knowledge. For senior digital portfolio roles, the scenarios concentrate where the role's tensions live: assurance findings with political consequences, divergence between self-reported and evidenced delivery, pressure from principals and programmes, the calibration of challenge and support, the stewardship of standards under deadline, and the development of the people and processes around you.

Typical format: 15 to 25 scenarios, untimed or generously timed, with scoring keyed to experienced-practitioner consensus and the organisation's behaviour framework — for government digital roles, expect honesty and transparency, ownership, collaboration, user and citizen focus, inclusion, proportionate escalation, and improvement orientation. Senior-level instruments discriminate less on avoiding obviously bad options and more on ordering the plausible ones: several responses will be defensible, and the scoring rewards those who consistently pick the response that serves the institution — its decisions, its information quality, its people — over the response that merely resolves the moment.

Employers use SJTs at this level because senior judgement is the product. Your role certifies information boards act on, arbitrates between programmes and process, and sets — by example — the professional norms your analysts and delivery community absorb. The scenarios below are built accordingly: each explanation identifies the behaviours at stake, works the options against each other comparatively, and names the transferable principle, because at senior level you are not only sitting these instruments — you are the practitioner consensus against which they are increasingly scored, and the coach through whom others prepare.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Information-integrity dilemmas** map to **Communicating analysis and insight** and your duty to provide stakeholders with performance and risk information: scenarios about softening, delaying or selectively presenting findings test whether negative and positive information reach decision makers with equal fidelity and speed.
- **Standards-under-pressure dilemmas** map to **Governance and assurance**: managing governance processes and ensuring standards are met is tested precisely where compliance is costly — deadlines, seniority pressure, sympathetic circumstances — and the strong responses hold the standard while opening the legitimate route.
- **Blocker and issue dilemmas** map to **Planning** and your duty to give guidance and deal with issues so teams deliver faster: scenarios probe whether you remove impediments at the right level — fixing systems rather than symptoms, escalating on materiality rather than mood.
- **Influence and conflict dilemmas** map to **Stakeholder relationship management**: resolving issues between programmes, influencing senior stakeholders without authority, and building long-term strategic relationships are the recurring substance of senior SJT items — with the scoring favouring evidence-led, relationship-preserving challenge.
- **Development and inclusion dilemmas** map to **Communicating analysis and insight** (sharing skills with the team and across government) and to the leadership dimension of your level: how you handle a struggling analyst, a dominated forum, or credit for others' work signals the environment you create.
- **Improvement dilemmas** map to **Strategic thinking** and **Governance and assurance**: the strongest senior responses convert incidents into process improvements — identifying and recommending changes rather than absorbing recurring problems case by case.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — The pre-board rating request**

Sunday evening, before Monday's portfolio board. A programme director texts you: her programme's rating in your portfolio report is amber; she believes it should be green, says the two issues driving amber were "resolved on Friday", and asks you to update the rating before the board sees the pack. You cannot verify the resolutions tonight. What is the most effective response?

- A) Update to green — she is accountable for the programme and says the issues are resolved.
- B) Keep amber, and reply that you will gladly reflect Friday's resolutions once verified: propose she brings the resolution evidence to the board verbally, note in the meeting that verification is underway, and update the rating in-cycle when confirmed.
- C) Keep amber and do not reply until after the board.
- D) Split the difference: present the programme as "amber improving, green by next cycle".

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The rating is the portfolio office's assessment, published on its verification — that is the entire reason boards trust it over programmes' self-assessments. Option A converts your rating into her rating on an unverifiable Sunday-night claim; if the "resolutions" prove partial (Friday resolutions often do), the board has been misled through your signature. Option B holds the evidential line without a trace of obstruction: the door is explicitly open ("gladly, once verified"), the board gets the freshest information through the honest channel — her verbal account plus your verification status — and the rating follows evidence at the speed evidence arrives. This is the response that keeps both the record and the relationship, and it models the exact norm you want every programme to internalise: ratings move on verification, not advocacy, and late-breaking good news is welcome in the meeting as news, not in the pack as fact. Option C wins the record and needlessly loses the relationship — silence reads as disregard, and she hears your answer first at the board. Option D invents a hybrid status that commits a future rating ("green by next cycle") no evidence supports — diplomatic drafting in service of unverified claims is still unverified reporting. The principle, which recurs through this section: at senior level your signature is the assurance; spend it only on what you have checked.

**Question 2 (easy) — The analyst's error, found after publication**

Your portfolio analyst discovers he transposed two programmes' forecast figures in the quarterly pack published to leadership last week — making one look £600k better and the other £600k worse than reality. He comes to you visibly anxious, correction in hand, and says: "I can slip the fix into next quarter's pack — nobody has queried it." What is the most effective response?

- A) Agree — the error nets to zero at portfolio level, and a correction notice would dent the office's credibility.
- B) Issue the correction now to the pack's recipients, thank him for surfacing his own error quickly, involve him in drafting the correction so he learns the repair process, and separately review how the transposition escaped the pack's quality checks.
- C) Issue the correction yourself without naming him, and quietly tighten your personal review of his work.
- D) Issue the correction and formally note the error in his performance record as a data-quality incident.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Four behaviours are in tension: information integrity, the psychological safety that keeps errors surfacing, the development of your analyst, and systemic improvement — and option B is the only response serving all four. The correction goes now because two programmes' forecasts are materially wrong in leadership's hands: a £600k swing in each direction is decision-relevant (funding conversations, variance narratives), and "nets to zero" — option A's logic — repeats the aggregation fallacy this guide has met twice already: leadership acts on programme-level figures, not the net. The thanks is not softness; it is the incentive structure: an analyst thanked for self-reporting reports next time faster, and one punished — option D's instinct — teaches the whole office that concealment is safer, which is how portfolio data quietly rots. Note the boundary: D would be right for concealment or recklessness; a self-surfaced first transposition is the textbook case for repair-and-learn. Involving him in the correction converts the worst week of his quarter into the process lesson he will teach others. And the quality-check review is the senior half: a transposition reaching publication means the pack's reconciliation control failed — that is a system finding, not a person finding. Option C is the subtle failure: it protects him from a correction that was never a punishment, denies him the repair experience, and substitutes surveillance for system fix — the error's cause survives while trust quietly erodes. Correct fast, thank the discloser, fix the control: the senior error liturgy.

**Question 3 (moderate) — The forum's quiet capture**

You chair the portfolio's monthly dependency forum. Over recent months, one programme's delivery director — articulate, senior, well-prepared — has increasingly dominated: dependencies affecting his programme get resolved first and favourably; two smaller services' items are routinely squeezed into the final minutes or deferred; their representatives have begun sending apologies. Minutes show every decision individually defensible. What is the most effective response?

- A) Nothing — decisions are defensible, attendance is voluntary, and strong preparation deserves its wins.
- B) Restructure the forum's mechanics: time-boxed agenda ordered by portfolio priority rather than by voice, dependencies triaged on evidence before the meeting, written positions from all affected parties circulated in advance — and personally re-recruit the two smaller services, telling them what has changed and why their items need their presence.
- C) Take the delivery director aside and ask him to contribute less.
- D) Rotate the chair role to the delivery director, giving him responsibility for balance.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Diagnose structurally: every decision defensible, yet the pattern skewed — this is capture by process, not misconduct, and the tell is the smaller services' withdrawal, which converts soft dominance into hard absence: a forum whose affected parties stop attending is manufacturing consent, and its "defensible" decisions are increasingly decided by who remains. Option A mistakes the absence of rule-breaking for the absence of a problem; chairs own outcomes, not just procedures. Option B fixes the machine: priority-ordered agendas remove the first-mover advantage of confidence; pre-circulated written positions let evidence compete where airtime cannot; pre-meeting triage shrinks the space where advocacy beats analysis; and the personal re-recruitment matters because withdrawal is self-reinforcing — the services need both the signal that the structure changed and the experience of their items landing fairly before trust returns. Note that B needs no villain: the delivery director's preparation remains an asset; the redesign simply stops preparation from being the only currency. Option C attacks the person instead of the structure — asking your best-prepared participant to be worse is both unfair and wasteful, and it leaves the mechanics that produced the skew intact for the next strong voice. Option D hands the captured instrument to its principal beneficiary — accountability theatre with predictable results. The senior principle: when a governance forum drifts, redesign its mechanics before managing its personalities; structures are fixable, and fixing them is your job as chair. This is developing governance processes in its most human form.

**Question 4 (moderate) — The improvement that implicates you**

Reviewing assurance data, your new analyst finds that response plans for critical findings — required within ten working days — have averaged nineteen days over the past year, and that the tracking spreadsheet's owner field for a third of overdue plans reads simply "portfolio office". The process design, including that vague owner field, is yours from two years ago. She asks, carefully, how you would like her to present the analysis. What is the most effective response?

- A) Tell her to present it fully and factually, including the process-design cause and its authorship, and add your own commitment to a redesign with named owners and escalating reminders — then take both finding and fix to leadership together.
- B) Tell her to present the averages but attribute the delays to programme teams' slow responses, which is partly true.
- C) Thank her, take the spreadsheet, and handle the matter yourself from here.
- D) Tell her to hold the analysis until the redesign is complete, so the finding and fix land together in a better light.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The scenario tests whether your improvement orientation survives self-implication — the exact point where many otherwise strong senior operators fail. Option A does four hard things at once. It lets the evidence speak whole: nineteen against ten is a standards breach in the portfolio's own compliance machinery, and the "portfolio office" owner field is the diagnostic heart — accountability assigned to everyone is assigned to no one, and that design choice is the finding. It names authorship without theatre: "the process design is mine" costs one sentence and buys the credibility of everything else you will ever present. It pairs finding with fix — named owners, escalating reminders — which is what distinguishes accountability from confession. And it protects the analyst's integrity at the moment she is calibrating what this office rewards: she asked "how would you like me to present it" because she is testing whether truth here is negotiable; your answer is the norm she will carry for years. Option B corrupts her first major analysis with a partial truth and teaches her that findings bend around seniority. Option C — absorbing the analysis — removes her from her own work and, however benign your intent, looks exactly like suppression; note that "I'll handle it" is C's fatal phrase even when handling means fixing. Option D delays a live compliance finding for presentational advantage: leadership's ten-day standard is being missed now, and the finding's owner does not get to schedule its disclosure. Ensuring standards are met includes the standard your own process is failing; the leader who audits themselves first earns the right to audit everyone else.

**Question 5 (moderate) — The director's summary**

At the investment committee, a director summarises your carefully caveated assessment — "the programme can achieve March if the supplier's November release lands on time and the migration dress-rehearsal succeeds" — as: "Portfolio office confirms March is achievable. Next item." The minutes will record it. The committee is moving on. What is the most effective response?

- A) Let it stand — interrupting a director over nuance is disproportionate, and the caveats are in your written paper anyway.
- B) Intervene briefly now: "One precision for the minutes — March is achievable subject to two conditions, the November release and the dress-rehearsal; we will report against both. " Then let the meeting proceed.
- C) Say nothing in the meeting but email the secretariat afterwards asking for the minutes to reflect your written paper.
- D) Interrupt to restate your full assessment, taking the committee back through the analysis.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Size the stakes first: minutes are the institutional memory of what was promised, and the gap between "confirms achievable" and "achievable subject to two named conditions" is precisely the gap in which accountability disputes are later fought. If the supplier release slips and March fails, the minuted version has you confirming a date that missed; the caveated version has you flagging the exact risk that materialised. Your conditions were not nuance — they were the assessment. Option B corrects the record at the moment it forms, in one sentence, with a forward commitment ("we will report against both") that converts the correction from pedantry into service; twenty seconds, no relitigation, and the committee actually gains — decision-makers act differently on conditional commitments, which is why you conditioned it. Option A trades the record for comfort and hopes the written paper wins a fight against the minute later; institutional practice runs the other way — the minute is what gets quoted. Option C is better than silence but repairs downstream: the committee itself heard, and acted on, the unconditional version; secretariat corrections reach the file, not the decision. Option D is the calibration failure at the other end — reopening the full analysis spends the committee's patience to deliver what one sentence could. The senior skill is exactly that calibration: defend the integrity of your assessment at the cost of one graceful sentence, in the room, every time — and note the symmetry with Question 1: whether the pressure flattens your caveats towards optimism or lets them survive, your assessment's exact size is the thing you never let others resize. Communicating negative and positive information means the conditions travel with the conclusion, all the way into the minutes.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Least effective: the recurring blocker**

For the third time in four months, a programme's release has been delayed because the central security team's review queue exceeded its published ten-day turnaround — this time by four weeks. The programme's delivery manager is furious; the security team lead, when you called, cited two vacancies and a major incident. Which response is LEAST effective?

- A) Convene the security lead and the portfolio's delivery managers to build a shared forward view of review demand, so the security team can see load coming and programmes can book slots early — and take the structural capacity gap to leadership with the data on all three delays.
- B) Work with the security lead on interim triage criteria — which review types genuinely need the full process, which can be expedited on defined conditions — and publish the criteria to all programmes.
- C) Escalate the pattern to leadership with quantified delivery impact, recommending they either fund the security team's vacancies or formally accept the delivery consequences.
- D) Advise the programme to schedule future releases assuming a six-week security review, and share that guidance with other programmes.

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** A least-effective question where all four options are active and none is scandalous — the discrimination is in what each does to the system. Options A, B and C attack the mismatch between review demand and review capacity from three legitimate angles: A builds the demand-visibility mechanism that lets both sides plan (and carries the evidence upward); B extracts triage value from existing capacity — the classic proportionate-assurance move; C forces the actual decision (fund it or own the consequences) to the level that holds the resources — escalation on materiality, properly evidenced. Any of these, or all three, is sound senior practice. Option D is the quiet institutionaliser: it accepts the failed turnaround as the new normal, rebrands a breach of a published standard as a planning parameter, and — by sharing the guidance portfolio-wide — converts one team's capacity crisis into everyone's permanent six-week tax. Note its seductions: it is realistic (releases will stop slipping against expectations), kind (no one is confronted), and immediately useful — which is exactly why passive normalisation is the most common real-world failure at senior level: it feels like pragmatism. But the ten-day standard exists because slow security review delays citizen-facing improvements; the senior duty is to force the standard-versus-capacity contradiction to resolution, not to pad every plan until the contradiction disappears from view. Deal with issues to help teams deliver faster — not help them schedule around delivering slower. Buffering is occasionally right as an explicitly interim measure inside options A–C; as the response, it is surrender with a spreadsheet.

**Question 7 (moderate) — The confidential heads-up**

A trusted delivery manager tells you privately: his programme director — who reports his programme's status upward — has for two cycles reported "minor slippage, recovering" while the internal burn-down shows the programme will miss its statutory deadline by six to eight weeks unless descoped. He shows you the internal data on his phone. He asks you not to reveal how you know, fearing for his position on the programme. What is the most effective response?

- A) Promise full confidentiality and act as if you know nothing until official reporting catches up.
- B) Tell him materiality means you must act, but you can act through means that protect him: the portfolio office has standing authority to verify any programme's reporting — commission an evidence-based review of the programme's milestone and burn-down data through the normal assurance route, which will surface the same facts officially, and keep his name out of it entirely.
- C) Confront the programme director immediately with what the delivery manager showed you.
- D) Report both the discrepancy and its source to the programme director's line manager the same day.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Hold both duties in view: a statutory deadline at risk behind two cycles of misreporting is materially unignorable — option A's promised inaction leaves a legal obligation heading for a six-week miss while you knowingly certify a portfolio picture you have reason to disbelieve; "act as if you know nothing" is complicity dressed as discretion. But the informant's protection is not sentiment — it is infrastructure: your early-warning network exists because people like him believe disclosure to you is safe, and options C and D each destroy that belief in a different way. C burns him almost arithmetically (the director will reconstruct who had that burn-down and that access); D adds the formal record to the burning. One breached confidence and your portfolio's informal intelligence — which routinely runs months ahead of official reporting — goes silent. Option B resolves the dilemma through the mechanism that exists precisely for it: the portfolio office needs no named source to exercise its standing verification authority; a routine-framed assurance review of the programme's delivery data will surface the burn-down facts officially, attribute them to the evidence rather than the informant, and put the descoping decision where it belongs — in governance, weeks earlier than official reporting would have allowed. The sequencing matters: verify through the institutional route first; the conversation with the programme director then happens on official findings, not private tip-offs. The senior principle: never promise silence you cannot keep — promise instead that disclosure will be protected, and own an institutional route that makes the promise true. Managing assurance processes means keeping both the information flowing and the informants safe; portfolios that protect messengers get messages.

**Question 8 (hard) — The strategic misalignment nobody owns**

Preparing the annual portfolio review, you find that three separate programmes are each building case-working capability — different suppliers, different technology, overlapping user groups — with combined spend of £7m. Each was approved individually across three years; each is individually green; no governance body ever saw them side by side, because the investment committee reviews proposals, not the portfolio's accumulated shape. What is the most effective response?

- A) Note the duplication in the annual review's appendix; all three programmes passed governance properly, so no action is warranted.
- B) Present the duplication prominently in the review with options analysis — consolidation candidates, interoperability minimums, or accepted divergence with rationale — and propose the systemic fix: a portfolio-shape review at the investment committee, so accumulated capability overlap becomes visible before the next approval, with your office maintaining the capability map that enables it.
- C) Halt the two later programmes' case-working workstreams pending a consolidation study.
- D) Raise the duplication privately with the three SROs and let them agree a way forward among themselves.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The finding is real but the deeper finding is structural, and the scoring separates candidates on whether they see both. £7m of overlapping capability, every pound properly approved: this is not a compliance failure — it is a governance design gap, the predictable product of a committee that evaluates each proposal against its own case but never against the portfolio's accumulated shape. Option B treats both layers at their proper altitude. The instance gets honest options analysis rather than a predetermined answer — consolidation is not automatically right: migration costs, contractual positions and genuine requirement differences can make accepted-divergence-with-rationale the value-for-money answer, and presenting options respects that the decision belongs to governance, informed by your analysis. The system gets the fix that prevents recurrence: a portfolio-shape view at the committee, fed by a capability map your office owns — which is strategic thinking's contribution-to-strategy duty in its most concrete form, and exactly what "evaluating current strategies to ensure business requirements are met" looks like in practice. Option A files a £7m finding in an appendix — proceduralism ("all approved properly") answering a question nobody asked; the issue was never propriety but value. Option C converts your finding into unilateral delivery intervention — halting funded programmes is a governance decision made on the options analysis you have not yet presented, and making it yourself both exceeds your authority and pre-judges the study. Option D delegates a portfolio-level trade-off to the three parties least able to resolve it — each SRO is accountable for their own programme's success, so voluntary consolidation asks someone to volunteer their own scope away; predictable outcome: polite meetings, no change. Surface the instance, fix the blindness: the senior double move, again.

**Question 9 (hard) — Rank the responses: the deadline and the data protection question**

Thursday. A programme launching Monday — ministerially announced, media expected — asks you to sign the portfolio's go-live readiness confirmation. Everything is green except one item: the data protection impact assessment (DPIA) was updated for a late design change (a new analytics module) but the data protection officer's (DPO) review of that update, normally five working days, has not happened. The programme director says the module is "plainly low-risk" and the DPO's office has confirmed they cannot review before Monday. Rank these responses from most to least effective:

1. Sign the confirmation with the DPIA item explicitly excepted: readiness confirmed except DPO review of the analytics update, with a recommendation the module launch disabled until reviewed — and put the residual decision (launch the module unreviewed, or launch without it) to the SRO and DPO's office today, escalating to the head of portfolio if unresolved by Friday noon.
2. Refuse to sign until the DPO review completes, whatever happens to Monday.
3. Sign the confirmation clean, noting the DPIA point verbally to the programme director — the module is probably low-risk and the announcement is immovable.
4. Sign clean, but email the DPO asking them to review urgently next week and confirm retrospectively.

- A) 1, 2, 4, 3
- B) 1, 2, 3, 4
- C) 2, 1, 4, 3
- D) 1, 4, 2, 3

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Work the ranking pairwise. Response 1 first, and by a distance: it refuses to launder the gap (the exception is in writing, in the confirmation itself), it engineers the risk down (disabling the module until reviewed converts "launch with unreviewed processing" into "launch without the new processing" — usually the whole answer, since the rest of the service is genuinely ready), and it routes the residual decision to the accountable parties on an explicit clock. This is the senior signature: neither the standard nor the deadline is sacrificed, because the confirmation instrument is used precisely — signed for what is true, excepted for what is not. Response 2 versus the two clean-signature options is the next discrimination: 2 is blunt — it may cost a ministerial launch that response 1 shows was salvageable, and its indifference to consequence ("whatever happens") is a calibration failure — but it never certifies what it has not verified. The instrument stays honest; only the optimisation is missing. Responses 3 and 4 both sign a confirmation stating something false — that readiness checks are complete when one is not — differing only in decoration. Between them: 4 at least creates a written trail and a review commitment, while 3 buries the gap in a verbal aside and substitutes the programme director's "plainly low-risk" for the DPO's actual review — seniority of assertion replacing the control, which is exactly what readiness confirmations exist to prevent; and if the module is not low-risk, the record shows your clean signature and a conversation nobody minuted. So: 1, 2, 4, 3 — option A. The portable rule: when a control cannot complete before a deadline, the honest instruments are exception, descope, or delay — never the clean signature with private reservations. Ensuring standards are met sometimes means signing precisely, not signing less.

**Question 10 (hard) — The talented analyst and the visible programme**

Your strongest portfolio analyst — eighteen months in role, clearly ready for more — has been asked for by name by a high-profile programme, on secondment for six months. Losing her would set back your reporting automation project by a quarter and leave you short for the annual review cycle. She wants to go but says she will stay if you need her. Your head of portfolio has left the decision with you. What is the most effective response?

- A) Keep her — the portfolio's needs are real, the request is only a secondment, and she has said she is willing to stay.
- B) Release her with visible support: negotiate the start date around the annual review's peak if possible, use the gap to stretch your second analyst into her responsibilities with your coaching, and agree with her what capability she will bring back — treating the secondment as the portfolio community's development pipeline working as designed.
- C) Propose a compromise: she splits her weeks between the secondment and your office for the six months.
- D) Release her only if the programme funds a backfill contractor for the full period.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Read what her "I'll stay if you need me" actually is: not indifference but deference — she has handed you the decision and will read your answer as evidence of what this profession does with its talented people. Option A takes the loyalty she offered and converts it into a cost she pays: the automation project keeps its quarter, and eighteen months from now your best analyst — passed over for the visible opportunity she earned — is interviewing elsewhere, having learned that being needed here means being kept here. Retention through capture is retention borrowed at interest. Option B is the senior calculus done honestly. The costs are real and managed rather than denied: the start-date negotiation respects the annual review; the second analyst's stretch converts the gap into a second development gain (succession depth is built exactly in these gaps, and your coaching duty — supporting the development of the people around you — is served, not suspended, by her absence); the agreed return-capability makes the secondment an investment with a specified yield. And the profession-level frame is the one your level requires: portfolio careers are built through cross-programme movement, the community's health depends on its best people circulating, and the office that visibly develops and releases talent recruits the next generation of it without trying. Option C — the split week — is the compromise that fails both sides: high-profile programmes need presence, not Tuesdays, and your office gets half an analyst with a divided head; it is option A wearing generosity. Option D makes her development conditional on a funding negotiation — sometimes backfill is negotiable and worth raising, but as a precondition it holds her opportunity hostage to inter-programme accounting. People development at senior level is measured by where your alumni are, not by how long you kept them.

**Question 11 (hard) — Most and least effective: the benchmark you commissioned**

You commissioned an external benchmark of your portfolio's delivery performance, expecting it to evidence improvement. Draft findings: delivery predictability genuinely improved 20%; but portfolio overhead costs run 35% above comparator organisations, and the benchmark attributes this partly to reporting processes you personally designed and defended. The consultancy asks whether you would like to "contextualise the overhead finding" before the final report goes to leadership. Which response is MOST effective, and which LEAST?

Responses:

1. Ask the consultancy to move the overhead finding to an annex and lead the report with predictability.
2. Let the report stand as drafted; add a management response accepting the overhead finding, committing to a costed simplification of the reporting processes — including the ones you designed — with dates, and present both finding and response to leadership yourself.
3. Challenge the benchmark's comparator set as not like-for-like, delaying the report until the methodology is re-examined.
4. Accept the report but present it to leadership through your head of portfolio, distancing yourself from the overhead finding.

- A) Most effective: 2; least effective: 1
- B) Most effective: 2; least effective: 4
- C) Most effective: 3; least effective: 1
- D) Most effective: 1; least effective: 3

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The scenario is Question 4's self-implication test at higher stakes and with a subtler temptation set — note that nothing here requires falsifying anything; every weak option merely arranges emphasis, methodology or messenger. Most effective: response 2, and the reasoning deserves full articulation. The report stands because you commissioned it to learn, and a benchmark pruned of its uncomfortable finding is a press release; the management response converts the finding into committed, costed, dated action — which is what leadership actually needs from a 35% overhead signal; the explicit inclusion of your own processes in the simplification, presented by you, buys the only commodity that matters at your level: the credibility of everything you will ever present after. There is also a harder truth: the overhead finding and the predictability finding may be causally linked — your heavyweight reporting may have bought the predictability — and response 2's costed simplification is where that trade-off gets tested honestly rather than asserted defensively. Least effective: response 1 — but weigh it against 3 and 4 carefully, because the discrimination is the question's point. Response 3 delays through methodology challenge: sometimes legitimate (comparator sets genuinely mislead), here suspect because the challenge follows the finding's direction — but a methodology re-examination at least leaves the finding intact to survive scrutiny, and if the comparators are sound the report lands late but whole. Response 4 accepts the whole report — finding, emphasis, methodology untouched — and games only the messenger; cowardly, visible to your head of portfolio, but the information reaches leadership intact. Response 1 alone reshapes the report itself: annexing the finding while leading with the favourable one manipulates what leadership reads and weighs, using your position as commissioner to launder your position as subject — the information itself arrives pre-spun. Ordering the failures: spin the report (1) is worse than delay the report (3) is worse than duck the presentation (4); and 1 is the worst because it is the one leadership cannot detect. Most 2, least 1: option A. Commission audits you might fail; publish the ones you do; respond with dated commitments — the senior improvement loop, run on yourself first.

**Question 12 (hard) — The minister's office and the portfolio's truth**

Wednesday afternoon. The minister's office calls your head of portfolio's office; with your head of portfolio abroad, the query reaches you: the minister will be asked in the House tomorrow about Programme Solway's much-reported delays, and the office wants "a line confirming the programme is back on track". Solway's position, per your latest verified data: recovery plan agreed and on schedule for six weeks; two of nine recovery milestones delivered, both on time; the critical third milestone — supplier performance fix — due in twelve days; delivery confidence 60% and rising. "Back on track" is neither clearly true nor clearly false. What is the most effective response?

- A) Provide the line requested — the recovery plan is on schedule, so "back on track" is defensible, and ministers' offices need usable lines.
- B) Provide a line that is both usable and exact: "A recovery plan is in place and on schedule; the first milestones have been delivered on time; the critical supplier fix completes within a fortnight, and delivery confidence is improving" — and offer the office a short factual briefing note tonight so the minister can handle follow-up questions accurately.
- C) Decline to provide any line until your head of portfolio returns — parliamentary lines are above your delegation.
- D) Refer the minister's office directly to Programme Solway's director for the line.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Two failures are on offer and option B refuses both. The first failure is the comfortable line: "back on track" asserts a completed recovery when the evidence shows a recovery two-ninths delivered with its critical test twelve days away and confidence at 60% — if the supplier fix slips, the minister has told the House something your data never supported, and the correction will be sourced to your office's Wednesday line; parliamentary accuracy is the sharpest version of the principle this whole section has drilled, because the record is Hansard and the accountability is constitutional. The second failure is the empty hands: option C treats your head of portfolio's absence as suspending the portfolio's duty to inform — but the data is verified, the facts are yours to state, and a minister's office left lineless does not go quiet; it improvises, and tomorrow's improvised answer is built from the press coverage instead of your dashboard. Option B threads it exactly as the civil service is supposed to: every clause verifiable against your data ("plan on schedule", "first milestones on time", "confidence improving" — note it claims the trajectory, never the destination), usable in the chamber as drafted, and reinforced by the briefing note that lets the minister survive follow-ups — which is the part the office will remember. Deference to your absent head is preserved by copying them immediately, not by paralysis. Option D abdicates the portfolio's assurance role at its most consequential moment: Solway's director is precisely the advocate whose reporting the portfolio office exists to verify; routing the minister to the advocate deletes the verification layer exactly when it matters most. The senior principle, final form: your role's product is information that power can safely act on — and the test of every line you provide is whether it still reads true after the twelve days run. Serve the need, keep the exactness: at this level they are the same job.

### Preparation tips

- **Study the behaviours you will be scored against.** Reread the Civil Service Behaviours for your grade and your organisation's values before the test. Senior SJT scoring keys cluster around integrity of information, proportionate escalation, structural rather than personal fixes, and self-applied standards — the throughlines of every question above.
- **Practise pairwise comparison, not option-picking.** Senior items offer several defensible responses; the marks live in the ordering. For each practice scenario, articulate why the second-best fails against the best — that articulation is the skill being measured.
- **Learn your recurring temptations.** The plausible failures at senior level are patterned: normalising the broken standard, absorbing what should be surfaced, spinning what should be reported, keeping who should be released, signing what needs excepting. Identify which pattern tempts you personally; the test — and the job — will find it.
- **Rehearse the exception instruments.** Signed-with-exceptions, caveated lines, dual-baseline reports, review-not-removal: much senior judgement is knowing the honest middle instrument between refusal and capitulation. Practise drafting them until each takes one sentence.
- **Run the twelve-day test.** For any line, rating or confirmation you are pressed for, ask: if the uncertain thing goes wrong on its natural timescale, does what I said still read true? Answers that survive their own futures are the only ones worth giving.
- **Mine your history for anchors.** Write down three real moments where you softened, delayed or absorbed — and what the response graded "most effective" here would have been. Lived recalibration transfers to test performance better than any amount of fresh reading.
- **Answer as the practitioner consensus.** Senior SJTs are normed on experienced professionals' judgements. Answer as the portfolio manager the profession would endorse — honest under pressure, structural in fixes, generous with credit, exact with information — and consistently so across all scenarios.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Certifying the unverified.** Ratings moved on Sunday-night texts, confirmations signed on "plainly low-risk", lines built on advocacy — every variant spends your signature on someone else's assertion. Verification is not distrust; it is the product.
- **Normalising the broken standard.** Padding plans around a failed turnaround, filing £7m findings in appendices, waiting cycles on evidenced divergence — accepting the unacceptable gracefully is the most common senior failure because it photographs as pragmatism.
- **Protecting people from information instead of with process.** Quiet corrections, absorbed analyses, unnamed handling — suppression with kind motives is still suppression, and it teaches your best people that truth here is negotiable.
- **Burning sources or promising silence.** Both destroy the informal intelligence network your assurance depends on. The honest promise is protected disclosure through institutional routes — build those routes before you need them.
- **Fixing personalities when the structure is the problem.** Dominated forums, captured agendas, chronically late responses: redesign mechanics first. Structural fixes scale and depersonalise; behavioural requests do neither.
- **Applying your standards to everyone but yourself.** The overhead finding on your process, the vague owner field you designed, the benchmark you commissioned — self-implication is where senior integrity is actually tested. Audit yourself first, visibly.
- **Confusing refusal with rigour.** Declining to sign, line, or decide until conditions are perfect can be as damaging as capitulation. The senior instruments — exceptions, caveats, conditional confirmations, escalation with a clock — exist precisely so that rigour and usefulness travel together. Know them cold.

On the day, trust your methods: estimate before calculating, verify before certifying, read to the exception, and compare options pairwise.
