## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the reasoning your role demands, presented through practical materials rather than abstract puzzles. At lead level the materials are organisational and the reasoning is diagnostic. You will meet bodies of evidence assembled by other people, standards being applied inconsistently across teams, findings that appear to conflict until you work out what each measured, and prioritisation problems where the units being ranked are programmes rather than tasks.

The format is typically an online test of fifteen to thirty minutes covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective against a norm group of comparable seniority. Adaptive platforms raise difficulty after correct answers, so a hard test is generally a good sign, and most employers receive an acuity report distinguishing speed from accuracy, which rewards precision.

Three things distinguish this section at your level.

The first is that most of the reasoning is about other people's work. Assuring research quality across an organisation means you rarely see the raw data. You see a report, a slide, a claim in a board paper, and you have to judge from the visible traces whether the reasoning underneath it holds. That is a specific skill — reading an output for the shape of the study behind it — and it is what several items below exercise.

The second is synthesis across sources gathered for different purposes. Strategic insight almost never comes from a single study. It comes from noticing that a discovery in one team, a live-service analytics pattern in another, and a support desk trend in a third are three views of the same underlying problem. That kind of reasoning requires holding several incomplete pictures in mind without forcing them into premature agreement.

The third is that the prioritisation questions are genuinely hard. When the units are programmes with different time horizons, different reversibility and different stakeholders, no option dominates. The discriminating factor is whether you reason from value of information — how much does a decision change as a result of what research tells you, and is there still time — rather than from importance, urgency or profile.

You need to memorise nothing. What you need to bring is the discipline not to let experience substitute for examination.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Auditing against a standard**

Your organisation's standard requires every research output to state: the research questions, the method, the sample composition including access needs, the analysis approach, the limitations, and the date. A report you are assuring states the research questions, the method, the analysis approach and the date, describes the sample as "12 participants recruited via the panel", and contains no limitations section. How many requirements are unmet?

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Work through all six. Research questions, method, analysis approach and date are present. The sample description gives a number and a recruitment route but says nothing about composition or access needs, so that requirement is unmet. Limitations are absent, so that is unmet too. Two requirements fail, giving B. Option A typically comes from accepting "12 participants recruited via the panel" as sufficient sample description, which is the more interesting error of the two: it is a statement about sample *source*, not sample *composition*, and the distinction is exactly what the standard exists to force. Note which requirements failed — access needs and limitations are the two most commonly omitted across almost every organisation, and if you are assuring at scale, those are the fields to check first.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction from an organisational rule**

Your standard states: "Research involving participants who may be considered vulnerable requires an approved safeguarding protocol, unless the research is conducted entirely through an organisation already covered by a standing partnership agreement." A programme lead reports: "Our study with people experiencing homelessness ran last month without an approved safeguarding protocol." Which must be true?

- A) The standard was breached.
- B) The study ran through a standing partnership organisation.
- C) Either the study ran entirely through a standing partnership organisation, or the standard was breached.
- D) Participants were placed at risk.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A conditional with a stated exception, plus facts silent on the exception, licenses only the disjunction. Options A and B each assume a branch. Option D is the interesting distractor at your level, because it is the conclusion your professional instinct reaches for — but running without a protocol is a governance failure, and whether anyone was actually placed at risk is a separate empirical question the facts do not answer. Only C holds in every case. Note the word "entirely" in the exception, which narrows it substantially: a study that used the partnership for most recruitment but approached some participants directly would not qualify, and that is precisely the kind of partial compliance teams tend to assume is sufficient.

**Question 3 (moderate) — Reading a study from its output**

A report you are assuring states: "Participants were positive about the new service. All 16 participants said they would recommend it." The report also notes that participants were recruited from the department's user panel, that sessions took place in the department's offices, and that the recommendation question was the final item in a facilitator-led session. What is the most important observation?

- A) Sixteen participants is too small a sample for a recommendation measure.
- B) Three separate features — panel recruitment, departmental setting, and a facilitator asking directly at the end — all bias in the same direction towards socially desirable responses, so unanimity here is evidence about the conditions rather than about the service.
- C) A recommendation question is not a good measure of service quality.
- D) The report should have used a validated scale.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Unanimity is itself the signal that something is wrong, and the strongest observation identifies why. Each factor alone would warrant caution: a departmental panel selects for people well disposed to the department, a government building primes deference, and a direct question from the facilitator at the end of a session invites the answer the participant believes is wanted. Because all three push the same way, they compound rather than partially cancel, and a result of sixteen out of sixteen is close to what you would predict from the conditions regardless of the service. Option C is a fair secondary point. Option A misidentifies the issue as size when it is validity — 160 participants under the same conditions would produce the same unanimity. Option D is a technical fix that leaves all three biases in place. When you feed this back, name the compounding, because fixing one factor changes nothing.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Strategic prioritisation across programmes**

You lead research across four programmes and can add substantial capacity to one this quarter:

1. Programme A is choosing a service model next month, committing four years of build.
2. Programme B runs a live service with a persistent 28% drop-off affecting 200,000 users a year.
3. Programme C launches in six weeks with screens already signed off.
4. Programme D is preparing a business case due in nine months.

Which is strongest?

- A) B, because it affects the most users.
- B) A, because the decision is imminent, commits four years, and research is the only input that could still change it — giving it by far the highest value of information.
- C) C, because launching untested is a risk.
- D) D, because business cases shape long-term investment.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Value of information is the right frame: how much does the decision change as a result of what research says, and is there time for it to? Programme A scores highest on both — a service model choice is precisely where research shifts answers rather than decorating them, the window is open now, and four years of commitment makes the cost of a wrong choice enormous. Programme B affects more users but has been stable for months and will remain so for three more; deferring costs comparatively little, and analytics may be the better first instrument there anyway. Programme C is the weakest despite sounding responsible: signed-off screens six weeks from launch means findings arrive with nowhere to go, which is research for reassurance rather than for decisions. Programme D has nine months. Note that at your level the profile trap takes the form of D rather than a named individual — business cases attract attention, and attention is not the same as decision leverage.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Cross-programme synthesis**

Three unrelated pieces of evidence land in the same month. A discovery in the benefits programme finds people struggle to prove their identity when they lack a passport or driving licence. Live-service analytics for a licensing service show a 45% drop-off at identity verification. The support desk reports that identity queries are its highest call driver across four services. What is the strongest synthesis?

- A) Three teams have identified usability problems with their identity steps.
- B) The organisation has a cross-cutting identity assurance problem affecting multiple services, most likely concentrated among people without common photographic documents — which is a strategic issue for the roadmap rather than four separate service fixes.
- C) Identity verification is difficult and should be simplified.
- D) The three findings are unrelated and should be reported separately.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The synthesis that adds value is the one that identifies a shared cause and reframes the unit of the problem. Three services showing difficulty at the same step, with a discovery pointing at document availability as the mechanism and a support desk confirming volume at scale, together indicate something structural rather than three coincidental interface issues. Option B also draws the consequence that matters: this belongs on a roadmap as a cross-cutting capability question, not in four separate backlogs where each team will build its own partial workaround. Option A describes the findings without synthesising them. Option C is true and useless as strategic insight, because "simplify it" tells nobody what to do. Option D misses the pattern entirely. Producing strategic insight from research programmes is a named part of your role, and this is its characteristic shape: noticing that several teams are describing one thing.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Feedback on a synthesis**

A researcher presents an organisational insight report drawing on eleven studies. One headline claim — "users across all services want proactive notifications" — is supported by material from three studies, all of which were about services with statutory deadlines. What is the most useful feedback?

- A) The claim is invalid and should be removed.
- B) Note that the supporting evidence comes exclusively from deadline-driven services, so the claim as written over-generalises; suggest either narrowing it to that service type or testing whether it holds in services without deadlines before it enters the roadmap.
- C) Suggest adding more studies.
- D) Approve it; three studies is a reasonable basis.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The evidence is real and the scope of the claim is wrong, and the distinction matters because this report is destined for a roadmap. Deadline-driven services create an obvious and specific reason to want proactive notification — you are at risk of missing something — which may not transfer to services people use once, or voluntarily, or where there is nothing to miss. Option B preserves the finding, corrects the scope, and offers two proportionate routes. Option A discards something real. Option D lets an over-generalised claim reach a roadmap where it will justify investment across services it was never evidenced for. Option C is not wrong in principle but is the expensive option before the cheap one — narrowing the claim costs nothing. Assuring quality at organisational level frequently comes down to this: the evidence is sound, and the sentence built on it reaches further than the evidence goes.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Diagnosing a systemic problem**

Across your organisation, six teams consistently report that recruitment produces digitally confident participants in work, despite services serving many people who are neither. All six use the central recruitment contract. What is the strongest diagnosis and response?

- A) Each team should supplement its recruitment locally.
- B) The contract's supply model is structurally mismatched to the organisation's user base; the response is to document the pattern with evidence across all six teams, specify what suitable supply would look like, and take it to whoever owns the contract — while enabling teams to use partnership routes in the meantime.
- C) The teams should write better screeners.
- D) Recruitment should be brought in-house.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Six teams with the same problem and the same supplier is a supply problem, not six recruitment problems, and diagnosing it at the right level is the whole point of your role. Option B does the systemic work — evidence across teams is what moves a contract, and a specification is what a contract owner can act on — while unblocking teams immediately through partnership routes so that six studies are not held hostage to a procurement timeline. Option A accepts the structural failure and distributes the cost across every team indefinitely. Option C misdiagnoses: a screener filters the pool the supplier provides, and no screener can produce a participant type the supply does not contain. Option D may eventually be right and is a large claim to make without first specifying what is needed and testing whether the existing route can meet it. Note the pattern worth generalising: when the same problem appears in several teams, the fix almost never belongs in the teams.

**Question 8 (harder) — Scoping a flaw**

A study you are assuring used a prototype with a broken save-and-return function throughout. The report makes four claims: (i) participants could not find the eligibility criteria; (ii) participants abandoned the journey when interrupted; (iii) sessions took longer than expected; (iv) participants misunderstood the declaration wording. Which claims are compromised?

- A) All four.
- B) None; the fault was incidental.
- C) Claims (ii) and (iii) are directly compromised, since an inability to save and return plausibly caused both the abandonment on interruption and the extended durations, while (i) and (iv) concern comprehension of content and are largely unaffected.
- D) Only claim (ii).

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The skill is scoping a flaw precisely rather than treating it as total or trivial. A broken save-and-return function makes interruption catastrophic rather than inconvenient, so claim (ii) is manufactured by the artefact; it also removes a natural pause point, plausibly extending sessions, so claim (iii) is compromised too. Claims (i) and (iv) concern whether participants could locate and understand content, which the fault does not touch — a participant who cannot find the eligibility criteria could not find them with a working save function. Option A discards two sound findings and, at organisational scale, teaches teams that any fault invalidates everything, which encourages concealment. Option B leaves two artefactual findings in circulation. Option D catches the obvious compromise and misses the duration one. An audit that condemns everything is as unhelpful as one that condemns nothing, because neither tells the team what it can rely on.

**Question 9 (harder) — Contradiction between programmes**

Two teams researching adjacent services reach opposite conclusions about the same user group: one reports that users prefer to complete everything in a single sitting, the other that users prefer to save and return. Both studies look methodologically sound. What is the strongest response?

- A) Commission a third study to settle it.
- B) Examine what differs between the two contexts before treating it as a contradiction — the services may impose different demands, such as whether third-party documents are needed — because a preference expressed about a fifteen-minute task and one about a three-week process are answers to different questions.
- C) Report both and note the inconsistency.
- D) Treat the larger study as authoritative.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Apparent contradictions between sound studies are usually context differences masquerading as disagreements, and the diagnostic move is to ask what each user was actually responding to. If one service can be completed in one sitting and the other requires waiting for documents from an employer or a landlord, then "prefer a single sitting" and "prefer to save and return" are both true statements about different situations, and the synthesis — that preference depends on whether the task can be completed with information the user already holds — is more useful than either finding alone. Option B reaches that. Option A spends money to answer a question that dissolves on inspection. Option C abdicates the synthesis your role exists to produce. Option D picks a winner on an irrelevant criterion. This is characteristic of cross-programme work: the interesting insight is frequently in the difference between contexts rather than in either result.

**Question 10 (harder) — Standards that are not being followed**

Your organisational research standard requires ethics review for research with people who may be considered vulnerable. Reviewing the past year, you find that eleven of fourteen qualifying studies went through review; the three that did not were all in one directorate, all under ministerial deadline pressure, and all conducted by experienced researchers. What is the strongest response?

- A) Report the three breaches to the head of profession and require retrospective review.
- B) Treat the concentration as diagnostic: three failures in one directorate under the same pressure suggests the review process is not usable under deadline conditions, so investigate what the process demands and how long it takes, then redesign it to have a fast route — while addressing the specific breaches.
- C) Remind all researchers of the requirement.
- D) Introduce a mandatory checkpoint before all fieldwork.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The distribution of the failures is the most informative thing in the data. If the three breaches were scattered across directorates and researchers, individual conduct would be the explanation. Concentrated in one directorate, under the same pressure, by experienced people who presumably know the requirement, they point at a process that cannot be complied with when time is short. Option B investigates that and fixes the cause while still handling the instances — the "while addressing the specific breaches" clause matters, because diagnosing a systemic cause is not a reason to ignore individual failures. Option A handles the instances and guarantees recurrence. Option C is the weakest response available to any compliance problem; people who know a rule and broke it under pressure will not be changed by being told the rule again. Option D adds friction to the eleven studies that complied in order to catch the three that did not, which is how compliance burdens accumulate and how the next workaround gets invented.

**Question 11 (harder) — Evidence for a roadmap decision**

The organisation is deciding whether to invest in a shared identity capability. Available evidence: qualitative findings from three programmes indicating identity difficulty; analytics showing high drop-off at identity across four services; a supplier's claim that their solution achieves 94% first-time success; and a support desk cost model. What is the most rigorous contribution you can make?

- A) Present the qualitative and analytics evidence supporting the investment.
- B) Set out what each source establishes and its limits — including that the supplier's 94% figure requires interrogation of its denominator and population before it can be compared with the organisation's own drop-off data — and identify the one or two things not yet known that would most change the decision.
- C) Recommend the investment on the strength of the converging evidence.
- D) Recommend further research before any decision.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Your contribution at a roadmap decision is not advocacy and not delay; it is making the evidence base legible so that the decision is taken knowingly. Option B does three things. It states what each source supports, which prevents the common failure of converging evidence being treated as stronger than the sum of its parts. It flags the supplier figure specifically, because a vendor's 94% is almost certainly measured on a different population and denominator than the organisation's drop-off rate — quite possibly counting only users who reached the verification step with acceptable documents — and comparing them directly would be a serious error. And it names the residual uncertainty that matters, which tells the board what a decision is actually resting on. Option A is advocacy dressed as evidence. Option C reaches a recommendation that may well be right on a basis that has not been examined. Option D defers a decision the evidence may already support.

**Question 12 (harder) — A pattern in your own assurance**

Reviewing a year of research outputs you have assured, you notice that studies from two teams are consistently strong and studies from four are consistently weak, and that the two strong teams both have researchers who joined from the same previous organisation. What is the most useful inference and action?

- A) The four weaker teams need training.
- B) Something transferable is likely operating in the two strong teams — a practice, a routine, a set of expectations — and the highest-value action is to find out what it is by examining how they actually work, then build it into the organisation's standards and community of practice rather than assuming the difference is individual talent.
- C) Recruit more researchers from that organisation.
- D) Increase assurance scrutiny on the four weaker teams.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The pattern is an opportunity to identify a practice rather than a reason to grade people. Researchers who bring consistently better outputs are usually carrying a routine with them — a peer review habit, an analysis protocol, a way of writing claims, a standard they apply before circulating anything — and that routine is transferable in a way that individual ability is not. Option B goes and looks, which is the step people skip. Option A assumes a knowledge deficit without diagnosing one, and training that does not target the actual mechanism rarely changes practice. Option C is an unusable inference from two people. Option D increases scrutiny without improving capability, which catches more problems and creates none of the capacity to prevent them. Your role names leading a community of practice to help the organisation continually improve, and this is exactly the raw material for it — a naturally occurring comparison that tells you what good looks like in your own context.

### Preparation tips

- **Read outputs for the study behind them.** Practise inferring recruitment, setting and question order from what a report says and does not say.
- **Scope flaws precisely.** Name which claims a fault compromises and which survive. Wholesale condemnation is a weak audit.
- **Ask who could not have appeared in this data.** The single highest-yield question you can put to any evidence.
- **Look at the distribution of failures.** Concentrated non-compliance is a process diagnosis; scattered non-compliance is a conduct one.
- **Frame prioritisation as value of information.** Decision leverage and remaining time, not importance or profile.
- **Practise synthesis deliberately.** Take three unrelated pieces of evidence from your organisation this month and ask what single thing they might all be describing.
- **Interrogate vendor figures for population and denominator.** They are almost never comparable to your own metrics as stated.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Accepting sample source as sample composition.** "Twelve from the panel" says nothing about who they were.
- **Treating converging evidence as automatically stronger.** Three sources with the same blind spot converge on the same error.
- **Reaching for training as the answer to a systemic problem.** Diagnose the mechanism first.
- **Reminding people of rules they already know.** The weakest available response to any compliance failure.
- **Adding friction for the compliant majority.** This is how burdens accumulate and workarounds get invented.
- **Picking a winner between contradictory studies.** The insight is usually in the difference between contexts.
- **Condemning a flawed study wholesale.** Teams need to know what they can still rely on, and blanket condemnation encourages concealment.
- **Letting fluency substitute for examination.** At your level, the costly errors are the confident ones.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A numeric reasoning assessment measures how accurately you handle quantitative material. At lead level the emphasis is almost entirely on interrogation rather than calculation. You will still need to compute correctly, but the questions that matter ask whether a figure means what somebody claims — and you are frequently the only person in the room able to establish that.

The format is typically a timed online test of fifteen to thirty minutes, each item presenting a table, chart or paragraph of figures with a multiple-choice question. Calculators are usually provided; the arithmetic is deliberately tractable. Time per item is tight, so the operative skill is locating what matters and disregarding what does not.

The professional stake is substantial and worth naming. Quantitative evidence carries unearned authority: a number in a board paper is treated as fact while a qualitative finding is treated as a view, regardless of the rigour behind either. At your level the numbers you encounter are increasingly consequential — business cases, vendor claims, benefits realisation figures, board-level performance metrics — and increasingly detached from the people who produced them. Being the person who asks what the denominator is, which population it represents, and how the metric is defined is a large part of what you are for. Your role's requirement to influence and negotiate with senior stakeholders frequently reduces to exactly that question, asked calmly, when a decision is already moving.

Four habits carry most of the load. Establish what the question asks before looking at the data. Identify the denominator and the population it represents before calculating. Estimate, calculate, compare. And sanity-check against the world — a figure implying that 40% of users complete a form in under three seconds is measuring something other than what it claims.

Distractors are constructed from a predictable catalogue: wrong denominator, percentage confused with percentage point, new value used as the base in a percentage change, rates averaged without weighting, vendor metrics compared to internal metrics without checking definitions. Recognising your answer among the options is not evidence that you are right; knowing the catalogue means you can often see the trap being laid.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The numerical skills tested map onto your named skills:

- **Interrogating quantitative claims** maps to **Research management, leadership and assurance**, where you assure research quality across the organisation.

- **Comparing rates between user groups** maps to **Inclusive research**, where gaps between groups are the first visible trace of exclusion.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Rate from counts**

Across a programme, 36 of 48 participants completed the core task unaided. What percentage is that?

- A) 66%
- B) 72%
- C) 75%
- D) 80%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 36 ÷ 48 = 0.75, so 75%, and the answer is C. Estimate first — 36 of 48 is three quarters, since 24 would be half. Options A and B come from dividing by 54 and 50. Worth noting even on an item this simple that aggregating 48 participants across a programme into a single rate is only meaningful if the studies tested comparable tasks with comparable populations; if they did not, the figure is arithmetically correct and referentially empty, which is a distinction you will be enforcing in other people's work.

**Question 2 (easy) — Over-recruitment at programme scale**

Your organisation's no-show rate averages 25%. A programme needs 60 completed sessions across four studies. How many bookings are required?

- A) 72
- B) 75
- C) 80
- D) 84

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** If 25% do not attend, 75% do, so bookings must satisfy 0.75 × bookings = 60, giving 60 ÷ 0.75 = 80, and the answer is C. The common error adds 25% to 60, producing 75 and pointing at B — an underestimate, because the percentage applies to the larger booking figure. Verify: 25% of 80 is 20, and 80 − 20 = 60. At programme scale the consequence is worth stating: a systematic under-recruitment of a few per cent does not distribute evenly across the sample, because the quotas that fail first are the hardest to fill — participants with access needs, participants from under-represented groups — which is precisely the composition your standards require.

**Question 3 (easy) — Disaggregated rates**

| Group | Participants | Completed |
|---|---|---|
| Digitally confident | 30 | 27 |
| Low digital confidence | 18 | 9 |
| Assistive technology users | 12 | 3 |

What is the overall completion rate, and what is the gap between highest and lowest group rates?

- A) 60% overall; 50 point gap
- B) 65% overall; 65 point gap
- C) 65% overall; 50 point gap
- D) 60% overall; 65 point gap

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Total completions are 27 + 9 + 3 = 39, across 30 + 18 + 12 = 60 participants, so 39 ÷ 60 = 65%. Group rates are 27 ÷ 30 = 90%, 9 ÷ 18 = 50%, and 3 ÷ 12 = 25%. The gap between highest and lowest is 90 − 25 = 65 points. So B. The substantive point sits behind the arithmetic: the overall figure of 65% describes no group in the study, and reporting it alone conceals that assistive technology users fail three times out of four. Whenever an aggregate can be disaggregated, it should be, and an organisational standard that does not require disaggregation by access need is a standard with a hole in it.

**Question 4 (easy) — Central figure and spread**

Eleven sessions produced task times in minutes: 6, 7, 8, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 14, 16, 71. Which summary is most defensible?

- A) Mean 15.6 minutes
- B) Median 10 minutes, range 6 to 71, with the 71-minute case described separately
- C) Median 10 minutes
- D) Mean 15.6 minutes, range 6 to 71

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The mean of 15.6 exceeds nine of the eleven observations, so it represents almost nobody. The median is the sixth value in the ordered list, which is 10, and that describes the group well. But the median alone conceals a session lasting more than four times the typical duration, which is very likely the most informative observation in the set. Option B supplies centre, spread and explicit treatment of the outlier. Option D pairs the range with a misleading centre. Option C hides the extreme case. The rule to enforce organisationally: a central figure never travels alone, and an outlier is a lead to be investigated rather than noise to be absorbed.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Weighted aggregation across studies**

Four studies: 12 participants at 50% completion; 20 at 65%; 8 at 75%; 20 at 80%. What is the overall completion rate across all 60 participants?

- A) 66%
- B) 68%
- C) 70%
- D) 72%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Convert each rate to counts before combining. Study 1: 50% of 12 = 6 completions. Study 2: 65% of 20 = 13. Study 3: 75% of 8 = 6. Study 4: 80% of 20 = 16. Total completions 6 + 13 + 6 + 16 = 41, across 60 participants, so 41 ÷ 60 = 0.683, which is approximately 68% and the answer is B. Note the coincidence lying in wait here: the unweighted mean of 50, 65, 75 and 80 is 67.5, which also rounds to 68. That near-agreement is luck, not method — change Study 3 to 30 participants and the two approaches diverge sharply — and relying on the unweighted figure because it once landed close is exactly the habit that produces a wrong answer when it matters. Always return to counts. And, as ever at your level, the prior question is whether aggregating these four studies is meaningful at all: if they tested different tasks with different populations, the combined figure is arithmetically correct and referentially empty.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Funnel denominators**

Funnel: 120,000 start, 90,000 pass identity, 54,000 upload evidence, 48,600 submit. What proportion of those who passed identity went on to submit?

- A) 40.5%
- B) 54%
- C) 60%
- D) 90%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The stem names users who passed identity, so the denominator is 90,000 and the part is 48,600: 48,600 ÷ 90,000 = 0.54, which is 54%, giving B. Option A uses 120,000, option C is the identity-to-upload rate, and option D is the upload-to-submit rate. All are true of this funnel; one answers the question. Strategically, note where the losses are: 30,000 lost at identity and 36,000 lost between identity and upload, against only 5,400 at submission. If you were allocating research capacity across this journey, the upload gap and the identity gap are where it belongs, and a team proposing to research the submission step would need a strong reason.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Percentage points in a benefits claim**

A benefits realisation paper states that a change raised completion from 55% to 66%, describing this as "a 20% improvement". Is the description accurate?

- A) No; it is an 11% improvement.
- B) Yes; 11 ÷ 55 = 20%, so a 20% relative increase is correct, though the paper should say "20% relative increase" or "11 percentage points" to avoid ambiguity.
- C) No; it is 11 percentage points, and calling it 20% is wrong.
- D) No; it should be 16.7%.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The relative increase is 11 ÷ 55 = 0.2, so 20% is arithmetically defensible. The problem is labelling rather than calculation: "a 20% improvement" is ambiguous and will be read by many readers as a rise from 55% to 75%. Option B gets both halves — the figure is right, the expression is unclear. Option C over-corrects into an error of its own; relative change is a legitimate way to express this. Option A confuses the units. Option D divides by the new value. The reason this matters at your level is governance rather than arithmetic: benefits papers routinely choose the flattering framing, usually without any intent to mislead, and being the person who asks for both numbers keeps the organisation's evidence honest without accusing anyone of anything.

**Question 8 (moderate) — A vendor claim**

A supplier states their identity solution achieves "94% first-time verification success". Your organisation's current rate, measured as verified users divided by users who reach the identity step, is 71%. What is the most important question before comparing them?

- A) Whether 94% is independently audited.
- B) What denominator the supplier uses — in particular whether it counts only users who present acceptable documents, or all users who reach the step, since excluding those without documents would remove precisely the population driving your 29% failure.
- C) Whether the supplier's figure is recent.
- D) Whether the supplier has government clients.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Two rates are comparable only if their denominators describe the same population, and vendor metrics very commonly do not. If the supplier's 94% counts successful verifications among users who submitted a valid passport or driving licence, then the users your organisation is losing — people without those documents — have been excluded from the denominator entirely, and the two figures measure different things. Adopting the solution could leave your failure rate essentially unchanged while the vendor's metric remains accurate. Option B identifies this precisely. Options A, C and D are reasonable due diligence questions that do not address the comparability problem, and asking them instead gives the appearance of scrutiny without its substance. This is among the highest-value contributions available to you in a procurement or roadmap discussion.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Reconstructing an implied value**

A business case claims that a 4 percentage point completion improvement on 1.2 million annual starts is worth £5.76 million a year. What is the implied value per additional completed application?

- A) £4.80
- B) £48
- C) £120
- D) £96

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A 4 percentage point improvement on 1.2 million starts gives 0.04 × 1,200,000 = 48,000 additional completions. Dividing the claimed benefit: £5,760,000 ÷ 48,000 = £120, so C. Option A divides by 1.2 million, valuing every start. Option B is the additional-completion count misread as pounds. The reason this calculation earns its place is that reconstructing an implied unit value takes thirty seconds and immediately tells you whether a business case is plausible — £120 per additional completed application might be entirely reasonable for a high-value transaction, and wildly implausible for a simple information service. Asking "so we are valuing each additional completion at £120 — is that right?" is a more effective challenge than disputing the total, because it is specific and answerable.

**Question 10 (harder) — A metric that conflates populations**

A board paper reports "average time to complete: 11 minutes" for a service your research showed most users taking over half an hour across multiple sittings. What is the most likely explanation, and the right intervention?

- A) The board paper is wrong and should be corrected.
- B) The metric almost certainly measures single sessions rather than end-to-end journeys, so multi-sitting completions are either excluded or counted only for their final short visit, and abandoned sessions may be included — the intervention is to ask for the definition and the distribution, and to propose an end-to-end measure alongside it.
- C) The research sample was atypical.
- D) The two figures cannot be reconciled.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Session-based analytics and journey-based reality diverge systematically for any service completed over more than one sitting, and the divergence always flatters the service. A user who spends forty minutes across three visits typically appears as a short final session, and users who abandon early may be dragging the mean down further. The figure is probably calculated correctly and defined in a way that cannot answer the question the board thinks it is answering. Option B identifies the mechanism and, crucially, proposes a constructive intervention rather than a dispute — asking for the definition is not an accusation, and offering an end-to-end measure gives the metric owner something to build. Option A picks a fight over a number that is not wrong. Option C blames the research. Option D forecloses a reconciliation that is both available and strategically useful.

**Question 11 (harder) — Sampling adequacy for an organisational claim**

A directorate proposes to measure whether a design change improved completion by running 40 moderated sessions before and 40 after. Baseline completion is 58%; a 6 percentage point improvement is considered meaningful. What is the strongest response?

- A) Agree; 80 sessions is substantial.
- B) Explain that a 6 point difference on 40 per group is about two participants and well within ordinary variation, so the comparison cannot detect it — recommend measuring the rate through live analytics across the whole user base, and spending the 80 sessions on mechanisms, which moderated research does uniquely well.
- C) Increase to 60 per group.
- D) Run it and report the observed difference with caveats.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Forty per group cannot distinguish a six point difference from chance; detecting a difference that size reliably requires several hundred per group. Option B says so and redirects rather than merely objecting, which is the substantive contribution: analytics measures rates across the full population at negligible marginal cost, while moderated sessions are the only instrument that explains why a rate is what it is. Dividing the work along that line is the recommendation. Option A endorses a design that cannot answer its own question. Option C moves towards adequacy without reaching it while still misallocating scarce capacity. Option D is the most damaging at organisational level, because caveated findings from underpowered comparisons get cited without their caveats, and once your organisation establishes that 40-session comparisons can measure rates, you will be arguing against that precedent for years.

**Question 12 (harder) — A claim already in a board paper**

A board paper states: "Research confirms 91% of users can complete the service independently." You trace it to a study of 34 participants, 31 of whom completed, recruited from the departmental panel. The board meets in three days. What is the most important thing to do?

- A) Ask for the figure to be removed.
- B) Contact the paper's author, explain that 34 panel-recruited participants cannot support a population rate, propose the accurate statement — that 31 of 34 panel participants completed unaided — and offer what the organisation does have, such as analytics, if the board needs a rate; then confirm the correction is made before the paper is tabled.
- C) Raise it at the board meeting.
- D) Note it for the next assurance review.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A qualitative sample from a self-selected panel has been converted into a population statistic in a document that will inform a decision, which is a category error rather than a rounding one. Option B does the four things that matter: it goes to the author first, it names precisely what the evidence supports, it offers an alternative source so the board is not simply left with a hole, and it closes the loop by confirming the change lands. That last element is the one people omit, and the correction that is agreed but not made is a familiar organisational failure. Option A asks for removal without offering a replacement, which invites resistance. Option C corrects the record in the most public and least recoverable setting, embarrasses the author, and makes you an adversary rather than an assurance function. Option D allows a board decision to proceed on a claim you know to be unsupported, which is precisely the failure your role exists to prevent.

### Preparation tips

- **Ask who could not have appeared in this data.** The single highest-yield question you can put to any figure.
- **Reconstruct implied unit values.** Thirty seconds of division tells you whether a business case is plausible.
- **Interrogate vendor denominators.** Vendor metrics and internal metrics are almost never defined the same way.
- **Ask for the definition and the distribution.** Most misleading metrics are correctly calculated and wrongly defined.
- **Return to counts before combining rates.** Unweighted averages over-weight your smallest study, and occasionally land near the right answer by coincidence, which is worse.
- **Know what sample sizes can detect.** No formal power calculation is needed to know that 40 per group cannot see six points.
- **Trace three figures in a live board paper back to source.** More instructive than any drill, and occasionally alarming.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Averaging rates without weighting.** The commonest real-world error in aggregated reporting.
- **Comparing vendor rates to internal rates.** Different denominators, different populations, same-looking numbers.
- **Converting qualitative samples into population rates.** The error most likely to reach a board paper.
- **Objecting on sample size when the frame is the problem.** More of the same data is no better.
- **Reporting an aggregate that can be disaggregated.** A single figure that describes no group conceals the group that is failing.
- **Accepting a mean without a spread or a definition.** Session-based metrics systematically flatter multi-sitting services.
- **Endorsing an underpowered comparison "with caveats".** Caveats do not travel; the number does.
- **Correcting a board paper in the board meeting.** Go to the author, and confirm the change lands.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately you read. At your level the documents that matter are governance frameworks, organisational standards, evaluation and audit reports, ministerial correspondence, board papers and contracts. A misread exception clause becomes a compliance failure across several teams; a misread finding in someone else's report becomes advice given confidently to a director.

The classic format presents a short passage of dense workplace prose, typically three to six sentences, followed by statements to classify as True, False or Cannot Say. True means the statement follows necessarily. False means the passage contradicts it. Cannot Say means the passage neither establishes nor excludes it. Alongside these come comprehension items, main-point items, and questions asking which of four summaries is most accurate.

The governing rule is that you answer from the passage alone, and here is the part that is specific to people at your level: expertise actively harms you in this section. The more governance prose you have read, the more readily you supply the clause you expect to be there, complete the sentence the way such sentences usually end, and feel confident while doing it. Senior candidates lose marks on verbal reasoning at rates that surprise them, and this is nearly always the mechanism. The counter-habit is mechanical — before marking anything True, put your finger on the words.

Cannot Say deserves particular attention, and it is the answer experienced people avoid most. Forming a view under incomplete information is what your job consists of, and the professional reflex is deeply embedded. In this section it costs marks. If you are filling a gap with knowledge or inference, however reasonable, the answer is Cannot Say.

Small words carry the load. "All" is not "most". "May" is not "must". "Should" is not "will". "Recommends considering" is not "recommends". "Solely", "entirely", "unless", "provided that", "subject to" and "where practicable" govern everything around them. Wrong answers are built almost entirely from swaps of these terms, so a statement that reads like a faithful paraphrase may still be False.

There is a direct professional payoff. The discipline of separating what a text states from what it implies is the same discipline you enforce when assuring other people's findings, and the same one you need when a stakeholder tells you what a policy document requires.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The verbal skills tested map onto your named skills:

- **Reading precisely** maps to **Research management, leadership and assurance**, where you set and assure standards including ethics and safeguarding.
- **Separating statement from inference** maps to **Analysis and synthesis**, where you help the organisation generate clear and valuable findings.
- **Identifying the main point** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**, where you direct stakeholder relationship strategy and communicate with senior stakeholders.
- **Handling qualified language** maps to **User-centred practice and advocacy**, where advocacy that overstates its evidence loses the credibility that makes it work.
- **Reading for absence** maps to **Inclusive research**, where noticing that a framework never mentions access needs has an ethical consequence.
- **Reading governance prose accurately** maps to **Research management, leadership and assurance**, where you assure quality across the organisation.

### Practice questions

*Passage A — for Questions 1 to 4*

"Directorates must submit an annual research assurance return to the head of profession, unless all research within the directorate has been conducted under a centrally managed programme. Returns must confirm that ethics review has been completed where required, and must list any studies where it was not. Where a directorate identifies a study conducted without required ethics review, it must notify the head of profession within ten working days of identifying it, separately from the annual return. Returns are published internally; notifications are not."

**Question 1 (easy)** — Statement: "Every directorate must submit an annual research assurance return." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The passage attaches an exception: directorates where all research was conducted under a centrally managed programme are exempt. The statement's "every" contradicts that, so it is False. This is the most reliable pattern in the section — an absolute term in the statement, an exception in the passage. Note that the exception says "all research", not "most research", which narrows it considerably and supplies the material for Question 3.

**Question 2 (easy)** — Statement: "Notifications of missed ethics review are published internally." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The final sentence states that returns are published internally and notifications are not. The statement contradicts this directly, making it False. The item tests whether you read a sentence that appeared to have finished its work; the semicolon clause carries the operative distinction, and a reader who registered "returns are published internally" and moved on would plausibly generalise it to notifications. Governance prose habitually places the qualification after the semicolon, and it is worth training yourself to slow down there rather than speed up.

**Question 3 (moderate)** — Statement: "A directorate where nine of ten studies ran under a centrally managed programme must submit a return." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The exemption applies only where *all* research within the directorate ran under a centrally managed programme. Nine of ten does not satisfy that condition, so the exemption does not apply and the general requirement stands — the statement follows and is True. The item rewards carrying "all" from the first sentence to a scenario several lines later. It also reflects something real: partial satisfaction of an exception condition is non-compliance, and directorates routinely assume the opposite, reasoning that they are substantially covered. Catching that assumption is a recurring part of assurance work.

**Question 4 (moderate)** — Statement: "A study conducted without required ethics review must be notified within ten working days of the study concluding." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The passage requires notification within ten working days of *identifying* the study, not of the study concluding. Those are different triggers and can be months apart — a study that concluded in March and was identified in an October review must be notified in October, not retrospectively. The statement misstates the trigger, so it is False. This is a small distinction with large practical consequences, because trigger events determine whether an organisation is compliant, and misreading one produces either false alarm or false comfort. Whenever a passage specifies a deadline, check what starts the clock.

*Passage B — for Questions 5 to 8*

"The internal audit of user research found that assurance arrangements varied considerably between directorates. Three directorates operate peer review before publication; the remainder rely on line management sign-off. Directorates with peer review reported fewer instances of findings being challenged after publication, although the audit did not examine whether the findings themselves were more robust. The audit recommends that the head of profession consider whether a common assurance approach would be proportionate."

**Question 5 (easy)** — Statement: "Some directorates do not operate peer review before publication." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The passage states that three directorates operate peer review and the remainder rely on line management sign-off, which establishes directly that some do not. The statement follows and is True. Answer this quickly and bank the time — the only conceivable ambiguity would be whether "the remainder" might be empty, and the passage forecloses that by describing what the remainder does.

**Question 6 (moderate)** — Statement: "Peer review produces more robust findings." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The passage reports fewer post-publication challenges in peer-reviewed directorates and then states explicitly that the audit did not examine robustness. Fewer challenges and greater robustness are different things — findings might be challenged less because they are better, or because they are blander, or because peer review smooths the language that invites challenge. The passage flags its own silence, so the answer is Cannot Say. This item is doubly signposted, and a candidate who still answers True has substituted a professional intuition for what the text supports. The intuition may be correct; it is not evidence, and at your level it is exactly the substitution that leads to an assurance recommendation resting on an audit finding the audit did not make.

**Question 7 (moderate)** — Statement: "The audit recommends introducing a common assurance approach." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The audit recommends that the head of profession *consider whether* a common approach would be proportionate. That is a recommendation to evaluate, including the possibility of concluding that it would not be proportionate. The statement asserts a recommendation to introduce, which the audit did not make, so it is False rather than Cannot Say — the passage tells you what was recommended and it was something different. This distinction runs through real governance writing, and misreading it produces a familiar sequence: a recommendation to consider becomes a commitment to deliver, and the delivery is then reported against as though it had been required.

**Question 8 (harder)** — Statement: "Line management sign-off is a weaker form of assurance than peer review." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The passage describes two approaches and reports one difference in outcome, while explicitly declining to assess robustness. It makes no judgement about relative strength. A reader may reasonably infer that peer review is stronger, and the passage neither supports nor excludes that, so the answer is Cannot Say. What makes this harder than Question 6 is that the passage's structure leans mildly towards peer review — it is mentioned first, it is associated with a favourable outcome — and structural lean is not content. Noticing when you are responding to a document's implied stance rather than its statements is one of the most transferable things this section trains, and it is directly relevant to reading audit reports, which frequently imply more than they assert.

*Passage C — for Questions 9 to 12*

"Research findings must not be altered to reflect the preferences of stakeholders. Presentation may be adapted to the audience provided the substance is unchanged. Where a researcher is asked to alter findings, the matter must be raised with the head of profession, who is accountable for the integrity of research outputs. Where findings cannot reach decision-makers before a decision is taken, the reason must be recorded; the record is reviewed annually."

**Question 9 (easy)** — Statement: "Findings may be presented differently to different audiences." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The second sentence permits adapting presentation to the audience provided the substance is unchanged, and the statement claims only that different presentation is permitted. It follows and is True. Note that the statement is weaker than the passage's full provision, omitting the substance condition — but a weaker statement that the passage supports is still True. Candidates occasionally mark such statements False for incompleteness, which confuses "does not say everything" with "says something wrong".

**Question 10 (moderate)** — Statement: "A researcher asked to alter findings must refuse." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The passage establishes that findings must not be altered to reflect stakeholder preferences, and that a request to alter findings must be raised with the head of profession. Neither states what the individual researcher must personally do at the moment of the request. A duty to escalate is not a duty to refuse — indeed the escalation route arguably exists so that an individual is not left to refuse alone, which is a meaningful protection. The passage neither imposes nor excludes a duty to refuse, so the answer is Cannot Say. The item rewards noticing that "must not be altered" is a rule about outcomes while "must be raised" is a rule about conduct, and that the two do not entail one another.

**Question 11 (harder)** — Statement: "A decision taken before findings were available breaches the requirements, unless recorded." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** Read the requirement carefully. The passage says that where findings cannot reach decision-makers before a decision, the reason must be recorded. It does not say that recording converts a breach into compliance; it says that recording is what is required in that situation. The statement frames the timing failure as a breach that recording excuses, which misdescribes the structure — there is no breach in the timing itself, only in a failure to record. This is a fine distinction and a consequential one: under the statement's reading, late findings are a compliance problem to be minimised; under the passage's, they are an expected occurrence to be documented honestly. Organisations that adopt the first reading get concealment.

**Question 12 (harder) — Main point**

Which best captures the main point of Passage C?

- A) Research findings must never be changed.
- B) The substance of research must remain independent of stakeholder preference, with adaptation permitted in form, escalation provided where independence is pressured, accountability located with the head of profession, and honesty required where timing fails.
- C) The head of profession is accountable for research integrity.
- D) Reasons must be recorded when findings arrive late.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The main point must span the passage without adding to it. Option A overstates: adaptation of presentation is expressly permitted, and the prohibition is specifically on altering substance to reflect stakeholder preference. Options C and D are each accurate about one clause and silent on the rest, making them details. Option B captures all four elements and the logic joining them — research is useful only if it is both independent and timely, so the passage protects independence with a rule, an escalation route and an accountable owner, and handles timing with a requirement of honesty rather than a prohibition. Main-point items reward the option broad enough to cover everything and disciplined enough to invent nothing, and three of these four options are true details promoted beyond their scope.

### Preparation tips

- **Set expertise aside mechanically.** Before marking True, point at the words. This is the senior candidate's specific failure mode and it needs a specific counter-habit.
- **Treat Cannot Say as decisive.** The professional reflex to form a view is what costs marks here.
- **Circle every qualifier.** All, every, entirely, solely, some, may, must, should, unless, provided that, subject to, where practicable, consider whether, proportionate.
- **Check what starts the clock.** Deadlines hang on trigger events, and the trigger is frequently not the obvious one.
- **Separate "recommends considering X" from "recommends X".** Audit and review prose runs on this.
- **Distinguish a failed condition from declared silence.** The first gives False; the second gives Cannot Say.
- **Notice structural lean.** Order of mention and favourable association are not assertions.
- **Practise on your own organisation's standards.** Write three statements about a page — one True, one False, one Cannot Say.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Answering from professional knowledge.** The dominant senior error.
- **Completing the clause you expected.** Governance prose has conventions; passages exploit them.
- **Missing "all", "entirely" and "solely".** These narrow exceptions dramatically, and partial satisfaction is non-compliance.
- **Reading fewer challenges as greater robustness.** Passages frequently distinguish outcome proxies from the thing itself.
- **Confusing a rule about outcomes with a rule about conduct.** "Must not be altered" and "must be raised" are different obligations.
- **Marking a weaker-but-supported statement False.** Incomplete is not incorrect.
- **Responding to implied stance.** Tone and structure are not content.
- **Rushing after a semicolon.** The operative qualification lives there disproportionately often.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment presents realistic workplace scenarios and asks what you would do. There is no passage to parse and no arithmetic. You get a dilemma of the kind your role produces, four or five candidate responses, and a question asking which is most effective and often which is least.

This section measures whether your judgement matches what the profession considers good practice at your level — and level is the whole calibration. Your role description places you as an expert practitioner leading and aligning research across several teams, ensuring teams take an evidence-based approach, setting and assuring standards including ethics and safeguarding, establishing and aligning programmes to organisational goals, directing stakeholder relationship strategy, negotiating with senior stakeholders, and leading a community of practice. The strongest answers therefore involve organisational rather than local action, capability building rather than problem solving, and holding positions in forums where the pressure is real.

Two features distinguish your level from a senior practitioner's.

The first is that you are usually acting on systems rather than instances. A senior researcher who fixes a flawed study has done their job. A lead researcher who fixes it without asking why the assurance process did not catch it has done half of theirs. Several items below turn on that distinction, and the reliable tell is whether an option addresses the mechanism that produced the problem.

The second is that your authority is largely informal. You do not line-manage most of the people whose practice you assure, you cannot compel a directorate, and you influence senior stakeholders who outrank you. Options that rely on instruction, mandate or escalation as a first move tend to score poorly, not because escalation is wrong but because it is a resource you spend rather than a tool you use freely.

Four principles carry most scenarios.

Participants and users come first, and at your level that includes participants in research you did not commission. When an option protects wellbeing, consent, dignity or access, it almost always outranks one protecting a schedule, a budget or an organisational relationship.

Protect the integrity of the evidence, and protect the people whose job it is to protect it. Options that leave a researcher alone under pressure from a director are weak even when they preserve the finding.

Build capability while solving the problem. An organisation whose research quality depends on your personal review does not have research quality; it has you.

Calibrate escalation to severity. Safeguarding and physical risk go up immediately and in parallel. Practice problems go to the person first.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The judgement dimensions map onto your named skills:

- **Ethics and safeguarding at organisational scale** map to **Research management, leadership and assurance**, where you set and assure standards including ethics and safeguarding.
- **Assurance and capability building** map to **Research management, leadership and assurance**, where you assure quality across the organisation, and to **User research methods**, where you lead a community of practice.
- **Strategic alignment** maps to **Research management, leadership and assurance**, where you establish and align programmes to organisational goals.
- **Influence and negotiation** map to **Stakeholder relationship management**, where you influence and negotiate with senior stakeholders to resolve issues and enable progress.
- **Advocacy with sceptics** maps to **User-centred practice and advocacy**, where you advocate for research with sceptical colleagues and stakeholders.
- **Organisational inclusion** maps to **Inclusive research**, where you help teams understand user diversity and advocate inclusive practice.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — A researcher under pressure from a director**

A senior researcher tells you a director has asked them to remove an accessibility finding from a board summary, saying it will "muddy the message". They are unsure what to do. What is the most effective response?

- A) Tell them to leave it in and to cite your instruction.
- B) Agree with them how the finding should be presented, including a constructive framing with proposed mitigation; offer to attend the meeting; and take the request itself up with the director yourself, since a director asking a researcher to remove findings is a matter at your level, not theirs.
- C) Contact the director and instruct them not to make such requests.
- D) Escalate to the head of profession.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Two things need to happen and option B does both. The finding must survive, and the researcher must come out of this more capable and less exposed. Agreeing the presentation together builds their judgement rather than substituting yours, and a constructive framing with mitigation gives them something to bring rather than a refusal. Offering to attend supplies backing without takeover. Crucially, taking the request up with the director is your job and not theirs — a power differential of that size is precisely what your role exists to absorb. Option A is least effective: it leaves them defending a position by proxy, teaches them nothing, and frames integrity as hierarchy. Option C addresses the director without preparing the researcher and treats a conversation as an instruction you are not positioned to give. Option D escalates something within your remit, though you would reasonably inform the head of profession if it recurs or if the director persists.

**Question 2 (easy) — Standards that exist and are not used**

Your organisation has a research standard nobody follows. Reports vary widely, and researchers describe it as unrealistic. What is the strongest approach?

- A) Reissue the standard with a communication from the head of profession.
- B) Find out why it is unrealistic by reviewing recent outputs with researchers, identify which requirements are genuinely unworkable and which are simply unfamiliar, revise the standard with them, and pair it with a lightweight routine — such as peer review — that makes compliance visible and useful rather than administrative.
- C) Introduce mandatory compliance checks.
- D) Provide training on the standard.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A standard that everybody ignores has failed as a standard, and reissuing it changes nothing except the date. Option B starts by taking the researchers' objection seriously as data — some requirements probably are unworkable under real conditions, and the ones that are not are usually unclear rather than unreasonable. Revising with them produces authorship and therefore defenders. Pairing it with a routine matters because standards without a moment of application are aspirations; peer review supplies that moment and delivers something researchers want anyway. Option A is least effective, being the purest form of doing nothing while appearing to act. Option C enforces a standard people have told you is unrealistic, which produces compliance theatre and resentment in equal measure. Option D is a useful component and insufficient alone, since training without a routine that requires the behaviour rarely changes practice.

**Question 3 (easy) — Community of practice that has gone flat**

The research community of practice you lead has declining attendance and has become a series of presentations people watch passively. What is the most effective response?

- A) Make attendance mandatory.
- B) Ask members what would make it worth their time, and reshape it around problems people currently have — for instance sessions where someone brings a live methodological dilemma, or a critique clinic on real outputs — so attending has an immediate return.
- C) Invite external speakers.
- D) Reduce frequency.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Declining attendance at a voluntary forum is straightforward feedback: it is not repaying the time. Option B treats it as such and rebuilds around utility, and the specific formats matter — a live dilemma or a critique clinic gives attendees something they can use that afternoon, which is what sustains a community, whereas presentations position them as an audience. Option A is least effective because mandating attendance at a forum people find useless produces a room of people doing email, and it destroys the voluntary quality that makes a community of practice different from a meeting. Option C may help and treats the symptom; interesting speakers raise attendance temporarily without changing why people stopped coming. Option D reduces the visible failure without addressing it, though a less frequent, genuinely valuable session is a reasonable outcome of the redesign in option B.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Assuring across a boundary**

A directorate you do not manage has published research whose central claim is unsupported, and it is being cited in a business case. What is the most effective response?

- A) Correct the record with the business case author.
- B) Speak to the researcher and their lead first, set out specifically what the evidence supports, and agree how the correction will be made and communicated to those already relying on it — with a clear next step if they decline.
- C) Raise it with the head of profession.
- D) Note it for the annual assurance return.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** The claim needs correcting and the relationship needs to survive, since you will be assuring this directorate's work for years. Option B goes to the people responsible first — they may have context you lack, and if they do not, the correction lands better from them — and it is not naive, because it includes a next step if they refuse. Note the element people omit: communicating the correction to those already relying on it, without which a corrected report sits alongside an uncorrected business case. Option A bypasses the researcher, damages a working relationship, and risks acting on your own misreading. Option C is premature as a first move and spends escalation capital you may need later. Option D is least effective by a distance: an annual return is a record, not an intervention, and a business case is moving now.

**Question 5 (moderate) — A pattern of ethics non-compliance**

Reviewing the year, you find that studies in one directorate repeatedly proceeded without required ethics review, always under ministerial deadline pressure, always by experienced researchers. What is the strongest response?

- A) Report the breaches and require retrospective review.
- B) Address the specific breaches, and separately investigate the review process itself — how long it takes, what it demands, whether a fast route exists — on the reasoning that experienced researchers who know the requirement and breach it under pressure are telling you the process cannot be complied with when time is short.
- C) Remind all researchers of the ethics requirement.
- D) Introduce a mandatory pre-fieldwork checkpoint for all studies.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** The concentration of the failures is the most informative feature. Scattered breaches by different people in different directorates would indicate a conduct problem; repeated breaches by experienced people in one directorate under the same pressure indicate a process that does not survive contact with a deadline. Option B handles both layers, and the "address the specific breaches" clause matters — diagnosing a systemic cause is not a reason to leave individual failures unaddressed, particularly where participants may have been affected. Option A treats the instances and guarantees recurrence. Option C is least effective and is the weakest available response to any compliance failure: people who knew the rule and broke it under pressure will not be changed by hearing the rule again. Option D imposes friction on every compliant study to catch a localised failure, which is how organisational burden accumulates and how the next workaround is invented.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Competing directorates**

Two directors want your research capacity in the same quarter. One has a failing live service; the other has a ministerially visible new programme. Both have raised it with your head of profession. What is the most effective response?

- A) Split capacity.
- B) Set out for each what research would deliver, what decision it informs, and the cost of deferral; recommend a sequence with reasoning; and put that recommendation to both directors and the head of profession together rather than negotiating separately.
- C) Prioritise the ministerial programme.
- D) Ask the head of profession to decide.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** This is a prioritisation problem being conducted as a political one, and the way through is to make the trade-off explicit and simultaneously visible. Option B does the analytical work and then handles the politics correctly: putting the recommendation to both at once prevents the parallel-negotiation dynamic in which each director is told something slightly different and trust collapses when they compare notes. Making a recommendation rather than presenting neutral options is essential — you are the expert on what research can deliver, and abdicating that wastes the role. Option A is least effective: splitting constrained capacity between two substantial needs usually produces two studies too thin to inform either decision. Option C ranks by visibility. Option D discards your judgement, though the head of profession should certainly be in the room.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Evidence that contradicts a public commitment**

Research across two programmes shows that a channel closure announced publicly will substantially disadvantage users without reliable digital access. Colleagues propose framing it as "opportunities for future enhancement". What is the most effective response?

- A) Accept the framing; the commitment is public.
- B) Report the findings accurately and do the constructive work alongside — quantify who is affected, specify what assisted provision would mitigate it, and present finding and mitigation together so the organisation has a route that is neither reversal nor denial.
- C) Report the findings starkly and let the organisation respond.
- D) Escalate to the head of profession before acting.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** The proposed framing protects the organisation from discomfort and delivers the worst outcome available: the disadvantage still occurs, nobody owns it, and evidence that could have driven mitigation has been defused. Option B keeps the finding intact and makes it actionable. Quantifying who is affected converts an abstract concern into something a delivery plan can address, and arriving with mitigation means the finding is not a problem dropped on people who genuinely cannot reverse a ministerial announcement. Option A is least effective — softening findings to accommodate a commitment is the precise failure your standards exist to prevent, and it falls hardest on the users least able to absorb it. Option C is accurate and unhelpful. Option D is worth doing in parallel, and is not a substitute for doing the work.

**Question 8 (harder) — A serious safeguarding risk in another team's fieldwork**

You learn that a team is researching a domestic abuse support service, recruiting through the service's own contact list, running video sessions with no protocol for confirming a participant is alone. What should you do?

- A) Raise it with the researcher and offer to help design a safer approach.
- B) Intervene immediately: contact the researcher and their lead, set out the specific risks — that contact via the service's list may reach people whose devices are monitored, and that a video session without an aloneness protocol may expose a participant to danger during the call — press for fieldwork to pause until safeguards are in place, and inform the head of profession in parallel.
- C) Report it to the data protection officer.
- D) Raise it at the next community of practice.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** The potential harm is severe, immediate and physical. Contacting someone through a domestic abuse service's list can alert a perpetrator monitoring their device, and a video call where the researcher cannot establish whether the participant is alone can place a person in danger during the session itself. That severity determines everything about the response: intervene now, press for a pause rather than an improvement plan, and inform the head of profession in parallel rather than sequentially, because setting and assuring safeguarding standards is explicitly your accountability. Option A applies the right instinct at the wrong severity and leaves fieldwork running while you help. Option C addresses a real but secondary concern. Option D is least effective by a wide margin: deferring a live physical-safety risk to a scheduled forum is indefensible, and the community of practice is where the lesson goes afterwards, not where the intervention happens.

**Question 9 (harder) — Strategic insight nobody asked for**

Synthesising across programmes, you conclude that the organisation's biggest user-facing problem is not in any single service but in the transitions between them — users falling between three services that each work adequately alone. No director owns this. What is the most effective response?

- A) Report it in your annual synthesis and move on.
- B) Build the case with evidence from all three services, quantify the population affected using support desk and analytics data, identify who would need to own it, and take it to a forum with cross-service authority — accepting that securing an owner for an unowned problem is itself the piece of work.
- C) Raise it with each of the three service owners separately.
- D) Propose a piece of research on the transitions.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Unowned problems are the characteristic failure of service-shaped organisations, and surfacing them is precisely what cross-programme synthesis is for. Option B recognises that the hard part is not the insight but the ownership: evidence from all three services makes the problem undeniable, quantification makes it comparable to problems that do have owners, and going to a forum with cross-service authority is the only route by which anything unowned gets owned. The final clause is the realistic one — this will take months and constitutes real work rather than a communication. Option A is least effective: the insight is correct and, filed in an annual synthesis, will change nothing. Option C produces three conversations in which each owner reasonably concludes it is mostly someone else's problem. Option D proposes more evidence when the evidence is already sufficient, which is a common and comfortable way of deferring a difficult conversation.

**Question 10 (harder) — Assurance failure you signed off**

Six months ago you assured a study whose screener excluded anyone who had contacted the support desk, systematically omitting users who had already struggled. Its findings shaped a live service change. What should you do?

- A) Add the case to your assurance guidance as a lesson.
- B) Notify the team and decision-makers now, scope precisely which conclusions are compromised and which stand, commission targeted follow-up with the excluded group, and examine why the assurance process did not catch it — then change the process.
- C) Commission follow-up research quietly and correct the record if findings differ.
- D) Raise it with the head of profession and ask how to handle it.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** Four things need doing and option B does all of them. Notification now matters because a live change is running on the findings. Precise scoping matters because conclusions about users who never needed help may remain sound, and overstating the damage is as unhelpful as understating it. Targeted follow-up addresses the gap. And examining the assurance process is the element specific to your level — you signed this off, so the more consequential question is not what the study missed but why your check did not surface it and what to add. Option A converts a live problem into a training anecdote. Option C conceals a known flaw while a service change proceeds, which is the least defensible option here. Option D is worth doing alongside, and routing your own error through someone else is slower and reads worse than owning it.

**Question 11 (harder) — A capability you cannot buy**

Your organisation cannot recruit experienced user researchers; posts stay vacant for months and teams are covered by junior staff working beyond their level. What is the strongest response?

- A) Keep advertising and escalate the recruitment difficulty.
- B) Treat it as a capability design problem rather than a recruitment problem: restructure how research capacity is deployed so that experienced researchers lead across teams rather than being embedded singly, invest in developing the junior staff you have with structured supervision, and build routes in — apprenticeships, secondments, conversion from adjacent roles — while continuing to recruit.
- C) Lower the experience requirements in job adverts.
- D) Use agency researchers to fill the gaps.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** Months of unfilled posts in a competitive market is not a problem that more advertising solves, and treating it as one guarantees years of the same. Option B accepts the constraint and redesigns around it: deploying experienced researchers across teams rather than one-per-team makes scarce expertise go further and gives junior staff supervision they currently lack; developing the people you have is the only supply route you fully control; and building entry routes addresses the pipeline rather than competing for the same small pool. Option C is least effective because lowering the stated requirement without changing the support structure produces exactly the situation you already have, with the added disadvantage of setting people up to fail. Option D is a legitimate short-term measure and a poor strategy, since agency capacity leaves when the contract ends and builds nothing. Attracting and building talent sits at the heart of research leadership, and it is a design problem more often than a market one.

**Question 12 (harder) — Setting a standard that will be resisted**

You intend to require that all research outputs disaggregate findings by access need. You anticipate resistance: teams will say samples are too small to disaggregate meaningfully. What is the strongest approach?

- A) Introduce the requirement and address objections as they arise.
- B) Engage the objection before introducing it, because it is partly correct: with small samples, disaggregation cannot produce rates. Frame the requirement as reporting who was in the sample and what each group experienced, rather than producing group statistics — then pair it with the recruitment support that makes including those participants feasible.
- C) Introduce it as guidance rather than a requirement.
- D) Require it only for studies above a certain sample size.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** The anticipated objection is not obstruction; it is a real methodological point, and a standard that ignores it will be both resisted and technically wrong. Option B resolves the tension by changing what is required: reporting composition and describing each group's experience is entirely feasible with eight participants, whereas reporting completion rates by group is not. That reframe makes the requirement workable and preserves what actually matters, which is that a report should never conceal whether disabled users were present or how they fared. The paired recruitment support is the other half — requiring inclusion without enabling it produces either non-compliance or tokenism. Option A is least effective because introducing a requirement you know to be contestable, and arguing it case by case, spends enormous credibility for a worse outcome. Option C removes the force that makes it happen. Option D exempts exactly the small qualitative studies where the omission is most common.

### Preparation tips

- **Answer as an organisational leader.** Ask what an option does to the system, not only to the instance.
- **Check whether the option builds capability.** An organisation whose quality depends on your personal review does not have quality.
- **Read the distribution of a problem.** Concentrated failures are process diagnoses; scattered ones are conduct diagnoses.
- **Calibrate escalation to severity.** Physical risk goes up immediately and in parallel; practice problems go to the person first.
- **Engage the strongest objection before you launch.** A standard that has already answered its critics survives.
- **Make trade-offs visible to all parties simultaneously.** Parallel private negotiation destroys trust when the parties compare notes.
- **Bring mitigation with bad news.** A finding plus a route forward is actionable; a finding alone invites softening.
- **Treat recruitment and capability shortfalls as design problems.** The market is rarely going to change for you.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Reissuing a standard nobody follows.** The purest form of appearing to act.
- **Reminding people of rules they already broke knowingly.** The weakest available compliance response.
- **Adding friction for the compliant majority.** How organisational burden accumulates and workarounds are invented.
- **Solving without asking why the system permitted it.** Half the job at your level.
- **Mandating attendance or compliance to fix a value problem.** Produces theatre and resentment.
- **Filing a strategic insight rather than finding it an owner.** Unowned problems stay unowned unless someone does the work.
- **Splitting constrained capacity to avoid a decision.** Two thin studies inform nothing.
- **Softening findings to accommodate a commitment.** It falls hardest on users least able to absorb it.

## Conclusion

You have reached the end, and it is worth registering the scale of it: forty-eight questions across four assessment types, with the reasoning worked through on each. That is a substantial piece of professional preparation, and more than most people at your level undertake before an assessment.

Look back at the ground. In the cognitive section you read studies from their outputs, scoped a flaw precisely enough to say which claims survived it, synthesised three unrelated findings into a cross-cutting identity problem, diagnosed six teams' recruitment failure as one supply problem, and read the concentration of ethics breaches as a process diagnosis rather than a conduct one. In the numeric section you weighted rather than averaged, interrogated a vendor's denominator, reconstructed an implied unit value from a business case, recognised a session-based metric masquerading as a journey measure, and declined to endorse a comparison that could not detect the difference it was built to find. In the verbal section you carried "all" across five lines, checked what started a deadline clock, separated fewer challenges from greater robustness, distinguished a rule about outcomes from a rule about conduct, and noticed where you were reading structural lean rather than content. And in the situational judgement section you absorbed a director's pressure on a researcher's behalf, intervened on a live safety risk, found an owner for an unowned cross-service problem, examined your own assurance process after your own sign-off failed, and designed a standard around its strongest objection rather than against it.

A thread runs through all of it. Almost every item was asking two things at once: do you know the limits of the evidence, and can you act on the system rather than the instance? Those are the two capabilities your role consists of. Organisations can generate opinions without you and read dashboards without you. What you supply is evidence that can be trusted at the strength stated, and a practice around you that keeps producing it when you are not looking.

A word about the specific difficulty of your level. The errors that cost you most are no longer the ones you would notice. They are made fluently, in areas where your experience runs deep enough that checking feels unnecessary — and the verbal reasoning section is the clearest demonstration, because expertise measurably costs marks there. The leads whose judgement holds up across a career are, with striking consistency, the ones who kept a habit of deliberate scepticism about their own reasoning long after it stopped feeling required.

If you want to build on this, a few things repay effort more than rereading. Audit a study you personally assured and ask who could not have appeared in its sample; the exercise is uncomfortable and instructive in roughly equal measure. Trace three figures in your organisation's current board papers back to source. Take the two teams whose outputs are consistently strongest and go and find out what they actually do differently, rather than assuming it is talent. Ask a peer at another department to critique your organisational standard as harshly as they would a stranger's. And the next time you are about to reissue something, ask instead why the first issue did not take.

Finally, for the day itself. An assessment samples your reasoning on one morning under artificial conditions. It is not a verdict on your judgement or your career. Read the stem twice, set expertise aside in the verbal section, find the denominator, trust Cannot Say, and remember that at your level the strongest answer is usually the one that is honest about uncertainty and acts on the mechanism rather than the symptom.

Good luck. The profession is stronger for leaders who prepare like this, and so are the people who depend on these services.
