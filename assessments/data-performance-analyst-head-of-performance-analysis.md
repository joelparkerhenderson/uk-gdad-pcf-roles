# Performance Analyst (Head of Performance Analysis) - Psychometric Assessment Resources

## Introduction

Welcome. This document is a practical preparation resource designed specifically for you as a head of performance analysis working within the UK Government Digital and Data profession. Whether you are preparing for an internal assessment, a promotion board, a cross-government move, or simply want to sharpen the mental toolkit you use every day, this guide will help you practise, reflect, and build confidence.

Psychometric assessments are structured, standardised exercises that measure the mental capabilities and behavioural judgement that underpin effective performance in a role. They are widely used across the Civil Service and the wider public sector because they are evidence-based, fair, and predictive of on-the-job success. For a leadership role such as yours, they are rarely about raw speed of calculation. Instead, they probe the deeper capabilities your role demands: interpreting complex performance data across multiple services, spotting inconsistencies in analytical outputs before they reach ministers or boards, weighing competing priorities when several teams escalate issues at once, and exercising sound judgement in politically sensitive situations.

This document covers four assessment types, each in its own section:

1. **Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment** - your ability to reason, deduce, spot patterns, check for errors, and solve problems using the kinds of artefacts you handle as a head of profession: KPI frameworks, quality assurance criteria, community capability plans, and escalation logs.
2. **Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment** - your ability to interpret and manipulate the numbers your role lives with: service performance metrics, completion rates, cost per transaction, capability budgets, and recruitment figures.
3. **Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment** - your ability to extract precise meaning from dense written material: strategy documents, service standard guidance, stakeholder emails, and quality assurance policies.
4. **Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment** - your behavioural judgement in realistic dilemmas involving senior stakeholders, your performance analysis community, cross-government collaboration, and the political dimensions of analysis.

Each section explains what the assessment measures, maps it explicitly to the skills in your role profile, provides a substantial set of practice questions with worked answers, and closes with preparation tips and common pitfalls.

How to use this guide: work through the practice questions honestly, under light time pressure where suggested, before reading the explanations. Treat the explanations as coaching rather than marking. Many of them model the reasoning habits - verify before trusting, read the exact wording, consider the strategic and political context - that distinguish strong heads of profession. Return to sections more than once; the value is in the reflection, not just the score.

You bring years of analytical leadership to this. This guide simply helps you show it at its best.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the mental processes you rely on daily as a head of performance analysis: logical deduction, pattern recognition, error checking, prioritisation, and structured problem solving. Unlike a generic IQ test, every question is grounded in artefacts you genuinely handle - performance measurement frameworks, quality assurance checklists, escalation logs, community capability matrices, and analytical outputs from multiple teams.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 15 to 30 minutes, with questions that mimic the everyday data formats of the role. Scoring is objective and usually benchmarked against a norm group of comparable professionals. Modern platforms may adapt question difficulty based on your answers, and employers commonly receive a breakdown of speed versus accuracy rather than a single score. You will usually be offered short, ungraded practice questions first so you can get comfortable with the interface - take them.

For a head-of-profession role, cognitive assessments matter because your decisions ripple. When you sign off a quality assurance framework, dozens of analysts apply it. When you deduce which escalation genuinely needs your intervention, whole programmes are unblocked or delayed. Assessors want evidence that you can reason accurately under pressure with realistic, messy inputs - and that you notice when something does not add up before anyone else does.

### What it measures for your role

Mapped to the skills in your role profile:

- **Analysis and insight**: questions test whether you can apply a range of techniques to interpret data, draw sound inferences, and spot the innovative reframing that resolves a stuck problem.
- **Quality assurance of data and analysis**: error-checking items test your ability to verify and validate figures, definitions, and logic across outputs produced by multiple teams - exactly the assurance role you hold.
- **Performance measurement**: pattern and consistency questions use KPI frameworks across multiple projects, testing whether you can see where frameworks are misaligned with business needs or with each other.
- **Understanding analysis across the product life cycle**: deduction questions probe whether you can identify risks that sit between life cycle phases, where they are easiest to miss.
- **Technical understanding**: several items test structured reasoning about analytics tooling choices and their consequences.
- **User-centred analysis**: prioritisation questions require you to keep the end user's outcome at the centre while juggling organisational pressures.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) - Pattern recognition in a KPI framework**

You are reviewing the KPI definitions used by four service teams. Three follow a consistent pattern; one does not.

- Team A: "Digital take-up = digital transactions ÷ total transactions, monthly, GOV.UK analytics"
- Team B: "Completion rate = completed journeys ÷ started journeys, monthly, GOV.UK analytics"
- Team C: "User satisfaction = satisfied responses ÷ total responses, monthly, exit survey"
- Team D: "Cost per transaction = total service cost ÷ digital transactions, monthly, finance system"

Which definition breaks the framework's internal consistency, and why?

A) Team A - digital take-up should be measured weekly
B) Team B - completion rate should use unique users, not journeys
C) Team C - satisfaction uses a survey rather than analytics
D) Team D - the denominator uses digital transactions rather than total transactions

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** The first three metrics each divide a subset by its own complete population: digital transactions of all transactions, completed journeys of started journeys, satisfied responses of all responses. Team D divides total service cost by digital transactions only, which inflates apparent efficiency of the digital channel by ignoring assisted-digital and offline transactions that the cost figure includes. Option C is a difference in data source, not a logical inconsistency - the framework can legitimately mix sources. This is the kind of denominator error that a head of performance analysis is expected to catch during quality assurance, because cost per transaction is one of the four mandatory KPIs for government services and an inconsistent definition would mislead cross-service comparison.

**Question 2 (easy) - Logical deduction from escalation rules**

Your escalation policy states: "An issue is escalated to the head of performance analysis only if it is unresolved after 10 working days AND it affects more than one service, OR it involves a data protection risk of any kind."

Which of the following issues should reach you?

A) A dashboard outage affecting one service, unresolved for 15 working days
B) A KPI disagreement affecting three services, resolved in 4 working days
C) A one-service analytics implementation that may have captured postcode-level personal data
D) A resource conflict between two services, unresolved for 8 working days

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Work through the logic precisely. Option A fails the first condition (one service only) and involves no data protection risk. Option B affects multiple services but was resolved, so it never satisfies "unresolved after 10 working days". Option D affects more than one service but has only been open 8 days. Option C involves a potential data protection risk, and the policy says such issues escalate "of any kind" regardless of duration or service count - the OR clause operates independently. Reading compound conditions exactly as written, rather than as you assume they were intended, is a core assurance habit: analysts across your community will apply these rules literally, so you must be able to as well.

**Question 3 (moderate) - Error checking across team outputs**

Two teams submit quarterly figures for the same shared service. Team X reports: starts 120,000; completions 96,000; completion rate 80%. Team Y, using the same source, reports: starts 120,000; completions 96,000; completion rate 84%. Both teams insist their arithmetic is right. What is the most likely explanation you should investigate first?

A) One team has made a simple division error
B) The teams are using different definitions of "completion", so the 96,000 figures coincidentally match but the rates were calculated on different filtered bases
C) The analytics platform double-counted starts for one team
D) One team rounded to the nearest whole percentage and the other did not

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Check the arithmetic yourself: 96,000 ÷ 120,000 = 0.80, so Team X's rate follows from its own numbers and Team Y's does not - 84% of 120,000 would be 100,800 completions. Since both teams "insist their arithmetic is right", the strongest hypothesis is that Team Y computed its rate on a different filtered base (for example, excluding journeys abandoned within 30 seconds as non-genuine starts, giving roughly 114,300 effective starts) and then reported the unfiltered counts alongside it. A rounding difference (D) cannot produce a 4-point gap. Option A is possible but the question asks what to investigate first, and definitional divergence is both more common and more damaging across a multi-team service - it is precisely what a consistent performance measurement framework exists to prevent. Your quality assurance framework should require teams to publish the exact base used for every rate.

**Question 4 (moderate) - Prioritisation as an escalation point**

It is Monday morning. Four items are waiting for you:

1. A lead analyst reports that a departmental board pack going out Thursday contains a KPI figure she believes is wrong.
2. A senior analyst asks you to approve a conference talk abstract due Friday.
3. A service owner emails, copied to your director, complaining that performance analysis "keeps blocking" his release with assurance requests.
4. A recruitment panel needs your sift decisions by Wednesday for a campaign that closes candidates' availability windows.

In which order should you address them?

A) 3, 1, 4, 2
B) 1, 3, 4, 2
C) 1, 4, 3, 2
D) 4, 1, 2, 3

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Item 1 comes first: a potentially wrong figure heading into board-level decision making is the highest-consequence, time-critical risk, and correcting analysis before it informs decisions is the essence of quality assurance. Item 3 is next: a stakeholder conflict already visible to your director is reputationally live and concerns your core duty to champion performance analysis - left until Wednesday it hardens into a narrative that assurance is a blocker. Item 4 has a hard Wednesday deadline but is a scheduled, bounded task; doing it after the two live risks still meets the deadline. Item 2 has the latest deadline and lowest consequence. Option A puts stakeholder soothing ahead of a possible error reaching a board, which inverts your professional duty; option C delays the director-visible conflict behind routine sift work. Note the pattern: consequence and irreversibility outrank mere deadline order.

**Question 5 (moderate) - Deductive reasoning about the product life cycle**

Your assurance framework requires: every service in public beta must have a performance framework signed off; every signed-off framework must include all four mandatory KPIs; and any service lacking a signed-off framework must not proceed to a live assessment. You learn that Service Q is booked for a live assessment next month, and that its framework is missing the cost per transaction KPI. What can you validly conclude?

A) Service Q's framework was never signed off
B) Service Q will fail its live assessment
C) Either Service Q's framework was signed off in breach of the rules, or Service Q is booked for assessment in breach of the rules, or both
D) Service Q is not in public beta

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Deduce carefully. If Service Q's framework were validly signed off, it would include all four mandatory KPIs - it does not, so any sign-off that exists breached the rules. If it has no signed-off framework, then booking a live assessment breached the rule that unsigned services must not proceed. One of those two breaches (or both) must have occurred; the evidence does not tell you which. Option A overreaches: a non-compliant sign-off may exist. Option B predicts an assessment outcome the rules do not determine. Option D does not follow at all - the rules about beta say nothing that lets you infer Q's phase from a missing KPI. Assessments reward the answer that captures exactly what follows, no more. In the real role, this disjunctive conclusion is actionable: you would check the sign-off record first, because it tells you which control failed and where between-phase risk is leaking through your life cycle assurance.

**Question 6 (moderate) - Pattern recognition in performance data**

A service's weekly completion rates over twelve weeks read: 71, 72, 70, 73, 71, 72, 58, 71, 72, 70, 73, 71. A lead analyst proposes reporting the twelve-week average of 70.3% to the board as "stable performance around 70%". What is the most important analytical objection?

A) The average should be weighted by weekly volume
B) A single week at 58 within an otherwise tight 70-73 band is an anomaly that needs investigation and separate narration, not absorption into an average
C) Twelve weeks is too short a period for any conclusion
D) The board should see medians, not means

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Eleven of the twelve values sit in a narrow 70-73 band; week seven's 58 is roughly 13 points below the band - a clear outlier, not noise. Averaging it away both slightly depresses the reported figure and, more importantly, buries a signal: something happened that week (an outage, a release, a data collection failure) that the board and the service team may need to know about. "Stable around 70%" is a compelling story, but your skill profile requires communicating stories that are relevant and accurate, and proactively presenting findings that inform wider decisions - an unexplained one-week collapse is such a finding. Options A, C and D are refinements that might each have merit in context, but none addresses the concealment of an anomaly, which is the material issue. Insight begins with noticing what breaks the pattern.

**Question 7 (moderate) - Problem solving on tooling**

Three teams use three different analytics tools; their figures for equivalent metrics differ by 3-8%, and cross-service reporting is becoming contentious. Consolidating everyone onto a single tool would cost significant money and retraining time, and each tool has features one team depends on. Which option best resolves the problem within your remit to align technology with business goals?

A) Mandate immediate migration to the most widely used tool
B) Let each team keep its tool but publish a caveat on every cross-service report
C) Keep the tools but define and assure a common measurement specification - shared metric definitions, tracking standards, and a reconciliation check - so figures become comparable, then review tooling consolidation as part of business planning
D) Stop producing cross-service reports until the tools agree

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The stated business problem is comparability, not tool multiplicity. Discrepancies of 3-8% between analytics platforms usually stem from definitional and implementation differences - session definitions, bot filtering, tag placement - which a common measurement specification directly addresses at low cost. Option A pays the maximum price immediately and destroys features teams depend on, without first testing whether cheaper alignment suffices; it may still be right eventually, which is why C folds consolidation into business planning rather than discarding it. Option B manages the symptom and trains stakeholders to distrust your numbers. Option D abandons a business need. This is the innovative-but-proportionate problem solving your role profile asks for: resolve the problem, sequence the investment, and keep the analytical capability aligned to business goals.

**Question 8 (hard) - Error checking a quality assurance summary**

An assurance dashboard you are about to present states:

- "42 analytical products reviewed this quarter; 36 passed first time (86%)."
- "Of the 6 that failed, 4 were resubmitted and passed, giving a final pass rate of 40/42 (95%)."
- "First-time pass rates by team: Team A 12/14, Team B 14/16, Team C 9/12."

Which figure is internally inconsistent?

A) The 86% first-time pass rate, because 36/42 does not round to 86%
B) The 95% final pass rate, because 36 + 4 does not equal 40
C) The team breakdown, because the team-level first-time passes sum to 35, not the 36 claimed in the headline
D) Nothing is inconsistent; all figures reconcile

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Verify every figure rather than trusting the summary. Headline rounding: 36 ÷ 42 = 85.7%, which legitimately rounds to 86%, so A fails. Final pass rate: 36 + 4 = 40, and 40 ÷ 42 = 95.2% ≈ 95%, so B fails. Team reviews: 14 + 16 + 12 = 42, matching the headline count of reviews. Team first-time passes: 12 + 14 + 9 = 35, which contradicts the headline claim of 36 first-time passes. So either one team's figures are wrong, or the headline is - and you cannot present the dashboard until you know which, because the discrepancy may hide a product that was recorded inconsistently between the team log and the central register. This is exactly the reconciliation habit your quality assurance framework should institutionalise: totals reported at different levels of aggregation must be cross-footed before anything goes to senior stakeholders. A single-unit discrepancy looks trivial, but it destroys confidence in the whole dashboard the moment a board member spots it - and someone eventually will.

**Question 9 (hard) - Deduction about capability planning**

You manage the community's capability through business planning. Your rules of thumb: every service in discovery or alpha needs at least 0.5 of a performance analyst; every beta service needs at least 1.0; every live service needs at least 0.5; and every lead analyst can supervise at most four analysts. Next quarter you will have 3 discovery, 2 alpha, 4 beta, and 6 live services, plus 2 lead analysts and 8 analysts (leads do not count towards service coverage). Which statement is true?

A) You have enough analysts and enough supervision capacity
B) You have enough analysts but not enough supervision capacity
C) You do not have enough analysts, but supervision capacity is sufficient for the analysts you have
D) You have neither enough analysts nor enough supervision capacity

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Compute demand: discovery and alpha are 5 services × 0.5 = 2.5; beta is 4 × 1.0 = 4.0; live is 6 × 0.5 = 3.0. Total demand = 9.5 analyst full-time equivalents against a supply of 8 - a shortfall of 1.5, so you do not have enough analysts. Supervision: 2 leads × 4 = capacity for 8 analysts, and you have exactly 8, so supervision suffices for your current staff (though note it would not stretch to the 9.5 you actually need - a subtlety worth raising in business planning). Option C is the only statement consistent with both calculations. In the real role this arithmetic becomes your recruitment case: a 1.5 FTE shortfall, plus the observation that hiring 2 more analysts would also require a third lead or an increased supervision ratio, is precisely the evidenced narrative that wins funding.

**Question 10 (hard) - Spotting a flawed inference in a team's analysis**

A senior analyst's report concludes: "Users who watched our guidance video completed the application at 91%, against 74% for those who did not. Embedding the video more prominently will therefore raise overall completion by up to 17 percentage points." As the assurance point, what is the strongest objection?

A) The sample sizes are not stated
B) The comparison is observational: motivated users may both watch videos and complete applications, so the 17-point gap cannot be read as the causal effect of the video
C) 91% is suspiciously high for any government service
D) The video should be tested with users before being embedded

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The report commits a classic selection-effect error: users self-selected into watching the video, and the trait driving that choice (motivation, digital confidence, time available) plausibly drives completion too. The causal effect of prominence could be far smaller, zero, or even negative for users who find video a barrier. Option A is a fair secondary point but even enormous samples would not fix confounding. Option C is an unfounded prior, and option D is a sensible next step rather than an objection to the inference. The assured recommendation would be to run an A/B test of video prominence and measure the difference experimentally. Coaching your analysts to distinguish observed association from causal claim - and to phrase recommendations accordingly - is one of the highest-leverage quality assurance interventions a head of performance analysis can make, because flawed causal claims travel fast once they reach decision makers who like their implications.

**Question 11 (hard) - Multi-constraint prioritisation across the life cycle**

Three risks land on your desk the same afternoon:

- Risk 1: A beta service's analytics tagging will break when the front-end framework upgrades in six weeks; fixing it needs two weeks of analyst time; without it, the service enters live assessment with no completion data.
- Risk 2: A live service's exit survey response rate has drifted from 12% to 3% over six months, quietly degrading the user satisfaction KPI everyone still quotes.
- Risk 3: A discovery team has commissioned an external agency to define KPIs without consulting your community, creating a framework that will likely need reworking at alpha.

Your realistic capacity allows you to intervene properly in only two this week. Which two, and why?

A) Risks 1 and 2 - both threaten the integrity of measurement on services already carrying users, while Risk 3 can be corrected at the alpha gate
B) Risks 1 and 3 - both are upstream and cheap to fix now, while Risk 2 is already six months old and can wait another week
C) Risks 2 and 3 - Risk 1 has a six-week runway so the team can handle it
D) Risks 1 and 2 and 3 - all must be addressed simultaneously by delegating

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Weigh consequence, reversibility, and timing. Risk 1 has a hard dependency chain: two weeks of work, six weeks of runway, but analyst scheduling means the window closes fast, and entering a live assessment without completion data is a severe, partly irreversible outcome. Risk 2 is actively corrupting a KPI that senior people currently rely on - every week it persists, decisions rest on a satisfaction figure drawn from a vanishing, increasingly unrepresentative 3% sample. Risk 3 is real but self-correcting through an existing control: your framework review at the alpha transition will catch it, and a brief note to the discovery team costs minutes, not an intervention. Option B undervalues live-service data integrity; option C gambles that a team that has not yet scheduled the fix will do so unprompted. Option D is seductive but dishonest about capacity - the question tests whether you can genuinely prioritise, and "do everything" is the classic evasion. Note that A also respects the life cycle principle from your skill set: risks between phases (Risk 3) are best handled by the phase-gate controls designed for them.

**Question 12 (moderate) - User-centred pattern recognition**

Four insights compete for the single slide you get at the quarterly service board:

1. Page load time improved 22% after the platform upgrade.
2. Assisted-digital phone applications rose 31% in the same quarter that the online journey's drop-out at the identity-verification step rose 19%.
3. Twitter sentiment about the department improved slightly.
4. The team closed 96% of analytics support tickets within SLA.

Which insight should take the slide?

A) Insight 1, because it shows technology investment paying off
B) Insight 2, because the paired movements suggest users are being pushed out of the digital channel by a specific broken step, which has cost, user-experience, and inclusion implications
C) Insight 3, because boards care about reputation
D) Insight 4, because it evidences your community's performance

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The head-of-profession instinct being tested is pattern recognition across metrics plus user-centred strategic thinking. Insights 1, 3 and 4 are each single, static, mildly positive facts requiring no decision. Insight 2 is a correlation between two movements that tells a story with a probable cause (the identity-verification step), a quantified consequence (31% more expensive phone handling), and an implied action (fix the step, watch both metrics revert). It concerns how well user needs are actually being met - some users abandoning, others diverted to a channel that costs more and may serve them worse or better depending on their needs. Your skill profile asks you to "present compelling findings that inform wider decisions" and to "apply strategic thinking to the problem of how to provide the best service for the end user": insight 2 is the only candidate that does both. Boards remember the analyst who brought them a decision, not a status report.

**Question 13 (hard) - Logical consistency of a strategy**

A draft performance analysis strategy contains four commitments:

1. "All performance reporting will use the central analytics platform by Q4."
2. "Teams may choose the tools best suited to their service's needs."
3. "No service will report a KPI that cannot be independently reproduced from source data."
4. "We will reduce the community's reporting workload by 20% this year."

A lead analyst says commitments 1 and 2 contradict each other. Do they?

A) Yes - mandating one platform and offering free tool choice are irreconcilable
B) No - commitment 1 governs reporting while commitment 2 governs analysis tooling, so both can hold provided the strategy makes that scope distinction explicit
C) No - commitment 2 is subordinate because it appears second
D) Yes - and commitment 3 also contradicts commitment 4, because reproducibility increases workload

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Test for genuine contradiction: two commitments contradict only if no state of the world satisfies both. A world where teams analyse data in whatever tools suit them (commitment 2) but publish their KPIs through the central platform (commitment 1) satisfies both - many organisations run exactly this model. The contradiction is apparent, not real, but the lead analyst's challenge is still valuable: the draft evidently fails to make the reporting/analysis distinction explicit, which is a drafting defect you should fix before publication, because strategies are read literally by busy people. Option C invents a precedence rule that does not exist. Option D overstates: reproducibility (3) has an upfront cost but often reduces steady-state workload by eliminating rework and disputes, so it is in tension with 4 at worst, not contradiction. Distinguishing contradiction from tension from ambiguity is a genuinely senior skill - strategies routinely contain tensions to be managed, and crying "contradiction" at each one paralyses the community, while missing a real one embarrasses it.

### Preparation tips

- **Rehearse with your own artefacts.** Take a real (suitably anonymised) KPI framework, dashboard, or assurance log and practise finding the inconsistency, the outlier, the flawed inference. The test formats mirror these artefacts closely, and familiarity breeds speed.
- **Verify before selecting.** As several questions above show, the tempting answer often fails a thirty-second check. Build the habit of doing the arithmetic or the logic yourself rather than pattern-matching to what "usually" is wrong.
- **Read compound conditions literally.** Escalation rules, assurance criteria, and strategy commitments in test questions are constructed exactly, with ANDs and ORs that matter. Slow down on them; they are where marks hide.
- **Practise timed prioritisation.** Give yourself two minutes to rank four competing demands and articulate why. Consequence and irreversibility first, deadlines second, comfort last.
- **Trust your seniority.** At head-of-profession level, the assessment expects strategic framing - the answer that fixes the system, not just the instance. When two options both "work", prefer the one that leaves a control, standard, or coached capability behind.
- **Manage your energy, not just your time.** These tests are 15-30 minutes of sustained concentration. Do a practice set at the same time of day as your real sitting, and note where your accuracy dips.

### Common pitfalls

- **Forcing an error to exist.** Assurance-flavoured questions sometimes reconcile perfectly. If your checking says the figures are sound, say so - do not invent a fault because the question "feels like" it wants one.
- **Answering from experience instead of from the material.** You know how government analytics usually works; the question describes how this scenario works. Use only the rules and data given.
- **Time-sink questions.** A multi-stage deduction you cannot crack in ninety seconds should be flagged and skipped; the easier marks you protect are worth more than the hard one you chase. This mirrors the spec guidance for cognitive tests generally, and it is doubly true in adaptive formats.
- **Ignoring scale and scope qualifiers.** "Per quarter", "digital transactions only", "excluding resubmissions" - the qualifier is usually the question.
- **Choosing the clever answer over the correct one.** Senior candidates sometimes over-engineer, selecting the most sophisticated option rather than the one the logic supports. Sophistication is not a scoring criterion; validity is.
