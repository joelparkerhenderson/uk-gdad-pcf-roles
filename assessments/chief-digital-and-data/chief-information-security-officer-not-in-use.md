## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive ability assessment is a targeted psychometric test that measures the exact mental processes needed for a specific role. Unlike a generic IQ test, it uses practical workplace scenarios and data to predict job performance. For a chief information security officer, that means the questions are built from the artefacts and situations you handle at executive level: organisation-wide risk registers, security maturity assessments, incident timelines and post-incident reviews, board and audit committee papers, control frameworks, supplier assurance findings, and strategy documents that must align your organisation's security posture with wider government security strategy.

The assessment measures how accurately and quickly you can recognise patterns, deduce conclusions from stated rules, check complex material for errors, prioritise competing demands, and solve structured problems. These are precisely the capabilities you exercise when you evaluate the current status and maturity of information and cyber security in your organisation, when you determine how to reach the level of maturity the organisation needs, and when you interrogate a plan or paper before it reaches the board.

Typical format and timing: these tests are usually delivered online and timed, most often between 15 and 30 minutes, with multiple-choice questions. Your results are compared against a benchmark or norm group rather than judged in isolation, and questions are strictly validated to ensure they predict job success without bias. Many platforms provide short, ungraded practice questions first so you can learn the interface, and some are adaptive, adjusting difficulty based on your previous answers. Employers frequently receive a breakdown of speed versus accuracy rather than a single number — a useful reminder that at executive level, disciplined accuracy under time pressure is the point, not raw pace.

One reassuring note: nothing in a well-constructed cognitive assessment depends on privileged knowledge. Everything you need is in the question. Your task is to reason cleanly from the material presented — the same discipline you apply when a programme director presents you with a persuasive but incomplete security case.

### What it measures for your role

Your role profile says you will evaluate maturity, understand risks across the organisation, advise the board, prepare the organisation for cyber attacks, and lead implementation of an information and cyber security strategy. Mapped to cognitive dimensions:

- **Pattern recognition** supports your evaluation of the current status and maturity of information and cyber security: spotting trends in incident data, phishing simulation results, audit findings, and control failures that reveal systemic weakness rather than isolated noise.
- **Logical deduction** supports your governance and advisory duties: reasoning correctly from policy conditions, risk appetite statements, and control rules when you advise the board and other leaders on how to mitigate risks in their areas and in future plans.
- **Error checking** supports your assurance role: finding the inconsistency in a risk register, the arithmetic slip in a dashboard, or the unsupported inference in a paper before it drives a poor decision.
- **Prioritisation** supports your leadership of strategy implementation and incident preparedness: sequencing remediation, investment, and response actions when everything is urgent and resources are finite.
- **Structured problem solving** supports your duty to enable the organisation to be innovative in a safe and secure way: decomposing a novel proposal into its security-relevant parts and reasoning about consequences before committing.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy)**

Your vulnerability management team applies the following triage rules to newly reported vulnerabilities:

- Rule 1: Any vulnerability that is being actively exploited in the wild must be remediated within 48 hours.
- Rule 2: Any vulnerability on an internet-facing system rated Critical must be remediated within 5 days, unless Rule 1 applies, in which case Rule 1 takes precedence.
- Rule 3: All other Critical vulnerabilities must be remediated within 14 days.
- Rule 4: High-rated vulnerabilities must be remediated within 30 days.

A Critical vulnerability is reported on an internal (not internet-facing) finance system. Threat intelligence confirms it is being actively exploited in the wild. What is the remediation deadline?

A) 5 days
B) 48 hours
C) 14 days
D) 30 days

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** Rule 1 applies to "any vulnerability that is being actively exploited in the wild" — it is not restricted to internet-facing systems. Active exploitation is confirmed, so the 48-hour deadline applies. The distractor A tempts you to apply Rule 2, but Rule 2 concerns internet-facing systems and this system is internal; the distractor C tempts you to apply Rule 3 because the system is internal, but Rule 3 covers "all other" Critical vulnerabilities, meaning those not already caught by an earlier rule. Rule 1 catches this one first. The cognitive skill here is applying rules in their stated order of precedence rather than by surface association — exactly the discipline you need when policy exceptions and escalation clauses interact.

**Question 2 (easy)**

Your quarterly phishing simulation click rates for the whole organisation over six quarters are: 18%, 16%, 14%, 12%, 10%, and then 15%. Which statement best describes the pattern?

A) A steady decline throughout, indicating the awareness programme is working consistently.
B) A steady decline of two percentage points per quarter, broken by a reversal in the final quarter that warrants investigation.
C) Random fluctuation with no meaningful trend.
D) A decline caused by employees learning to recognise the specific simulation template.

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** Quarters one to five fall by exactly two percentage points each quarter (18, 16, 14, 12, 10), a clean linear decline. The sixth quarter rises to 15%, a five-point reversal that breaks the pattern. Option A ignores the reversal. Option C is wrong because five consecutive equal decrements are clearly not random. Option D goes beyond the data: the numbers alone cannot tell you *why* the decline occurred. The executive habit being tested is noticing when a trend breaks and flagging it for investigation — perhaps a new starter cohort, a harder simulation template, or genuine culture slippage — rather than presenting the board with a comfortable "improving" narrative that the latest data no longer supports. Encouraging a culture of cyber security awareness depends on reading these signals honestly.

**Question 3 (moderate)**

An extract from your corporate risk register uses the scoring rule: Risk score = Likelihood (1-5) × Impact (1-5). Scores of 15 and above are "Severe"; 8-14 are "Major"; 7 and below are "Moderate". Four rows read:

- Risk A: Likelihood 4, Impact 4, Score 16, Rating Severe
- Risk B: Likelihood 3, Impact 5, Score 15, Rating Major
- Risk C: Likelihood 2, Impact 3, Score 6, Rating Moderate
- Risk D: Likelihood 5, Impact 2, Score 10, Rating Major

Which row contains an error?

A) Risk A
B) Risk B
C) Risk C
D) Risk D

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** Check each row against both the multiplication and the banding. Risk A: 4 × 4 = 16, and 16 ≥ 15, so Severe is correct. Risk B: 3 × 5 = 15, and the rule says scores of 15 *and above* are Severe — yet the row says Major. The arithmetic is right but the banding is wrong. Risk C: 2 × 3 = 6, and 6 ≤ 7, so Moderate is correct. Risk D: 5 × 2 = 10, and 10 falls in 8-14, so Major is correct. The error is a boundary error, the most common defect in real risk registers. As the executive who must understand risks across the organisation and advise the board, boundary discipline matters: a mis-banded 15 can mean a Severe risk never reaches the board's attention. The tested skill is systematic verification — checking every row against every rule, not stopping at the first plausible answer.

**Question 4 (moderate)**

Your access control policy states: "Privileged access to production systems may be granted only if the requester has completed privileged-user training within the last 12 months AND holds a current security clearance. Emergency break-glass access may be granted without these conditions, but only during a declared major incident and only with retrospective review within 72 hours."

Which one of the following conclusions follows logically from the policy?

A) Anyone who has completed privileged-user training within the last 12 months may be granted privileged access.
B) A requester with a current clearance but whose training lapsed 14 months ago cannot be granted standard privileged access.
C) Break-glass access always indicates a policy violation.
D) Retrospective review within 72 hours is required for all privileged access.

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** The policy makes training-within-12-months AND current clearance jointly *necessary* conditions for standard privileged access. Option B describes a requester who fails the training condition, so standard privileged access cannot be granted — this follows directly. Option A confuses a necessary condition with a sufficient one: training alone does not guarantee access, because clearance is also required (and "may be granted" never promises access even when conditions are met). Option C is false: break-glass access is explicitly permitted by the policy during a declared major incident, so it is not a violation. Option D over-extends the 72-hour review, which applies only to break-glass access, to all privileged access. Executive-level governance work constantly turns on the difference between "only if" (a gate) and "if" (a permission); this question tests whether you keep that distinction under time pressure.

**Question 5 (moderate)**

During a ransomware incident, your incident manager must sequence five actions. The playbook imposes these constraints:

- Isolation of affected network segments must happen before forensic imaging.
- Forensic imaging must happen before any system rebuilds.
- Notification to the National Cyber Security Centre must happen within the first four actions.
- Executive briefing must happen immediately after isolation.
- System rebuilds must be the final action.

Which sequence satisfies all constraints?

A) Isolation, Executive briefing, Forensic imaging, NCSC notification, System rebuilds
B) Isolation, Forensic imaging, Executive briefing, NCSC notification, System rebuilds
C) NCSC notification, Isolation, Forensic imaging, Executive briefing, System rebuilds
D) Isolation, Executive briefing, NCSC notification, System rebuilds, Forensic imaging

**Answer: A**

**Explanation:** Test each option against every constraint. Option A: isolation first; executive briefing immediately after isolation (satisfied); forensic imaging after isolation (satisfied); NCSC notification is the fourth action, within the first four (satisfied); rebuilds last, after imaging (satisfied). All constraints hold. Option B fails because the executive briefing does not immediately follow isolation — forensic imaging intervenes. Option C fails the same "immediately after" test: the executive briefing is fourth while isolation is second. Option D places forensic imaging after system rebuilds, violating both the imaging-before-rebuilds constraint and the rebuilds-last constraint. The underlying skill is constraint satisfaction — holding several ordering rules in mind simultaneously — which is exactly what ensuring the organisation can detect, respond to and recover from an attack demands of the person at the top of the incident command structure. Note also the practical lesson embedded in the correct answer: leadership communication sits early in the sequence, not as an afterthought.

**Question 6 (moderate)**

Your supplier assurance framework records: "All suppliers holding OFFICIAL-SENSITIVE data must hold Cyber Essentials Plus certification. Supplier Nexora holds Cyber Essentials Plus certification." A programme director concludes: "Therefore Nexora is approved to hold OFFICIAL-SENSITIVE data." Which statement best evaluates this conclusion?

A) The conclusion is valid: Nexora meets the stated requirement.
B) The conclusion is invalid: certification is stated as a necessary condition for holding the data, not a sufficient one, and other requirements may exist.
C) The conclusion is valid provided Nexora's certification has not expired.
D) The conclusion is invalid because Cyber Essentials Plus is not a real certification scheme.

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** The framework states a one-way requirement: holding OFFICIAL-SENSITIVE data → must hold Cyber Essentials Plus. It does not state the converse: holding Cyber Essentials Plus → approved to hold the data. Concluding the converse is the formal fallacy of affirming the consequent. Nexora's certification removes one barrier but the statement gives no basis to conclude approval; contractual, data protection, and residual-risk conditions may all apply. Option A commits the fallacy. Option C is a distractor that adds a real-world caveat but still endorses the invalid inference. Option D imports outside knowledge (falsely — and the instruction is to reason from the text regardless). This is one of the most consequential reasoning errors in supplier security: real breaches have followed from treating a baseline certificate as blanket approval. As the leader who ensures the organisation delivers services in a safe and secure way, catching this fallacy in a programme paper is a core part of your assurance instinct.

**Question 7 (challenging)**

Your annual maturity assessment rates five capabilities on a 1-5 scale against a target of 3.5, with these rules: a capability "Meets target" if its current score is at least 3.5; a capability is "On track" if it is below target but improved by at least 0.5 since last year; otherwise it is "Off track". The results:

| Capability | Last year | This year |
|---|---|---|
| Incident response | 3.1 | 3.6 |
| Risk management | 2.8 | 3.2 |
| Security architecture | 3.4 | 3.4 |
| Supplier assurance | 2.2 | 2.8 |
| Awareness and culture | 3.6 | 3.3 |

How many capabilities are "On track" (but not meeting target)?

A) One
B) Two
C) Three
D) Four

**Answer: A**

**Explanation:** Apply the rules row by row, remembering that the categories are exclusive. Incident response: 3.6 ≥ 3.5, so it Meets target — and because it meets target, it cannot also be counted as "On track", even though it improved by 0.5. Risk management: 3.2 < 3.5 and improvement is 3.2 − 2.8 = 0.4, which is below the 0.5 threshold, so Off track. Security architecture: 3.4 < 3.5 with improvement of 0.0, so Off track. Supplier assurance: 2.8 < 3.5 and improvement is 2.8 − 2.2 = 0.6, which is at least 0.5, so On track. Awareness and culture: 3.3 < 3.5 and the change is −0.3, a decline, so Off track — and notably it met target last year, a regression a security leader should probe, since culture is the asset your whole mandate rests on. Exactly one capability is On track. If you selected B, you almost certainly counted Incident response because it improved by 0.5 — the trap the question sets. Assessment writers deliberately construct overlapping-looking categories with exclusive definitions, and real RAG dashboards do the same: interrogate the category definitions before you accept the counts, because a mis-counted status summary is one of the commonest ways boards are accidentally misled about the maturity journey you are accountable for leading.

**Question 8 (challenging)**

You must schedule a red team exercise, subject to these constraints: it cannot run during the annual change freeze (the whole of December); it must finish at least four weeks before the external audit begins on 1 April, i.e. by 4 March at the latest; it requires the SOC's new detection platform, which goes live on 15 January; and it needs three consecutive weeks of SOC support. The SOC cannot support the exercise during its planned failover test, which occupies the last two weeks of January (the weeks beginning 15 and 22 January). Treating weeks as beginning on the 1st, 8th, 15th, and 22nd of each month for simplicity, what is the earliest possible start date?

A) 15 January
B) 1 February
C) 15 February
D) 1 March

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** Tabulate the constraints and test candidates in order. The platform go-live rules out anything before 15 January, and December is excluded anyway by the change freeze. Candidate 15 January: the three consecutive weeks would be those beginning 15 January, 22 January, and 1 February — but the SOC failover test occupies the weeks of 15 and 22 January, so this start collides for two of its three weeks. Rejected. Candidate 1 February: the three weeks are those beginning 1, 8, and 15 February, ending around 21 February. The failover test is over (it ended with the week of 22 January), the platform is live, and 21 February is comfortably before the 4 March audit gate. All four constraints are satisfied, so 1 February is feasible — and since every earlier date fails, it is the earliest. For completeness: 15 February would end around 7 March, missing the audit gate by three days, and 1 March is later still, so C and D fail even though they avoid the failover clash. The skill being measured is methodical constraint-tracking: tabulate every constraint, test candidates one at a time, and resist the intuition that "later is safer" — here the two later options both fail a deadline the earliest feasible option meets. This is precisely how you should pressure-test a programme plan that claims a red team exercise, an external audit, a failover test, and a change freeze can all coexist in one quarter.

**Question 9 (challenging)**

A board paper argues: "Our organisation has not suffered a significant cyber incident in three years. Our security spending has risen 40% over the same period. Therefore our increased spending has made us secure, and we can now safely reduce the security budget to fund other priorities." Which is the strongest criticism of this reasoning?

A) The paper does not state the absolute size of the budget, so the 40% figure is meaningless.
B) The absence of a detected incident does not establish security; the inference confuses absence of evidence with evidence of absence, ignores threat-landscape change, and assumes a causal link between spending and the incident-free period that the facts given do not establish.
C) Three years is too short a period to measure anything.
D) Security budgets should never be reduced under any circumstances.

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B identifies the compound logical failure. First, "no significant incident" may mean "no *detected* incident" — a detection-capability gap masquerading as safety. Second, even if the period was genuinely incident-free, correlation between rising spend and the quiet period does not demonstrate causation; the threat environment, luck, or attacker targeting choices could explain it. Third, even if past spending did cause past security, it does not follow that reduced future spending preserves future security against an evolving threat. Option A raises a fair presentational point but not the core fallacy. Option C is arbitrary — length of period is not the central defect. Option D is dogma, not analysis, and a chief information security officer who argues "never cut" loses credibility with boards; your role is to advise on risk consequences, not to defend budgets reflexively. Dismantling exactly this argument — calmly, precisely, and with the board's objectives in mind — is one of the highest-value cognitive acts your role performs.

**Question 10 (moderate)**

Your monthly security dashboard reports: 1,240 alerts triaged, of which 62 were escalated as incidents. The dashboard states an escalation rate of 5%. The same dashboard reports the four weekly alert totals as 310, 295, 330, and 315. Which statement is correct?

A) Both the escalation rate and the weekly totals are consistent with the monthly figure.
B) The escalation rate is correct, but the weekly totals sum to 1,250, not 1,240.
C) The escalation rate should be 0.5%, and the weekly totals are consistent.
D) Both the escalation rate and the weekly totals contain errors.

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** Check the rate: 62 ÷ 1,240 = 0.05 = 5%, so the stated rate is correct. Check the weekly totals: 310 + 295 = 605; 605 + 330 = 935; 935 + 315 = 1,250. That is 10 more than the reported monthly total of 1,240, so the components and the total disagree. Option B captures exactly this. The discrepancy is small — under 1% — which is precisely why it matters as a test item: dashboards that fail internal consistency checks on small numbers cannot be trusted on large ones, and a security leader who spot-checks totals sets a standard that propagates through the reporting chain. The tested skill is routine cross-footing: whenever a document offers both components and a total, verify they reconcile before relying on either.

**Question 11 (challenging)**

Your incident classification scheme defines: a "Major" incident requires (i) confirmed data exfiltration OR service outage exceeding four hours, AND (ii) more than 10,000 citizens affected. A "Significant" incident requires either (i) or (ii) but not both. An incident occurs: a service outage lasts six hours, and forensic analysis confirms no exfiltration; the affected service has 8,500 registered users, all of whom lost access. How should it be classified?

A) Major, because the outage exceeded four hours.
B) Significant, because condition (i) is met via the outage but condition (ii) is not, since 8,500 is below the 10,000 threshold.
C) Not classifiable, because there was no exfiltration.
D) Major, because losing access for six hours is intuitively serious.

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** Decompose the definition. Condition (i) is a disjunction: exfiltration OR outage over four hours. The outage lasted six hours, so (i) is satisfied despite there being no exfiltration — option C wrongly treats exfiltration as required, ignoring the OR. Condition (ii) requires more than 10,000 citizens affected; 8,500 falls short. Major requires (i) AND (ii); only (i) holds, so it is not Major — eliminating A and D, the latter of which substitutes intuition for the stated scheme. Significant requires exactly one of the conditions, which is precisely the case here. The skill is parsing nested AND/OR logic accurately — the same parsing you must apply to regulatory reporting thresholds, contractual breach clauses, and government incident-categorisation frameworks, where an error in either direction is costly: over-classifying erodes credibility, under-classifying delays mandatory notifications and the crisis-management response you are accountable for making effective.

### Preparation tips

- **Practise on your own artefacts.** Take this week's risk register extract, dashboard, or board paper and interrogate it the way these questions do: What rule structure is actually being applied? Do the components reconcile with the totals? Which inference is not supported by the stated facts? Executive cognitive performance is a habit, and habits are built on real material.
- **Slow down on rule and logic questions; move briskly on pattern questions.** Deduction items punish skimming — map the ANDs, ORs, "only if" clauses and precedence rules explicitly, on paper if permitted. Pattern items usually yield to a first structured look.
- **Verify before you challenge, and accept "no error" as a possible finding.** Some error-checking items are internally consistent; confirming correctness is part of the measured skill.
- **Manage the clock like a portfolio.** With 15 to 30 minutes, no single item deserves more than its proportionate share. Flag and return rather than sinking time — the same discipline you apply to a crowded audit committee agenda.
- **Treat the sitting professionally.** A quiet room, a good screen, and an alert mind measurably improve performance at every level of seniority. Give the assessment the same set-up you would give a National Security Council briefing.

### Common pitfalls

- **Answering from experience instead of from the material given.** Your depth of expertise will tempt you to import context ("in my organisation we would..."). Assessments score reasoning from the presented facts; let experience sharpen your reading, never replace it.
- **Mistaking a necessary condition for a sufficient one.** As Questions 4 and 6 showed, governance text is full of one-way requirements. Read "only if" as a gate, not a permission, and never affirm the consequent.
- **Pattern-matching to the dramatic conclusion.** Senior security leaders are trained to spot danger; test writers exploit this with alarming distractors that do not logically follow.
- **Time-sink questions.** A single over-constrained scheduling item can consume five minutes. Bank the accessible marks first and return if time allows.
- **Trusting stated totals and categories.** Cross-foot the arithmetic, re-derive the banding, and re-check category definitions — boundary and category errors are the assessment's favourite hiding places, just as they are in real dashboards.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numerical reasoning assessment evaluates your ability to analyse, interpret, and make sound decisions using numerical data drawn from the real data-handling demands of your role. It focuses on business mathematics — percentages, ratios, cost-benefit estimations, budget arithmetic, and trend interpretation — rather than abstract formulae. For a chief information security officer, the inputs look like your actual working material: security programme budgets, incident metrics such as detection and recovery times, phishing and training statistics, supplier risk scores, maturity assessment data, and the quantified risk figures you use when you advise the board on how to mitigate risks.

Typical characteristics: questions present tables, charts, or short data extracts; most assessments allow or provide an on-screen calculator, because they prioritise interpretation over mental arithmetic; and there is a strict time limit — often around a minute to ninety seconds per question — requiring quick, accurate extraction of the relevant figures. Precision matters, but so does judgement about which numbers are relevant and which are distractors, since real dashboards always contain more data than any single decision needs.

Why this matters at your level: budget authority and risk quantification sit at the heart of your role. You determine how to get the organisation to the level of information and cyber security maturity it needs, which is fundamentally an investment-allocation problem; you evaluate current status and maturity, which is a measurement problem; and you ensure the organisation can detect, respond to, and recover from attacks, which you evidence through metrics. A chief information security officer who commands the numbers earns the board's confidence; one who fumbles a percentage in a funding discussion loses ground that expertise alone cannot recover.

### What it measures for your role

Mapped to your role profile duties:

- **Budget and investment arithmetic** supports creating and implementing a strategy for information and cyber security: allocating finite funding across capability areas, comparing options, and justifying the allocation to the board.
- **Risk quantification** supports understanding risks across the organisation and advising the board and other leaders: expected-loss calculations, likelihood-times-impact reasoning, and cost-benefit comparison of controls.
- **Operational metrics interpretation** supports ensuring the organisation can detect, respond to and recover from an attack: mean time to detect, mean time to recover, alert volumes, and coverage percentages.
- **Programme measurement** supports evaluating the current status and maturity of security and implementing practices to increase maturity: maturity scores, training completion rates, phishing simulation trends, and remediation throughput.
- **Capacity planning** supports leading the organisation in implementing the strategy: staffing calculations for security operations, on-call rotas, and workload ratios.

### Practice questions

An on-screen calculator is assumed throughout. Show yourself the working before checking each explanation — the explanations show the full arithmetic.

**Question 1 (easy)**

Your annual security budget is £8.4 million. It is allocated as follows: 35% to security operations, 25% to identity and access management, 15% to supplier assurance, 12% to awareness and culture, and the remainder to innovation and emerging technology. How much is allocated to innovation and emerging technology?

A) £1,008,000
B) £1,092,000
C) £1,260,000
D) £924,000

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** The named allocations total 35% + 25% + 15% + 12% = 87%. The remainder is 100% − 87% = 13%. Then 13% of £8.4 million = 0.13 × £8,400,000 = £1,092,000. Distractor A is 12% of the budget (the awareness figure), and C is 15% (the supplier assurance figure) — both punish careless reading rather than weak arithmetic. The habit being tested is completing the percentage inventory before calculating: in board papers, "the remainder" is where quiet reallocations hide, and enabling the organisation to be innovative in a safe and secure way starts with knowing precisely what you are investing in it.

**Question 2 (easy)**

Last year 5,200 of your 6,500 staff completed mandatory security awareness training. This year 5,985 of 6,650 staff completed it. By how many percentage points did the completion rate improve?

A) 8 percentage points
B) 10 percentage points
C) 12 percentage points
D) 15 percentage points

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** Last year's rate: 5,200 ÷ 6,500 = 0.80 = 80%. This year's rate: 5,985 ÷ 6,650 = 0.90 = 90%. The improvement is 90% − 80% = 10 percentage points. Note the language: a rise from 80% to 90% is 10 percentage points, but a 12.5% relative increase (10 ÷ 80 = 0.125) — assessments and board papers both punish confusing the two. When you report culture-change metrics to the executive committee, choosing the right one of these framings, and being able to defend it, is part of encouraging a culture of cyber security awareness with credibility.

**Question 3 (moderate)**

Your security operations centre reports mean time to detect (MTTD) fell from 36 hours to 9 hours after the new monitoring platform went live, while mean time to recover (MTTR) fell from 96 hours to 72 hours. Which statement is accurate?

A) MTTD improved by 75% and MTTR by 25%.
B) MTTD improved by 27% and MTTR by 24%.
C) MTTD improved by 75% and MTTR by 33%.
D) Both metrics improved by the same proportion.

**Answer: A**

**Explanation:** MTTD improvement: (36 − 9) ÷ 36 = 27 ÷ 36 = 0.75 = 75%. MTTR improvement: (96 − 72) ÷ 96 = 24 ÷ 96 = 0.25 = 25%. Option A states both correctly. Option C's 33% comes from dividing the MTTR change by the *new* value (24 ÷ 72) instead of the original — a classic base error. Option B misreads the absolute changes (27 and 24 hours) as percentages. The executive relevance is direct: detection improved far more than recovery, so your next investment case should target recovery capability — orchestrated rebuild, tested backups, exercised playbooks — because ensuring the organisation can detect, respond to *and recover* from an attack is a chain, and the chain now has a clearly weakest link that the percentages expose and the raw hours understate.

**Question 4 (moderate)**

Patch compliance across your four main estates is: cloud platform 98% of 12,000 devices; office endpoints 91% of 30,000 devices; legacy data centre 76% of 5,000 devices; operational technology 60% of 3,000 devices. What is the overall weighted patch compliance rate across all devices, to the nearest percentage point?

A) 81%
B) 89%
C) 91%
D) 93%

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** Compute compliant devices per estate: cloud 0.98 × 12,000 = 11,760; office 0.91 × 30,000 = 27,300; legacy 0.76 × 5,000 = 3,800; operational technology 0.60 × 3,000 = 1,800. Total compliant: 11,760 + 27,300 + 3,800 + 1,800 = 44,660. Total devices: 12,000 + 30,000 + 5,000 + 3,000 = 50,000. Weighted rate: 44,660 ÷ 50,000 = 0.8932 ≈ 89%. Distractor A is the simple (unweighted) average of the four percentages: (98 + 91 + 76 + 60) ÷ 4 = 81.25%, wrong because the estates differ hugely in size. The lesson generalises: unweighted averages of percentages misrepresent any estate-level metric, and a chief information security officer who lets an unweighted 81% or a cherry-picked 98% reach the board is misinforming the risk owners they are duty-bound to advise. Note too the management insight the working exposes: the two small estates contribute disproportionately to the non-compliant population — 1,200 + 1,200 = 2,400 of the 5,340 non-compliant devices — which is where your remediation practices to increase maturity should aim first.

**Question 5 (moderate)**

You are evaluating a control investment. A successful phishing-led compromise is estimated to occur 1.5 times per year on average, with an average cost per incident of £400,000. A proposed email security and hardware-key programme costs £280,000 per year and is expected to reduce incident frequency by 60%. What is the expected annual net benefit of the programme?

A) £80,000
B) £120,000
C) £360,000
D) £600,000

**Answer: A**

**Explanation:** Current expected annual loss: 1.5 × £400,000 = £600,000. The programme reduces frequency by 60%, avoiding 0.6 × £600,000 = £360,000 of expected loss per year. Net benefit = avoided loss − programme cost = £360,000 − £280,000 = £80,000. Distractor C is the gross avoided loss, forgetting the programme's cost; D is the pre-programme expected loss; B is the residual arithmetic error of applying 60% to a single incident's cost (0.6 × £400,000 = £240,000, then £240,000 − £280,000 gives a negative — so B is simply a lure). Expected-value reasoning like this is the quantitative backbone of advising the board on how to mitigate risks: a positive but modest £80,000 net benefit invites the follow-up questions a strong chief information security officer raises unprompted — confidence intervals on the frequency estimate, unquantified benefits such as credential-theft resistance, and whether the same £280,000 would buy more risk reduction elsewhere in the portfolio.

**Question 6 (moderate)**

Your security operations centre must maintain 24/7 coverage with a minimum of 3 analysts on shift at all times. Each analyst works an average of 37.5 hours per week, and 20% of rostered analyst time is lost to leave, training, and absence. How many analysts do you need on the roster, rounded up to the next whole person?

A) 14
B) 17
C) 21
D) 24

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** Required coverage: 24 hours × 7 days × 3 analysts = 504 analyst-hours per week. Effective hours per analyst after the 20% loss: 37.5 × 0.80 = 30 hours per week. Analysts needed: 504 ÷ 30 = 16.8, which rounds up to 17. Option A (14) comes from ignoring the 20% loss factor entirely (504 ÷ 37.5 = 13.44 → 14); option C reflects adding an unnecessary further buffer on top of the loss factor; option D effectively applies the 20% loss twice. If you computed 16.8 and rounded down to 16, remember that capacity calculations round up — 16 analysts supply only 480 effective hours against a 504-hour requirement, leaving shifts uncovered. Staffing arithmetic like this sits directly under your duty to lead implementation of the security strategy and to ensure the organisation can detect and respond to attacks around the clock: an under-resourced SOC is a strategy failure that no policy document can paper over, and a chief information security officer who can reproduce this calculation live in a funding negotiation is far harder to refuse.

**Question 7 (moderate)**

Your organisation's cyber security maturity is assessed across five domains, weighted by criticality: Govern (weight 3, score 3.8), Identify (weight 2, score 3.0), Protect (weight 3, score 3.4), Detect (weight 2, score 4.0), Respond and Recover (weight 2, score 2.6). What is the weighted average maturity score, to one decimal place?

A) 3.2
B) 3.3
C) 3.4
D) 3.6

**Answer: C**

**Explanation:** Multiply each score by its weight: Govern 3 × 3.8 = 11.4; Identify 2 × 3.0 = 6.0; Protect 3 × 3.4 = 10.2; Detect 2 × 4.0 = 8.0; Respond and Recover 2 × 2.6 = 5.2. Sum of weighted scores: 11.4 + 6.0 + 10.2 + 8.0 + 5.2 = 40.8. Sum of weights: 3 + 2 + 3 + 2 + 2 = 12. Weighted average: 40.8 ÷ 12 = 3.4 exactly. The classic error is dividing by the number of domains (40.8 ÷ 5 = 8.16, obviously wrong, which at least self-corrects) or forgetting a weight when summing the denominator; truncation and transposition supply the remaining distractors. Two management observations from the same table are worth more than the average itself: the weakest domain, Respond and Recover at 2.6, is a direct challenge to your duty to ensure the organisation can recover from an attack; and a composite score of 3.4 can conceal exactly that weakness — which is why, when you evaluate the current status and maturity of security, you should always present the domain profile alongside the headline number, and why a board that only ever sees the composite is a board you are not fully informing.

**Question 8 (challenging)**

Ransomware incident costs across government bodies of your size averaged £1.2 million per incident this year and have grown at 15% per year. Your current annual probability of a serious ransomware incident is estimated at 20%. If costs continue growing at 15% per year, what is the expected annual loss from ransomware in two years' time, assuming the 20% probability is unchanged?

A) £276,000
B) £317,400
C) £312,000
D) £1,587,000

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** Project the cost per incident forward two years with compound growth: £1,200,000 × 1.15 = £1,380,000 after one year; £1,380,000 × 1.15 = £1,587,000 after two years. Expected annual loss = probability × cost = 0.20 × £1,587,000 = £317,400. Distractor A applies only one year of growth (0.20 × £1,380,000 = £276,000); C uses simple rather than compound growth (£1,200,000 × 1.30 = £1,560,000, then 0.20 × £1,560,000 = £312,000); D is the projected cost per incident, forgetting to apply the probability. Compounding correctly matters in every forward-looking risk paper you sign: threat-driven cost growth compounds, and an investment case built on simple growth will understate the risk you are asking the board to fund against. This is the arithmetic backbone of determining how to get to the level of security maturity the organisation needs *before* the threat curve gets there.

**Question 9 (moderate)**

Your supplier risk scoring gives each supplier a score out of 100, combining: security questionnaire (40% weight), independent audit rating (35% weight), and incident history (25% weight). Supplier Meridian scores 85 on the questionnaire, 60 on the audit, and 92 on incident history. Suppliers scoring below 75 overall require an improvement plan. What is Meridian's overall score, and is an improvement plan required?

A) 78, no improvement plan required
B) 79, no improvement plan required
C) 74, improvement plan required
D) 76, no improvement plan required

**Answer: A**

**Explanation:** Weighted components: questionnaire 0.40 × 85 = 34.0; audit 0.35 × 60 = 21.0; incident history 0.25 × 92 = 23.0. Overall: 34.0 + 21.0 + 23.0 = 78.0. Since 78 ≥ 75, no improvement plan is triggered — option A. Distractor C tempts those who miscompute the audit component or transpose weights. But notice what the composite hides, because this is the executive point: Meridian's *audit* score of 60 is far below the other components, and audits are the only independently verified input — self-reported questionnaires and historical luck carry 65% of the weight. A chief information security officer reviewing this framework should question whether the weighting itself is sound, since a supplier can pass on self-assessment strength while independent verification signals trouble. Assessments test whether you can compute the score; your role requires you also to challenge the scoring design — both start with command of the arithmetic.

**Question 10 (challenging)**

Your incident response retainer offers two pricing models. Model A: £150,000 annual fee including 200 consultancy hours, additional hours at £250 per hour. Model B: no annual fee, all hours at £400 per hour. Last year you used 640 hours. At what annual usage level (above 200 hours) do the two models cost the same, and which model was cheaper at last year's usage?

A) Break-even at about 667 hours; Model B was cheaper last year.
B) Break-even at 375 hours; Model A was cheaper last year.
C) Break-even at about 667 hours; Model A was cheaper last year.
D) Break-even at 375 hours; Model B was cheaper last year.

**Answer: A**

**Explanation:** Let h be annual hours (h > 200). Model A cost: £150,000 + £250 × (h − 200). Model B cost: £400 × h. Set them equal: 150,000 + 250h − 50,000 = 400h → 100,000 = 150h → h = 666.7 hours. Below that usage Model B is cheaper; above it, Model A's included hours and lower marginal rate win. Check last year's 640 hours: Model A = 150,000 + 250 × (640 − 200) = 150,000 + 110,000 = £260,000; Model B = 400 × 640 = £256,000. Model B was cheaper by £4,000 — and 640 sits just below the 666.7-hour break-even, consistent with the algebra. The distractor break-even of 375 hours is the naive calculation 150,000 ÷ 400, which ignores Model A's 200 included hours and its marginal rate — precisely the shortcut a supplier's glossy pricing summary may encourage. Two executive lessons: first, always model the *marginal* structure of a contract, not just the headline fee; second, notice how close 640 is to the crossover — if incident volumes are trending up, next year Model A likely wins, so the procurement decision should be made on projected usage, not last year's. Pricing thresholds like this are exactly where retainer costs surprise departments mid-incident, and mid-incident is the worst possible time to discover them.

**Question 11 (moderate)**

Your phishing-resistant authentication rollout has covered 5,400 of 9,000 targeted staff after 6 months. The programme must reach 95% coverage by month 12. If the rollout continues at its current average monthly rate (capped, of course, at the total population), will it hit the target, and what will the margin be in staff numbers at month 12?

A) Yes, with 450 staff to spare.
B) Yes, with 2,250 staff to spare.
C) No, it will fall 150 staff short.
D) No, it will fall 450 staff short.

**Answer: A**

**Explanation:** Work step by step. Current rate: 5,400 staff ÷ 6 months = 900 staff per month. Remaining capacity at that rate: 6 × 900 = 5,400 more staff, giving an uncapped projection of 10,800 — more than the 9,000 population, so the projection caps at full coverage of 9,000 by month 12. The target is 95% of 9,000 = 8,550 staff. Margin at month 12: 9,000 − 8,550 = 450 staff to spare. Distractor B (2,250) is delivery capacity versus remaining need (5,400 − 3,150), a real number but not the month-12 coverage margin the question asks for; C and D assume the rate halves or that the cap applies at the target rather than the population. The linear-projection habit — rate, remaining time, cap at the population, compare with target — is one you will use constantly when tracking strategy implementation milestones. Equally important is the sceptical follow-up the numbers invite: early rollouts cover the willing and easy first, so the *last* 20% (contractors, operational staff, legacy-system exceptions) almost never proceeds at the average rate. A strong chief information security officer reports "on track on current trend" and, in the same breath, names the tail risk to the trend.

**Question 12 (easy)**

Security operations consumed £2,940,000 of its £2,800,000 annual budget. What is the overspend as a percentage of budget?

A) 3%
B) 5%
C) 7%
D) 10%

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** Overspend: £2,940,000 − £2,800,000 = £140,000. As a percentage of budget: 140,000 ÷ 2,800,000 = 0.05 = 5%. The common error is dividing by actual spend instead of budget (140,000 ÷ 2,940,000 = 4.76%, which rounds to 5% here anyway — the question is kind, but many are not, so fix the habit: variance percentages use budget as the base unless stated otherwise). At executive level you will rarely compute variances yourself, but you will constantly *consume* them, and knowing the base convention lets you spot when a team presents an overspend against a favourable base to soften bad news.

**Question 13 (challenging)**

You are comparing two remediation strategies for a legacy estate of 400 applications. Strategy X: remediate in place at an average of £18,000 per application, addressing all 400. Strategy Y: migrate 280 of the applications to an assured platform at £25,000 each, decommission 70 at £4,000 each, and remediate the remaining 50 in place at £18,000 each. The security team estimates Strategy X reduces estate risk by 60%, and Strategy Y by 85%. Which strategy has the lower cost per percentage point of risk reduction?

A) Strategy X, at £120,000 per point.
B) Strategy Y, at £96,800 per point.
C) Strategy X, at £96,800 per point.
D) Strategy Y, at £120,000 per point.

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** Cost of Strategy X: 400 × £18,000 = £7,200,000. Cost per point of risk reduction: £7,200,000 ÷ 60 = £120,000 per point. Cost of Strategy Y: migrations 280 × £25,000 = £7,000,000; decommissions 70 × £4,000 = £280,000; in-place remediations 50 × £18,000 = £900,000; total £7,000,000 + £280,000 + £900,000 = £8,180,000. Cost per point: £8,180,000 ÷ 85 = £96,235, approximately £96,800 allowing for rounding in the option — and clearly below £120,000 either way. So Strategy Y, despite costing nearly £1 million more in absolute terms, buys risk reduction more efficiently: option B. The distractors cross-match the strategies and unit costs to catch candidates who compute one side only. The underlying technique — normalising options to cost per unit of benefit rather than comparing headline totals — is the core arithmetic of determining how to get to the level of security maturity the organisation needs within a finite settlement, and it is the form in which spending review teams will expect your investment cases to be argued. Note also what the calculation deliberately leaves out, because your judgement must add it: confidence in the risk-reduction estimates, delivery risk of a 280-application migration, and the run-cost difference after year one. Efficiency arithmetic frames the decision; it does not make it.

### Preparation tips

- **Rebuild your fluency with bases.** Almost every numeric trap at this level is a base error: percentage of what, weighted by what, growth from which year. When you practise, say the base out loud before you divide.
- **Use your own management information.** Recompute one figure from your monthly security pack each week — a weighted compliance rate, an expected-loss estimate, a variance. It doubles as quality assurance on your reporting chain and keeps your arithmetic warm.
- **Practise with the calculator you will use.** On-screen calculators reward familiarity. Do not let interface fumbling tax your time budget.
- **Estimate before you compute.** A rough order-of-magnitude check ("about 90%", "roughly £300k") catches transposition and decimal errors instantly, and estimation is itself a scored executive skill.
- **Keep perspective on speed.** At around a minute per question, two accurate answers beat three rushed ones. Accuracy first, then pace.

### Common pitfalls

- **Misreading scale qualifiers.** Confusing monthly with annual figures, or missing "in thousands" on a table header, is the single most common error in numerical assessments — and in board papers.
- **Unweighted averages of percentages.** As Question 4 showed, averaging estate percentages without weighting by size misstates reality, sometimes dramatically.
- **Percentage points versus percent.** A rise from 80% to 90% is 10 points but 12.5% relative growth; choose deliberately and label clearly.
- **Over-calculating.** Some questions yield to estimation or elimination in seconds; performing full-precision arithmetic on every option is a time sink. Match effort to what the decision needs.
- **Getting stuck on one multi-stage problem.** Skip, bank the accessible marks, return if time allows — the same triage you expect of your SOC under alert load.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment evaluates how well you understand, analyse, and draw correct conclusions from written business information, mirroring the exact reading demands of your role. For a chief information security officer, the passages look like your daily reading: security policies and standards, incident notifications, board risk appetite statements, supplier contract clauses, government strategy extracts, and correspondence from auditors, regulators, and senior leaders.

The defining discipline of the format is fact-based logic: you must answer strictly from the text provided, ignoring outside knowledge — even when you know a great deal more about the subject than the passage says. Most questions offer three options: **True** (the statement definitely follows from the passage), **False** (the statement definitely contradicts the passage), or **Cannot Say** (the passage does not provide enough information to decide). Some assessments add multiple-choice comprehension questions about the main argument, tone, or best summary. Timing is tight — typically less than a minute per question — simulating the pace at which you must digest papers before a board meeting.

Why this matters at your level: your role trades in precise language. The difference between "must" and "should" in a policy you sign, between "no evidence of exfiltration" and "no exfiltration" in an incident report you brief to the permanent secretary, or between "we will comply" and "we intend to comply" in a supplier's response, can carry regulatory, legal, and reputational consequences. You also create the environment and culture that ensures information security — and culture is built substantially through documents: strategies people can follow, policies people can obey, and communications people trust. Reading with forensic precision is the upstream skill for writing with it.

### What it measures for your role

Mapped to your role profile duties:

- **Precise extraction of rules and obligations** supports creating and implementing an information and cyber security strategy that aligns with wider government security strategy: strategies inherit obligations from framework documents, and misreading one propagates the error organisation-wide.
- **Distinguishing stated fact from inference** supports understanding risks across the organisation and advising the board: an incident report's careful hedging ("no evidence of") must never be briefed upward as certainty.
- **Spotting contradiction and consistency** supports your governance and assurance duties: policies, standards, and contracts must agree with each other, and you are the person most likely to catch it when they do not.
- **Reading tone and argument** supports encouraging a culture of cyber security awareness and advising leaders: knowing what a carefully drafted paragraph is *doing* — reassuring, hedging, shifting responsibility — is executive-level comprehension.
- **Working at pace under pressure** supports crisis management: during an incident, you will read and approve high-stakes text in minutes, not days.

### Practice questions

For each True / False / Cannot Say item, judge the statement strictly against the passage. Difficulty is labelled per question.

**Passage 1 — Information security policy extract**

"All staff must complete security awareness training within four weeks of joining the organisation and annually thereafter. Contractors engaged for more than 30 days are subject to the same requirement. Line managers are responsible for ensuring their staff complete training on time. Staff who have not completed training by the deadline will have their system access reviewed, and access may be suspended until training is complete. Exemptions may be granted only by the chief information security officer, and only where a documented business case demonstrates that training cannot reasonably be completed on time."

**Question 1 (easy).** True, False, or Cannot Say: Contractors engaged for 21 days are required to complete the security awareness training.

**Answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The policy states that contractors engaged for *more than 30 days* are subject to the requirement. A 21-day contractor falls outside that stated class, so this passage imposes no training requirement on them — but silence is not contradiction. The passage neither requires the 21-day contractor to train nor exempts them; a different policy or the engaging manager might still require it. Because the statement is neither established nor contradicted by the text, the verdict is Cannot Say. If you answered False, you treated the 30-day threshold as exhaustive — reading the passage as saying "and nobody below the threshold need train", which it does not say. There is a sharp drafting lesson here for you as a policy owner: thresholds that are silent below the line generate precisely this ambiguity, and your own policies should state explicitly what happens to the sub-threshold case, because staff and suppliers will otherwise resolve the silence in whichever direction suits them.

**Question 2 (easy).** True, False, or Cannot Say: Staff who miss the training deadline will have their system access suspended.

**Answer: False**

**Explanation:** The passage says access will be *reviewed*, and *may* be suspended. The statement converts a discretionary consequence ("may be suspended") into an automatic one ("will be suspended"), which the passage does not support — indeed it contradicts the passage's careful modality. "Will be reviewed" is the only automatic consequence. This must/may distinction is the single most tested — and most operationally important — verbal pattern for a security leader: enforcement communications that overstate the policy erode trust and are unenforceable, while ones that understate it invite non-compliance. Precision about consequences is part of encouraging good security practices credibly.

**Question 3 (moderate).** True, False, or Cannot Say: An exemption request supported by a documented business case will be granted by the chief information security officer.

**Answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The passage states two necessary conditions for an exemption: only the chief information security officer may grant it, and only where a documented business case demonstrates the training cannot reasonably be completed on time. It nowhere states that a qualifying request *will* be granted — "may be granted only by" establishes who can grant and under what minimum conditions, not an obligation to grant. The statement asserts an outcome the passage leaves discretionary, but it does not contradict the passage either (some such requests might be granted). Hence Cannot Say. Note the subtlety versus Question 2: there, the statement contradicted the passage's explicit "may"; here, the statement is merely unproven. Distinguishing "contradicted" from "unsupported" is the heart of the True/False/Cannot Say format — and of accurate risk reporting.

**Passage 2 — Incident report to the executive committee**

"At 03:12 on Tuesday, monitoring detected anomalous outbound traffic from a server in the citizen services environment. The server was isolated at 03:47. Forensic analysis has so far found no evidence that citizen data was exfiltrated. The affected server hosted a caching service and did not store citizen records, although it processed them in transit. The intrusion vector is believed to be a compromised administrator credential belonging to a third-party support contractor. All administrator credentials for the environment have been rotated as a precaution. The incident has been reported to the National Cyber Security Centre. An assessment of whether the incident meets the threshold for notification to the Information Commissioner's Office is under way and will conclude within 72 hours of detection."

**Question 4 (easy).** True, False, or Cannot Say: Citizen data was not exfiltrated during the incident.

**Answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The report says forensic analysis "has so far found no evidence" of exfiltration. Absence of evidence at an interim stage is not proof of absence: analysis is continuing, and the caching server *processed* citizen records in transit, so exfiltration remains possible. The statement asserts a definite negative the passage does not establish; nor does the passage confirm exfiltration occurred. Cannot Say. This is arguably the most important verbal habit in your entire role: the gap between "no evidence of exfiltration" and "no exfiltration" is exactly the gap between a defensible executive brief and a misleading one — and executives who erase that gap when briefing upward create legal exposure and destroy the trust their crisis communications depend on.

**Question 5 (moderate).** True, False, or Cannot Say: The server was isolated within 45 minutes of detection.

**Answer: True**

**Explanation:** Detection at 03:12; isolation at 03:47. Elapsed time: 03:47 − 03:12 = 35 minutes, which is within 45 minutes. The statement follows arithmetically from two facts stated in the passage, so it is True. Verbal reasoning passages frequently embed small calculations like this; the skill is noticing that "within 45 minutes" is a claim about the stated timestamps, not about anything outside the text. When you brief response performance to the board — a duty flowing directly from ensuring the organisation can detect and respond to attacks — timeline precision of exactly this kind is what separates an authoritative account from an impressionistic one.

**Question 6 (challenging).** True, False, or Cannot Say: The third-party contractor's credential was compromised through a phishing attack.

**Answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The passage says the intrusion vector "is believed to be a compromised administrator credential belonging to a third-party support contractor". It states nothing about *how* the credential was compromised — phishing, malware, credential stuffing, purchase from a criminal marketplace, or insider action are all consistent with the text. Your professional experience may whisper that phishing is the most likely mechanism; the discipline of the format is to silence that whisper and answer from the passage alone. Note also the hedge "is believed to be": even the credential vector itself is presented as a working hypothesis, not established fact. Reading hedges accurately protects you twice over — in assessments, and when deciding which parts of an interim incident report are solid enough to repeat to ministers, the media, or the National Cyber Security Centre.

**Question 7 (moderate).** Which of the following best describes what the report says about regulatory notification?

A) The Information Commissioner's Office has been notified.
B) The incident did not meet the threshold for notifying the Information Commissioner's Office.
C) The National Cyber Security Centre has been notified, and an assessment about notifying the Information Commissioner's Office is in progress with a stated deadline.
D) Both the National Cyber Security Centre and the Information Commissioner's Office will be notified within 72 hours.

**Answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage states two distinct facts: the incident "has been reported to the National Cyber Security Centre" (done), and an assessment of whether the ICO notification threshold is met "is under way and will conclude within 72 hours of detection" (in progress, with a deadline for the *assessment*, not the notification). Option A converts an assessment into a completed notification; B pre-judges the assessment's outcome; D misreads the 72 hours as a notification deadline for both bodies and asserts a notification decision not yet made. Only C tracks the text exactly. Keeping the two regulatory tracks distinct — security notification versus data protection assessment — is precisely the kind of detail on which your credibility with the board, the regulator, and government security colleagues rests during crisis management.

**Passage 3 — Board risk appetite statement extract**

"The board has a low appetite for risks affecting the confidentiality and integrity of citizen data, and accepts that achieving this may constrain the pace of service innovation. The board has a moderate appetite for availability risks to internal corporate services, where short interruptions are tolerable if recovery is swift. The board expects security investment proposals to demonstrate proportionality: controls should be commensurate with the risks they mitigate, and gold-plating is to be avoided. Where a proposed digital service cannot meet the board's appetite for confidentiality risk, the service may proceed only with explicit board approval recorded in the minutes."

**Question 8 (moderate).** True, False, or Cannot Say: A proposed digital service that cannot meet the board's confidentiality risk appetite must be cancelled.

**Answer: False**

**Explanation:** The passage explicitly provides a route for such a service to proceed: "the service may proceed only with explicit board approval recorded in the minutes." The statement's claim of mandatory cancellation directly contradicts this provision, so the answer is False — not Cannot Say. The structure to notice is "may proceed only with X": it does not guarantee proceeding, but it definitively refutes "must be cancelled". This is governance language you both consume and draft. When you advise the board and other leaders on how to mitigate risks in their plans, the difference between a hard stop and an escalation gate determines whether innovation is blocked or governed — and your role profile is explicit that you enable the organisation to be innovative in a safe and secure way, which is exactly what an escalation gate operationalises.

**Question 9 (moderate).** True, False, or Cannot Say: The board tolerates short interruptions to citizen-facing digital services if recovery is swift.

**Answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** Read the availability sentence precisely: the moderate appetite for availability risk applies to *internal corporate services*. The passage says nothing about the board's appetite for availability risk to *citizen-facing* services — the citizen-data sentence concerns confidentiality and integrity, not availability. The statement transplants the tolerance from one service class to another; the passage neither supports nor contradicts it (the board might tolerate citizen-service interruptions, or might not — the text is silent). Cannot Say. This is the "wrong object" trap: the sentence sounds familiar because every word appears in the passage, but the qualifier has moved. Risk appetite statements are precisely where such transplants cause real damage, because programme teams quote them selectively to justify decisions — and you are the officer best placed to catch the misquote.

**Question 10 (challenging).** True, False, or Cannot Say: The board considers some level of security control spending to be potentially excessive.

**Answer: True**

**Explanation:** The passage states that "gold-plating is to be avoided" and that controls "should be commensurate with the risks they mitigate". Gold-plating means implementing controls beyond what the risk warrants — that is, excessive control spending — and instructing that it be avoided entails the board considers such excess possible and undesirable. The statement follows from the text by a short, safe inferential step; True/False/Cannot Say formats do permit such immediate entailments, as distinct from speculative leaps. If you answered Cannot Say, you may be over-applying the "no inference" rule: the standard is that conclusions must be *warranted by* the text, not that they must be verbatim quotations. Calibrating that line — tight inference allowed, speculation forbidden — is the highest-level skill in the format, and in reading board direction generally: proportionality language like this is the board telling you, their chief information security officer, that your investment advice will be judged on value for money as well as on risk reduction.

**Passage 4 — Supplier contract security schedule extract**

"The Supplier shall notify the Authority of any actual or suspected security incident affecting the Services within 24 hours of becoming aware of it. The Supplier shall provide an initial written assessment within 72 hours of notification, and shall thereafter provide updates at intervals agreed with the Authority's security team. The Supplier shall bear the costs of forensic investigation for incidents arising from the Supplier's failure to comply with the security obligations in this Schedule. Nothing in this clause limits the Authority's right to conduct its own investigation, and the Supplier shall provide reasonable assistance to any such investigation at no additional charge."

**Question 11 (moderate).** True, False, or Cannot Say: The supplier must pay the costs of forensic investigation for every security incident affecting the services.

**Answer: False**

**Explanation:** The cost obligation is conditional: the supplier bears forensic costs "for incidents arising from the Supplier's failure to comply with the security obligations in this Schedule". An incident could arise without any supplier non-compliance — a novel attack defeating fully compliant controls, or a failure on the Authority's side — and for such incidents this clause assigns the supplier no forensic-cost liability. The statement's "every security incident" over-extends the conditional obligation into an unconditional one, contradicting the drafted scope. False. Contract questions in verbal assessments almost always turn on such scope-limiting phrases; in your assurance work, the same reading determines whether an invoice dispute after a supplier incident starts from a strong or weak position — worth settling in your favour at drafting time, not at invoice time.

**Question 12 (easy).** True, False, or Cannot Say: The supplier must notify the Authority within 24 hours of a suspected incident, even if the incident is not yet confirmed.

**Answer: True**

**Explanation:** The clause requires notification of "any actual or suspected security incident... within 24 hours of becoming aware of it". "Suspected" is explicitly included, so confirmation is not a precondition of the notification duty. The statement tracks the text directly. The word to prize here is "or": suppliers under commercial pressure routinely read "actual or suspected" as "confirmed", delaying notification while they investigate — and 24 hours of silence at the start of a supply-chain incident can be the difference between contained and catastrophic for the departments you protect. When you review supplier contracts and incident playbooks, this is a clause to test in exercises, not just in prose.

**Question 13 (challenging).** A draft communication from your press office reads: "The department takes security extremely seriously and has invested significantly in cyber defences. At no point were citizens' records accessed by unauthorised parties." Your incident report states that forensic analysis is ongoing and has so far found no evidence of unauthorised access to citizen records. Which statement best evaluates the draft against the report?

A) The draft is consistent with the report and can be released.
B) The draft overstates the report: "at no point were records accessed" asserts a certainty the ongoing investigation has not established, and should be revised to reflect the evidential position.
C) The draft understates the report and is too cautious.
D) The draft contradicts the report because records were accessed.

**Answer: B**

**Explanation:** The report's position is interim and evidential: analysis is *ongoing*, and *so far* no evidence of unauthorised access has been found. The draft converts this into an absolute negative — "at no point were citizens' records accessed" — which the investigation has not established and, if later falsified, would convert a well-managed incident into a public trust crisis and possible regulatory aggravation. Option B captures precisely this gap and the remedy: language such as "we have found no evidence that citizens' records were accessed, and our investigation continues" is both honest and reassuring. Option A misses the modality gap; C inverts it; D asserts a fact found nowhere in either text. Approving crisis communications is one of the most consequential verbal tasks your role performs — ensuring that information and cyber security aspects of crisis management are effective explicitly includes making sure the organisation's public statements never outrun its evidence.

**Question 14 (moderate).** True, False, or Cannot Say (against Passage 4): The Authority must pay the supplier for assistance with the Authority's own investigations.

**Answer: False**

**Explanation:** The final sentence states the supplier "shall provide reasonable assistance to any such investigation at no additional charge". The statement claims the opposite — that payment is required — so it contradicts the text directly. False. The phrase worth noticing is "no additional charge", which anticipates exactly the dispute that arises in practice: suppliers arguing that incident cooperation is out-of-scope billable work. Verbal assessments reward spotting that the passage has already answered the question; supplier management rewards the same reading applied before signature.

**Passage 5 — Government cyber strategy extract**

"Departments should adopt a 'defend as one' approach: cyber security data, expertise, and capabilities are to be shared across government so that the collective defence is greater than the sum of departmental efforts. Lead departments will be designated for cross-cutting risks. Each organisation remains accountable for its own risk decisions; shared capability does not transfer accountability. Departments are expected to achieve the baseline outcomes by the end of the strategy period, and those handling significant volumes of citizen data are expected to exceed them."

**Question 15 (moderate).** True, False, or Cannot Say: Under the strategy, a department that uses shared cross-government security capabilities transfers accountability for its risk decisions to the designated lead department.

**Answer: False**

**Explanation:** The passage addresses this directly and in both halves: "Each organisation remains accountable for its own risk decisions; shared capability does not transfer accountability." The statement asserts precisely the transfer the passage rules out, so it is False — not Cannot Say. Candidates sometimes hesitate because the passage also mentions designated lead departments for cross-cutting risks, which sounds like it might carry accountability with it; but leading a cross-cutting risk and owning another organisation's risk decisions are different things, and the accountability sentence is unqualified. For you, this is more than a comprehension point: it is the constitutional grammar of your role within the wider government security community — you may draw on shared capability, and your strategy must support wider government security strategy, but the risk decisions of your organisation stay yours to advise on and your board's to own. When strategy documents allocate responsibilities across organisations, reading the accountability sentences with exactly this precision is what prevents a "someone else's job" gap opening between departments — the gap through which cross-government incidents historically arrive.

### Preparation tips

- **Rehearse the three verdicts until they are reflexes.** True means the passage *establishes* the statement; False means the passage *contradicts* it; Cannot Say means neither. Most errors at executive level are False/Cannot Say confusions — contradicted versus merely unsupported — so drill that boundary deliberately.
- **Read the statement first, then hunt the passage.** With under a minute per question, scanning for the relevant clause beats re-reading the whole passage. This mirrors how you already read a 40-page contract for its notification clause.
- **Underline modality.** Must, may, shall, should, will, so far, believed to be, no evidence of — these small words decide most answers, in assessments and in every incident report you will ever sign.
- **Suppress your expertise deliberately.** You know more about cyber security than any passage will state. Answer from the text alone; treat every question as if the passage were the entire universe.
- **Practise on your real reading.** Take a policy, a contract schedule, or a strategy extract from this week's box and write three True/False/Cannot Say statements about it. Drafting the questions teaches the format faster than answering them.

### Common pitfalls

- **Bringing outside knowledge.** The most common failure for experts: answering what you know rather than what the passage says. The format punishes it, and so do lawyers.
- **Converting "may" into "will" and "no evidence of" into "none".** Modality drift is the assessment's favourite trap and the press office's favourite temptation — Questions 2, 4, and 13 all turn on it.
- **The wrong-object trap.** Every word of a statement can appear in the passage while a qualifier quietly moves from internal services to citizen services, as in Question 9. Check what each tolerance, obligation, or threshold attaches to.
- **Over-applying "Cannot Say".** Immediate entailments — like Question 10's gold-plating inference — are True. Cannot Say is for genuine silence, not for any answer requiring a moment's thought.
- **Poor time discipline.** Reading every passage exhaustively before looking at the questions spends your minute where it earns nothing. Statement first, clause second, verdict third.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment evaluates your decision-making, interpersonal skill, and professional conduct through realistic workplace dilemmas. Unlike logic or numeric tests, it has no purely objective right answers: several responses to each scenario may seem reasonable, and the task is to identify which best fits the role's responsibilities and the organisation's values. For a chief information security officer in UK government, those values are well defined: focus on user needs, transparency and honesty, collaboration across teams and departments, accessibility and inclusion, protection of citizens and public trust, and careful stewardship of public money.

The typical format presents a short scenario followed by four or five possible actions. You are asked either to select the **most effective** and **least effective** actions, or to rank or rate all options from best to worst. Timing is usually generous or untimed, because the assessment measures your natural judgement rather than your speed. Scoring compares your choices against those of experienced practitioners and against the behavioural framework for the role.

At executive level, situational judgement assessment is arguably the most faithful of the four types to your real work. Your cognitive, numeric, and verbal skills are exercised in service of decisions — and the decisions in this section are the ones your role profile actually assigns you: leading the organisation through incidents and crises, advising boards that may not welcome the advice, building a security culture without becoming the department of "no", handling suppliers, enabling innovation safely, and holding the line on standards when the pressure to bend them is intense.

### What it measures for your role

Mapped to your role profile duties:

- **Board influence and risk advice** — understanding risks across the organisation and advising the board and other leaders on how to mitigate risks in their areas and in future plans, including when your advice is unwelcome.
- **Incident and crisis leadership** — ensuring the organisation is prepared for cyber attacks, can detect, respond to and recover from an attack, and that the information and cyber security aspects of crisis management are effective.
- **Culture building** — creating an environment and culture that ensures security, and encouraging cyber security awareness and good security practices without fear or blame.
- **Safe innovation** — enabling the organisation to be innovative in a safe and secure way, balancing delivery ambition against risk appetite.
- **Strategic stewardship** — creating and implementing a security strategy aligned with organisational and wider government security strategy, and increasing maturity over time rather than chasing quick wins.

### Practice questions

For each scenario, decide your most and least effective actions before reading the commentary.

**Scenario 1 (moderate) — The unwelcome risk report**

You have completed your annual evaluation of the organisation's security maturity. The results are candid: two critical capability gaps in recovery and supplier assurance. The chief operating officer, who chairs the executive committee, asks you privately to "soften the language" in the board paper because the department is seeking budget approval for a flagship digital programme and "now is not the time to look weak on the basics."

Possible actions:

A) Agree to soften the language; you can raise the gaps informally with board members later.
B) Refuse outright and submit the paper unchanged without further discussion with the chief operating officer.
C) Keep the evidence and risk ratings intact, offer to reframe the paper so it pairs each gap with a costed remediation plan and links fixing the basics to the flagship programme's success, and tell the chief operating officer you cannot change the assessed position.
D) Escalate immediately to the permanent secretary that the chief operating officer attempted to interfere with risk reporting.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: A.**

**Explanation:** Option C preserves the non-negotiable — the board must see the true risk position, because your advisory duty runs to the board, not to the comfort of any executive — while behaving like a leader rather than an auditor: reframing candid findings as a funded improvement story linked to what the organisation cares about is exactly how effective chief information security officers convert bad news into investment. Option A is least effective because it corrupts the risk reporting channel at its source; informal corridor briefings do not discharge a formal advisory duty, and if the recovery gap later materialises during the flagship programme, the record will show the board was knowingly misinformed — destroying both organisational trust and your personal integrity. Option B protects the content but wastes the relationship and the opportunity: refusing "without further discussion" treats a stakeholder to be influenced as an obstacle to be defied. Option D is disproportionate as a first move — the chief operating officer's request, while improper, is best answered directly and professionally first; escalation remains available if pressure continues. Transparency and honest reporting are core public sector values, but so is collaborative influence.

**Scenario 2 (challenging) — Mid-incident disclosure pressure**

A ransomware attack has taken down several internal systems. Recovery is under way and forensic analysis is incomplete — you do not yet know whether personal data was taken. A national journalist has the story and will publish within hours. The director of communications proposes a statement saying "no data has been compromised" to prevent panic, and the minister's office is pressing for reassurance it can repeat in the House.

Possible actions:

A) Support the proposed statement; if exfiltration is confirmed later, the statement can be corrected then.
B) Advise that the statement must say only what the evidence supports — systems affected, services impacted, investigation ongoing, no evidence *so far* of data compromise — and brief the minister's office on why over-assurance now would be worse later, offering agreed forms of words.
C) Advise saying nothing at all until forensic analysis is complete.
D) Let the communications team decide; public statements are their professional domain, and your job is the technical response.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A.**

**Explanation:** Option B is the disciplined crisis posture: honest, current, and evidentially bounded. It gives the journalist and the House accurate information, keeps trust intact whichever way the forensics resolve, and treats the minister's office as a partner by giving it usable, safe language — which is what "ensuring information and cyber security aspects of crisis management are effective" means in practice. Option A is least effective: "no data has been compromised" is an absolute claim the evidence cannot support, and if falsified it converts a cyber incident into an integrity scandal — the correction will land on the minister who repeated it, with your advice on the record beneath it. Option C confuses caution with safety: silence for the hours or days forensics may take cedes the narrative, breaches the public's legitimate expectation of candour from government, and is practically untenable once the story is publishing anyway. Option D abdicates: communications colleagues own the channel, but the evidential boundary of what can truthfully be said about a security incident is precisely your professional advice to give. Transparency within the limits of evidence is the value under test.

**Scenario 3 (moderate) — The senior bypasser**

You discover that a director-general routinely forwards OFFICIAL-SENSITIVE papers to a personal email account "to read on the train", in clear breach of policy — the same policy for which three junior staff received formal warnings last quarter.

Possible actions:

A) Quietly fix the exposure by having the security team monitor the personal account traffic, avoiding an awkward conversation with a very senior colleague.
B) Speak with the director-general directly: explain the specific risk, the breach, and the precedent problem, agree an immediate stop and a secure alternative that solves their genuine mobility need, and record the conversation through the standard incident process applied to anyone else.
C) Send an all-staff email reminding everyone of the policy, without naming anyone.
D) Report the director-general to the permanent secretary for disciplinary action without speaking to them first.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A.**

**Explanation:** Option B does the three things this situation demands: it stops a live data-exposure risk; it treats a senior leader identically to junior staff — the precedent point is not cosmetic, because security culture is destroyed faster by visible double standards than by any phishing campaign; and it solves the underlying user need (reading papers on the move) with a secure alternative, which is how good security leaders convert violators into advocates. Recording it through the standard process protects fairness in both directions. Option A is least effective on every axis: it leaves the exposure unresolved in substance, creates a covert monitoring arrangement of dubious propriety, and institutionalises the double standard. Option C is the classic passive evasion — the person who most needs the message is least likely to apply it to themselves, and everyone else learns that seniority buys exemption. Option D applies the right principle disproportionately: escalation before conversation forfeits the chance to fix the behaviour and the need together, though it would become appropriate if the behaviour continued after B. Creating a culture where security applies equally at every grade is the duty being tested.

**Scenario 4 (moderate) — After the click**

A payroll administrator clicked a convincing phishing link and entered their credentials, then immediately reported it themselves. The SOC contained the account within minutes and no loss occurred. The administrator's line manager emails you proposing formal disciplinary action "to send a message", and asks you to name the individual in the monthly security bulletin as a cautionary example.

Possible actions:

A) Support the disciplinary proposal; consequences drive behaviour change.
B) Decline both requests: thank and publicly reinforce the *reporting* behaviour (anonymising the case), explain to the manager that punishing self-reporting teaches staff to hide incidents, and use the anonymised case in awareness material focused on how fast reporting enabled containment.
C) Take no position; disciplinary matters belong to the line manager and human resources.
D) Agree to anonymised inclusion in the bulletin but stay silent on the disciplinary question.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A.**

**Explanation:** Option B is the culture-building answer your role profile demands. The single most valuable security behaviour in this story is the immediate self-report — it is why containment took minutes and losses were zero. Punishing it, or publicly shaming the person, would teach the entire organisation that reporting a mistake is dangerous, and the next clicked link will surface days later via the SOC instead of seconds later via the user. Option B also respects the manager by engaging with the reasoning rather than pulling rank. Option A is least effective: it optimises for the appearance of firmness at the cost of the reporting culture that actually protects the organisation — a textbook case of an ideal-sounding action that is practically corrosive. Option C is an abdication dressed as propriety: disciplinary decisions do belong to line management and human resources, but advising on the *security consequences* of the proposed action is squarely your job. Option D handles the bulletin but ducks the more damaging half of the proposal. Encouraging a culture of cyber security awareness means engineering incentives so that human error surfaces fast — blame is a detection-delay mechanism.

**Scenario 5 (challenging) — Innovation versus assurance**

The digital delivery director wants to launch a public-facing service using a new AI-based document processing tool in six weeks, tied to a ministerial announcement. Your team's security review has found the supplier's data-handling practices unclear: citizen documents may transit infrastructure outside agreed jurisdictions. The delivery director says the concern is "theoretical", the announcement cannot move, and asks you to sign off now with conditions to be resolved later.

Possible actions:

A) Sign off with post-launch conditions; the announcement is a legitimate organisational priority and the risk is unproven.
B) Refuse sign-off and inform the delivery director the launch cannot proceed until the supplier completes a full assurance cycle, however long that takes.
C) Treat it as a joint problem on the critical path: immediately convene your team, the supplier, and the delivery team to pin down the data-flow facts within days; offer a scoped launch option (for example, restricted document types or in-jurisdiction processing only) that fits the announcement; and give the delivery director and senior risk owner a clear written statement of what is and is not yet assured so any residual risk is accepted knowingly at the right level.
D) Escalate to the permanent secretary that the delivery director is attempting to bypass security assurance.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: A.**

**Explanation:** Option C operationalises the most distinctive duty in your role profile: enabling the organisation to be innovative in a safe and secure way. It converts an ambiguity ("may transit infrastructure outside agreed jurisdictions") into facts fast — ambiguity, not risk appetite, is the actual blocker here; it engineers a launch option that honours the announcement while containing exposure; and it places residual-risk acceptance where it belongs, with the named risk owner, in writing, so governance is preserved without your becoming the single point of "no". Option A is least effective: signing off on citizen-data handling you have not understood, against your own team's findings, with "conditions to be resolved later" — post-launch conditions on a live public service are notoriously unenforceable — fails citizens, the law, and the minister the announcement is meant to serve. Option B protects security but at maximum cost to delivery and to the relationship, and treats the six-week constraint as illegitimate when it is merely inconvenient; it is the "department of no" caricature that erodes your influence for the next ten decisions. Option D escalates a solvable disagreement into a conflict before attempting resolution. User needs, pace, and safety are all public sector values — the skill is composing them, not choosing one.

**Scenario 6 (moderate) — The exercise that went too well**

You commission a red team exercise. The testers achieve domain administrator access within four days, undetected. Your head of security operations, who is respected and hardworking, asks you to present the findings to the executive committee "at a high level only", omitting the detection failure, because "the team's morale will be crushed and we already know what to fix."

Possible actions:

A) Present the full findings, including the detection failure, framed as exactly what the exercise was for — finding weaknesses before an adversary does — alongside the remediation plan, and separately work with the head of security operations on how the team hears the results constructively.
B) Present at a high level as requested; the technical detail would not be understood by the committee anyway.
C) Present the full findings and single out the detection failure as the security operations team's failure, so accountability is clear.
D) Postpone the presentation until remediation is complete so the story is better.

**Most effective: A. Least effective: D.**

**Explanation:** Option A holds two truths together: governance requires that the executive committee, which owns the risk, sees the genuine result — an undetected four-day path to domain administrator is exactly what it pays red teams to find — and leadership requires that the operations team experiences the exercise as learning, not humiliation. Handling those in separate conversations is the executive craft. Option D is least effective, narrowly worse than B: it not only withholds the finding but delays remediation oversight and, if a real incident occurred in the interval, would leave the organisation's leaders unaware of a known critical weakness — a governance failure with the executive committee's name on the risk and yours on the delay. Option B launders the result; "they would not understand" is the oldest self-serving filter in security reporting, and committees fund what they understand to be broken. Option C is transparent but destructive: public blame of a named team teaches every team to manage findings rather than fix them, the precise opposite of the culture you are charged with creating. Exercises exist to make the organisation prepared for attacks; that purpose survives only if their results travel truthfully.

**Scenario 7 (challenging) — The supplier breach that is not yours**

A major supplier notifies you, within its contractual 24 hours, of a confirmed breach on its side affecting services it provides to your organisation and to two other government departments. Early indications are that your organisation's data is probably unaffected, but the supplier's account manager asks you, "as a partner", not to inform the other departments yet — "we will coordinate disclosure once we know more, and premature sharing could cause unnecessary alarm."

Possible actions:

A) Honour the supplier's request; it is their breach and their disclosure to coordinate.
B) Immediately share what you know with the security leads of the two departments and with the appropriate central government security coordination function, tell the supplier you have done so and why, and continue working with the supplier on the joint response.
C) Inform the central coordination function only, and let it decide whether the other departments should know.
D) Demand the supplier disclose to the other departments within 24 hours, and take no other action while you wait.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A.**

**Explanation:** Option B reflects the structural fact your role profile encodes: you belong to both the Government Digital and Data profession and the Government Security profession, and cross-government protection outranks any single supplier relationship. The other departments may be actively exposed *now*; hours matter in containment, and their chief information security officers hold the same duty to their organisations that you hold to yours. Telling the supplier openly what you have shared preserves the partnership honestly rather than covertly. Option A is least effective: it privileges a supplier's communications preferences over the security of government, leaves peer organisations exposed to a known threat, and — if their data proves affected — makes you complicit in the delay; "avoiding alarm" is the perennial cover for avoiding accountability. Option C is better than A but launders your direct knowledge through an intermediary, adding delay when you could do both (B includes the coordination function). Option D sets a deadline but tolerates up to 24 further hours of peer exposure you could eliminate with one phone call, and outsources a duty of candour that is also yours. Collaboration across departmental boundaries is a core public sector value precisely because attackers do not respect those boundaries.

**Scenario 8 (moderate) — Budget cut, strategy intact**

Fiscal pressure requires you to cut 15% from next year's security budget. Your leadership team proposes three options: cancel the multi-year recovery-capability programme (your maturity assessment's weakest domain); stop the security culture and awareness programme ("soft stuff goes first"); or thin every budget line by 15% equally so no programme is visibly cancelled.

Possible actions:

A) Adopt the equal thinning; it is fairest and avoids difficult choices about priorities.
B) Cancel the recovery programme; it has the longest payback and the cut is temporary.
C) Re-prioritise from the risk picture: protect the highest-risk-reduction investments (recovery, given the maturity gap), find the 15% from lower-yield lines and deferrable refreshes, and present the board a revised plan stating explicitly which risks the reduced budget leaves less mitigated and who owns them.
D) Refuse to propose any cuts and tell the finance director that security cannot be reduced without unacceptable risk.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: B.**

**Explanation:** Option C is strategy under constraint, which is the actual job: determining how to reach the maturity the organisation needs does not stop when money tightens — it becomes more important, because every remaining pound must buy maximum risk reduction. Naming the newly under-mitigated risks and their owners keeps the board's risk acceptance informed and explicit, which is your advisory duty working properly. Option B is least effective because it cuts precisely where your own evidence says the organisation is weakest — recovery — meaning the cut is aimed at maximum risk rather than minimum; "temporary" multi-year programme cancellations rarely restart, and an unrecoverable organisation fails catastrophically rather than marginally. Option A, equal thinning, is the abdication of prioritisation disguised as fairness: it degrades everything, protects nothing, and signals to the board that the security budget contains no considered priorities at all — inviting the next cut. Option D is both unrealistic and self-defeating: security competes for public money like everything else, and a chief information security officer who cannot articulate trade-offs forfeits the credibility that wins the argument next year. Stewardship of public money and honest risk advice are the values in tension — C composes them.

**Scenario 9 (moderate) — The quiet whistleblower**

A mid-level engineer asks to see you privately and, visibly nervous, tells you that their team has been instructed by their delivery manager to disable a set of security logging controls to hit a performance target, and to "not make a fuss about it". The engineer fears career consequences for speaking to you.

Possible actions:

A) Thank the engineer, promise confidentiality, verify the claim technically through the SOC (logging states are independently checkable) before acting, then address the control change through governance channels without exposing the source, and follow up personally to ensure the engineer suffers no detriment.
B) Immediately call the delivery manager and ask whether the engineer's account is true, mentioning who raised it so the manager can respond fairly.
C) Take no action until the engineer is willing to make a formal written complaint; unverified verbal reports are not a basis for intervention.
D) Publicly commend the engineer's courage at the next all-hands to encourage others to speak up.

**Most effective: A. Least effective: B.**

**Explanation:** Option A gets every element right: protection of the source (the engineer's fear is realistic, and how this case is handled will be observed — silently — by everyone deciding whether to raise the next concern); independent verification before accusation (logging configurations leave evidence, so the claim can be tested without exposing anyone); action through governance so the control decision is corrected at the level that made it; and personal follow-up, because a culture where reporters suffer quiet retaliation is a culture where you hear nothing until the breach. Option B is least effective: naming the source to the person they fear is a direct betrayal that endangers the engineer and teaches the organisation that speaking to the chief information security officer is unsafe — one such story can silence a department for years. Option C mistakes bureaucratic process for diligence; you have an independently verifiable claim about a live security degradation, and conditioning action on the reporter's willingness to accept formal exposure inverts the risk. Option D, though well meant, breaches the promised discretion just as surely as B. Your role profile's first line — creating an environment and culture that ensures security — is built or broken in exactly these moments.

**Scenario 10 (challenging) — Crisis roles under fire**

A destructive cyber attack is under way against a critical citizen-facing service. The crisis management team convenes. The chief executive, under intense pressure, begins directing technical containment actions personally — ordering systems shut down ad hoc, bypassing the incident commander you appointed and trained. One instruction just issued would destroy volatile forensic evidence needed to understand the attack; another would take down a dependent service used by emergency services.

Possible actions:

A) Comply with the chief executive's instructions; in a crisis, unity of command under the most senior person present matters more than process.
B) Intervene immediately and privately-but-firmly in the meeting: recommend the two specific instructions be halted, giving the concrete consequences (evidence destruction; emergency services impact), and propose the agreed crisis structure be restored — the chief executive directing strategy, communications, and ministerial interface while the incident commander directs technical response, reporting to them.
C) Let the instructions proceed but document your objections carefully for the post-incident review.
D) Announce to the room that the chief executive is making the incident worse and that everyone should follow the incident commander instead.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C.**

**Explanation:** Option B is what "ensuring that information and cyber security aspects of crisis management are effective" means at the moment it is hardest: stopping specific, imminent, high-consequence errors with concrete reasons a non-technical leader can act on instantly, and repairing the command structure by giving the chief executive a vital role rather than a rebuke — strategy, communications, and the ministerial interface genuinely need them, which is why the crisis design puts them there. Effective challenge in the room, respectful in form and unmistakable in substance, is precisely the executive behaviour situational judgement tests probe at chief level. Option C is least effective — worse even than A's misguided deference, because C sees the harm, has the standing and knowledge to prevent it, and chooses self-protection instead: documenting objections while emergency services lose a dependent system is the definitive failure of the role. Option A at least errs honestly, but seniority does not convert a bad technical order into a good one, and crisis doctrine assigns command by function, not grade, for exactly this reason. Option D makes the right point in the wrong way: publicly humiliating the chief executive mid-crisis fractures the crisis team's cohesion when it is most needed and converts a correctable error into a standoff. Protecting citizens comes first; preserving working relationships is how you stay able to protect them tomorrow.

**Scenario 11 (moderate) — The accessibility objection**

Your team proposes mandatory hardware security keys for all staff. The disability staff network raises that the chosen keys are difficult for staff with limited dexterity, and the rollout guidance assumes abilities some staff do not have. Your programme lead responds that exceptions can use the old, weaker method, and that redesigning now would delay the security improvement for everyone.

Possible actions:

A) Proceed as planned; a small exception group on the legacy method is an acceptable trade-off for rapid risk reduction.
B) Pause and engage the staff network and accessibility specialists to select authentication options that are both phishing-resistant and usable by everyone, adjusting the rollout so no group is left on weaker protection, even at the cost of some delay.
C) Proceed for the majority now and promise to solve accessibility "in phase two".
D) Abandon the hardware key programme; if it cannot be done for everyone it should not be done at all.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A.**

**Explanation:** Option B recognises what options A and C miss: the "exception" design does not merely inconvenience staff with disabilities — it assigns them *weaker security protection*, creating a two-tier arrangement in which a definable group is both excluded from an improvement and left as the attack surface adversaries will find (attackers profile for the weakest authentication in an estate). Accessible, phishing-resistant options exist, and finding them is an engineering task, not a concession. Option A is least effective because it normalises exactly this discrimination-by-architecture and, on pure security logic, undermines its own goal — an estate is as strong as its weakest tier. Option C is A with a promise attached; "phase two" accessibility fixes share the fate of post-launch security conditions from Scenario 5, and equality duties in the public sector are not deferrable by roadmap. Option D over-corrects into paralysis, abandoning a major risk reduction rather than doing the inclusive design work. Accessibility and inclusion are not external constraints on your security culture — a culture that "ensures the security of information and technology" for the whole organisation must, by its own definition, work for every member of it.

### Preparation tips

- **Anchor on the role's duties, not your personal style.** Before the assessment, re-read your role profile. The scoring key rewards the behaviour the *role* requires — informed risk acceptance at the right level, honest reporting, culture-building, safe enablement of innovation — even where your personal instinct might be more directive or more accommodating.
- **Look for the option that solves the problem *and* preserves the relationship.** At chief level, the most effective option almost always both protects the substantive security outcome and keeps the stakeholder engaged. Options that win the issue but burn the relationship, or vice versa, are usually middle-ranked.
- **Check who owns the risk.** Many scenarios turn on placing a decision at the right level: residual risk belongs to named senior owners, disciplinary decisions to line management, public statements to communications — with your advice feeding each. Options where you either seize others' decisions or abandon your advisory duty are rarely the keyed answer.
- **Distinguish first moves from last resorts.** Escalation, refusal, and formal reporting are legitimate tools that score poorly as *opening* moves when a direct, professional conversation is available — and score well when the scenario says the conversation has already failed.
- **Practise articulating the "why".** For each practice scenario, write one sentence justifying your most and least choices in terms of public sector values — user needs, transparency, collaboration, accessibility, protection of citizens. If you cannot name the value at stake, re-read the scenario.

### Common pitfalls

- **Choosing the idealistic answer over the effective one.** "Refuse all compromise" and "escalate immediately" sound principled but are usually low-scoring because they abandon influence, proportionality, and the possibility of a better outcome — as in Scenarios 1, 5, and 8.
- **Selecting passive or deferring options.** Waiting for forensics to finish, postponing bad news, taking no position, or documenting objections instead of acting — passivity is the most reliably penalised behaviour in situational judgement scoring, and Scenario 10's option C shows why.
- **Ignoring the culture signal your action sends.** At chief level, every decision is also a broadcast: punishing self-reporting, exempting seniors, exposing whistleblowers, or blaming teams each teach the organisation a lesson that outlasts the case. Options that fix the instance while poisoning the culture are traps.
- **Over-weighting the immediate stakeholder.** The person in front of you — the supplier account manager, the delivery director, the chief operating officer — is rarely the party your duty runs to. Citizens, the board, peer departments, and staff who are not in the room usually hold the stronger claim.
- **Forgetting that enabling is part of the role.** Assessors for security leadership roles specifically screen out "department of no" instincts. Where an option exists that achieves the mission safely — a scoped launch, a secure alternative, a reframed paper — it will usually outrank both unconditional approval and unconditional refusal.

You have covered the four assessment types most relevant to your role as chief information security officer: cognitive ability exercised on risk registers, maturity data, and board papers; numerical reasoning applied to budgets, staffing, expected loss, and programme metrics; verbal reasoning tested against policies, incident reports, risk appetite statements, and contract clauses; and situational judgement examined through the board-level, crisis-level, and culture-level dilemmas your role genuinely presents.

A consistent thread runs through all four sections, and it is worth naming: precision in service of trust. The board trusts your risk advice because your reasoning is sound and your numbers reconcile; ministers and the public trust your organisation's statements because they never outrun the evidence; your staff trust the security culture because it is fair at every grade and safe to report into; and the wider government security community trusts you because you collaborate across boundaries when it matters. Psychometric assessments sample these capabilities in miniature — every modality distinction, weighted average, and most/least judgement in this guide is a small rehearsal of the real thing.

As next steps, consider three habits. First, keep practising little and often: one passage, one calculation, or one scenario a week, ideally drawn from your own board pack, keeps the skills warm far better than an occasional cramming session. Second, use the frameworks around you — your role sits within both the Government Digital and Data profession and the Government Security profession, and both offer communities, standards, and development routes worth drawing on. Third, pass it on: the reasoning disciplines in this guide are exactly what your deputies and rising security leaders need, and coaching them is both capability building for your organisation and consolidation for you.

Assessments, in the end, measure a snapshot; your effectiveness is built daily, in how you read, reason, decide, and lead. You have invested real effort in sharpening all four today. Keep going — your organisation, and the citizens it serves, are safer for it.
