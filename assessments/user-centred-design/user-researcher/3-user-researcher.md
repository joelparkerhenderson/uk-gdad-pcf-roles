# User Researcher - Psychometric Assessment Resources

## Introduction

Welcome. This guide is written for you as a user researcher in the UK Government Digital and Data profession — someone embedded in a multidisciplinary team, responsible for planning and carrying out research activities, and expected to work independently. That last phrase changes a great deal about what follows. You are no longer the person who executes a plan somebody else designed; you are the person who defines the scope and purpose of research, chooses methods, involves the team, builds consensus with stakeholders, and presents findings that colleagues can actually use. The assessments in this guide are pitched at that level of responsibility.

You may be preparing for a formal assessment as part of a recruitment or promotion process, or you may simply want to sharpen the reasoning your job depends on. Either way, it is worth naming what these assessments are and are not. They are not intelligence tests, they do not measure your worth, and they are not designed to catch you out. They are standardised exercises that sample the thinking a role genuinely uses — and in a role like yours, that thinking is unusually visible. Every week you read evidence and decide what it supports, weigh a method against a question and a constraint, interpret a table of completion rates, read a standard and apply it, and make judgement calls in rooms where the incentives do not all point towards good research.

There is a particular reason this matters for user researchers specifically, and it is worth being blunt about it. Your professional credibility rests on being the person in the room whose claims can be trusted. Designers advocate for their designs, product managers advocate for their roadmap, policy colleagues advocate for their policy — and you are expected to say what the evidence shows, at the strength it shows it, including when that is inconvenient. That role only works if your reasoning is genuinely sound. The skills these assessments measure are, almost exactly, the skills that make you worth listening to.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the reasoning your role genuinely uses, presented through practical materials rather than abstract shapes and sequences. At your level the materials get more realistic and the reasoning gets less mechanical: you will meet research plans with competing constraints, evidence that points two ways at once, and questions where the right answer depends on distinguishing what the data supports from what it merely suggests.

The format is usually an online test of fifteen to thirty minutes, covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective against a norm group at a comparable level, so the comparison is with other experienced practitioners rather than with the general population. Many platforms are adaptive, raising difficulty after correct answers — if the test feels hard, that is usually a good sign. Most employers receive an acuity report showing speed against accuracy rather than a single score, which means precision is rewarded and reckless pace is not.

For a user researcher, the relevance of this section is direct. Independent working means nobody checks your reasoning before it reaches the team. When you decide that four participants hitting the same wall constitutes a finding while two constitutes a hypothesis, that judgement goes straight into a report. When you spot that a screening criterion would systematically exclude the users the study most needs, you spot it or nobody does. When you notice that an analytics figure and a qualitative observation appear to contradict each other, working out which is measuring what is your job alone. These are cognitive acts, and the quality of them determines the quality of everything downstream.

What rises at this level, compared with a more junior one, is the expectation that you reason under genuine ambiguity. Several questions below have no clean answer, only a best one, and the discriminating factor is whether you can identify the strongest reading of incomplete evidence rather than the most comfortable one. Some ask you to hold two things true at once — that a pattern is worth acting on and that it is not yet established. That combination is uncomfortable and it is exactly what good research judgement looks like.

You still need to memorise nothing. Every rule, definition and figure appears in the question. But you will need to apply judgement to information rather than merely process it, and you will need to notice when a question is testing whether you know the limits of your own evidence.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The cognitive dimensions map onto the skills named in your role summary:

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **Analysis and synthesis**, where you apply techniques for analysing data and synthesising findings, and present clear findings colleagues can understand and use. Distinguishing a genuine pattern from an artefact of your sample is the core analytic act.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Research management, leadership and assurance**, where you ensure research has a clearly defined scope and purpose, and to **User-centred practice and advocacy**, where you advocate for the user by sharing insights. Advocacy built on an overreach collapses the first time someone checks it.
- **Error checking** maps to **Research management, leadership and assurance**, where you build your understanding of good practice standards and improve your practice through reflection and feedback. Catching flaws in your own plans before fieldwork is cheaper than catching them afterwards.
- **Prioritisation** maps to **Agile research practices**, where you understand the benefits and limitations of agile working and design research so findings can be embedded in an agile workflow. Timing is a methodological decision, not an administrative one.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **User research methods**, where you understand when to use methods and how to apply them correctly, and to **Inclusive research**, where you work with colleagues to include many kinds of users in appropriate research activities.
- **Reasoning about people and positions** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**, where you build and reach consensus with stakeholders and use evidence to explain decisions. Understanding why someone holds a position is a reasoning task before it is a relational one.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Error checking a research plan**

Your team's plan standard requires every research plan to state: the research questions, the method, the recruitment approach, the sample composition including access needs, the analysis approach, and how findings will be shared. You review a colleague's draft plan, which states the research questions, the method, the recruitment approach and the analysis approach, and includes a line saying "findings will be presented at the sprint review". How many required elements are missing?

- A) 0
- B) 1
- C) 2
- D) 3

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Check each of the six requirements against the draft. Research questions, method, recruitment approach and analysis approach are all present — that is four. The line about presenting at the sprint review satisfies the sixth requirement about how findings will be shared, which brings the count to five. The missing element is the sample composition including access needs. So the answer is B. The trap is C, which catches candidates who overlook that "findings will be presented at the sprint review" is a valid, if brief, statement of the sharing approach. Note also which element is the missing one: sample composition including access needs is the requirement whose absence most reliably produces research that fails the people it most needs to serve, which is why it belongs in the standard in the first place.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction from a governance rule**

Your organisation's standard states: "Research involving participants under 18 may proceed only with documented approval from the head of user research, unless the research is conducted entirely through an existing school partnership under the standing agreement." A colleague says: "We ran sessions with 16-year-olds last week without documented approval from the head of user research." Which must be true?

- A) The standard was breached.
- B) The research was conducted through the school partnership.
- C) Either the research ran under the school partnership, or the standard was breached.
- D) The sessions were unethical.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A conditional rule with one stated exception, plus facts that do not tell you whether the exception applies, yields only the disjunction. Options A and B each assume one branch without evidence. Option D imports an ethical judgement the question does not support — research can be conducted under a standing agreement, entirely properly, without a specific approval document. Only C holds in every possible case. This structure recurs constantly in assessments, and it is worth recognising instantly. It is also worth noticing what the correct answer implies for your conduct: the right professional move is to ask which route was used, not to assume the worst. At your level, where you are building your understanding of good practice standards and giving colleagues feedback, the difference between an enquiry and an accusation matters.

**Question 3 (moderate) — Pattern recognition across mixed evidence**

Your qualitative sessions with ten participants found that seven struggled to understand the eligibility criteria. Analytics show that only 4% of users abandon the service at the eligibility page. A product manager concludes that the qualitative finding must be wrong. What is the strongest analysis?

- A) The product manager is right; analytics reflect real behaviour at scale.
- B) The qualitative finding is right; analytics cannot show comprehension.
- C) The two measure different things — abandonment is not comprehension — so people may be proceeding past the page while misunderstanding it, which is potentially worse than abandoning.
- D) The datasets are incompatible and cannot be reconciled.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The apparent contradiction dissolves once you name what each source measures. Analytics measure behaviour: whether someone left the page. The sessions measure understanding: whether someone knew what the criteria meant. A user who does not understand the eligibility criteria but proceeds anyway generates no abandonment signal at all — and may go on to submit an application they were never eligible for, wasting their time and the department's. That is a worse outcome than abandonment, not a lesser one, and it is entirely invisible in the analytics. Option A privileges scale over relevance. Option B is defensive and misses the chance to make the stronger argument. Option D gives up on a reconciliation that is straightforward. Being able to explain why two sources differ, rather than defending your own, is one of the most useful things you can do in a room where quantitative data is treated as automatically more authoritative.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Prioritisation across competing research needs**

You support one team and have capacity for one substantial study in the next three weeks. Four candidates:

1. A new eligibility screen goes into build in two weeks; no research has been done on it.
2. A live service has a 38% drop-off at document upload, ongoing for months.
3. A policy team wants exploratory research on a service concept for next year.
4. Your team wants to re-test a screen you researched six weeks ago after minor copy changes.

Which should you prioritise, and why?

- A) Option 1, because research must precede build.
- B) Option 2, because it affects the largest number of real users now.
- C) Option 1, because it is the only one where research findings can still change a decision within the window, while options 2 and 3 remain valuable but are not time-boxed in the same way.
- D) Option 3, because early-stage research has the highest leverage.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The deciding factor in agile research prioritisation is not importance in the abstract but whether findings can still change a decision. Option 1 has a hard window: research done in the next two weeks influences what gets built, and research done afterwards becomes a critique of something already made. Option 2 matters enormously and affects more users, but it has persisted for months and will still be there in four weeks, so deferring it costs relatively little. Option 3 has real leverage but no deadline pressure. Option 4 is the weakest, since minor copy changes rarely justify a full re-test. Note that C and B differ not in their assessment of importance but in their assessment of timing, and C makes the reasoning explicit rather than asserting a rule. Option A states the right conclusion for the wrong reason — "research must precede build" is a slogan, not an argument, and it would rank option 3 oddly.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Error checking a sampling approach**

A study plans twelve participants for a service used by around four million people annually. A stakeholder objects that twelve is far too few to be representative. Which response is most rigorous?

- A) Agree, and increase the sample to fifty.
- B) Explain that qualitative research does not aim for statistical representativeness — it aims to find and understand problems — and that twelve well-chosen participants across the relevant user groups will surface the substantial usability issues, while a rate would require different methods.
- C) Explain that twelve is the industry standard.
- D) Explain that sample size does not matter in qualitative research.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The stakeholder's objection conflates two different goals, and the rigorous response separates them rather than either conceding or dismissing. Qualitative sessions are designed to discover what problems exist and why they occur, and a modest sample spread across the relevant user groups does that efficiently. Establishing how prevalent a problem is across four million users is a different question requiring analytics or a survey. Option B says this and points to the alternative, which respects the underlying concern. Option A concedes on the wrong basis and buys a much more expensive study that still would not deliver representativeness. Option C appeals to convention rather than reasoning, and conventions do not survive a determined challenge. Option D is simply wrong — sample size and, more importantly, sample composition matter a great deal in qualitative work; what changes is what they are for.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Deduction about what a finding licenses**

Nine of twelve participants failed to notice a "save and return" link. Which conclusion is best supported?

- A) 75% of users do not notice the link.
- B) The link's discoverability is a substantial problem, evidenced across most participants and varied user groups, and warrants design work and a follow-up measure of whether the change helps.
- C) The link should be moved to the top of the page.
- D) The link is invisible.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Nine of twelve is a strong qualitative signal, strong enough to act on, and option B states it at the right strength while specifying what acting looks like. Option A converts twelve participants into a population rate, which is the single most common overreach in research reporting and the one most likely to be quoted back at you in a meeting you are not in. Option C names a specific solution, and prescribing a design is usually not the researcher's call — your job is to establish the problem clearly enough that designers can solve it, and to help evaluate whether the solution worked. Option D overstates: participants did not notice the link, which is different from the link being invisible, and the distinction matters because it points at different causes such as visual hierarchy, wording, or the user's mental model of the task. Notice that B also builds in the follow-up measurement, which is what turns a finding into a change you can defend.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Applied problem solving with a constraint**

You need research on a service used mainly by people over 70, many of whom are not online. Your organisation's recruitment supplier works exclusively through an online panel, procurement for a new supplier takes eight weeks, and you have four. What is the strongest approach?

- A) Use the online panel and note the limitation.
- B) Delay the research until a suitable supplier is procured.
- C) Work within the four weeks using routes that do not require procurement — partnering with organisations that already work with older people, running sessions in community venues or by telephone, and arranging incentives that do not require an online account — while starting the procurement in parallel for future rounds.
- D) Run the research with staff who have older relatives.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The constraint is procurement, not recruitment, and the strongest analysis separates the two. Partnering with charities, day centres, libraries and local support organisations is a legitimate and widely used route that does not depend on a supplier contract, and telephone or in-person sessions remove the digital barrier that the online panel creates. Cash or high-street vouchers avoid the online-account problem that quietly excludes exactly this group. Option C also starts the procurement anyway, which fixes the structural problem for next time — solving the instance and the system together. Option A produces findings about digitally confident older people, which is a different population from the one the service serves, and the limitation note does not repair that. Option B forfeits four weeks and, in most organisations, the research never happens. Option D is not research; convenience samples of colleagues' relatives carry no credibility and would rightly be challenged.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Reasoning about a stakeholder position**

A policy lead has repeatedly resisted research findings suggesting the application form is too long. You learn that the form's length results from statutory requirements they are responsible for defending. What is the most useful reframing?

- A) The policy lead is being obstructive and should be escalated.
- B) The resistance is likely about the perceived implication that they should breach statutory duties; reframing the research around how to make the required questions easier to answer, rather than fewer, addresses the real user problem without asking them to do the impossible.
- C) The findings should be softened to avoid the conflict.
- D) The research should focus on a different part of the service.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Understanding why somebody resists is a reasoning task, and the answer here reframes the problem so both the user need and the statutory constraint can be satisfied. If the questions must be asked, the researchable question becomes how to ask them: sequencing, plain language, explaining why each is needed, saving progress, and splitting the form across sessions. All of those can substantially improve completion without removing a single statutory question. Option A treats a structural conflict as a personality problem and escalates a relationship you need. Option C compromises the findings, which is never the answer. Option D abandons a real user problem. Your role names building and reaching consensus with stakeholders and using evidence to explain decisions, and this is what that looks like in practice: you do not change what the evidence says, you change the question the evidence is asked to answer.

**Question 9 (harder) — Distinguishing signal from artefact**

Across three rounds of research on a benefits service, participants have consistently described the process as "straightforward". Recruitment has run through the department's existing user panel, which is made up of people who have previously completed applications successfully and volunteered for research. What is the strongest analysis?

- A) The service is straightforward; three rounds is consistent evidence.
- B) The finding is likely an artefact of the sampling frame: a panel of people who completed successfully and then volunteered systematically excludes those who failed, gave up, or were deterred — so the consistency reflects who was asked, not how the service performs.
- C) More rounds should be run to confirm the finding.
- D) The panel should be discontinued.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This is survivorship bias, and recognising it is one of the highest-value analytic skills in applied research. Every person in the panel has cleared two filters — they completed the process, and they had enough spare capacity and goodwill to volunteer afterwards. People who abandoned the application, were rejected, found it distressing, or lack the time and confidence to join a panel are absent by construction. Their absence is invisible in the data, which is precisely what makes it dangerous: the consistency across three rounds feels like strong evidence and is actually three samples from the same filtered pool. Option A mistakes repetition for corroboration. Option C would produce a fourth consistent round and greater false confidence. Option D overcorrects — the panel is useful for some questions, and the fix is to supplement it with recruitment routes that reach non-completers, not to abandon it.

**Question 10 (harder) — Prioritising under a genuine trade-off**

You have three weeks. You can run either six sessions with a broad mix of users covering the whole journey at moderate depth, or twelve sessions concentrated on the two steps where analytics show the largest drop-offs. The team is about to rebuild those two steps and has no plans to change the rest. Which is stronger, and why?

- A) The broad study, because whole-journey understanding is always more valuable.
- B) The concentrated study, because the team can only act on the two steps in question, and twelve sessions there will produce findings robust enough to guide a rebuild — while broad findings about screens nobody will change cannot influence anything.
- C) The broad study, because concentrating on two steps risks missing context.
- D) Split the time evenly between both.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Research value is a function of what a team can act on, and here the actionable surface is precisely two steps. Twelve sessions concentrated there gives you enough depth and enough participants to distinguish reliable patterns from individual quirks, which is what a rebuild decision needs. Option A applies a general preference without checking it against the situation; whole-journey understanding is valuable when a team can change the whole journey. Option C raises a real risk, and the mitigation is straightforward — brief context questions at the start of each session preserve enough journey awareness without diluting the focus. Option D is the weakest, producing a study too shallow to guide the rebuild and too narrow to map the journey, which is the characteristic failure of splitting a constrained resource down the middle. Designing research so findings can be embedded into an agile workflow is a named skill in your role, and this question is that skill in miniature.

**Question 11 (harder) — Reconciling contradictory evidence**

A survey of 2,000 users reports 81% satisfaction with a service. Your fourteen qualitative sessions found substantial frustration, with eleven participants describing significant difficulties. What is the most rigorous way to reconcile these?

- A) Trust the survey; 2,000 responses outweigh fourteen sessions.
- B) Trust the sessions; observed behaviour beats self-report.
- C) Examine what each is measuring and who answered: satisfaction surveys typically reach people who completed the journey and often ask about the outcome rather than the process, while your sessions observed the process itself — so both can be accurate about different things, and the discrepancy is itself a finding worth investigating.
- D) Average the two and report moderate satisfaction.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The instinct to declare a winner is the error. Satisfaction surveys are usually served at the end of a journey, so people who abandoned rarely see them; they often ask whether the user got what they needed, which someone can answer positively about a process they found gruelling; and satisfaction is a judgement about outcome, whereas your sessions measured the experience of getting there. All of this can be true simultaneously. Option C also identifies the genuinely interesting move: the gap between "I got what I needed" and "that was hard" is worth reporting in its own right, because a service can be effective and punishing at the same time, and only one of those shows up in the headline number. Options A and B both pick a side on the basis of a general heuristic rather than the specifics. Option D is the worst answer — averaging incommensurable measures produces a number that describes nothing.

**Question 12 (harder) — A flaw in your own study**

Midway through analysis you realise your discussion guide asked, "What made the upload step difficult?" — presupposing difficulty — in all ten sessions. Eight participants described difficulties. What is the strongest response?

- A) Report the finding, noting the wording in the limitations.
- B) Discard the study.
- C) Treat the difficulty frequency as unusable because it was elicited by a leading question, but examine whether the specific difficulties participants described are substantive and consistent, since a leading question can prompt a topic without inventing the content — then verify with a short neutral follow-up round before reporting anything as established.
- D) Re-run all ten sessions with corrected wording.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The leading question compromises one thing precisely: any claim about how many participants found the step difficult, because you asked them all to assume they did. It does not automatically invalidate the substance of what they described. If eight participants independently described the same specific obstacle — say, that the page rejects photographs taken on a phone without explaining why — that content is unlikely to have been conjured by the question's framing, and it converges on something real. Option C separates the contaminated claim from the salvageable content and then does the right thing: verifies before asserting. Option A under-corrects, since a limitations note does not stop the frequency claim being quoted. Option B discards genuine evidence. Option D is disproportionate and expensive when a short targeted round will do. Recognising the precise extent of a flaw, rather than treating it as total or negligible, is characteristic of experienced analytic judgement — and admitting the flaw at all is what your role's commitment to improving practice through reflection actually requires.

### Preparation tips

- **Rehearse the strength-of-claim ladder.** For any finding, be able to say instantly whether it is an observation, a pattern, a supported finding, or a measured rate — and what each licenses.
- **Learn the standard biases by name.** Survivorship, selection, confirmation and leading-question effects account for most of the harder items here and most of the real errors in the field.
- **Ask what each source measures.** When two sources disagree, the reconciliation is almost always in the definitions, not in the numbers.
- **Practise on your own plans.** Take a research plan you wrote and check it against your organisation's standard, line by line. Finding your own gaps is the cheapest form of assurance.
- **Time-box a drill.** Ten minutes, four questions. You are practising composure and pace, not speed.
- **Think in terms of actionability.** For prioritisation items, ask which findings could still change a decision inside the window.
- **Read the question's final sentence twice.** At this level the stem often specifies something narrower than the scenario implies.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Converting participants into percentages.** "Nine of twelve participants" must never become "75% of users".
- **Declaring a winner between data sources.** Reconcile by definition first; a contradiction is usually a category error.
- **Mistaking repetition for corroboration.** Three rounds from the same biased sampling frame is one biased sample, three times.
- **Treating a flaw as total or negligible.** Identify exactly which claims a flaw compromises and which survive.
- **Prescribing solutions instead of establishing problems.** Naming the design fix is usually not your call, and it weakens the finding.
- **Applying general preferences without checking the situation.** "Whole-journey research is better" is a heuristic, not an argument.
- **Escalating a structural conflict as a personality problem.** Ask what constraint the other person is defending.
- **Forgetting boundary and scope wording.** "Under 18", "entirely through", "only if" — these single phrases decide deduction items.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A numeric reasoning assessment measures how accurately you handle the quantitative material your role produces and consumes. At your level that material is not limited to counting participants. You read analytics funnels to decide where research is needed, interpret survey results alongside your own qualitative findings, calculate whether recruitment is on track, compare completion rates between user groups to detect accessibility gaps, and — increasingly — have to explain to stakeholders what a number does and does not mean.

The format is typically a timed online test of fifteen to thirty minutes, with each item presenting a table, chart or short paragraph of figures followed by a multiple-choice question. Time per item is tight, so the operative skill is locating the relevant figures quickly and disregarding the deliberately irrelevant ones. Most platforms provide a calculator; where they do not, the arithmetic is straightforward by design.

What distinguishes this section at your level is interpretation. Several questions below have a correct calculation attached to a wrong conclusion, and telling them apart is the test. A researcher who can compute a percentage but cannot say whether it means anything is not much use in a prioritisation meeting; a researcher who can say "that figure is a proportion of a self-selected subset, so it cannot bear the weight you are putting on it" is invaluable.

There is a professional reason to take this section seriously beyond the assessment itself. Quantitative data carries unearned authority in most organisations. A number in a slide is treated as fact while a qualitative finding is treated as an opinion, regardless of the relative rigour behind each. If you cannot interrogate the number confidently, you will lose arguments you should win — and, worse, you will occasionally win arguments you should lose. Numerical fluency is what lets you say "where does that figure come from, and what is its denominator?" in a room where nobody else will.

Four habits carry most of the load. Establish what the question asks before looking at the data. Identify the denominator before calculating anything. Estimate roughly, then calculate precisely, and compare. And sanity-check the result against the world — a figure implying that 40% of users complete a form in under three seconds is measuring something other than what it claims to.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The numerical skills tested map onto your named skills:

- **Reading tables and funnels accurately** maps to **Analysis and synthesis**, where you apply analysis techniques and present findings colleagues can use.
- **Comparing rates between groups** maps to **Inclusive research**, where you work with colleagues to include many kinds of users. A gap between groups is often the first visible trace of an accessibility barrier.
- **Interpreting quantitative evidence alongside qualitative** maps to **User research methods**, where you understand when to use methods and how to apply them correctly.
- **Sampling and recruitment arithmetic** maps to **Research management, leadership and assurance**, where you ensure research has a clearly defined scope and purpose.
- **Stating figures at defensible strength** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**, where you use evidence to explain decisions and build consensus.
- **Detecting misleading numbers** maps to **User-centred practice and advocacy**, where advocacy depends on your evidence surviving scrutiny.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Completion rate**

Of 24 participants, 18 completed the core task unaided. What percentage completed unaided?

- A) 66%
- B) 72%
- C) 75%
- D) 80%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Divide the part by the whole: 18 ÷ 24 = 0.75, so 75%, and the answer is C. Estimate first as insurance — 18 of 24 is clearly three quarters, since 12 would be half and 24 all — and the calculation confirms it. Option A is 18 ÷ 27, option B is 18 ÷ 25, and both are the kind of mis-keyed entry that time pressure produces. It is worth noting, even on a question this simple, that you would not normally report this as "75% of users"; it is 18 of 24 participants, and the distinction is the subject of several harder items below.

**Question 2 (easy) — Over-recruitment**

Your no-show rate is 25%. You need 15 completed sessions. How many should you book?

- A) 17
- B) 19
- C) 20
- D) 24

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** If 25% do not attend, 75% do, so you need bookings whose 75% equals 15: 15 ÷ 0.75 = 20, giving C. The common error is adding 25% to 15 to get 18.75 and rounding to 19, which underestimates because the percentage applies to the larger booking figure, not the target. Verify: 25% of 20 is 5, and 20 − 5 = 15, which matches. This calculation is worth having at your fingertips, because under-recruitment is one of the quietest ways a study ends up unable to support the claims it was designed to make.

**Question 3 (easy) — Reading across a table**

| User group | Participants | Completed |
|---|---|---|
| First-time applicants | 15 | 9 |
| Repeat applicants | 10 | 9 |
| Assisted digital | 5 | 1 |

Which group had the highest completion rate?

- A) First-time applicants
- B) Repeat applicants
- C) Assisted digital
- D) First-time and repeat applicants were equal

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Convert each to a rate rather than comparing the completion column, which is where the trap sits: first-time and repeat applicants both show 9 completions, but from different bases. First-time is 9 ÷ 15 = 60%. Repeat is 9 ÷ 10 = 90%. Assisted digital is 1 ÷ 5 = 20%. Repeat applicants are highest, so the answer is B, and option D is the distractor for anyone who compares raw counts. Beyond the arithmetic, note what the table shows: the assisted digital group, though small, is completing at a fifth of the repeat rate, and that gap deserves attention out of proportion to the group's size in this sample.

**Question 4 (easy) — Median versus mean**

Seven participants took these times in minutes: 4, 5, 6, 6, 7, 8, 40. Which pair of figures best summarises the group?

- A) Mean 10.9, range 4 to 40
- B) Median 6, range 4 to 40
- C) Mean 10.9 alone
- D) Median 6 alone

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The mean is 76 ÷ 7 = 10.86, which no participant came near — six of seven finished within eight minutes. The median is the fourth value in the ordered list, which is 6, and that genuinely represents the typical experience. But the median alone hides the 40-minute session, which is likely the most informative data point you have, so the range must accompany it. Option B gives both. Option C reports a figure that describes nobody. Option D conceals the outlier. Option A pairs the range with the misleading central figure. The general rule worth carrying: report a central figure and a measure of spread together, and describe any extreme case explicitly rather than letting a summary statistic absorb it.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Weighted average**

Round one: 8 participants, mean satisfaction 4.5. Round two: 12 participants, mean satisfaction 3.5. What is the overall mean across all 20?

- A) 3.6
- B) 3.9
- C) 4.0
- D) 4.1

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Averages from unequal groups cannot be averaged directly. Work with totals: round one contributes 8 × 4.5 = 36 score points, round two contributes 12 × 3.5 = 42, giving 78 across 20 participants, so 78 ÷ 20 = 3.9, and the answer is B. Option C is the trap — averaging 4.5 and 3.5 gives 4.0 and ignores that round two had half again as many participants, pulling the combined figure towards its lower mean. Whenever you combine averages from groups of different sizes, return to totals. This is among the most frequently botched calculations in real reporting, not just in tests, and it usually flatters whichever group was smaller.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Funnel denominators**

A service funnel: 20,000 start, 15,000 pass identity checks, 9,000 upload evidence, 8,100 submit. What proportion of those who passed identity checks went on to submit?

- A) 40.5%
- B) 54%
- C) 60%
- D) 90%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The question names users who passed identity checks, so the denominator is 15,000. The part is 8,100. So 8,100 ÷ 15,000 = 0.54, which is 54%, giving B. Option A uses 20,000, answering a whole-funnel question that was not asked. Option D uses 9,000, answering about the upload-to-submit step. Option C is 9,000 ÷ 15,000, a different step again. All four are true statements about this funnel and only one answers the question — which is precisely the trap. Underline the group the stem names before you touch the figures, and re-read the stem after calculating.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Percentage points and relative change**

Completion rose from 30% to 42% after a redesign. Which statement is accurate?

- A) Completion rose 12%.
- B) Completion rose 12 percentage points, a 40% relative increase.
- C) Completion rose 40 percentage points.
- D) Completion rose 28.6%.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The arithmetic gap is 42 − 30 = 12 percentage points. The relative increase is 12 ÷ 30 = 0.4, or 40%. Option B labels both correctly. Option A is ambiguous and generally read as a relative change, making it wrong. Option C misapplies the percentage-point label. Option D divides by the new value. The professional stake here is real: "completion rose 40%" and "completion rose 12 percentage points" describe the same change, and the first sounds more than three times as impressive. You will encounter stakeholders who reach for the flattering version, sometimes without realising, and being able to name the distinction calmly is part of keeping the organisation's evidence honest.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Base rates and small subgroups**

In a study of 200 survey respondents, 6 identified as using a screen reader. Of those 6, 4 reported difficulty submitting. A colleague writes: "67% of screen reader users cannot submit." What is the strongest objection?

- A) The percentage is calculated incorrectly.
- B) Four of six is a very small base; converting it to 67% implies a precision the sample cannot support, and the claim generalises from six self-selected survey respondents to all screen reader users.
- C) Screen reader users should not be analysed separately.
- D) The survey should have recruited more screen reader users.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The arithmetic is right — 4 ÷ 6 is indeed about 67% — which rules out A and makes this a question about interpretation rather than calculation. The problem is that a percentage taken from a base of six conveys spurious precision: one additional respondent would move the figure to 57% or 71%, and the underlying sample is six people who happened to answer a survey. Option B names both faults, the small base and the generalisation. Option C is wrong in principle; disaggregating by user group is exactly how accessibility problems surface, and refusing to look is worse than looking carefully. Option D is a reasonable recommendation for the future but not an objection to the claim. The right way to report this is "four of the six respondents who use a screen reader reported difficulty submitting, which warrants dedicated research" — honest about the base, and clear that it is a signal worth pursuing rather than a rate.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Working back to a base**

A stakeholder says 12% of applications are abandoned at the evidence step, and that this represents 3,600 applications annually. How many applications are started annually?

- A) 30,000
- B) 36,000
- C) 43,200
- D) 300,000

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** If 12% equals 3,600, the total is 3,600 ÷ 0.12 = 30,000, giving A. Stepwise if you prefer: 12% is 3,600, so 1% is 300, and 100% is 30,000. Verify by reversing: 12% of 30,000 is 3,600, which matches. Option B multiplies by ten and divides by one, option C adds 20%, and option D misplaces a decimal. The reason this calculation matters in your role is that stakeholders quote percentages far more often than bases, and the base changes what a figure means entirely — 12% abandonment is a different problem at 30,000 applications a year than at three million, both in the number of people affected and in what a fix is worth.

**Question 10 (harder) — A rate that is not what it appears**

A dashboard reports "average time on the eligibility page: 22 seconds", and a product manager concludes users find the page easy. Your sessions showed participants spending several minutes reading it. What is the most likely explanation, and the right response?

- A) The dashboard is wrong and should be ignored.
- B) The average is probably dominated by users who bounce off the page within a few seconds, pulling the mean down, and it may exclude sessions that timed out — so the figure conflates fast leavers with fast readers, and the right response is to ask for the distribution rather than the mean.
- C) The participants in your sessions were unusually slow.
- D) The two measures are incompatible.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A mean time-on-page mixes together two very different populations: people who glanced and left, and people who stayed and read. If a substantial share bounce within a few seconds, they drag the mean far below the experience of anyone actually engaging with the content. Many analytics implementations also handle the final page of a session or a timeout inconsistently, which distorts the figure further. The right response is not to reject the dashboard but to ask for the distribution — a median, or better a histogram — which will typically reveal two clusters rather than one average. Option A discards useful data. Option C blames the participants without evidence. Option D gives up on a reconciliation that is available. Asking "can I see the distribution?" is one of the highest-value questions a researcher can bring to an analytics conversation.

**Question 11 (harder) — Comparing groups with a confound**

Completion rates: users on desktop 82% (n=4,100), users on mobile 51% (n=6,300). A stakeholder proposes prioritising a mobile redesign. What is the most rigorous addition to this analysis?

- A) Nothing; the gap is clear and large.
- B) Check whether the two groups differ in other relevant ways — for instance whether mobile users are disproportionately first-time applicants, or arriving from a different referral route, or attempting the journey in circumstances that differ — because device may be a marker for those differences rather than the cause of the gap.
- C) Recommend against the redesign until qualitative research is done.
- D) Recalculate the rates.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A 31 percentage point gap across more than ten thousand users is a real and substantial signal, and B does not dispute it. What B adds is the question of what device is standing in for. Mobile users may skew towards people applying away from home, on the move, without their documents to hand, or towards demographics that differ in digital confidence — any of which could produce much of the gap independently of screen size. This matters because the remedy differs: a responsive redesign fixes a layout problem but not a "users do not have their documents with them" problem, and building the wrong fix is expensive. Option A acts on an unexamined correlation. Option C is too absolute — the redesign may well be right, and qualitative work should inform it rather than gate it. Option D implies an arithmetic doubt the question does not raise.

**Question 12 (harder) — Sample sizing for a comparison**

You want to know whether a design change improved completion. Current completion is 60%, and the team considers a 5 percentage point improvement meaningful. A colleague proposes testing with 20 participants before and 20 after. What is the strongest response?

- A) Agree; 40 participants is a reasonable sample.
- B) Explain that detecting a 5 percentage point difference reliably requires a sample far larger than 40 — with 20 per group, the expected difference is well within the range of ordinary variation — so this comparison should be made with analytics on live traffic, while the 40 sessions are better spent understanding why users succeed or fail.
- C) Increase to 50 per group.
- D) Run the sessions and report the difference whatever it is.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** With 20 participants per group, a 5 percentage point difference amounts to one participant, which is indistinguishable from chance — you would need hundreds per group to detect a difference that small with any confidence. Option B says this and, crucially, redirects rather than merely objecting: live analytics can measure the rate across thousands of users at no recruitment cost, while moderated sessions are far better suited to explaining mechanisms than to measuring small differences. That division of labour is the substantive point. Option A endorses a design that cannot answer its own question. Option C increases the sample without approaching sufficiency, and still spends scarce moderated sessions on the wrong task. Option D is the most damaging, because reporting an observed difference from an underpowered comparison invites the team to act on noise. Knowing which method can answer which question — and saying so before the study runs rather than after — is a defining professional skill at your level.

### Preparation tips

- **Establish the denominator before anything else.** Underline the group the question names. Most numeric errors in real reporting are denominator errors.
- **Return to totals when combining averages.** Never average two means from unequal groups.
- **Ask what a metric actually measures.** Time on page, satisfaction, abandonment — each conflates populations in predictable ways.
- **Learn to say "can I see the distribution?"** It is the single most useful question to bring to a dashboard.
- **Know roughly what sample sizes can detect.** You do not need formal power calculations, but you should know that 20 per group cannot detect a five-point difference.
- **Practise on your own service's analytics.** Calculate step-by-step drop-offs and see which denominators change the story.
- **Rehearse the percentage point sentence.** It comes up constantly and it is easy marks once automatic.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Dividing by the wrong total.** Funnel items are built around this.
- **Averaging averages.** It flatters whichever group was smaller.
- **Percentages from tiny bases.** Four of six is not "67%" in any useful sense.
- **Treating a correlation as a mechanism.** Device, channel and demographic are often proxies for one another.
- **Accepting a mean without a spread.** Bimodal data hides completely behind a single average.
- **Adding a percentage instead of dividing when over-recruiting.** For a 25% no-show rate, divide by 0.75.
- **Reporting an underpowered comparison.** An observed difference that cannot be distinguished from noise is worse than no comparison.
- **Trusting an answer because it appears among the options.** Distractors are constructed from the exact errors candidates make.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately you read. In a role that runs on documents — service standards, ethics guidance, evaluation reports, policy submissions, stakeholder correspondence, and above all the transcribed words of participants — reading accuracy is not a peripheral skill. It is the substrate everything else rests on.

The classic format presents a short passage of dense workplace prose, typically three to six sentences, followed by statements to classify as True, False or Cannot Say. True means the statement follows necessarily. False means the passage contradicts it. Cannot Say means the passage neither establishes nor rules it out. Alongside these come comprehension questions, main-point questions, and items asking which of four summaries is most accurate.

The governing rule is that you answer from the passage alone. Your professional knowledge — of data protection, of the Service Manual, of what your organisation actually does — must be set aside entirely. If the passage says something you know to be wrong, the passage still governs. This is counter-intuitive for experienced practitioners, and it is the largest single source of lost marks at senior levels specifically: the more you know, the more readily you supply a link the text does not make.

Cannot Say deserves particular attention because candidates avoid it. It feels like a failure to commit, so people reach for True or False to appear decisive. In practice Cannot Say is frequently correct, and selecting it confidently signals good reading. The reliable diagnostic: if you are filling a gap with knowledge or inference, the answer is Cannot Say.

Small words carry the load. "All" is not "most". "May" is not "must". "Should" is not "will". "Some evidence suggests" is not "research shows". "Unless", "provided that", "subject to" and "where practicable" flag conditions that govern everything after them. Wrong answers are constructed almost entirely from swaps of these words, so a statement that reads like a faithful paraphrase may still be False.

For a user researcher this section is closer to your daily work than any other. The discipline of distinguishing what a participant said from what you inferred they meant is the same operation as distinguishing what a passage states from what it implies. Every time you resist writing "users want X" when what you have is "three participants said Y", you are exercising the muscle this section tests.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The verbal skills tested map onto your named skills:

- **Reading precisely** maps to **Research management, leadership and assurance**, where you build your understanding of good practice standards. A standard applied from memory is not being applied.
- **Separating statement from inference** maps to **Analysis and synthesis**, where you present clear findings colleagues can understand and use. Findings that smuggle in an inference are the commonest failure mode, and it begins as a reading error.
- **Identifying the main point** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**, where you tailor communication to stakeholders' needs. Finding the one thing a long submission actually asks for is a service to everyone.
- **Handling qualified language** maps to **User-centred practice and advocacy**, where advocacy that overstates its evidence loses the credibility that makes it effective.
- **Reading for absence** maps to **Inclusive research**, where noticing that a plan never mentions access needs is a reading skill with an ethical consequence.
- **Reaching consensus from text** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**, where you use evidence to explain decisions — and evidence in writing must be read as written by all parties.

### Practice questions

*Passage A — for Questions 1 to 4*

"Services must be researched with users at every stage of development, including after go-live. Research plans must be reviewed by a second user researcher before fieldwork begins, unless the research consists solely of usability sessions on an existing prototype. Where a service is used predominantly by people who may be considered vulnerable, an ethics review is additionally required. Findings from all research must be recorded in the organisation's repository within ten working days."

**Question 1 (easy)** — Statement: "All research plans require review by a second user researcher." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The passage attaches an exception: plans consisting solely of usability sessions on an existing prototype are exempt from second-researcher review. The statement's "all" contradicts that exception, making it False. This is the most reliable pattern in the section — an absolute term in the statement, a conditional or exception in the passage. Train yourself to treat "all", "every", "always" and "never" as prompts to go back and check for a qualifier, because test writers place them deliberately.

**Question 2 (easy)** — Statement: "Research must continue after a service goes live." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The first sentence requires research at every stage of development and adds "including after go-live", stating the point explicitly. The statement follows and is True. The "including" clause is doing deliberate work here: the author anticipated that readers might assume research stops at launch and closed that assumption off. When you meet an "including" clause in a passage, treat it as the author flagging a case people commonly miss — and expect a question about it.

**Question 3 (moderate)** — Statement: "A study involving usability sessions and interviews on an existing prototype does not require second-researcher review." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The exemption applies where research consists *solely* of usability sessions on an existing prototype. A study that also includes interviews does not meet that condition, so the exemption does not apply and review is required — the statement is contradicted, making it False. The load-bearing word is "solely", and it is easy to skim past. This item is a good example of why reading the exception clause as carefully as the rule matters: exceptions are usually narrower than they first appear, and candidates who register "prototype usability sessions are exempt" without registering "solely" will answer True.

**Question 4 (moderate)** — Statement: "A service used mainly by people who may be vulnerable requires both a second-researcher review and an ethics review." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The ethics review requirement is clear — it applies where a service is used predominantly by people who may be considered vulnerable, and the word "additionally" indicates it sits on top of other requirements. But whether second-researcher review also applies depends on whether the research consists solely of prototype usability sessions, which the statement does not tell you. If it does, the exemption applies and only the ethics review is required. Because both scenarios remain possible, the answer is Cannot Say. This item rewards holding two conditions in mind simultaneously, which is precisely the kind of item that separates careful readers from fast ones.

*Passage B — for Questions 5 to 8*

"The review of research practice across the department found that teams generally followed the recruitment standard, though several reported difficulty recruiting participants with learning disabilities. Two teams had conducted research without a published plan; both were operating under time pressure from ministerial commitments. The review did not examine the quality of analysis. It recommends that the head of user research consider whether additional recruitment support is required."

**Question 5 (easy)** — Statement: "Some teams found it hard to recruit participants with learning disabilities." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The passage states that several teams reported difficulty recruiting participants with learning disabilities. "Several" supports "some", and the statement adds nothing beyond what is written. It is True. Note the passage's careful verb — teams "reported" difficulty — and the statement's compatible phrasing. Had the statement said "recruiting participants with learning disabilities is difficult", it would convert reported experience into an established general fact, and the answer would become Cannot Say.

**Question 6 (moderate)** — Statement: "The quality of analysis across the department is adequate." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The passage says explicitly that the review did not examine the quality of analysis. Silence on a topic — particularly declared silence — establishes nothing in either direction, so the answer is Cannot Say. Candidates sometimes reason that a review finding no problems implies no problems exist, which is exactly the inference this item is built to catch. A review that did not look cannot be evidence that there was nothing to see. The workplace parallel is worth noting too: an assurance exercise that scopes something out has told you nothing about it, and treating its silence as reassurance is a common organisational error.

**Question 7 (moderate)** — Statement: "Time pressure from ministerial commitments caused the two teams to skip publishing a plan." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The passage states two facts side by side: the teams conducted research without a published plan, and both were operating under time pressure from ministerial commitments. Juxtaposition is not causation. The time pressure may have been the cause, or the teams may have had other reasons and the pressure is incidental context the review chose to mention. The passage does not assert the causal link, so neither can you. This is the same discipline as Question 6 in the cognitive section, and it is worth noticing how often verbal reasoning tests probe causal overreach — it is one of the most common reading errors among intelligent, experienced readers, because supplying a plausible mechanism feels like comprehension.

**Question 8 (harder)** — Statement: "The review recommends providing additional recruitment support." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The review recommends that the head of user research *consider whether* additional recruitment support is required. That is a recommendation to evaluate, not a recommendation to provide. The statement collapses the two, asserting a recommendation the review did not make, so it is False rather than Cannot Say — the passage tells you what the recommendation was, and it was something different. This distinction between "recommends considering X" and "recommends X" appears constantly in real governance documents, and misreading it in the workplace produces the awkward situation of a team implementing something nobody actually asked for.

*Passage C — for Questions 9 to 12*

"Participants must be able to withdraw at any point without giving a reason. Where a participant withdraws, personal data collected from them must be deleted within five working days, unless the data has been anonymised such that the participant can no longer be identified. Anonymised research data may be retained indefinitely for the purposes of analysis and assurance. Researchers should explain the withdrawal process at the start of each session; where sessions are unmoderated, this explanation must appear in the participant information."

**Question 9 (easy)** — Statement: "A participant must give a reason if they wish to withdraw." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The first sentence states that participants must be able to withdraw at any point without giving a reason. The statement directly contradicts this, so it is False. Straightforward, and worth answering quickly to preserve time for the harder items. Note in passing that the underlying principle is substantive rather than procedural: requiring a reason creates pressure to justify, which undermines the freedom the right is supposed to guarantee.

**Question 10 (moderate)** — Statement: "Anonymised data from a participant who withdraws must be deleted within five working days." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The deletion requirement applies to personal data and carries an explicit exception for data anonymised such that the participant can no longer be identified. The following sentence confirms that anonymised research data may be retained indefinitely. The statement contradicts both, so it is False. This item tests whether you carried the exception clause forward across a sentence boundary — the exception appears at the end of one sentence and is reinforced at the start of the next, and a reader skimming for the deletion rule may register only the five-day requirement.

**Question 11 (harder)** — Statement: "In an unmoderated study, a researcher need not explain the withdrawal process." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The final sentence says that where sessions are unmoderated, the explanation must appear in the participant information. So the obligation does not disappear in unmoderated studies; it changes form, moving from spoken explanation to written material — and it also hardens, since "should explain" becomes "must appear". The statement asserts that no explanation is needed, which contradicts the passage, making it False. The item rewards reading the whole sentence: a reader who stops at "where sessions are unmoderated" and assumes an exemption is coming will get this wrong, because what follows is a substitution rather than an exemption.

**Question 12 (harder) — Main point**

Which best captures the main point of Passage C?

- A) Personal data must be deleted when participants withdraw.
- B) Participants retain control over their involvement and their identifiable data, with the organisation's retention rights limited to data from which they cannot be identified, and the process must be communicated in whatever form the session takes.
- C) Anonymised data may be kept indefinitely.
- D) Researchers must explain the withdrawal process at the start of each session.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The main point must span the passage without adding to it. Options A, C and D are each accurate about one sentence and silent on the rest, making them details rather than main points — and A additionally omits the anonymisation exception, which is load-bearing. Option B captures the unifying logic: participant control is the principle, identifiability is the boundary that determines what the organisation may keep, and communication of the process adapts to the session format. Main-point items reward the option broad enough to cover everything and disciplined enough to invent nothing. The characteristic wrong answer is a true detail promoted beyond its scope, and three of the four options here are exactly that.

### Preparation tips

- **Point at the words.** Before marking anything True, identify the phrase that makes it true. If you cannot, it is not True.
- **Treat Cannot Say as a confident answer.** If you are supplying a link, that is your diagnostic, not a sign of weakness.
- **Circle every qualifier.** All, some, several, most, may, must, should, solely, unless, provided that, subject to, predominantly, where practicable.
- **Distinguish "recommends considering X" from "recommends X".** Governance prose is full of this and it decides items.
- **Distinguish declared silence from absence of a rule.** A review that did not examine something has told you nothing about it.
- **Read exception clauses across sentence boundaries.** They are frequently reinforced or completed in the following sentence.
- **Practise on real governance documents.** Take a page of your organisation's research standard and write three statements — one True, one False, one Cannot Say. Building items teaches you how they are built.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Answering from expertise.** The more you know, the more readily you supply what the passage did not say. This is the senior practitioner's characteristic error.
- **Avoiding Cannot Say.** It converts correct readings into wrong answers more than any other habit.
- **Missing "solely", "only" and "entirely".** These narrow an exception dramatically and are easy to skim.
- **Reading juxtaposition as causation.** Two facts placed side by side are two facts.
- **Treating a substitution as an exemption.** "Where X, this must appear in Y" changes the form of an obligation, not its existence.
- **Accepting hardened verbs.** "Should" becoming "must" changes the claim.
- **Confusing a true detail with the main point.** Three of four wrong options in main-point items are usually accurate but partial.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment presents realistic workplace scenarios and asks what you would do. There is no passage to parse and no arithmetic. You get a dilemma of the kind your role produces, four or five candidate responses, and a question asking which is most effective and often which is least. Some versions ask for a full ranking; others ask you to rate each option on a scale.

This section measures whether your judgement matches what the profession considers good practice at your level, and level is everything. Your role description places you as an independent practitioner: embedded in a multidisciplinary team, responsible for planning and carrying out research, expected to work independently, ensuring research has a clearly defined scope and purpose, building consensus with stakeholders, and getting advice from someone more experienced when you need it. The strongest answers therefore involve exercising real professional judgement, holding a position under pressure when the evidence warrants it, and knowing which decisions genuinely belong to someone else.

That last point is worth dwelling on, because it distinguishes your level from a junior one. A junior researcher escalates a governance question. You are expected to know the answer to most of them, handle the situation, and escalate only what genuinely requires authority you do not hold — ethics approvals, decisions that commit the organisation, safeguarding disclosures, and anything where your own judgement is compromised by being too close to it.

Four principles carry most scenarios.

Participants and users come first. When an option protects wellbeing, consent, dignity or access, it almost always outranks one protecting a schedule, a budget or somebody's comfort. Ethics is not a layer on top of research; it is a condition of it.

Protect the integrity of the evidence. You are the person in the room whose claims are supposed to be trustworthy. Options that inflate, soften, hide or mislabel findings are close to automatically wrong, however reasonable the motive.

Address problems directly, early, with the person involved. Private, specific, non-accusatory conversation beats public correction, silence, or routing around someone.

Fix the instance, then the system. At your level, spotting the systemic version of a recurring problem — and doing something about it — is a marker of the seniority you are working towards.

One trap: options that are pleasant, and options that are decisive, are both attractive for reasons unrelated to effectiveness. Neither agreeableness nor boldness is the criterion.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The judgement dimensions map onto your named skills:

- **Ethical judgement and participant care** map to **Inclusive research** and the ethical core of **User research methods**.
- **Defining and defending scope** maps to **Research management, leadership and assurance**, where you ensure research has a clearly defined scope and purpose, and get support when you need it.
- **Building consensus and handling resistance** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**, where you build and reach consensus and use evidence to explain decisions.
- **Involving the team** maps to **Analysis and synthesis** and **Agile research practices**, where effectively involving your team and working with different roles are named requirements.
- **Advocating for users with sceptical colleagues** maps to **User-centred practice and advocacy**.
- **Reflecting and improving** maps to **Research management, leadership and assurance**, where you improve your practice through reflection and feedback.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — A disclosure during a session**

A participant discussing a housing service mentions they are currently at risk of eviction and becoming distressed. Your study is about the online form. What is the most effective response?

- A) Acknowledge it briefly and steer back to the form, since the study has limited time.
- B) Acknowledge what they have said with care, check whether they want to continue or stop, offer the support signposting your team prepared, and follow your organisation's safeguarding process afterwards.
- C) Stop the session and contact a housing charity on their behalf.
- D) Continue and record the disclosure as evidence of user need.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** A disclosure of this kind outranks the research question without qualification. Option B has four components and each earns its place: acknowledgement shows the person they were heard, checking restores their control at a moment when they have little, signposting offers something concrete without you improvising a support role you are not trained for, and following the safeguarding process ensures the organisation's mechanism operates rather than the judgement resting on you alone. Option A subordinates a person's crisis to a schedule. Option C acts on someone's behalf without their agreement, removing agency. Option D is least effective because converting distress into a data point is a category error about what you are there for. The preparatory work matters as much as the response: knowing the safeguarding process and holding a signposting list before fieldwork is what makes B available to you in the moment.

**Question 2 (easy) — Scope creep from a stakeholder**

A week into a study on the application journey, a stakeholder asks you to add questions about a proposed new feature "since you're talking to users anyway". What is the most effective response?

- A) Add the questions; it is efficient use of the sessions.
- B) Explain that adding an unrelated topic risks both the original research questions and the quality of answers on the new one, and propose either a short separate study or adding the topic to the next round with proper design time.
- C) Refuse, since the plan is agreed.
- D) Add the questions but only ask them if there is time at the end.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** The stakeholder's instinct is understandable and their underlying need is legitimate, so the answer is not refusal but redirection. Adding a topic mid-study has two costs that are easy to underestimate: session time is finite, so something gets squeezed; and questions bolted on without design time tend to be leading, vague, or asked when the participant is tired. Option B names both costs and offers two routes forward, which is what makes it consensus-building rather than obstruction. Option A is least effective because it quietly degrades the study you already committed to. Option C is technically defensible and relationally poor, treating an agreed plan as a wall rather than a reason. Option D is the worst of both: unstructured questions asked in the last five minutes to tired participants produce data too weak to use but reportable enough to be quoted.

**Question 3 (easy) — Findings that arrive too late to matter**

You present findings at a sprint review and the team responds warmly, but the screens in question shipped two days ago. This has happened twice before. What is the most effective response?

- A) Accept it; delivery pace is set by the product manager.
- B) Raise it in the team's retrospective with the specific examples, quantify what the late findings would have changed, and propose a concrete process change such as a research checkpoint before screens are locked.
- C) Speed up your analysis so findings arrive sooner.
- D) Stop presenting findings that arrive after decisions.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** This is a process problem and needs a process forum, evidence, and a proposal. Option B supplies all three: the retrospective is the team's own mechanism for exactly this, specific examples make the cost visible in a way general complaint cannot, and a named change gives people something concrete to agree to. Option A abdicates something your role explicitly covers — designing research so findings can be embedded into an agile workflow is a named skill, and that means shaping the workflow as well as the research. Option C treats a timing problem as a speed problem, and will exhaust you without solving it, though faster interim sharing may be part of the answer. Option D is least effective because withholding findings punishes users and the team rather than the process, and it removes the evidence you need to make the case.

**Question 4 (moderate) — A colleague's analysis overreaches**

Reviewing a peer's draft report, you find the claim "users prefer the new design" supported by six of nine participants expressing a preference at the end of a session in which they used the new design second. What should you do?

- A) Approve it; six of nine is a clear majority.
- B) Speak to them privately, note that the claim generalises from nine participants and that order effects may explain some of the preference, and suggest rephrasing to what the evidence supports along with a way to test the preference properly.
- C) Add a comment in the document flagging the problem.
- D) Raise it with their line manager.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** There are two distinct problems: the generalisation from nine participants to "users", and the order effect, since a design seen second benefits from a warmed-up participant who has already learned the task. Option B addresses both in the right channel — private, specific, and constructive, ending with a way forward rather than a verdict. Your role names improving practice through reflection and feedback, and giving feedback well is the other half of that. Option A lets a claim through that will not survive scrutiny, which damages your colleague more than an awkward conversation would. Option C is not wrong and may be the right medium in some teams, but a document comment on a substantive methodological point can read as more public and less generous than a conversation. Option D is least effective as a first move: escalating a correctable draft error over the head of a peer who has had no chance to respond damages trust disproportionately.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Pressure to reach a particular conclusion**

A senior stakeholder tells you, before fieldwork, that the research "needs to demonstrate that the new service works, because the business case depends on it". What is the most effective response?

- A) Design the research to focus on what works well.
- B) Say clearly that the research will report what participants actually experience, and reframe the conversation around what they need to know — including what would have to be true for the business case to hold, and what evidence would test that.
- C) Agree, and report the findings honestly regardless.
- D) Report the conversation to your head of profession immediately.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** The request as phrased asks you to predetermine a conclusion, which would make the research worthless to everyone including the stakeholder. Option B declines the premise without a confrontation and redirects to a question you can genuinely help with: business cases rest on assumptions, and testing those assumptions is real and valuable research. Framing it as "what would have to be true?" turns an adversarial moment into a collaborative one. Option A is least effective because designing to find positives is a rigged study, and rigged studies eventually surface. Option C avoids the confrontation now and guarantees a worse one later, when findings arrive that the stakeholder believed had been agreed away — leaving expectations uncorrected is not honesty. Option D may become necessary if the pressure persists or escalates, and mentioning it to your head of profession for support is sensible, but the first move is a direct professional conversation you are entirely equipped to have.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Recruitment that excludes**

Your recruitment supplier consistently delivers participants who are digitally confident and in work, though the service serves many people who are neither. The supplier is contracted centrally and other teams are satisfied. What is the most effective response?

- A) Accept the sample and note the limitation in reports.
- B) Document the mismatch with specific evidence across rounds, supplement immediately through community organisations for the current study, and raise the systemic gap with whoever manages the contract, proposing what a suitable supply would look like.
- C) Complain about the supplier to the contract manager.
- D) Stop using the supplier and recruit independently.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** There are two problems here on different timescales, and the strongest answer addresses both. The immediate study needs participants it is not getting, and community organisations, support services and local partnerships can supply them within a normal fieldwork window. The systemic problem is a contract that does not meet the department's actual research needs, and fixing it requires evidence and a specification rather than a complaint. Option B does the instance and the system, which is the pattern to look for. Option A is least effective because a limitations note does not make the findings represent the excluded users, and repeating it round after round normalises their absence. Option C raises the issue without evidence or a proposal, which rarely moves a contract. Option D solves your study and abandons the systemic problem, and unilateral recruitment may also cut across payment, safeguarding and data protection arrangements.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Disagreement about what the evidence shows**

You and a designer disagree about a finding. You believe participants failed the task because the label is ambiguous; they believe it is because the button is visually weak. Both readings fit the sessions. How should you proceed?

- A) Assert your interpretation, since analysis is your responsibility.
- B) Acknowledge that the sessions support both readings, review the evidence together looking for observations that discriminate between them, and if none exist, design a quick test that would.
- C) Defer to the designer, since design is their responsibility.
- D) Report both interpretations in the findings without resolving them.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** When two hypotheses fit the same evidence, the professional move is to look for a discriminating observation — did participants read the label aloud and misinterpret it, or did they scan past the button entirely without reading anything? Those look different in a recording. If the sessions genuinely cannot distinguish them, a small test can. Option B also does something valuable relationally: reviewing evidence together is one of the most effective ways to build a designer's engagement with research, and your role names effectively involving your team in analysis. Option A is least effective because owning analysis does not mean owning interpretation unilaterally, and asserting where the evidence is genuinely ambiguous is exactly the overreach that erodes trust in research. Option C abdicates. Option D is honest but leaves the team without a basis for action when a resolution is available.

**Question 8 (harder) — An ethical problem in someone else's study**

You learn that another team is recruiting participants for research on a debt service by approaching people in the queue at a support centre, without a consent process for the initial approach and with sessions conducted in the waiting area. What should you do?

- A) Mind your own business; it is not your team.
- B) Raise it promptly with the researcher involved and, given the vulnerability of the participants and the setting, also with your head of user research — describing what you observed factually and asking what safeguards are in place.
- C) Report it to the organisation's data protection officer.
- D) Post about the problem in the research community channel.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** The concerns here are serious and specific: people approached while queueing for debt support may feel unable to decline, the waiting area offers no privacy for a conversation about debt, and the setting makes genuinely voluntary consent difficult. That combination warrants raising it with both the researcher and the head of user research, and B does so factually rather than accusatorially, leaving room for safeguards you may not know about. Going to the head of profession as well as the individual is proportionate here specifically because of participant vulnerability — this is not a peer-feedback situation. Option A ignores a live risk to people. Option C is not wrong if personal data is being mishandled, but the primary issue is research ethics, and the head of user research owns that. Option D is least effective: a public channel invites a pile-on, damages a colleague before the facts are established, and does nothing to protect the participants who are in that waiting area today.

**Question 9 (harder) — Being asked to shorten the story**

Your findings are nuanced: the service works well for confident users, poorly for first-time applicants, and is effectively unusable for people using screen readers. A stakeholder asks for "a one-line summary for the board". What is the most effective response?

- A) "The service works well for most users, with some issues for others."
- B) "The service works for experienced users but fails first-time applicants and is not usable with a screen reader" — a single line that preserves the distinction that matters, offered with the detail behind it.
- C) Refuse to reduce nuanced findings to one line.
- D) "The service has usability problems."

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** The request is reasonable — boards genuinely need a line — and the skill is compression without distortion. Option B is one sentence and it preserves the only distinction a board actually needs to act on: the service's performance varies by user group, and one group cannot use it at all. Option A is least effective because "works well for most users, with some issues for others" is technically defensible and functionally a lie by emphasis, burying an accessibility failure inside "some issues". Option D is so vague it conveys nothing and invites the board to assume the problems are minor. Option C refuses a legitimate need and will simply result in someone else writing the line, almost certainly worse. Tailoring communication to stakeholders' needs is a named skill in your role, and it means finding the shortest true version, not resisting brevity.

**Question 10 (harder) — Your own study has a flaw you did not catch**

After reporting findings that influenced a design decision, you realise your screener excluded anyone who had previously abandoned an application — so your sample contained only successful users. What should you do?

- A) Note it in the next report's limitations.
- B) Tell the team and the decision-maker straight away, explain precisely which conclusions are affected and which still stand, and propose a targeted follow-up with the excluded group.
- C) Quietly run a follow-up study and correct the record if the findings differ.
- D) Say nothing; the design decision was reasonable anyway.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** A screener that excluded non-completers means your study could not see the failures it was meant to find, and a decision has already been made on it. Option B does the three things that matter: it tells the people who acted on the finding, it scopes the damage precisely rather than either minimising or catastrophising, and it proposes the fix. The scoping matters — some conclusions about how successful users navigate the journey may remain perfectly sound, and saying so is not defensiveness but accuracy. Option A defers disclosure past the point of usefulness. Option C conceals the flaw while investigating it, which means the team continues building on evidence you know to be compromised. Option D is least effective and is the one that ends careers: reasoning that the decision was probably fine anyway substitutes your guess for the evidence you were employed to provide. Reporting your own methodological error promptly is uncomfortable, and it is also the clearest possible demonstration that your other findings can be trusted.

**Question 11 (harder) — Consensus that would harm users**

The team has converged on a solution that everyone likes and that your research indicates will not work for the users who struggle most. Challenging it will reopen a decision people are relieved to have made. What is the most effective response?

- A) Let it go; the team has decided and consensus has value.
- B) Raise it once, clearly and specifically, showing what the evidence indicates will happen to those users and what a mitigation might look like — then, if the team proceeds, ensure the risk is recorded and propose measuring the outcome after release.
- C) Escalate to the service owner over the team's head.
- D) Continue raising it in every meeting until the team changes course.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** You have a professional obligation to say the thing, and you also have to work with these people next week. Option B threads that: raise it once and properly, with evidence and a constructive mitigation rather than a veto; then, if the decision stands, do the two things that protect users and preserve your position — get the risk documented so the decision is a knowing one rather than an accidental one, and set up a measurement so reality gets a vote later. Option A is least effective because consensus that harms the users who struggle most is not a value, and staying silent makes you complicit in a decision you had the evidence to inform. Option C escalates before using the direct route and will cost you the team's trust. Option D substitutes persistence for persuasion, and a researcher who relitigates every decision stops being listened to on any of them.

**Question 12 (harder) — Advocating with a sceptical colleague**

A developer dismisses your findings as "just what a few people said" and says real evidence comes from analytics. Repeated in front of the team, this is affecting how findings are received. What is the most effective response?

- A) Explain the methodological basis of qualitative research in the next team meeting.
- B) Invite them to observe the next two sessions, then talk afterwards about what they saw — and separately, look for a place where analytics and sessions together answer a question neither could alone, so the complementarity is demonstrated rather than argued.
- C) Ask the delivery manager to address the behaviour.
- D) Present findings with more quantitative framing to satisfy them.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Scepticism about qualitative research almost never yields to a methodological explanation, because the objection is rarely really methodological — it is that watching a spreadsheet feels like evidence and hearing about a stranger's frustration does not. Observation changes that more reliably than any argument, because watching a real person fail at something you built is difficult to dismiss. The second half of option B matters just as much: demonstrating that analytics located the problem and sessions explained it makes the developer's own preferred evidence part of the case rather than the opposition. Option A is not wrong and rarely works alone. Option C converts a professional disagreement into a management issue and will entrench the position. Option D is least effective because dressing qualitative findings in quantitative clothing — "60% of users" from six participants — concedes the false premise and manufactures exactly the overreach that would justify the scepticism.

### Preparation tips

- **Answer as an independent practitioner.** Exercise judgement, hold positions the evidence supports, and escalate only what genuinely needs authority you do not have.
- **Protect the evidence first.** Any option that inflates, softens, buries or mislabels a finding is almost certainly wrong.
- **Look for options that solve the instance and the system.** At your level this is the signature of the best answer.
- **Prefer redirection to refusal.** "Not that, but here is what would work" outperforms "no" in nearly every stakeholder scenario.
- **Raise it once, properly.** Repeated objection is weaker than one well-evidenced challenge plus a documented risk.
- **Know your escalation triggers.** Safeguarding, participant vulnerability, ethics approvals and data incidents go up; methodological disagreements and stakeholder pressure you handle.
- **Discuss real cases with peers.** Your organisation's actual norms are what this section measures, and colleagues are the best source.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Predetermining findings under pressure.** Agreeing to a conclusion in advance is the one error that invalidates everything else you do.
- **Escalating over a colleague's head as a first move.** Except where participants are at risk, speak to the person first.
- **Refusing rather than redirecting.** A flat no to a legitimate need means someone else meets it worse.
- **Compressing findings into comfort.** "Some issues for others" is how an accessibility failure disappears.
- **Delaying disclosure of your own error.** Speed and precise scoping are what turn a mistake into evidence of integrity.
- **Arguing methodology with sceptics.** Demonstrate; do not lecture.
- **Manufacturing quantification to be taken seriously.** It concedes the premise and creates the overreach.
- **Treating consensus as a terminal value.** A comfortable decision that harms excluded users is not a good outcome.

## Conclusion

You have reached the end, and it is worth pausing over what that involved. Forty-eight questions across four assessment types, each with the reasoning worked through — that is a substantial piece of professional preparation, and rather more than most people do before an assessment.

Look back at the ground covered. In the cognitive section you separated a finding from an artefact of your sampling frame, reconciled analytics that appeared to contradict your sessions, prioritised on the basis of what could still change a decision rather than what felt most important, and identified precisely which claims a leading question compromised and which survived it. In the numeric section you handled weighted averages, funnel denominators, percentage points, base rates from tiny subgroups, and the question of what a sample of twenty per group can and cannot detect. In the verbal section you held two conditions in mind at once, distinguished declared silence from absence, refused to read juxtaposition as causation, and caught "recommends considering X" pretending to be "recommends X". And in the situational judgement section you worked through a safeguarding disclosure, a stakeholder asking you to predetermine a conclusion, a colleague's overreaching analysis, an ethical problem in another team's study, a consensus that would harm the users who struggle most, and a flaw in your own screener that had already influenced a decision.

Notice what unites almost all of it. Every section, in its own idiom, was testing whether you know the limits of your own evidence and will say so. That is not a coincidence and it is not a quirk of how these guides get written. It is the thing your role is actually for. A team can generate opinions without you; a team can read a dashboard without you. What you bring is a claim that can be trusted at the strength you state it — and everything in this guide, from denominators to Cannot Say to refusing to convert nine participants into 75% of users, is a facet of that single capability.

A word about where you sit. Working independently is a real threshold. It means nobody proofreads your reasoning before it reaches a decision, and it means the standard you hold yourself to is the standard the organisation gets. It also means the errors that matter most are no longer the ones you would notice — they are the ones you are confident about. That is the honest case for practising this kind of reasoning deliberately rather than trusting experience: experience makes you fluent, and fluency makes overreach feel like insight.

If you want to build on this, a few things repay the effort more than rereading. Take a study you completed and interrogate your own sampling frame — who could not have appeared in it, and what would they have told you? Ask a peer to review your next research plan against your organisation's standard and to be genuinely critical; then do the same for theirs. Practise compressing a nuanced set of findings into one true sentence, because that skill decides whether your work reaches a board intact. Sit with an analyst for an hour and ask how the metrics you rely on are actually calculated — the answers are frequently surprising and occasionally alarming. And when you next disagree with a designer about what the evidence shows, resist settling it by seniority and look for the discriminating observation instead; it is the most enjoyable part of the job.

Finally, for the day itself. An assessment is a sample of your reasoning taken on one morning under artificial conditions. It is not a verdict on your judgement, your career, or your value to the people who use these services. Go in rested, read the stem twice, trust Cannot Say, find the denominator, and remember that careful beats fast.

Good luck. The users of government services are better served for having researchers who prepare like this.
