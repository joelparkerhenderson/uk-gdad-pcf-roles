## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the reasoning your role genuinely uses, presented through practical materials rather than abstract shapes and sequences. Instead of rotating cubes, you will work with the things a junior user researcher actually handles: a discussion guide, an analysis grid, a participant tracker, a set of session notes, a short extract from a research plan or a service standard.

The format is normally an online test of fifteen to thirty minutes. Question types cluster into five families — pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving — and scoring is objective, comparing your performance against a norm group at a similar level. Two platform features are worth knowing in advance. Many tests are adaptive, meaning difficulty rises after correct answers, so a test that suddenly feels hard is usually a test you are doing well on. And most employers receive an acuity report showing speed against accuracy rather than a single score, which means careful work is rewarded and reckless speed is not.

For a junior user researcher, this section is more relevant than it might first appear, because the core of your craft is disciplined noticing. You notice that four participants hesitated at the same screen while one struggled somewhere else entirely. You notice that the discussion guide contains a leading question. You notice that the analysis grid has a participant's quote filed under a theme it does not really support. You notice that the tracker shows two sessions booked without recorded consent. Each of those is a small cognitive act — comparison, deduction, error detection — and together they are most of what separates research that holds up from research that does not.

At your level the questions expect a little more than mechanical rule-application. Your role description says you understand when to use basic research methods and how to apply them correctly, and that you present clear findings colleagues can understand and use. So some questions below ask you to choose between reasonable options, or to judge how strongly a finding can be stated. There is still no memorisation required — every rule, definition and figure you need is inside the question — but you will be asked to exercise judgement as well as accuracy.

One practical note before you begin. Wrong options in cognitive tests are not random. They are built from specific, predictable errors: counting mentions instead of people, stopping at the first rule breach, forgetting an exception clause, ranking by seniority instead of consequence. Learning the shape of those errors is most of the preparation.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The cognitive dimensions map onto the skills named in your role summary:

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **Analysis and synthesis**, where you apply basic techniques for analysing research data and synthesising findings. Synthesis begins with seeing which observations genuinely group together and which only appear to.
- **Error checking** maps to **Research management, leadership and assurance**, where you explain the importance of working to a research plan and following standards. A standard only protects anyone if somebody verifies it has been met.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Analysis and synthesis** and to **User-centred practice and advocacy**, where you explain how user-centred practices build services that adapt to changing behaviour and needs. Knowing what your evidence licenses — and refusing to go beyond it — is the difference between advocacy and assertion.
- **Prioritisation** maps to **Agile research practices**, where you describe agile working and the roles in a multidisciplinary digital team. Working in short cycles with competing demands means choosing constantly, and being able to explain the choice.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **User research methods**, where you plan and conduct a project with support and know when to apply which basic method, and to **Inclusive research**, where you show awareness of the diversity of users and the need for accessible services.
- **Reading instructions exactly** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**, where you explain what stakeholders find important and why. Delivering what was actually asked for is the quiet foundation of every good working relationship.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Error checking a discussion guide**

Your team's guide-writing standard says questions must: be open rather than closed, avoid suggesting an answer, avoid asking about two things at once, and avoid jargon. You review this draft question: "Did you find the streamlined evidence upload journey easy and quick to use?" How many of the four standards does it break?

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Test the question against each standard in turn rather than stopping at the first fault. It begins "Did you", which invites a yes or no, so it is closed — that is one. The word "streamlined" tells the participant the team believes the journey is streamlined, and "easy and quick" invites agreement, so it suggests an answer — that is two. It asks about two attributes, easy and quick, in a single question, so a participant who found it quick but confusing has nowhere to go — that is three. And "evidence upload journey" is internal team jargon that a member of the public would not use — that is four. All four standards are broken, so the answer is D. A rewritten version might be: "Tell me about the last time you sent us a document. What was that like?" The habit being tested is completing the full check rather than stopping when you have found enough to be dissatisfied.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction from a research standard**

Your team's standard says: "Findings may be published on the internal research repository only if they have been peer reviewed by another user researcher, unless they have been marked as interim by the lead user researcher." A colleague says: "I published my findings this morning, and they were not peer reviewed." Which one of the following must be true?

- A) The standard was breached.
- B) The findings were marked as interim by the lead user researcher.
- C) Either the findings were marked as interim, or the standard was breached.
- D) The findings are unreliable.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The rule is conditional with one stated exception, and the facts do not tell you whether the exception was used. Option A assumes it was not; option B assumes it was; neither is established. Option D introduces a claim about reliability that nothing in the question supports — findings can be unreviewed and still perfectly sound. Only C holds in every possible version of events. This "rule plus exception plus incomplete facts" structure is the most common deduction pattern in these tests, and recognising it quickly is worth real marks. It is also worth recognising at work: the collegial move is to ask whether the interim route was used before suggesting anyone has broken a standard.

**Question 3 (easy) — Pattern recognition across an analysis grid**

You are analysing eight sessions. The number of participants who mentioned each difficulty is: unclear eligibility wording, 6; slow page loading, 2; could not find contact details, 5; confusing document formats, 6; session timed out, 1. Which pair of difficulties has the strongest combined claim to be the focus of the team's next design work?

- A) Slow page loading and session timeout
- B) Unclear eligibility wording and confusing document formats
- C) Could not find contact details and session timeout
- D) Slow page loading and could not find contact details

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Rank by how many different participants raised each issue, because breadth across participants is what makes a qualitative finding solid. Unclear eligibility wording and confusing document formats were each raised by six of eight participants, which is the highest pair. Contact details at five is close behind, and would be a reasonable third priority. Slow loading at two and timeouts at one are single or near-single reports and may reflect individual circumstances such as connection quality. So B is correct. Two cautions worth carrying into real analysis: frequency is a strong signal but not the only one, since a rare problem that locks someone out entirely can outrank a common irritation; and counting participants rather than mentions is essential, because one talkative participant can otherwise dominate a theme.

**Question 4 (easy) — Prioritisation with sessions running**

It is 9:00 am. You have sessions at 10:00, 11:30 and 14:00, and your senior researcher is on leave. These four things need attention:

1. The 10:00 participant has emailed asking to move to 10:30 because of a childcare issue.
2. A developer has asked you to confirm which prototype build to use in today's sessions — they need to know before 09:30.
3. Yesterday's session notes are unwritten.
4. A researcher in another team has asked you to review their discussion guide this week.

What should you do first?

- A) Confirm the prototype build with the developer.
- B) Reply to the 10:00 participant.
- C) Write yesterday's notes while they are fresh.
- D) Review the other team's discussion guide.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Two items have deadlines this morning, so the choice is really between items 1 and 2. Item 2 has the earlier hard deadline at 09:30 and it blocks someone else's work — the developer cannot proceed without your answer, and if the wrong build is deployed, all three of today's sessions are compromised. It also takes about a minute. Item 1 is important and courteous, but the deadline is 10:00, and moving to 10:30 has knock-on effects you can think about for five more minutes without harm. So A first, then B immediately after. Item 3 fits between sessions; item 4 has a week. The general principle is that a short task which unblocks another person and protects the whole day's work comes before a task which affects one session, even a sympathetic one. Both get done well before either deadline.

**Question 5 (easy) — Error checking a participant tracker**

Your team requires four records per completed session: signed consent, recorded verbal permission, access needs noted, and incentive logged. The tracker shows: P1 all four; P2 missing the incentive log; P3 missing verbal permission and access needs; P4 all four; P5 missing signed consent. How many records are missing in total, and how many participants are affected?

- A) 3 records, 3 participants
- B) 4 records, 3 participants
- C) 4 records, 4 participants
- D) 5 records, 3 participants

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Count the two things separately, because the question asks for both. Missing records: P2 contributes one, P3 contributes two, P5 contributes one — that is four in total. Affected participants: P2, P3 and P5 — that is three. So the answer is B. The wrong options are each built from a specific slip: A counts participants twice, C miscounts P3 as a single gap, and D over-counts records. Beyond the arithmetic, notice that the four missing records are not equally serious. A missing incentive log is an administrative gap; a missing signed consent for a completed session is a governance problem that your lead needs to know about today. Counting correctly is the first step; weighting by seriousness is what makes the count useful.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Choosing between methods**

Your team wants to know why users abandon an application at the evidence upload step. Analytics show a 42% drop-off there. You have two weeks and a modest budget. Which approach best fits the question?

- A) A survey sent to everyone who abandoned, asking why they stopped.
- B) Moderated usability sessions in which participants attempt the upload step with their own documents, plus a review of the analytics to select who to recruit.
- C) A/B testing two versions of the upload page.
- D) A card sort to understand how users categorise document types.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Match the method to the question. You need to know *why* people abandon, which is a question about behaviour and reasoning in context, and that is what moderated sessions are for — you can watch where the difficulty actually occurs and hear the participant's account of it as it happens. Using their own documents matters, because the upload problem may be about file formats, sizes or scanning rather than the interface. Option A would tell you what people say afterwards, which is useful but weak: people who abandoned may not respond, and recalled reasons are unreliable. Option C can tell you which version performs better but not why either fails, and two weeks is tight for a meaningful test. Option D answers a different question entirely — categorisation, not abandonment. Your role asks you to understand when to use basic methods and how to apply them correctly, and this is that skill in one question: start from what you need to know, not from the method you enjoy most.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Deduction about strength of findings**

In a study with ten participants, seven struggled to understand the phrase "relevant supporting evidence". Which statement is best supported?

- A) Most users of the service do not understand the phrase.
- B) The phrase is confusing and must be changed.
- C) Seven of ten participants struggled with the phrase, which is a strong signal that it needs testing with clearer alternatives.
- D) The phrase is fine because three participants understood it.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Ten participants is a solid qualitative sample and seven out of ten is a strong pattern, but it still describes participants rather than the user population, so option A overreaches by generalising. Option B jumps from evidence to a mandated solution and skips the step where you find out what wording works better — "must be changed" also removes the team's design judgement from the equation. Option D inverts the logic and ignores the majority signal entirely. Option C states the evidence accurately and proposes a proportionate next step. Notice how much more useful C is in practice than A: it gives the team a reason to act *and* tells them what acting looks like. Presenting findings that colleagues can understand and use is a named skill in your role, and pitching the strength correctly is most of what that means.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Pattern recognition with a confound**

Six participants tested a new form. The four who completed it quickly all used a laptop; the two who struggled both used a phone. A colleague concludes: "The form works well on laptops and badly on phones." What is the most rigorous response?

- A) Agree — the pattern is clear and consistent.
- B) Note that device is a plausible explanation, but with two phone users it is a hypothesis to test rather than a finding, and other differences between those participants could also explain it.
- C) Reject the idea, since two participants cannot show anything.
- D) Recommend removing phone access until the form is fixed.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The pattern is real and it is worth taking seriously — a perfect split like this is exactly the sort of thing that should prompt investigation. But two phone users is a very thin base, and the two groups may differ in other ways: the phone users might also have been less digitally confident, or older, or working with documents they had photographed rather than scanned. Any of those could be doing the work that device appears to be doing. Option B holds both truths at once, which is what rigour looks like. Option A treats a promising hypothesis as an established finding. Option C dismisses a genuine signal, which is its own failure — small numbers are a reason for caution, not for blindness. Option D proposes a drastic action on very thin evidence. The practical next step is obvious once you see it: recruit more phone users and find out.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Applied problem solving mid-session**

Twenty minutes into a session, you realise the prototype link you sent the participant is an older build that does not contain the screens you need to test. You have forty minutes left. What is the most sensible way to think about this?

- A) End the session and rebook, since the data will be worthless.
- B) Establish what the old build still allows you to learn, tell the participant honestly that you have sent the wrong version, and either switch them to the correct link or adapt the remaining time to questions the old build can still answer.
- C) Carry on and report the findings as if they related to the new build.
- D) Say nothing and quietly change the topic to a general interview.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Separate what has failed from what has not. The specific screens are unavailable; the participant, their time, their goodwill and forty minutes are not. Option B does three right things in order: it assesses what is salvageable, it is honest with the participant, and it takes the practical step of switching the link if that is quick. Being straightforward here costs nothing — participants are almost always relaxed about a technical hiccup and mainly want to know it was not their fault. Option A discards forty usable minutes. Option C is the worst of the four, because attributing findings to the wrong build corrupts the evidence base in a way nobody may catch. Option D is dishonest by omission and produces data whose provenance you will have to fudge later. Afterwards, the systemic fix — a check on build links before sending, or a single canonical link the team maintains — is worth more than the recovery itself.

**Question 10 (moderate) — Reading a funnel for research focus**

Analytics for a service show: 10,000 users start, 8,500 complete the eligibility section, 5,100 complete evidence upload, 4,800 submit. Where should research effort be focused first, and why?

- A) The start of the journey, because 1,500 users are lost there.
- B) Evidence upload, because 3,400 users are lost there and it is the largest single drop.
- C) Submission, because it is the step closest to the outcome.
- D) All steps equally, since every loss matters.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Work out each step's loss before choosing. Start to eligibility loses 1,500. Eligibility to upload loses 8,500 − 5,100 = 3,400. Upload to submission loses only 300. The evidence upload step is losing more than twice as many users as any other, so it is the obvious first focus, giving B. It is worth also expressing these as rates, because that is often more informative: the upload step loses 3,400 of 8,500, which is 40%, against 15% at the first step and about 6% at submission. Option A picks a real but smaller loss. Option C mistakes proximity to the outcome for size of problem. Option D sounds even-handed but is not a research plan — with finite time, you go where the evidence points. This is a good example of quantitative data setting the agenda for qualitative work.

**Question 11 (harder) — Prioritisation when you are alone**

Your senior researcher is off sick. At 10:00 the following are true:

1. A participant due at 10:45 has disclosed a hearing impairment and asked whether captions will be available.
2. Recruitment for next week closes at 12:00 and you are two participants short of the plan.
3. A minister's office has asked, via your delivery manager, for a one-page summary of user needs by 16:00.
4. A designer wants twenty minutes today to talk through a screen before they build it tomorrow.

What is the most sensible order?

- A) 3, 2, 1, 4 — the ministerial request outranks everything.
- B) 1, 2, 4, 3 — participant access first, then the hard recruitment deadline, then the designer, then the summary.
- C) 2, 3, 1, 4 — hard deadlines in time order.
- D) 1, 3, 2, 4 — access, then the most senior stakeholder.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Rank by deadline and consequence together. Item 1 is 45 minutes away and concerns whether a participant can take part at all; it needs an answer now, and an honest one — if captions cannot be arranged, the participant deserves to know in time to decide. Item 2 has a hard 12:00 deadline with an irreversible consequence: miss it and next week's study runs short. Item 4 is short, has a real deadline of tomorrow's build, and twenty minutes spent now prevents rework. Item 3 has a 16:00 deadline and is largely drafting work you control, so it fits last while still landing comfortably. Option A ranks by prestige, which is the classic error. Option C sorts purely by clock time and lets the access question slide past the point of usefulness. Option D is defensible but places a 16:00 drafting task ahead of a 12:00 hard cut-off, which risks the recruitment entirely.

**Question 12 (harder) — Applied problem solving about sampling**

Your team has run three rounds of research on a service used heavily by people with limited English. Every participant so far has been a fluent English speaker, because recruitment has run through an online panel with an English-only screener and sessions have been conducted in English without interpreters. What is the strongest analysis?

- A) The sample is fine; the service is in English, so English speakers are the relevant users.
- B) The method itself excludes a key user group at three separate points — panel access, screener language and session language — so the findings cannot speak for those users, and fixing it requires changing recruitment routes, screener design and session delivery together.
- C) Add a note to the report acknowledging the limitation.
- D) Run more sessions through the same panel until some participants with limited English appear.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The strongest analysis identifies the mechanism and its full extent. Exclusion is happening at three distinct points, which is why three rounds have produced the same profile — this is structural, not accidental, and fixing only one point will not solve it. Option B names all three and points at the corresponding fixes: recruiting through community organisations and support services rather than an online panel, translating or simplifying the screener, and providing interpreters or bilingual researchers. Option A is wrong on the facts and on the principle: a service delivered in English is still used by people whose English is limited, and often those users struggle most, which makes them the highest-value participants rather than an edge case. Option C documents the problem without addressing it, which is better than silence but far from sufficient when three rounds remain fixable. Option D repeats a filtering method and expects different results. This connects directly to the inclusive research awareness your role names — recruitment method determines whose experience counts as evidence.

### Preparation tips

- **Practise on your own team's artefacts.** Take a real discussion guide and check every question against the four standards in Question 1. You will exercise the exact skill being tested and improve the guide at the same time.
- **Write the rules down before you reason.** For deduction questions, note each condition as a short line. Freeing your working memory is worth more than reading fast.
- **Decide what you are counting.** People, mentions, records, rule breaches — these are different numbers, and the question always specifies one.
- **Check every option before committing.** These items nearly always include one nearly-right distractor built from a specific error.
- **Rehearse the "rule plus exception" pattern.** Once you can spot it, those questions become quick marks rather than slow traps.
- **Do a timed drill.** Set ten minutes and attempt four questions. You are practising composure under a clock, not speed.
- **Warm up on the day.** Log in early, use the platform's ungraded practice items, keep paper and pen to hand, and treat the first question as a warm-up rather than a verdict.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Stopping at the first fault.** "How many standards does this break" means all of them need checking.
- **Counting mentions instead of participants.** One talkative person can invent a theme that does not exist.
- **Treating a striking pattern as a finding.** A perfect split across six participants is a hypothesis worth testing, not a conclusion.
- **Generalising from participants to users.** "Seven of ten participants" is evidence. "Most users" is a claim you have not earned.
- **Forgetting the exception clause.** Rules with "unless" almost always generate a "cannot determine" answer.
- **Ranking by seniority.** A ministerial request with a 16:00 deadline does not outrank a participant's access question at 10:45.
- **Dismissing small numbers entirely.** Caution about two participants is right; blindness to what they showed is not.
- **Ignoring the boundary words.** "At least", "or over", "within two working days" — single words decide these questions.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A numeric reasoning assessment measures how accurately you handle the numbers your job produces. This is the section people dread most, so let us be clear about what it is not. It is not a maths exam. There are no equations to solve and no formulae to remember. Most platforms provide a calculator, and where they do not, the arithmetic is deliberately simple. What is measured is whether you can read a table correctly, identify which figures matter, choose the right operation, and interpret the result without overstating it.

The format is typically a timed online test of fifteen to thirty minutes, in which each item presents a small table, chart or paragraph of figures followed by a multiple-choice question. Time per item is tight, often around a minute, so the real skill is locating the relevant numbers fast and ignoring the deliberately included irrelevant ones.

Some user researchers arrive here convinced that numbers are somebody else's department. That belief is worth dismantling, because a junior user researcher works with numbers constantly. You report that six of eight participants hit a problem. You calculate whether your recruitment is on track and how much to over-recruit for no-shows. You read an analytics funnel to decide which step to study. You compare completion rates between participants who use assistive technology and those who do not. You check that an incentive budget covers your planned sessions. None of that is statistics, but all of it is numerical care, and errors here have consequences — a miscalculated drop-off sends a team to investigate the wrong screen for a fortnight.

At your level the content stays modest: percentages, percentage change, proportions, averages, ratios, and reading values from tables and simple charts. What rises slightly is the expectation that you interpret sensibly as well as calculate correctly. Several questions below have a right arithmetic answer and a wrong conclusion attached to it, and telling them apart is the skill.

Four habits will carry you through most items. Work out what the question is actually asking before you look at the data. Identify the denominator before you calculate anything. Estimate roughly, then calculate precisely, and compare the two. And sanity-check the result — if you have concluded that three of eight participants is 62%, a two-second check catches it.

One warning about the options. Distractors in numeric tests are constructed from specific predictable errors: dividing by the wrong total, confusing a percentage with a percentage point, using the new value as the denominator in a percentage change. Finding your answer among the options is therefore not evidence that you are right. But knowing the traps means you can often see them coming.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The numerical skills tested map onto your role's named skills:

- **Reading tables accurately** maps to **Analysis and synthesis**, where you apply basic analysis techniques. Participant trackers, completion grids and analytics exports all arrive as tables, and a misread row becomes a wrong finding.
- **Percentages and proportions** map to **Inclusive research**, because comparing groups of different sizes fairly is how accessibility gaps become visible, and to **Analysis and synthesis** more broadly.
- **Percentage change** maps to **User-centred practice and advocacy**, where you explain how user-centred practice helps services adapt. Showing that a change improved completion requires stating the improvement in a way that is both accurate and honest about its size.
- **Averages and spread** map to **User research methods**, where reporting a study honestly means knowing when a mean conceals more than it reveals.
- **Counting, ratios and budgeting** map to **Research management, leadership and assurance**, where working to a plan means tracking the numbers in it — participants, sessions, incentives, days.
- **Stating figures at the right strength** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**, where you explain what stakeholders find important. Stakeholders want a number; your job is to give them one that is honest about its own limits.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Completion rate**

You ran sessions with 16 participants. 12 completed the core task without help. What percentage completed it without help?

- A) 60%
- B) 67%
- C) 75%
- D) 80%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Divide the part by the whole and multiply by 100: 12 ÷ 16 = 0.75, so 75%, and the answer is C. Estimate first as a check — 12 out of 16 is clearly three quarters, since 8 would be half and 16 would be all — and the calculation confirms it. Option B is what you get from 12 ÷ 18, and option D from 12 ÷ 15, both of which are the kind of slip a mis-typed calculator entry produces under time pressure. Building the habit of a three-second estimate before every calculation is the cheapest error insurance available in this section.

**Question 2 (easy) — Over-recruiting for no-shows**

Your no-show rate has been running at 20%. You need 12 completed sessions. Roughly how many participants should you book?

- A) 13
- B) 14
- C) 15
- D) 18

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** If 20% do not attend, then 80% do, so you need a number of bookings whose 80% is 12. That is 12 ÷ 0.8 = 15, giving C. The common error is to add 20% to 12, which produces 14.4 and tempts you towards B — but adding 20% of the target is not the same as allowing for 20% of the bookings failing, because the 20% applies to the larger number. Check the correct answer: 20% of 15 is 3, so 15 bookings minus 3 no-shows leaves 12 completed sessions, which matches. This is a genuinely useful calculation to have at your fingertips, because under-recruiting is one of the most common ways a research round quietly falls short of its plan.

**Question 3 (easy) — Reading a comparison table**

| Task | Completed (of 20 participants) |
|---|---|
| A | 17 |
| B | 11 |
| C | 20 |
| D | 15 |

Which task had a completion rate of exactly 55%?

- A) Task A
- B) Task B
- C) Task C
- D) Task D

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Convert the target percentage into a number of people before scanning: 55% of 20 is 11. Task B shows 11, so the answer is B. This is far faster than calculating four separate rates, and it is a habit worth adopting whenever a question gives you a target percentage and a constant total. For completeness the rates are: A 85%, B 55%, C 100%, D 75%. Notice how quickly the arithmetic goes when the total is 20, because every participant is worth exactly 5 percentage points — spotting that kind of shortcut is one of the things that separates a comfortable pace from a rushed one.

**Question 4 (easy) — Mean session length**

Six sessions lasted 55, 60, 45, 50, 65 and 45 minutes. What was the mean length?

- A) 50 minutes
- B) 53 minutes
- C) 55 minutes
- D) 57 minutes

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Sum the values: 55 + 60 = 115, plus 45 = 160, plus 50 = 210, plus 65 = 275, plus 45 = 320. Divide by 6: 320 ÷ 6 = 53.33, which rounds to 53 minutes, giving B. The deviation shortcut also works well here: guess 55, and the differences are 0, +5, −10, −5, +10, −10, which sum to −10; divide that by 6 to get −1.67 and subtract from 55 to reach 53.33. Either route is fine, but the deviation method involves much smaller numbers, which matters when you are working quickly and cannot afford an addition error.

**Question 5 (easy) — Ratio in a research plan**

Your plan requires participants in a 3:2 ratio of existing users to new users. You need 20 participants. How many should be new users?

- A) 6
- B) 8
- C) 12
- D) 14

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A 3:2 ratio divides the group into 3 + 2 = 5 shares. With 20 participants, each share is 20 ÷ 5 = 4. Existing users take three shares, which is 12, and new users take two shares, which is 8. The question asks for new users, so the answer is B. Option C answers the other half of the split, which is the trap for anyone who calculates correctly and then reads the final sentence carelessly. Write down what each share represents before you calculate, then re-read the question before selecting. Getting this wrong in real recruitment produces a sample weighted towards experienced users, which reliably makes a service look easier than it is.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Comparing groups of unequal size**

In a study, 18 of 24 participants who do not use assistive technology completed a task, and 3 of 8 participants who use assistive technology completed it. What is the gap in completion rates, in percentage points?

- A) 15
- B) 25
- C) 37.5
- D) 40

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Convert both to rates before comparing. The first group is 18 ÷ 24 = 0.75, or 75%. The second is 3 ÷ 8 = 0.375, or 37.5%. The gap is 75 − 37.5 = 37.5 percentage points, so the answer is C. Notice the deliberate near-coincidence: 37.5 is both the second group's rate and the size of the gap, which is the sort of thing that causes a careless mis-selection, so check which quantity the question asked for. Notice also that comparing the raw counts of 18 and 3 would have been meaningless, because the groups differ in size — converting to rates is the only fair comparison. A gap this large is a serious accessibility signal and would justify immediate investigation.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Percentage change**

Task completion rose from 48 participants in one round to 60 in the next, out of the same total each time. What is the percentage increase in the number completing?

- A) 12%
- B) 20%
- C) 25%
- D) 60%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Percentage change divides the difference by the original value. The difference is 60 − 48 = 12, and 12 ÷ 48 = 0.25, which is 25%, giving C. Option A mistakes the raw difference for a percentage. Option B is 12 ÷ 60, dividing by the new value instead of the old — the single most common error in this question type. Option D simply echoes a figure from the stem. The rule to fix in memory is short: for percentage change, the denominator is always where you started. It is worth saying that aloud once or twice before an assessment, because under time pressure the new number is the one sitting most recently in your working memory, and it is the wrong one.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Working back to a total**

In a survey, 15% of respondents said they had abandoned an application because they could not upload a document. That was 54 people. How many people responded in total?

- A) 270
- B) 324
- C) 360
- D) 810

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** If 15% equals 54, the total is 54 ÷ 0.15 = 360, so the answer is C. If dividing by a decimal is uncomfortable under pressure, step it out: 15% is 54, so 5% is 54 ÷ 3 = 18, and 100% is 18 × 20 = 360. Verify by reversing it — 15% of 360 is 54, which matches. Option A is 54 ÷ 0.2, and option D is 54 × 15, both of which are recognisable slips. This calculation matters in practice because stakeholders quote percentages far more often than base numbers, and 15% of 360 respondents carries very different weight from 15% of 20.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Choosing the right denominator**

A service funnel shows: 12,000 users start, 9,000 reach the identity check, 6,300 reach evidence upload, 5,670 submit. What percentage of users who reached evidence upload went on to submit?

- A) 47%
- B) 63%
- C) 76%
- D) 90%

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Identify the group the question names before touching the numbers. It asks about users who reached evidence upload, so the denominator is 6,300, not 12,000. The part is 5,670. So 5,670 ÷ 6,300 = 0.9, which is 90%, giving D. Option A is 5,670 ÷ 12,000, which answers a question about the whole funnel that nobody asked. This trap appears in almost every numeric assessment that includes funnel data, and it has a real-world twin: telling a team that 90% of people who reach upload go on to submit is a completely different message from telling them 47% of all starters finish. Both are true; only one answers the question in front of you.

**Question 10 (moderate) — Weighted thinking**

Two rounds of research produced satisfaction scores. Round one: 6 participants, mean score 4.0. Round two: 14 participants, mean score 3.0. What is the mean score across all 20 participants?

- A) 3.0
- B) 3.3
- C) 3.5
- D) 3.7

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** You cannot average the two means directly, because they come from groups of different sizes. Work with totals instead. Round one contributes 6 × 4.0 = 24 score points. Round two contributes 14 × 3.0 = 42. The combined total is 66, spread across 20 participants, so 66 ÷ 20 = 3.3, giving B. Option C is the trap: averaging 4.0 and 3.0 to get 3.5 ignores the fact that round two had more than twice as many participants and therefore pulls the combined figure much closer to 3.0. Whenever you combine averages from groups of different sizes, go back to totals — this is one of the most frequently mishandled calculations in real reporting, not just in tests.

**Question 11 (harder) — Percentage points versus percentage change**

Before a redesign, 25% of participants completed a task unaided. After it, 40% did. Which statement is accurate?

- A) Completion rose by 15%.
- B) Completion rose by 15 percentage points, a 60% relative increase.
- C) Completion rose by 60 percentage points.
- D) Completion rose by 37.5%.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The arithmetic gap between two percentages is measured in percentage points: 40 − 25 = 15 percentage points. The relative increase is a separate calculation: 15 ÷ 25 = 0.6, which is a 60% increase. Option B states both correctly and labels each properly. Option A uses "15%" loosely, which is ambiguous and usually read as a relative change, making it wrong. Option C misapplies the percentage-point label to the relative figure. Option D is 15 ÷ 40, using the new value as the denominator. Why this matters beyond the test: "completion rose 60%" and "completion rose 15 percentage points" describe the identical change, and the first sounds roughly four times more impressive. Choosing which to report, and labelling it honestly, is an integrity question as much as a numerical one.

**Question 12 (harder) — When the average misleads**

Eight participants took these times, in minutes, to complete a task: 2, 3, 3, 4, 4, 5, 5, and 46. A colleague reports "the average task time was 9 minutes". What is the best response?

- A) The calculation is wrong; the average is 4 minutes.
- B) The calculation is right, but the mean is a poor summary here: seven of eight participants finished within 5 minutes, and one extreme case is pulling the figure up. Report the median of 4 minutes with the range, and describe the 46-minute session separately.
- C) The figure is fine as reported.
- D) The 46-minute session should be excluded as an anomaly.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Check the arithmetic first: the values sum to 72, and 72 ÷ 8 = 9, so the mean is correct and option A is wrong. But the mean describes nobody's actual experience — seven participants finished in five minutes or less, and one took 46. The median, being the average of the fourth and fifth values, is 4 minutes, which represents the group far better. Option B says all of this and proposes the right fix. Option C lets a misleading summary stand. Option D is the most damaging answer: excluding an inconvenient data point is a serious research failing, and that 46-minute session is almost certainly the most interesting thing in the dataset — something went badly wrong for that person, and finding out what is the entire point of doing research. Report the distribution, and treat the outlier as a lead rather than a nuisance.

### Preparation tips

- **Drill four operations only.** Percentages, percentage change, averages and ratios cover nearly everything at this level. Ignore anything more advanced.
- **Find the denominator before you calculate.** Underline the group the question names. Most real-world numeric errors are denominator errors.
- **Estimate first, always.** Three seconds of "about three quarters" catches most serious mistakes before they happen.
- **Go back to totals when combining averages.** Never average two means from groups of different sizes.
- **Learn the percentage point distinction until it is automatic.** Write yourself one sentence about it and re-read it before any assessment.
- **Practise on your team's real data.** Calculate the completion rate, no-show rate and median session length from an actual tracker. It is more memorable than abstract drills and it makes you useful.
- **Get familiar with an on-screen calculator.** Fumbling an unfamiliar interface eats the seconds you need for thinking.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Dividing by the wrong total.** Funnel questions are built around this single error.
- **Confusing percentages with percentage points.** Both labels are correct for different quantities; they are not interchangeable.
- **Averaging averages.** Two means from unequal groups cannot be combined by adding and halving.
- **Comparing raw counts across unequal groups.** Convert to rates before drawing any conclusion.
- **Adding a percentage to the target instead of dividing.** Over-recruiting for a 20% no-show rate means dividing by 0.8, not adding 20%.
- **Trusting an answer because it is on the list.** Distractors are built from the exact mistakes candidates make.
- **Rounding too early.** Carry a decimal place through and round only at the end.
- **Reporting a mean without checking the spread.** Glance at the range first; if one value is far from the rest, the mean will mislead.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately you read. That sounds almost too basic to be worth testing, yet it is one of the strongest predictors of performance in document-driven work — and user research is document-driven work. You read service standards, ethics guidance, policy summaries, accessibility requirements, discussion guides, consent forms, stakeholder emails and, above all, the words participants actually say. Reading any of those slightly wrong changes what you do next.

The classic format gives you a short passage of dense workplace prose, usually three to six sentences, followed by statements to classify as True, False or Cannot Say. True means the statement follows necessarily from the passage. False means the passage contradicts it. Cannot Say means the passage neither confirms nor contradicts it, so answering would require information you have not been given. Alongside these you will meet comprehension questions, main-point questions, and questions asking which of four summaries is most accurate.

The governing rule is that you answer from the passage alone. Everything you know about research ethics, data protection or the GOV.UK Service Manual must be set aside. If the passage contradicts your professional knowledge, the passage still wins. This feels unnatural and it is the largest single source of lost marks: candidates recognise a statement as true of the world and mark it True even though the passage never said it. That is a Cannot Say.

Cannot Say deserves special attention because people avoid it. It feels evasive, so candidates reach for True or False to appear decisive. In fact Cannot Say is very often correct, and selecting it confidently signals good reading rather than weak judgement. The reliable test is this: if you find yourself supplying a link the passage does not make, you have found a Cannot Say.

Watch the small words, because they carry the meaning. "All" is not "most". "May" is not "must". "Should" is not "will". "Some evidence suggests" is not "research shows". "Subject to" and "unless" flag conditions that change everything downstream. Test writers construct their wrong answers almost entirely from swaps of exactly these words, so a statement that reads almost identically to the passage may still be False.

For a junior user researcher this is a section where you can genuinely excel, because it demands the same discipline good research demands. Distinguishing what a participant said from what you inferred they meant is the same mental move as distinguishing what a passage states from what it implies. Get better at one and you get better at the other.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The verbal skills tested map onto your named skills:

- **Reading precisely** maps to **Research management, leadership and assurance**, where you explain the importance of following standards. A standard followed as remembered rather than as written is not being followed.
- **Separating statement from inference** maps to **Analysis and synthesis**, where you apply basic analysis techniques and present clear findings colleagues can use. Findings that quietly smuggle in an inference are the commonest way research goes wrong, and it starts as a reading error.
- **Identifying the main point** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**, where you explain what stakeholders find important and why. A long email usually contains one thing the sender actually needs.
- **Handling qualified language** maps to **User-centred practice and advocacy**. Advocacy that overstates evidence burns trust; advocacy pitched correctly builds it.
- **Reading for what is absent** maps to **Inclusive research**. Noticing that a plan never mentions access needs is a reading skill with an ethical consequence.
- **Following written method descriptions exactly** maps to **User research methods**, where applying a method correctly is a named requirement.

### Practice questions

*Passage A — for Questions 1 to 4*

"Teams must publish a research plan before fieldwork begins. The plan must state the research questions, the methods to be used, and the recruitment approach. Where a team intends to research with participants who may be considered vulnerable, the plan must also include a safeguarding section, approved by the lead user researcher. Plans may be revised during fieldwork, provided that any revision is dated and the reason recorded."

**Question 1 (easy)** — Statement: "A research plan must be published before fieldwork starts." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The first sentence states this directly, so the statement follows necessarily and is True. This is the most straightforward item type in the section, and the right approach is to answer it quickly and confidently so that you bank time for harder items later. Candidates sometimes lose marks on easy items by hunting for a trick that is not there — if the passage plainly says the thing, mark it True and move on.

**Question 2 (easy)** — Statement: "Every research plan must contain a safeguarding section." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The passage requires a safeguarding section only where the team intends to research with participants who may be considered vulnerable. The statement extends that requirement to every plan, which the passage does not support and effectively contradicts, because the conditional wording implies plans without such participants do not need one. So the answer is False. The word doing the work is "every" — absolute terms should always send you back to check whether the passage attached a condition. Here it plainly did.

**Question 3 (moderate)** — Statement: "A plan revised in week two of fieldwork without a recorded reason complies with the requirements." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The final sentence permits revision during fieldwork only on condition that the revision is dated and the reason recorded. A revision without a recorded reason fails one of the two stated conditions, so it does not comply, and the statement is contradicted — False. Note the structure: "provided that" introduces conditions, and failing any one of them breaks compliance. Contrast this with Question 4 below, where the passage is silent rather than conditional; the distinction between a failed condition and an unmentioned matter is exactly what separates False from Cannot Say.

**Question 4 (moderate)** — Statement: "The research plan must state the number of participants." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The passage lists three things the plan must state — research questions, methods, and recruitment approach — plus a conditional fourth. Participant numbers are not among them. But a list of requirements is not automatically an exhaustive list, and the passage never says that nothing else is required or that numbers are excluded. It is simply silent, so the answer is Cannot Say rather than False. You might reasonably argue that a recruitment approach usually implies a number, and that instinct is exactly what the question is testing: "usually implies" is not "states", and your professional inference is not the passage's content.

*Passage B — for Questions 5 to 8*

"The evaluation found that participants recruited through community organisations were more likely to report difficulty with the online form than those recruited through the research panel. The evaluation team notes that the two groups differed in several respects, including age and prior use of digital services. Further work is planned for the autumn, funding permitting. The team recommends that recruitment for future rounds should draw on both routes."

**Question 5 (easy)** — Statement: "Participants recruited through community organisations reported more difficulty than panel participants." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The first sentence states that community-recruited participants were more likely to report difficulty with the online form, which is what the statement says. It follows necessarily, so the answer is True. Note that the passage's careful phrase is "more likely to report difficulty" rather than "found it harder", and the statement respects that by saying "reported more difficulty". Had the statement claimed they "found the form harder to use", that would convert a report into an objective fact about performance, and the answer would shift to Cannot Say.

**Question 6 (moderate)** — Statement: "The difference in reported difficulty was caused by the recruitment route." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The passage records an association and then explicitly flags that the two groups differed in other respects, including age and prior digital experience. Either of those could be responsible for the difference, so causation is not established. The passage neither confirms nor denies that recruitment route is the cause, making this a Cannot Say. This is a genuinely important distinction, and not only for the test: treating an association as a cause is one of the ways a research team ends up fixing the wrong thing. The passage's own authors were careful here, and the question rewards you for reading that care rather than skating past it.

**Question 7 (moderate)** — Statement: "Further work will take place in the autumn." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The passage says further work is planned for the autumn, funding permitting. Two things stand between the plan and the statement: whether funding will be forthcoming, and the difference between a plan and a certainty. The statement asserts the work will happen, which the passage does not support, so the answer is Cannot Say. "Funding permitting" is a conditional flag of exactly the same family as "subject to", "provided that" and "if approved", and conditional flags point overwhelmingly towards Cannot Say. Train yourself to notice them; they are among the most reliable signposts in the whole section.

**Question 8 (harder)** — Statement: "The evaluation team believes a single recruitment route is insufficient for future rounds." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The final sentence recommends that future recruitment should draw on both routes. Recommending both is, in substance, saying that either one alone would not be adequate, so the statement follows. The answer is True. This item is harder than it looks because the passage never uses the word "insufficient" — you have to recognise that "should draw on both" and "one alone is insufficient" express the same position. That is legitimate reading rather than inference: the statement restates the recommendation without adding to it. Compare it with a statement such as "the panel route is unreliable", which would add a claim the passage does not make and would be a Cannot Say.

*Passage C — for Questions 9 to 12*

"Researchers should offer participants a choice of session format, including telephone and face-to-face options where practicable. Remote sessions must not be the only option offered for research on services used predominantly by people at risk of digital exclusion. Incentives should be offered in a form the participant can readily use; vouchers requiring an online account are not suitable for all participants. Where a participant withdraws, any data already collected should be deleted unless it has been fully anonymised."

**Question 9 (easy)** — Statement: "Remote sessions may sometimes be the only format offered." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The prohibition in the passage is specific: remote sessions must not be the only option for research on services used predominantly by people at risk of digital exclusion. That narrow prohibition implies that in other circumstances — research on a service used mainly by digitally confident people, say — remote-only would not breach this rule. So the statement is supported and the answer is True. Reading the scope of a prohibition accurately is the skill here. A rule that forbids something in defined circumstances is telling you something about the undefined circumstances too, which is why "must not be the only option for X" is not the same as "must never be the only option".

**Question 10 (moderate)** — Statement: "Vouchers are never a suitable incentive." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The passage says vouchers requiring an online account are not suitable for all participants. Two qualifiers are being dropped by the statement: the specific type of voucher, and "not suitable for all" rather than "not suitable for any". "Not suitable for all participants" means some participants cannot use them, not that nobody can. The statement's absolute "never" contradicts this, so the answer is False. This is a compact demonstration of why the small words matter — "all" placed after a negative is one of the trickiest constructions in English, and test writers know it.

**Question 11 (harder)** — Statement: "If a participant withdraws, all data collected from them must be deleted." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The passage requires deletion of data already collected unless it has been fully anonymised. The statement says all data must be deleted, dropping the exception, and it also hardens "should" into "must". Either change alone would be enough to make the statement inaccurate; together they clearly contradict the passage, so the answer is False. When you meet a statement that looks like a faithful summary, run two checks in sequence: did the passage attach an exception, and did the passage use a softer verb? Both checks take a couple of seconds and between them they catch most of the wrong answers in this section.

**Question 12 (harder) — Main point**

Which of the following best captures the main point of Passage C?

- A) Remote research is generally inappropriate for government services.
- B) Participants should be able to take part, and be rewarded, in ways that work for them, with their withdrawal respected — and research practice should adapt accordingly.
- C) Incentives must be carefully chosen.
- D) Researchers must delete data when participants withdraw.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The main point must cover the whole passage without adding to it. Option A overstates a targeted restriction into a general judgement the passage does not make. Options C and D are each true of a single sentence, which makes them details rather than main points — and D also drops the anonymisation exception. Option B captures all four elements: format choice, protection for people at risk of digital exclusion, usable incentives, and respect for withdrawal, unified by the idea of adapting practice to participants rather than the reverse. Main-point questions reward the option broad enough to cover everything and disciplined enough to invent nothing, and the wrong options are typically a true detail promoted too far, or a plausible generalisation the passage never made.

### Preparation tips

- **Answer only from the passage.** Before marking anything True, point at the words that make it true. If you cannot, it is not True.
- **Trust Cannot Say.** It is a confident, evidence-based answer. If you are supplying a link the passage does not make, that is your signal.
- **Circle the qualifiers.** All, some, most, may, must, should, unless, provided that, subject to, funding permitting, predominantly. These decide the majority of items.
- **Read the statement first, then the passage.** Knowing what you are checking makes the second read fast and targeted.
- **Separate a failed condition from silence.** A broken "provided that" gives you False; an unmentioned topic gives you Cannot Say.
- **Watch negatives combined with "all".** "Not suitable for all" means "not suitable for some", not "suitable for none".
- **Practise on real documents.** Take a page of the GOV.UK Service Manual and write three statements about it — one True, one False, one Cannot Say. Building the questions teaches you how they are built.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Answering from professional knowledge.** What you know about consent is irrelevant if the passage does not say it.
- **Avoiding Cannot Say to look decisive.** This converts correct readings into wrong answers more than any other habit.
- **Missing the exception clause.** "Unless" and "provided that" appear early and are forgotten by the time you read the statement.
- **Accepting hardened verbs.** "Should" becoming "must" changes the claim even when the sentence looks familiar.
- **Treating association as causation.** A passage that reports a difference between groups and then names confounds is telling you not to conclude a cause.
- **Confusing a true detail with the main point.** Main-point items punish accurate but partial options.
- **Rushing the second half of a passage.** Attention fades, and exception clauses are frequently placed at the end.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment presents realistic workplace scenarios and asks what you would do. There is no passage to decode and no arithmetic. Instead you get a dilemma of the kind your role produces, four or five candidate responses, and a question asking which is most effective and often which is least. Some versions ask you to rank all options; others ask you to rate each on a scale from counterproductive to highly effective.

This section measures judgement rather than knowledge, and specifically whether your instincts match what the profession considers good practice at your level. That last clause is the key to answering well. The same scenario has different best answers for a trainee, a junior, and a head of profession. Your role description places you precisely: embedded in a multidisciplinary team, carrying out research activities, with some practical experience, needing regular guidance, working in combination with a more senior researcher. So the strongest answers usually involve competent independent action within your remit, combined with prompt honesty about anything beyond it. Neither struggling in silence nor escalating everything scores well.

Four principles will carry you through most scenarios, and they generalise far beyond any single item.

Participants and users come first. When an option protects a participant's wellbeing, consent, dignity or access needs, it will almost always beat an option that protects a schedule, a budget or somebody's convenience. Research ethics is not a compliance layer on top of the work; it is the work.

Act within your remit and be transparent at its edges. At your level you are expected to run sessions, make in-the-moment calls, and handle routine stakeholder contact yourself. You are not expected to make governance decisions alone, to redesign a study unilaterally, or to absorb a data incident without reporting it. The pattern is: do what you competently can, then tell the right person promptly.

Address problems directly, early, and with the person involved. Options that involve an honest private conversation beat options that involve public criticism, silence, or going around someone.

Fix the instance, then improve the system. The strongest options frequently do both — they solve today's problem and feed something back so it is less likely to recur. At junior level, noticing the systemic version of a problem is a real marker of progression.

One trap worth naming. Some options appeal because they are pleasant: agreeing, smoothing over, being maximally helpful to whoever asked last. Others appeal because they are decisive: taking charge, sorting it out alone. Neither pleasantness nor boldness is the criterion. The criterion is what produces the best outcome for users, for the research and for the team, given your experience.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The judgement dimensions map onto your named skills:

- **Ethical judgement and participant care** map to **Inclusive research** and to the ethical core of **User research methods**.
- **Working to a plan and surfacing deviations** maps to **Research management, leadership and assurance**.
- **Involving colleagues in research and analysis** maps to **Analysis and synthesis**, where effectively involving your team is explicitly named, and to **Agile research practices**.
- **Handling stakeholder requests, pressure and disagreement** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**.
- **Presenting findings honestly and usefully** maps to **Analysis and synthesis** and to **User-centred practice and advocacy**.
- **Knowing when to ask** maps to your role level, which states that you work in combination with a more senior researcher and need regular guidance. Asking well is a skill, not a shortfall.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — A participant becomes distressed**

You are facilitating a session. The participant is describing an application they made after losing their job, and becomes tearful. What is the most effective response?

- A) Continue with the next task to help them move past it.
- B) Pause, acknowledge it gently, remind them they can stop or take a break at any time, and let them decide how to continue.
- C) End the session immediately.
- D) Offer advice about the benefits they might be entitled to.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Option B does three things well: it acknowledges what is happening rather than pretending it is not, it restores control to the participant by reminding them of their options, and it lets them choose. Many participants want to continue, and being asked rather than rescued is respectful. Option A steamrollers a person who has just shown you they are struggling. Option C removes their agency in the opposite direction, though it becomes right if they are seriously distressed or if they ask to stop. Option D is least effective because giving benefits advice takes you outside your role and competence, and getting it wrong could cause real harm to someone in a vulnerable position — the right move is to have a signposting list of support organisations prepared in advance, offered at the end, rather than improvised guidance mid-session. Afterwards, note that this topic carries emotional weight so the guide and the safeguarding approach can reflect it, and check in with yourself too.

**Question 2 (easy) — You are asked to use a method you have not used**

Your senior researcher suggests running a diary study for the next round and asks you to lead it. You have read about diary studies but never run one. What is the most effective response?

- A) Accept, and work it out as you go.
- B) Accept, saying you have not run one before and asking for a review of your plan and a check-in after the first two days.
- C) Decline and suggest usability sessions instead, which you know well.
- D) Accept, then ask a peer in another team to plan it for you.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** Your role explicitly involves planning and conducting research with support, so option B is your role description in action. Note its structure: it accepts the opportunity, names the gap honestly, and makes two small specific requests rather than an open-ended plea for help. A plan review and an early check-in are cheap for your senior and catch the two points where diary studies most often go wrong — an unclear prompt and low participant engagement in the first days. Option A risks a fortnight of unusable data. Option C is least effective because it swaps a method chosen for the research question for one chosen for your comfort, which is the wrong basis for a methodological decision. Option D outsources your development and leaves your senior with a false picture of your capability.

**Question 3 (easy) — An observer wants to join at short notice**

A policy colleague messages an hour before a session asking to observe. Your consent form told the participant that "members of the research team" would be present. What should you do?

- A) Let them join; observers are normal.
- B) Explain that you need the participant's agreement, ask the participant at the start whether they are comfortable with an observer, and proceed accordingly — briefing the observer beforehand on staying muted.
- C) Refuse, since the consent form does not cover it.
- D) Let them join without telling the participant, to avoid making it awkward.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Consent has to be informed, and a participant who agreed to "the research team" has not agreed to a policy official watching. Option B resolves this properly: it asks the participant, gives them a genuine chance to say no, and prepares the observer so their presence does not disrupt the session. Briefing observers is not a formality — an unbriefed observer unmuting mid-session is one of the commonest ways a session gets derailed. Option A skips the participant's agreement. Option C is over-rigid; the consent form can be supplemented by a clear verbal request, and shutting out policy colleagues weakens the link between research and decisions. Option D is least effective because concealing an observer is a straightforward breach of trust, and if the participant later discovers it, the damage extends well beyond one session.

**Question 4 (easy) — Conflicting demands on your morning**

Your senior researcher has asked you to prepare the analysis grid this morning. Your delivery manager then asks you to spend the morning helping with recruitment, calling it urgent. What is the most effective response?

- A) Do the recruitment, since the delivery manager is more senior.
- B) Do the analysis grid and tell the delivery manager you are unavailable.
- C) Tell the delivery manager about the existing task, offer a specific workable option, and ask them and your senior researcher to confirm the priority.
- D) Attempt both and work through lunch.

**Correct answer: C. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Two legitimate instructions have collided, and while the resolution is not yours to impose, surfacing it promptly and helpfully is. What lifts option C above bare escalation is the offer of a specific option — "I could do recruitment this morning and have the grid ready by three, if that works" — which turns you from a bottleneck into a problem-solver. Option A ranks by seniority; the delivery manager probably does not know about the grid. Option B is abrupt and leaves an urgent need unmet. Option D is least effective because working through lunch hides the conflict, normalises over-commitment, and means the team never learns it is asking one person for two mornings. In an agile team, making competing demands visible is a contribution, not a complaint.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Findings a colleague dislikes**

You present findings showing that a feature a designer has worked on for weeks caused problems for five of seven participants. The designer responds in the session that your sample was too small and the participants were unrepresentative. What is the most effective response?

- A) Concede that the sample was small and offer to redo the research.
- B) Acknowledge the limits of a seven-person sample honestly, then walk through what participants actually did rather than what they said, and propose testing again with a wider sample to check the pattern.
- C) Defend the research firmly and point out that five of seven is a strong signal.
- D) Take it offline and discuss with your senior researcher before responding.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** The designer's objection is partly legitimate and partly defensive, and option B handles both without needing to sort them apart. Conceding the real limit costs you nothing and buys credibility. Shifting the conversation from opinions to observed behaviour — what participants clicked, where they stopped, what they said aloud while stuck — moves the discussion onto ground that is much harder to dispute. Proposing a further round respects the objection while keeping the finding alive. Option A capitulates and implies the research was worthless, which it was not. Option C is factually right but wins an argument at the cost of a working relationship, and a defensive designer will not act on the findings anyway. Option D is reasonable as a follow-up but avoids a conversation you are capable of having; do B now, and debrief with your senior afterwards.

**Question 6 (moderate) — A gap in the research plan**

Halfway through fieldwork you realise the plan makes no provision for participants who use screen readers, and the prototype has never been tested with one. Four sessions remain. What should you do?

- A) Complete the four sessions as planned and note the gap in the report.
- B) Raise it with your senior researcher today, propose recruiting at least one screen reader user for the remaining sessions or a follow-up round, and check whether the prototype is testable with assistive technology at all.
- C) Test the prototype with a screen reader yourself and report what you find as a finding.
- D) Pause the study until the gap is fixed.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** You have spotted a real gap while there is still time to do something about it, which is the whole value of noticing early. Option B does three useful things at once: it involves the person who can authorise a change, it proposes a concrete and proportionate fix, and it checks a prerequisite — because if the prototype is fundamentally untestable with a screen reader, recruiting a participant would waste their time and put them through a poor experience. Option A is least effective because documenting a gap you had time to close is a weak substitute for closing it. Option C confuses your own exploration with research — your experience of a screen reader you do not normally use tells you almost nothing about how a daily user would fare, and reporting it as a finding would be misleading, though it is a perfectly good way to check basic testability. Option D is disproportionate when four sessions can still yield value.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Being asked for the answer you do not have**

At a show and tell, a senior stakeholder asks, "So what percentage of users can complete this?" Your study was eight qualitative sessions. What is the most effective response?

- A) Give an estimate based on your sessions — around 60%.
- B) Explain that qualitative sessions show what problems exist and why, not how common they are across the user base, describe what you did find, and suggest analytics or a survey if a rate is needed.
- C) Say you do not know.
- D) Say the question cannot be answered.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** The stakeholder is asking a reasonable question, just of the wrong data. Option B does not simply refuse; it explains the distinction in one sentence, delivers the value you do have, and points at the method that would answer their actual question. That last part matters — it treats the stakeholder as a colleague with a legitimate need rather than someone to be corrected. Option A is least effective because converting five of eight into "around 60% of users" manufactures a statistic, and once that number is in a slide deck it will outlive every caveat you attach to it. Options C and D are honest but unhelpful, and D is also wrong, since the question is answerable with different data. Knowing what your method can and cannot support, and saying so plainly without being obstructive, is one of the most valuable things a researcher does in a room full of stakeholders.

**Question 8 (moderate) — A colleague's practice concerns you**

Observing a colleague's session, you hear them ask several leading questions and then, in the debrief, summarise the participant as having "loved the new design". You do not think that reflects what happened. What should you do?

- A) Correct them in the debrief in front of the team.
- B) Speak to them privately, describe the specific questions and the specific summary that concerned you, and ask how they saw it.
- C) Say nothing; they are your peer and it is not your session.
- D) Mention it to your senior researcher without speaking to the colleague.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** Two things went wrong — the leading questions and the overstated summary — and both are correctable if raised well. Option B is private, specific and genuinely open. Describing behaviour rather than character ("you asked whether they found it easier, which suggests the answer" rather than "you led the participant") makes it possible for them to hear it. Asking how they saw it is not politeness; you may be missing context, and a conversation goes better than a verdict. Option A embarrasses a colleague publicly, which reliably produces defensiveness rather than change. Option C is least effective because letting an inaccurate summary stand means a design decision may be made on it. Option D bypasses a peer who has had no chance to respond — reasonable if the pattern repeats after you have raised it, or if the issue is serious, but not as a first move.

**Question 9 (harder) — A safeguarding concern**

During a session about a housing service, a participant mentions in passing that they are currently sleeping in their car and have not eaten today. Your study is about the online form. What should you do?

- A) Continue with the discussion guide, since the comment is not relevant to your research question.
- B) Acknowledge what they have said with care, check whether they want to continue, offer the support signposting information your team holds, and report the disclosure to your senior researcher straight after the session in line with your safeguarding process.
- C) Stop the session and call a support service on their behalf.
- D) Note it in your findings as evidence of user need and move on.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A disclosure like this outranks the research question absolutely. Option B has four parts and each matters: acknowledging shows the person they were heard, checking whether they want to continue restores their control, signposting gives them something concrete without you acting as a support worker you are not trained to be, and reporting afterwards means the organisation's safeguarding process does its job rather than the decision resting on a junior researcher alone. Option A is least effective because treating a person's crisis as off-topic is a serious failure of care. Option C takes over rather than supporting, and acting on someone's behalf without their agreement removes their agency at a moment when they have little. Option D is worse still in one respect: it converts a person's distress into a data point. This is precisely the situation your team's safeguarding section exists for, which is why knowing that process before fieldwork begins is part of preparing properly.

**Question 10 (harder) — Pressure to change a finding**

Your report says three of eight participants could not complete identity verification. A senior stakeholder asks you to remove that finding from the summary slide because "it will derail the meeting" and says it can go in the appendix. What should you do?

- A) Move it to the appendix as asked.
- B) Keep it in the summary, and offer to present it alongside a proposed next step so the meeting has something to decide rather than just a problem to react to.
- C) Refuse and say the finding stays where it is.
- D) Escalate immediately to your senior researcher without responding.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** The stakeholder's concern about derailing the meeting is real, but the solution they have proposed hides a significant finding from the people who need it. Option B addresses the underlying worry directly: meetings derail when a problem arrives without a path forward, so arriving with "here is what we found and here is what we propose doing about it" usually converts the moment from confrontation into decision. Option A is least effective — burying a finding about identity verification, which is often where the most excluded users are lost, means a decision gets made on an incomplete picture, and you become complicit in that. Option C holds the line but offers nothing and turns a solvable disagreement into a standoff. Option D is worth doing as well, and you should tell your senior about this conversation either way, but responding constructively first is within your competence and is what your role expects.

**Question 11 (harder) — A mistake of your own**

You realise you have been using an old version of the discussion guide for the last three sessions, missing two questions the team agreed were essential. What should you do?

- A) Add the two questions for the remaining sessions and say nothing about the first three.
- B) Tell your senior researcher and the team immediately, explain exactly which sessions are affected, and propose options — adding the questions now, and either a short follow-up with the three participants or noting the limitation clearly in the analysis.
- C) Contact the three participants yourself to ask the missing questions.
- D) Note it in the report's limitations section at the end of the study.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Three sessions are missing data the team specifically agreed mattered, which affects what the analysis can support, so the team needs to know now rather than at reporting. Option B reports promptly, states the scope precisely, and — importantly — comes with options rather than just a problem. That combination is what turns an admission of error into a display of competence. Option A is least effective because concealing it means the analysis will be built on an inconsistency nobody else knows about. Option C is not unreasonable as an action but should not be taken unilaterally; re-contacting participants has consent and burden implications, and the decision belongs with your senior. Option D delays disclosure until the point of least usefulness. Everyone makes mistakes like this; what distinguishes people is the speed and completeness of the telling.

**Question 12 (harder) — Research being squeezed out**

Over three sprints, research findings have arrived after the relevant design decisions were already made. The team is polite about your work but nothing changes as a result of it. What is the most effective response?

- A) Accept it; the team's pace is not something a junior researcher can change.
- B) Bring specific examples to your senior researcher and to the team's retrospective, showing where earlier findings would have changed a decision, and propose a concrete change such as a short research slot before design decisions are locked.
- C) Work faster so findings arrive sooner.
- D) Stop doing research until the team commits to acting on it.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** This is a systemic problem, and systemic problems need evidence and a forum, not effort or protest. Option B supplies both: specific examples make the cost visible in a way that general complaint never does, the retrospective is the team's own mechanism for exactly this kind of issue, and proposing a concrete process change gives people something to say yes to. Involving your senior researcher first gives you support and a sense check. Option A gives up on something your role genuinely can influence — agile working is a named skill, and shaping how research fits the cycle is part of it. Option C treats a timing and process problem as a speed problem, and will exhaust you without fixing anything. Option D is least effective because withholding research punishes users rather than the process, and it will damage relationships you need. Changing how a team works takes patience, evidence and allies, and this is how a junior researcher starts building all three.

### Preparation tips

- **Answer as a junior researcher.** Competent independent action within your remit, plus prompt honesty beyond it. Not heroics, not helplessness.
- **Start from the participant.** When an option protects wellbeing, consent or access, assume it is strongest until you find a reason otherwise.
- **Look for options that do two things.** Fixing today's instance and improving tomorrow's process is the signature of a strong answer.
- **Prefer the direct private conversation.** It beats public correction, silence, and going over someone's head, in almost every scenario.
- **Bring an option, not just a problem.** Escalation with a proposed solution scores higher than escalation alone.
- **Read the least-effective question on its own terms.** The worst option is usually the one that conceals something or causes active harm, not simply the opposite of the best.
- **Talk through real scenarios with colleagues.** Ask your senior what they would have done. You will learn your organisation's actual norms, which is what this section measures.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Ranking by seniority.** Who is asking matters far less than what the consequences are.
- **Silence out of deference.** "Not my session" and "they are more experienced" are how good observations go unshared.
- **Escalating everything, or nothing.** Both extremes score poorly; attempt what you competently can, then tell the right person.
- **Delaying bad news.** Every mistake scenario rewards speed. Early problems are cheap; late ones are expensive.
- **Manufacturing numbers from qualitative data.** "Five of eight participants" must never become "60% of users".
- **Capitulating when challenged.** Acknowledge real limits, then hold the finding. Conceding everything is as unhelpful as conceding nothing.
- **Treating a person's disclosure as data.** Safeguarding comes before the research question, always.
- **Fixing the instance and ignoring the cause.** Adapting one session while the recruitment process keeps excluding people is half an answer.

## Conclusion

That is the end of the guide, and it is worth taking a moment to notice what you have just done. Forty-eight practice questions across four assessment types, with the reasoning behind every one of them — that is a serious piece of preparation, and considerably more than most candidates manage.

Look back at the ground you covered. In the cognitive section you checked a discussion guide against four standards at once, worked through rule-plus-exception deductions, ranked competing demands on a morning when your senior was away, chose between research methods on the basis of the question rather than the habit, and traced how a recruitment approach can exclude a whole user group at three separate points. In the numeric section you handled completion rates, over-recruitment for no-shows, ratios, weighted averages and funnel denominators, and you learned to tell a percentage from a percentage point and to notice when a mean is hiding an outlier. In the verbal section you practised reading exactly what is written — separating a failed condition from simple silence, refusing to treat association as causation, and catching "should" when it quietly became "must". And in the situational judgement section you worked through a distressed participant, an unbriefed observer, a designer defending their work, a safeguarding disclosure, a stakeholder who wanted a finding moved to the appendix, and a mistake of your own that needed reporting at once.

Notice how little of that was really about tests. Every one of those is a working skill. A researcher who counts participants rather than mentions, reads the standard as written, states findings at the strength the evidence supports, brings an option rather than just a problem, and reports their own errors within the hour — that is not a good test-taker, that is a good researcher. Preparing for these assessments and getting better at the job turn out to be very nearly the same activity, which is the most encouraging thing about the whole exercise.

A word about where you are. Junior user researcher is a genuinely interesting stage. You are past the point of needing someone beside you for everything, and not yet at the point where you are expected to have an answer for everything. Your role description captures it well: you carry out real research activities, you plan and conduct projects with support, you involve your team, and you present findings colleagues can use — while still working in combination with someone more experienced. Use that combination deliberately. The researchers who progress fastest from here are not the ones who ask for the least help; they are the ones who ask precise questions, act on the answers, and gradually need to ask less.

If you want to build on this, a few things will help more than rereading. Run a session and ask a colleague to observe you specifically for leading questions — it is uncomfortable and it is the fastest way to improve your facilitation. Volunteer to write the analysis grid for someone else's study, because analysing data you did not collect teaches you what good notes look like. Read your organisation's research standard and its safeguarding process properly, once, before you need them. Ask your senior how they would have handled the trickiest scenario in the last section, and expect their answer to differ in places from this guide, because real judgement is contextual and hearing the reasoning is how you build your own. And practise stating findings out loud at the right strength — "five of eight participants", not "most users" — until the accurate version is the one that comes naturally.

Finally, for the day itself. An assessment is a snapshot taken on one morning. It is not a verdict on your ability, your potential, or your future in this profession. The researchers whose work you admire have all had assessments that went badly and days when the right answer arrived ten minutes too late. What actually determines how far you go is the quality of the attention you pay to the people who use these services, and your willingness to keep learning — and someone who has read this far clearly has plenty of both.

Good luck. Go carefully, ask well, and keep noticing.
