# Head of Content Design - Psychometric Assessment Resources

## Introduction

Welcome. This guide is written for you as a head of content design in the UK Government Digital and Data profession — an expert practitioner with broad industry experience who defines and assures best practice while influencing, leading and mentoring others. Your role description adds that you develop a strategy for content that meets the organisation's objectives, lead a team capable of executing that strategy, work with senior stakeholders to influence organisational strategy, and prioritise and collaborate with counterpart colleagues across government.

That is a role in which almost nothing you achieve is executed by you. You may be approaching a formal assessment as part of a selection process, or you may be here because reasoning that has become second nature is worth periodically re-examining. Both are good reasons, and the second is the more valuable: at your level the risk is not gaps in technique but that technique has become automatic, automatic reasoning feels certain, and nobody in your organisation is positioned to check you before your judgement reaches a decision.

Four features of the role shape everything that follows. First, you are accountable for content produced by people you did not train, on services you have not seen, judged from the output rather than from the thinking behind it — which makes diagnostic reasoning at a distance a core competence. Second, you own a strategy, which means you are accountable for alignment as well as quality: content that is well written and pulls against the organisation's objectives is a failure of your role. Third, you influence senior stakeholders who substantially outrank you, in forums where content is rarely the loudest input; your influence rests on evidence being unassailable and on your judgement being trusted. Fourth, you build capability and collaborate across government: the practice you leave behind matters more than any page you assure.

There is a discipline-specific difficulty worth naming plainly. Content quality is unusually hard to argue about because everybody reads and most people believe they can write, which means content leadership is chronically vulnerable to being treated as a matter of taste. The remedy is evidence, expressed in currencies other people already use. A great deal of this guide is about that.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the reasoning your role demands, presented through practical materials rather than abstract puzzles. At head-of-profession level the materials are organisational and the reasoning is diagnostic and strategic. You will meet content produced by other people, standards applied unevenly, evidence gathered for other purposes, and prioritisation problems whose units are programmes and shared assets.

The format is typically an online test of fifteen to thirty minutes covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective against a norm group of comparable seniority. Adaptive platforms raise difficulty after correct answers, and acuity reports distinguish speed from accuracy.

Three things characterise this section at your level.

The first is reasoning at a distance. You rarely see the work; you see a page, a report, a claim in a board paper, and must judge from visible traces whether the thinking behind it holds.

The second is that the interesting questions concern mechanisms. Bad content is nearly always produced by something: a policy that is not settled, a sign-off process teams route around, a template missing a field, a stakeholder relationship in which nobody feels able to say no, or a recruitment market that has left five teams without an experienced content designer. Fixing the page leaves the mechanism intact.

The third is that prioritisation is genuinely hard because nothing dominates, and the discriminating factor is whether you reason from what your input can still change rather than from importance or visibility.

You need to memorise nothing. What you need is the discipline not to let fluency substitute for examination.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Auditing against a standard**

Your standard requires every transactional page to: use words users would use, say what happens next, state how long it will take, avoid requesting held information, offer a route to help, and name the responsible team. A page uses internal terminology, says the case will be assessed, gives no timescale, re-requests a held reference, has a "contact us" link in the footer, and names no responsible team. How many requirements are unmet?

- A) 3
- B) 4
- C) 5
- D) 6

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Work through all six. Internal terminology fails the first. "The case will be assessed" satisfies the second, minimally. No timescale fails the third. Re-requesting held information fails the fourth. The footer link satisfies the fifth, thinly. No named team fails the sixth. Four unmet, giving B. Option C typically comes from rejecting the footer link, which is a defensible content judgement and the wrong audit judgement — the discipline is to apply the standard as written. At your level that distinction is the useful output: if a footer link satisfies your standard for offering help, the standard needs a sentence about proximity, and you are the person who changes it. Auditing repeatedly against a standard you know to be too loose, and marking pages compliant that you believe are not, is how a standard loses its meaning inside an organisation.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction from a governance rule**

Your standard states: "Content may be published without second review only if it is a factual correction approved by the head of content design." A directorate reports publishing without second review. Which must be true?

- A) The standard was breached.
- B) It was an approved factual correction.
- C) Either it was an approved factual correction, or the standard was breached.
- D) The content should be withdrawn.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The rule is phrased as "only if", making the approved factual correction the sole route to publishing without review — so the disjunction has exactly two branches. Options A and B each assume one. Option D prescribes an action rather than stating what must be true, and at this level a sensible action is frequently offered as a distractor in a logic item. Only C holds. Note that the approval named is yours, which adds a practical point: if you did not approve it, you know which branch is true without asking, and if your approval can be given informally enough that you are unsure, that is a defect in the process you own. Rules routing approval through a single named person need a record, or they generate exactly this ambiguity.

**Question 3 (moderate) — Reading the thinking behind a page**

A page you are assuring is well written, follows the style guide, and answers its heading question clearly. It also generates the highest contact volume in the service. What is the most important observation?

- A) The page must have a defect the style guide does not cover.
- B) A page that reads well and still generates calls is usually answering a question users are not asking, arriving after an expectation set elsewhere, or correctly explaining a rule too conditional for prose — so the diagnostic step is establishing what callers actually say, which the contact centre already knows.
- C) The contact centre needs a better script.
- D) The page should be rewritten more simply.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The informative fact is the combination: well written, compliant, clear, and still generating calls. That rules out the obvious explanations and points outside the page. Option B names the realistic candidates and identifies the cheapest way to distinguish them, and each implies a different intervention: if callers ask whether their situation qualifies, the rule is too conditional for prose and needs a different content form; if they arrive from a letter saying something else, the fix is upstream; if they ask something the page never addresses, the heading answers the wrong question. Option A assumes a defect before checking anything. Option C treats the symptom, and at your level authorising a script is the intervention that permanently obscures the content problem — the calls stop being visible without stopping. Option D rewrites a page that already works.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Strategic prioritisation**

You can direct substantial content capacity to one of four things this quarter:

1. A new service's core journey content is being written next month and will set patterns reused across eight services.
2. A live page has generated steady contact volume for a year.
3. A programme wants exploratory content work for next year.
4. A directorate wants a style guide refresh.

Which is strongest?

- A) Item 2, because it affects users now.
- B) Item 1, because the decisions are imminent, will be reused across eight services, and content input is the only thing that could still shape them — and an established pattern is far harder to change than to set.
- C) Item 4, because a style guide affects everything.
- D) Item 3, because early work has the highest leverage.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The right frame is what your input can still change and whether there is time, and item 1 carries a multiplier the others lack. Patterns set once and reused across eight services embed good or bad decisions eight times, and undoing an established pattern later means coordinating eight teams and overcoming the fact that people defend what they have adopted. Item 2 matters and has been stable for a year. Item 3 has no deadline pressure. Item 4 is the interesting distractor at your level: a style guide does affect everything, and refreshing one that teams already ignore changes nothing, so its value depends entirely on a diagnosis nobody has made — which makes it a lower-value use of a quarter than it appears. Option A ranks by current impact and misses the compounding.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Cross-service synthesis**

Three items reach you in a month. A team reports users struggling with a benefit rule. A second service's search log shows the rule's key phrase as its top term. The contact centre reports queries about the rule across five services. What is the strongest synthesis?

- A) Three teams have found content problems.
- B) One rule that cannot be understood as expressed is appearing across at least five services — so it is a single content problem to be solved once, with an agreed and properly tested form of words, rather than five teams each writing their own.
- C) The rule should be simplified.
- D) Each team should improve its own page.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The synthesis that adds value identifies one problem behind several symptoms and reframes the unit of work. Five services struggling with the same rule is not five content problems; it is one, and solving it once produces an explanation that is consistent, tested and maintained in a single place. Option D is the default in most organisations and produces five different explanations, at least one wrong, all diverging over time — the exact situation that generates the inconsistency an audit finds two years later. Option A describes without synthesising. Option C is true and instructs nobody; simplifying the rule is policy's, explaining it clearly is yours. Note that this is precisely the material your cross-government collaboration is for: a rule in five of your services almost certainly appears in other departments, and one agreed explanation is worth considerably more than several.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Feedback on reasoning**

A lead proposes removing a warning paragraph because eight of ten participants called it "off-putting". What is the most useful intervention?

- A) Approve it; eight of ten is a clear signal.
- B) Note that "off-putting" describes a reaction to tone rather than evidence that the information is unnecessary, and that the settling question is whether users who skip the warning go on to make the mistake it exists to prevent — so the finding supports rewriting, not removing, unless separate evidence shows the risk is not real.
- C) Reject the proposal.
- D) Suggest testing with more participants.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The evidence is real and supports a different conclusion from the one drawn. Participants disliking a paragraph tells you about its tone and nothing about whether the information prevents harm. Option B makes that separation and names what would settle it — the outcome for users who skip the warning rather than their feelings about it. This distinction matters enormously at organisational scale, because "users found it off-putting" is among the most common justifications for deleting exactly the content that protects people, and the deletion tests beautifully: a page without a warning is more pleasant to read. If that reasoning is accepted once at your level it becomes precedent. Option A approves a removal on evidence that does not support it. Option C rejects without redirecting. Option D adds participants to a study asking the wrong question.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Diagnosing a systemic problem**

Six teams consistently publish content omitting timescales. Each has a plausible local reason — the timescale is uncertain, policy would not confirm it, the template has no field. What is the strongest diagnosis and response?

- A) Remind all teams that timescales are required.
- B) Six teams doing the same thing for plausible local reasons is one systemic cause with six rationalisations; establish which it is — the template lacks a field, teams cannot get a timescale confirmed, or the standard is silent on what to publish when a timescale is uncertain — and fix that, since the remedies are entirely different.
- C) Add timescales to the content checklist.
- D) Accept it; some timescales genuinely cannot be given.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Six teams behaving identically for individually plausible reasons is the signature of a systemic cause, and the plausibility is what makes case-by-case challenge exhausting and ineffective. Option B names the realistic candidates, and they imply completely different remedies: a template field is a half-hour job; getting policy to confirm timescales is a negotiation you should conduct once on all six teams' behalf; and the third — that the standard is silent on what to publish when a timescale is genuinely uncertain — is the most likely and most interesting, because the answer is not silence but a form of words such as "we usually reply within X and will tell you if it will take longer". Option A is the weakest available compliance response. Option C adds a checklist item where teams already know the requirement. Option D accepts the rationalisation without testing it.

**Question 8 (harder) — Scoping a flaw**

A study you are assuring recruited entirely through the service's online panel. Four findings: (i) participants did not understand a legal phrase; (ii) participants found the journey easy to navigate; (iii) participants completed in under ten minutes; (iv) participants did not notice a footer link. Which are most compromised?

- A) All four.
- B) None; the sample is adequate.
- C) Findings (ii) and (iii) are most compromised, because a panel recruited through the online service consists of people already comfortable with it, which flatters navigation and speed — while (i) and (iv) concern comprehension and noticing, less dependent on prior familiarity though not wholly independent of it.
- D) Only (i).

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The skill is scoping precisely rather than treating a sampling flaw as total or negligible. A panel recruited through the online service consists of people filtered twice — for using it successfully and volunteering afterwards — which most affects findings about ease and speed. Comprehension of an unfamiliar legal phrase is less dependent on familiarity. Option C says this including the honest qualifier. Option A discards findings that survive, and at your scale that does real damage: a head of profession who condemns wholesale teaches the organisation that declaring a sampling limitation invalidates the work, which is a direct incentive to stop declaring it — and you will then be assuring studies whose limitations are invisible, which is worse than the original situation because you no longer know what you are missing. Option B leaves two flattered findings circulating, and flattered findings generate confidence.

**Question 9 (harder) — Content that should not exist**

A page explaining how to correct a common form error is heavily used and well rated across four services using the same form component. What is the strongest analysis?

- A) The page works and should be maintained in all four.
- B) Heavy use of a recovery page measures how often the upstream defect occurs, and the defect is in a shared component used by four services — so the highest-value work is on the component, after which the page shrinks or disappears in all four at once.
- C) Make the page more prominent.
- D) Each service should maintain its own version.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Two things combine. A page existing to help people recover from a mistake is a symptom, and heavy use measures the failure rate rather than the page's quality — good ratings mean users are grateful to be rescued from a problem they should not have had. And the defect sits in a shared component, so fixing it once removes the problem from four services and makes four pages unnecessary. Option B identifies both. Option A maintains the symptom four times. Option C makes recovery easier while leaving the failure rate untouched. Option D is the worst structural answer, entrenching four separately maintained pages for one shared defect — and it is the option organisations drift into without deciding, which is how content estates become unrationalisable.

**Question 10 (harder) — Reading the distribution of a problem**

Your standard requires plain-language review before publication. Across the year, seventeen of twenty services complied; the three that did not were all in one directorate, all under ministerial deadline pressure, all by experienced content designers. What is the strongest response?

- A) Report the three and require remediation.
- B) Treat the concentration as diagnostic: three failures in one directorate under identical pressure by experienced people indicates a process that cannot be complied with when time is short — investigate what the review demands and how long it takes, build a fast route, and remediate the three alongside.
- C) Remind all content designers of the requirement.
- D) Introduce a mandatory pre-publication gate for every service.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The distribution is the most informative feature. Scattered breaches would indicate conduct or capability; three concentrated under identical pressure by experienced people indicate a process that does not survive a deadline. Option B investigates the mechanism and fixes it, while the remediation clause preserves the point that a systemic diagnosis is not a reason to leave individual failures unaddressed — those services have unreviewed content live now. Option A treats the instances and guarantees recurrence. Option C is the weakest available compliance response. Option D imposes friction on seventeen compliant services to catch three, which is how organisational burden accumulates — and you will be defending that gate in two years when a director general asks why content sign-off is slow, having forgotten that you introduced it.

**Question 11 (harder) — Strategy and evidence**

Your content strategy commits the organisation to "one voice across all channels". A review finds that the letters, which reach the most excluded users, are produced by a separate team using different standards and could not be brought into line within two years. What is the strongest response?

- A) Restate the commitment and press for compliance.
- B) Treat the finding as evidence the strategy was written without knowing what it committed to, and revise it — replacing an unachievable universal with a sequenced commitment that names letters as a priority precisely because they reach the most excluded users, with a realistic horizon.
- C) Exclude letters from the strategy's scope.
- D) Escalate the letters team's non-alignment.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A strategy commitment that cannot be met within two years is not a stretch target; it is a statement that will be quietly ignored, and every commitment in the document loses force alongside it. Option B does the harder and more useful thing: revises the strategy to say something true. The detail that makes it strong is the sequencing rationale — letters reach the most excluded users, so they are the highest priority rather than the awkward exception, and a strategy that says so is both more honest and more defensible than one that pretends the problem does not exist. Option C solves the embarrassment by excluding the users who most need the commitment, which inverts the purpose. Option A presses for something impossible. Option D treats a structural constraint as a compliance failure by another team, which will fail and cost you the relationship you need to fix it.

**Question 12 (harder) — A pattern across your organisation**

Reviewing a year of output, you find two teams consistently produce strong content and six do not. Both strong teams run a weekly session where drafts are read aloud. What is the most useful inference and action?

- A) The six weaker teams need training.
- B) Reading drafts aloud is a cheap, transferable routine that plausibly explains the difference — go and watch a session to establish what it actually does, then build it into the organisation's standards and expectations rather than attributing the difference to talent.
- C) Recruit more content designers like those in the strong teams.
- D) Increase assurance scrutiny on the six weaker teams.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The pattern is an opportunity to identify a practice, not to grade people. Reading drafts aloud is among the most reliably effective content routines in existence — it exposes sentences that cannot be said, qualifiers nobody would speak, and the exact points where a reader would stop — and it is free, needs no training, and transfers in a way individual skill does not. Option B goes and watches first, the step almost everyone skips, and it matters because the session may be doing something other than what its name suggests: the value might be in the reading aloud, or in the fact that two people look at every draft, or in the deadline it creates. Those imply different things to propagate. Option A posits a knowledge deficit without diagnosing one. Option C misattributes a routine to individuals. Option D catches more problems and creates no capacity to prevent them.

### Preparation tips

- **Read outputs for the thinking behind them.** A page that is compliant and still failing is telling you the problem is elsewhere.
- **Ask what produces bad content.** A policy, a template, a process, a relationship, a recruitment market — rarely a writer.
- **Scope flaws precisely.** Wholesale condemnation teaches the organisation to stop declaring limitations.
- **Weight decisions by reuse.** Patterns set once and repeated eight times are worth eight times the attention.
- **Separate a reaction to tone from evidence about need.** Accepting that conflation once makes it precedent.
- **Read the distribution of failures.** Concentrated non-compliance diagnoses a process.
- **Revise a strategy commitment that cannot be met.** An ignored commitment devalues the ones beside it.
- **Go and watch before inferring a cause.** The routine may not be doing what its name suggests.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Marking pages compliant against a standard you believe is too loose.** Change the standard.
- **Rewriting a page that already works.**
- **Approving deletion of protective content because it tests as off-putting.**
- **Reminding teams of rules they already know and cannot meet.**
- **Adding friction for the compliant majority.** You will defend that gate later.
- **Letting five teams each solve one shared problem.**
- **Excluding from a strategy the users who most need it.**
- **Refreshing a standard nobody follows.** Diagnose why first.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A numeric reasoning assessment measures how accurately you handle quantitative material. At your level the emphasis is almost wholly on interrogation. You will need to compute correctly, but the questions that matter ask whether a figure means what somebody claims — and in most rooms you enter, you may be the only person who will ask.

The format is typically a timed online test of fifteen to thirty minutes, each item presenting a table, chart or paragraph of figures with a multiple-choice question. Calculators are usually provided and the arithmetic is deliberately tractable. Time per item is tight, so the operative skill is locating what matters and discarding what does not.

The professional stake is unusually high for your discipline, for two connected reasons. Content quality is hard to argue about because everybody reads, which leaves content leadership vulnerable to being treated as taste. And content work is chronically under-resourced because its value is hard to express in numbers other people use. Both problems have the same remedy. A head of content design who can say "two in five calls to this service ask when something will happen, which is 43,200 calls a year at roughly £6 each" is making an argument a director general recognises; one who describes clarity is not. Your role names influencing organisational strategy, and that conversation happens in numbers.

Four habits carry the load. Establish what the question asks before looking at the data. Identify the denominator and the population it represents. Estimate, calculate, compare. And sanity-check against the world.

Distractors come from a predictable catalogue: wrong denominator, percentage confused with percentage point, new value as the base in a percentage change, rates averaged without weighting.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The numerical skills tested map onto your named skills:

- **Interrogating quantitative claims** maps to defining and assuring best practice.
- **Aggregating across services** maps to **Strategic thinking (content design)**, where a content strategy needs a view of the estate.
- **Quantifying content value** maps to influencing organisational strategy and to leading a team capable of executing one, since capability requires resource.
- **Comparing groups fairly** maps to **User focus (content design)**.
- **Presenting figures at defensible strength** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management (content design)**.
- **Detecting misleading metrics** maps to **User focus**, where a wrong measure misdirects content work across several teams at once.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Rate from counts**

Of 6,400 users who answered a feedback prompt, 1,600 said they did not find what they needed. What percentage?

- A) 20%
- B) 25%
- C) 30%
- D) 40%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 1,600 ÷ 6,400 = 0.25, so 25%, and the answer is B. Estimate first: 1,600 of 6,400 is a quarter. Options A and C come from dividing by 8,000 and 5,333. Worth noting what the figure is: the percentage of people who *answered the prompt*, not of everyone who saw the page. Prompts are answered disproportionately by people with a strong reaction, which makes the absolute level unreliable and comparisons between pages far more useful. That distinction belongs in the reporting convention you own, because otherwise the absolute figure gets quoted at a board as a satisfaction rate, and you will spend a year correcting it.

**Question 2 (easy) — Comparing pages fairly**

| Page | Views | Negative feedback |
|---|---|---|
| A | 60,000 | 1,800 |
| B | 12,000 | 1,440 |
| C | 30,000 | 900 |

Which page has the highest negative rate, and what does the pattern tell you?

- A) Page A, 3%, because it has the most negative responses
- B) Page B, 12%, and Pages A and C sitting identically at 3% establishes a baseline that makes 12% interpretable
- C) Page C, 3%
- D) Page A, 30%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Rates are: A 1,800 ÷ 60,000 = 3%; B 1,440 ÷ 12,000 = 12%; C 900 ÷ 30,000 = 3%. Page B is highest, giving B. The second half of that option is what makes it the strongest answer at your level: two pages sitting identically at 3% is not incidental information, it is what establishes a normal range and turns 12% from a number into a finding. Without a baseline, 12% could be typical for this kind of content. Option A confuses the largest absolute count with the highest rate, which is the standard trap — though Page A's 1,800 disappointed users remain a legitimate separate concern, and a report giving only one of the two figures is incomplete.

**Question 3 (easy) — Reading a contact log**

A month of contact centre calls: "when will I hear" 4,800, "what documents" 3,000, "how do I cancel" 800, "fee query" 800, "other" 2,600. What percentage concern timescales, and how many calls a year?

- A) 40%, 57,600
- B) 40%, 4,800
- C) 36%, 57,600
- D) 45%, 57,600

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Total the list: 4,800 + 3,000 + 800 + 800 + 2,600 = 12,000. Then 4,800 ÷ 12,000 = 0.4, which is 40%. Annualising: 4,800 × 12 = 57,600 calls a year. So A. Option B stops at the monthly figure, the commonest error in this question type because the calculation feels finished before the annualisation. The annual figure is the one that matters at your level: 57,600 calls a year, at whatever your organisation's cost per call is, converts a missing sentence into a business case. Two in five calls asking when something will happen is a content problem with a one-sentence solution, and expressing it in annual volume and cost is how the sentence competes for attention against a redesign.

**Question 4 (easy) — Centre and spread**

Word counts of nine pages in a guide: 130, 140, 150, 155, 165, 170, 180, 200 and 3,000. Which summary is most defensible?

- A) Mean 476 words
- B) Median 165, range 130 to 3,000, with the 3,000-word page flagged as probably several pages that have not been split
- C) Median 165
- D) Mean 476, range 130 to 3,000

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The values sum to 4,290, and 4,290 ÷ 9 = 476.7, so the mean is correct and describes no page in the guide. The median is the fifth value, 165, which describes it well. But the median alone conceals a page eighteen times the typical length, the most informative item in the set. Option B supplies centre, spread and the content-specific interpretation that makes it strongest: a 3,000-word page among 150-word pages is not a statistical outlier to be noted but almost certainly a page doing several jobs, which is a structural finding. Option D pairs the range with a misleading centre. Option C buries the interesting item. Write into your reporting conventions that a central figure never travels alone.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Weighted aggregation**

Four pages have negative feedback rates of 4% on 30,000 views, 10% on 6,000 views, 6% on 12,000 views, and 8% on 12,000 views. What is the overall rate across all 60,000 views?

- A) 5.8%
- B) 6.5%
- C) 7%
- D) 8%

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Convert to counts. Page one: 4% of 30,000 = 1,200. Page two: 10% of 6,000 = 600. Page three: 6% of 12,000 = 720. Page four: 8% of 12,000 = 960. Total 1,200 + 600 + 720 + 960 = 3,480, across 60,000 views, so 3,480 ÷ 60,000 = 0.058, which is 5.8% and the answer is A. Option C is the unweighted mean of 4, 10, 6 and 8, which is exactly 7 and over-weights the small pages against the 30,000-view page that should dominate. Always return to counts: the unweighted average answers what the average *page* does, while the weighted figure answers what the average *user* experiences, and those diverge whenever traffic is uneven. Pair it with a second habit at your level — re-check the total before it leaves your hands, because a figure attributed to the head of profession is one nobody downstream will question.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Did the change work?**

Before a rewrite, 2,400 of 6,000 users answered a prompt negatively. After, 1,800 of 7,200 did. Did the page improve, and by how much in percentage points?

- A) Yes, by 600 responses
- B) Yes, by 15 percentage points
- C) Yes, by 25 percentage points
- D) No, it got worse

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Convert to rates because the totals differ. Before: 2,400 ÷ 6,000 = 40%. After: 1,800 ÷ 7,200 = 25%. The improvement is 15 percentage points, giving B. Option A compares raw counts across different-sized groups, and note the direction: more people answered after the change, so the raw fall of 600 understates the improvement. That is worth registering because the usual warning about raw counts assumes they flatter, and they can equally conceal a genuine win — which matters at your level for how teams report to you, since a team using raw counts may be underselling good work as easily as overselling poor work, and either way you are drawing conclusions about capability from a distorted picture.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Percentage change and interpretation**

Views of a guidance page fell from 100,000 to 75,000 a month after a form was simplified. What is the change, and what does it most likely mean?

- A) 25% decrease, and the guidance is failing to reach people
- B) 25% decrease, which may be the intended outcome — if the form no longer produces the confusion the guidance resolved, fewer people needing it is success
- C) 33% decrease, and the guidance is failing
- D) 33% decrease, which may be success

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The difference is 25,000, and 25,000 ÷ 100,000 = 0.25, a 25% decrease, so options C and D miscalculate by dividing by the new figure. Between A and B, interpretation decides it. A fall in views of guidance that exists to resolve confusion is what you would expect if the confusion has gone, and treating page views as a success measure for that content gets the sign backwards. At your level the practical point is about expectation-setting: your teams will be asked to justify content work in traffic terms, and for a significant class of content success looks like decline. Establishing that with a board *before* the numbers arrive is far easier than explaining it afterwards, and it protects teams from a metric that would otherwise punish them for succeeding.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Reconstructing an implied value**

A business case claims that reducing contact volume by 50,000 calls a year is worth £300,000. What is the implied cost per call, and what does that tell you?

- A) £6 per call, a plausible order of magnitude for a short telephone interaction
- B) £60 per call, implausibly high
- C) £0.60 per call, implausibly low
- D) £600 per call

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** £300,000 ÷ 50,000 = £6 per call, giving A. Whether £6 is plausible depends on the service, and for a short telephone interaction it is a reasonable order — figures cited across government generally sit between a few pounds and low tens of pounds depending on call length and channel. The value of the arithmetic is the check rather than the number: reconstructing an implied unit value takes thirty seconds and tells you whether a case is grounded. If it had come out at £60, the case needs explaining; if at £0.60, the saving is understated and content work is worth more than the paper claims — which is worth knowing, because an understated benefit is as damaging to your resourcing argument as an inflated one is to your credibility.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Choosing the denominator**

A journey shows 100,000 start, 80,000 reach the evidence page, 60,000 reach the declaration, 57,000 submit. What proportion of those reaching the declaration submitted?

- A) 57%
- B) 60%
- C) 71%
- D) 95%

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** The stem names users who reached the declaration, so the denominator is 60,000 and the part is 57,000: 57,000 ÷ 60,000 = 0.95, giving D. Option A uses 100,000, answering a whole-journey question nobody asked. This is the most common trap in funnel data and it determines where content effort goes across an estate: 95% of people reaching the declaration submit, so the declaration is not the problem. The losses are 20,000 between the start and the evidence page and 20,000 between evidence and declaration. Being able to establish that in fifteen seconds is what lets you redirect a team's plan in the meeting rather than after they have spent a quarter on the wrong page.

**Question 10 (harder) — A metric that hides a split**

Average time on a guidance page is 50 seconds. Sessions show participants either glancing for a few seconds or reading carefully for two to three minutes. What is the most useful response?

- A) The average is wrong.
- B) The average is probably correct and uninformative: it sits between two clusters and describes neither, so ask for the distribution — and note that a single edit may serve one group at the other's expense, which is the argument for structure rather than length.
- C) The participants were unrepresentative.
- D) The page should be shorter.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** When behaviour splits into two clusters the mean lands in the gap and describes nobody. Fifty seconds is consistent with half the users spending five seconds and half two and a half minutes, and those are different problems: glancers may be skipping content they need, readers may be struggling with it. Option B asks for the distribution and names the consequence that matters — shortening helps the glancers and removes what the readers needed, while a summary at the top serves both, so the answer is structural. Option D is the change that looks obvious and may be exactly wrong, and at your level it is worth noticing how often "make it shorter" arrives as an instruction from someone who has seen only the average. Option A disputes a correct figure. Option C blames the research.

**Question 11 (harder) — Percentage points and relative change**

A page's usefulness score rose from 50% to 62%. Which statement is accurate?

- A) A 12% increase.
- B) A 12 percentage point increase, equivalent to a 24% relative increase.
- C) A 24 percentage point increase.
- D) A 19.4% increase.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The arithmetic gap is 62 − 50 = 12 percentage points. The relative increase is 12 ÷ 50 = 0.24, or 24%. Option B labels both. Option A is ambiguous and generally read as relative. Option C misapplies the percentage-point label. Option D divides by the new value. The stake is unusually direct for content leadership, because your teams write the sentences these figures appear in, which means your reporting convention determines whether the organisation's content reporting is honest. Requiring both figures as a standing convention costs nothing, protects your teams from any suggestion that the flattering framing was chosen, and prevents the situation where a colleague later notices the selection and discounts everything else in the document.

**Question 12 (harder) — Sampling adequacy**

A directorate proposes measuring whether a rewrite improved completion by running 30 moderated sessions before and 30 after. Baseline is 60%; a 5 percentage point improvement is meaningful. What is the strongest response?

- A) Agree; 60 sessions is substantial.
- B) Explain that a 5 point difference on 30 per group is one or two participants and well inside ordinary variation, so the comparison cannot detect it — recommend measuring the rate with live analytics across all users and spending the 60 sessions on mechanisms, which moderated research does uniquely well.
- C) Increase to 50 per group.
- D) Run it and report the difference with caveats.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Thirty per group cannot distinguish a five point difference from chance; reliable detection needs hundreds. Option B says so and redirects rather than merely objecting: analytics measures the rate across the whole user base at no recruitment cost, while sessions are the only instrument explaining why. Option A endorses a design that cannot answer its own question. Option C moves towards adequacy without approaching it. Option D is the most damaging at your level for a reason specific to content: it establishes a precedent that content effectiveness can be measured on thirty people, which will be cited the next time a rewrite is judged on a handful of sessions and found not to have "worked" — and you will be defending your teams against a standard you allowed.

### Preparation tips

- **Find the denominator and the population first.**
- **Total the list, then annualise.** A monthly figure understates a case twelvefold.
- **Return to counts before combining rates, and re-check the total.** Your figures travel unquestioned.
- **Reconstruct implied unit values.** An understated benefit hurts your resourcing as much as an inflated one hurts your credibility.
- **Read for the baseline before the exception.** Two pages at the same rate are what make the third interpretable.
- **Set expectations about metrics before the numbers arrive.** Especially where success looks like decline.
- **Ask for the distribution.** A mean between two clusters invites the wrong edit.
- **Write both framings into the reporting convention you own.**

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Dividing by the wrong total.**
- **Stopping at a monthly figure.**
- **Averaging page-level rates without weighting.**
- **Letting an absolute feedback rate be quoted as a satisfaction rate.**
- **Treating page views as a success measure for problem-solving content.**
- **Accepting "make it shorter" from someone who has seen only the average.**
- **Endorsing an underpowered comparison.** It sets a precedent used against your teams.
- **Confusing percentages with percentage points.** Your teams write the sentence.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately you read. For most disciplines it is a proxy skill; for yours it is close to the job, which makes this the section where you should score best and where a settled habit costs most.

The classic format presents a short passage of dense workplace prose, typically three to six sentences, followed by statements to classify as True, False or Cannot Say. True means the statement follows necessarily. False means the passage contradicts it. Cannot Say means the passage neither establishes nor excludes it. Alongside these come comprehension items, main-point items, and questions asking which of four summaries is most accurate.

The governing rule is that you answer from the passage alone, and here is the part specific to your level: expertise actively costs marks. The more policy and governance prose you have read and translated, the more readily you supply the clause you expect, complete the sentence the way such sentences usually end, and feel entirely confident doing it. Candidates at your level underperform on verbal reasoning at rates that surprise them, and this is nearly always the mechanism. The counter-habit must be mechanical.

Cannot Say is the answer experienced leaders avoid most. Forming a view under incomplete information is what your job consists of, and here it is a liability — particularly where the claim is one you would like to be true, which is the specific trap this section sets for people who own a strategy.

Small words carry the load. "All" is not "most". "May" is not "must". "Should" is not "will". "Consider whether" is not "recommend". "Unless", "provided that" and "solely" govern everything around them.

The payoff is direct. Your teams are handed policy prose and asked what it requires; you define the standard for how they answer that and assure the result. Getting it wrong in either direction is serious at organisational scale: understate a requirement across an estate and many users miss something that matters; overstate it and the organisation has published obligations government does not impose.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The verbal skills tested map onto your named skills:

- **Reading precisely** maps to defining and assuring best practice.
- **Separating statement from inference** maps to **User focus (content design)**.
- **Identifying the main point** maps to the core task your teams perform and you set the standard for.
- **Handling qualified language** maps to **Strategic thinking (content design)**, where knowing what is required and what is recommended tells you where the organisation has room.
- **Reading for absence** maps to **User focus**.
- **Reading cross-government prose accurately** maps to your role level's collaboration with counterparts across government, where shared standards are the medium.

### Practice questions

*Passage A — for Questions 1 to 4*

"Organisations must submit an annual content assurance return to the cross-government profession board, unless all content within the organisation has been produced under a centrally managed programme. Returns must confirm that plain-language review was completed where required and list any services where it was not. Where an organisation identifies content published without required review, it must notify the board within ten working days of identifying it, separately from the annual return. Returns are published across government; notifications are not."

**Question 1 (easy)** — Statement: "Every organisation must submit an annual content assurance return." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The passage attaches an exception: organisations where all content was produced under a centrally managed programme are exempt. The statement's "every" contradicts that, so False. This is the most reliable pattern in the section. Note that the exception says "all content", not "most", which narrows it substantially and supplies Question 3.

**Question 2 (easy)** — Statement: "Notifications of missed review are published across government." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The final sentence states that returns are published across government and notifications are not. The statement contradicts this, so False. The item tests whether you read past the point where a sentence appeared to finish — the semicolon clause carries the operative distinction, and a reader who registered the first half would generalise it. Governance prose places qualifications after semicolons with striking regularity, and slowing down there rather than speeding up is a habit worth building when you read at volume. It also has a practical edge: the distinction between what is published and what is not is exactly the sort of thing colleagues ask you to confirm before deciding whether to declare something.

**Question 3 (moderate)** — Statement: "An organisation where nineteen of twenty services produced content under a centrally managed programme must submit a return." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The exemption applies only where *all* content was produced centrally. Nineteen of twenty does not satisfy that, so the exemption does not apply and the requirement stands — True. The item rewards carrying "all" from the first sentence to a scenario several lines later. It reflects a recurring organisational error: partial satisfaction of an exception condition is non-compliance, and organisations routinely assume the reverse on the reasoning that they are substantially covered. Catching that before a return is due, rather than after it is late and published across government, is squarely your job.

**Question 4 (moderate)** — Statement: "Content published without required review must be notified within ten working days of publication." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The passage requires notification within ten working days of *identifying* the content, not of publishing it. Those triggers can be months apart — content published in February and identified in a September audit must be notified in September, and would not be retrospectively late. The statement misstates the trigger, so False. Small distinction, large consequence: trigger events determine compliance, and misreading one produces either unnecessary alarm or false comfort, both expensive when reported to a cross-government board on your signature. Whenever a passage specifies a deadline, establish what starts the clock before anything else.

*Passage B — for Questions 5 to 8*

"The cross-government review of content assurance found considerable variation between departments. Five departments operate peer review before publication; the remainder rely on individual sign-off. Departments with peer review reported fewer post-publication corrections, although the review did not examine whether their content was more accurate. The review recommends that the profession board consider whether a common assurance approach would be proportionate."

**Question 5 (easy)** — Statement: "Some departments do not operate peer review before publication." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The passage states that five departments operate peer review and the remainder rely on individual sign-off, establishing directly that some do not. True. Answer quickly and bank the time. The only conceivable ambiguity — whether "the remainder" might be empty — is closed by the passage describing what the remainder does.

**Question 6 (moderate)** — Statement: "Peer review produces more accurate content." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The passage reports fewer post-publication corrections and then states explicitly that accuracy was not examined. Those are different things, and the gap is instructive: peer-reviewed departments might produce more accurate content, or might catch errors before publication rather than after — which produces the same statistic and is a real benefit — or might simply be less likely to have errors reported. The passage flags its own silence, so Cannot Say. This is the specific trap for someone in your position, because "peer review improves accuracy" is a claim you would like to be true and would probably cite when arguing for a common approach. Citing it and then being asked for the evidence is how a good proposal loses.

**Question 7 (moderate)** — Statement: "The review recommends introducing a common assurance approach." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The review recommends that the board *consider whether* a common approach would be proportionate, which includes concluding it would not be. The statement asserts a recommendation to introduce, which was not made, so False rather than Cannot Say: the passage tells you what was recommended and it was something different. This pervades review prose, and at your level you are likely to be both the person the recommendation reaches and the person who writes what the organisation did about it — so misreading it commits you to something nobody asked for and then reports delivery against it.

**Question 8 (harder)** — Statement: "Individual sign-off is a weaker form of assurance than peer review." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The passage describes two approaches and reports one difference in outcome while explicitly declining to assess accuracy. It makes no judgement about relative strength. A reader may reasonably infer that peer review is stronger, and the passage neither supports nor excludes that, so Cannot Say. What makes this harder than Question 6 is that the passage's structure leans towards peer review — mentioned first, associated with a favourable outcome — and structural lean is not content. Noticing when you are responding to a document's implied stance rather than its assertions is among the most transferable things this section trains, and it applies directly to review reports, which routinely imply more than they will state so readers draw the conclusion the authors lacked evidence to assert.

*Passage C — for Questions 9 to 12*

"Content must not be altered to reflect the preferences of stakeholders where doing so would make it inaccurate or materially incomplete. Presentation and tone may be adapted to the audience provided the substance is unchanged. Where a content designer is asked to alter content in a way they believe breaches this, the matter must be raised with the head of content design, who is accountable for the integrity of published content. Where content cannot be reviewed before publication, the reason must be recorded; records are reviewed annually."

**Question 9 (easy)** — Statement: "Tone may be adapted for different audiences." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The second sentence permits adapting presentation and tone provided the substance is unchanged, and the statement claims only that tone may be adapted. It follows and is True. The statement is weaker than the passage's full provision, omitting the substance condition — but a weaker statement the passage supports is still True. Candidates occasionally mark such statements False for incompleteness, confusing "does not say everything" with "says something wrong". Note that this clause is the one that resolves most real disputes about softening content: tone is negotiable, substance is not.

**Question 10 (moderate)** — Statement: "A content designer asked to make content less complete must refuse." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The passage establishes that content must not be altered where doing so would make it materially incomplete, and that a designer asked to do so must raise it with the head of content design. Neither states what the individual must personally do at the moment of the request. A duty to escalate is not a duty to refuse — and the escalation route exists precisely so that an individual is not left to refuse alone, which is a substantive protection and, since the passage names your role as accountable, the protection you are supposed to provide. Cannot Say. Note also that the prohibition is conditional on the alteration making content inaccurate or *materially* incomplete, and "materially" is doing work.

**Question 11 (harder)** — Statement: "Removing any information from a page at a stakeholder's request breaches the requirements." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The prohibition is conditional twice over: alteration must not occur where it would make content inaccurate or *materially* incomplete. Removing information that is not material — a redundant sentence, a repeated explanation — is not caught, and the second sentence expressly permits adapting presentation provided substance is unchanged. The statement's "any information" contradicts both, so False. This distinction is fine and consequential, and one you should expect to explain repeatedly. Read as absolute, the clause makes every stakeholder edit suspect and makes content designers defensive about reasonable requests; read as written, it identifies exactly what is prohibited, which is making content wrong or leaving out something that matters. Standards read as absolutes lose their force, because people who know the absolute reading is unworkable start ignoring the clause entirely.

**Question 12 (harder) — Main point**

Which best captures the main point of Passage C?

- A) Content must never be changed at a stakeholder's request.
- B) The substance of published content must remain accurate and materially complete regardless of stakeholder preference, with tone and presentation adaptable, escalation provided where a designer believes that line is being crossed, accountability located with the head of content design, and honesty required where review cannot happen in time.
- C) The head of content design is accountable for content integrity.
- D) Reasons must be recorded when content cannot be reviewed before publication.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The main point must span the passage while adding nothing. Option A overstates: adaptation of tone and presentation is expressly permitted and the prohibition is specifically on making content inaccurate or materially incomplete. Options C and D are each accurate about one clause and silent on the rest. Option B captures all four elements and the logic joining them — published content is useful only if it is accurate and arrives in time, so the passage protects accuracy with a conditional rule, an escalation route and an accountable owner, and handles timing with a requirement of disclosure rather than a prohibition. Note that B preserves "materially", which A discards; writing a summary that keeps every qualifier and adds nothing is the skill you define the standard for.

### Preparation tips

- **Point at the words.** Before marking True, identify the phrase that makes it true.
- **Trust Cannot Say.** Especially where the claim is one that would support a proposal you favour.
- **Circle every qualifier.** All, every, solely, may, must, should, unless, provided that, materially, consider whether.
- **Establish what starts the clock.** Deadlines hang on trigger events, and the trigger is often not the obvious one.
- **Notice doubly conditional prohibitions.** "Must not, where it would make content inaccurate or materially incomplete" is narrow.
- **Separate "recommends considering X" from "recommends X".** You are likely the recipient and the reporter.
- **Notice structural lean.** Order of mention and favourable association are not assertions.
- **Practise on cross-government standards.** Write three statements about a page — one True, one False, one Cannot Say.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Answering from professional knowledge.** The dominant error at your level.
- **Marking True on a claim that would help your case.**
- **Missing "all" and "materially".** Partial satisfaction of an exception is non-compliance, and not every omission is material.
- **Reading a conditional prohibition as absolute.** It makes the standard unworkable and then ignored.
- **Reading a proxy outcome as the thing itself.** Fewer corrections is not greater accuracy.
- **Confusing a rule about outcomes with a rule about conduct.**
- **Responding to implied stance.** Review reports imply more than they assert.
- **Rushing after a semicolon.**

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment presents realistic workplace scenarios and asks what you would do. There is no passage to parse and no arithmetic. You get a dilemma of the kind your role produces, four or five candidate responses, and a question asking which is most effective and often which is least.

This section measures whether your judgement matches what the profession considers good practice at your level. Your role places you as an expert practitioner who defines and assures best practice, develops a content strategy meeting organisational objectives, leads a team capable of executing it, works with senior stakeholders to influence organisational strategy, and collaborates with counterparts across government. The strongest answers involve acting on systems rather than instances, building capability and conditions rather than solving problems personally, absorbing pressure on behalf of the people you lead, and using influence where you hold no formal authority.

Three features distinguish your level.

The first is that almost nothing you do is executed by you. An option solving a problem through your personal intervention is usually second-best, because it does not survive your absence and does not scale past your calendar.

The second is that content leadership is chronically vulnerable to being treated as taste, because everybody reads. That makes evidence your primary instrument, and it makes options that bring evidence in other people's currencies systematically stronger than options that assert quality.

The third is that you absorb pressure downwards. When a director general leans on a content designer, the content designer should not be holding that line.

Four principles carry most scenarios. Users come first, and content failures are quiet — nobody rings to say they misunderstood and gave up. Protect the accuracy of what is published. Ask what a request is for. And fix the source, not the instance.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The judgement dimensions map onto your named skills:

- **Defining and assuring best practice** maps to your role level's core accountability.
- **Developing and defending a strategy** maps to **Strategic thinking (content design)** and to influencing organisational strategy.
- **Leading a capable team** maps to your role level's requirement to lead a team able to execute the strategy, and to **Agile working (content design)**.
- **Influence with senior stakeholders** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management (content design)**.
- **Protecting users** maps to **User focus (content design)** and **User-centred content design**.
- **Cross-government collaboration** maps to your role level's named prioritisation and collaboration with counterparts.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — A content designer under pressure**

A lead tells you a director general has asked a content designer to remove a warning from a page because it "undermines the message". What is the most effective response?

- A) Tell the lead to instruct their designer to refuse.
- B) Agree with the lead how the warning should be presented — including whether the tone can change while the substance stays — and take the conversation with the director general yourself, since a request of that kind at that level is a matter for you.
- C) Contact the director general and cite the standard.
- D) Approve the removal.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Two things must happen: the warning survives in substance, and neither the lead nor the content designer is left negotiating with a director general. Option B does both, and the tone clause matters — "undermines the message" is often a genuine objection to phrasing rather than to the information, and offering to change how it reads while keeping what it says resolves most of these conversations without anyone losing. Taking it yourself is your job: a power differential of that size is what your role absorbs. Option D is least effective, removing content that protects users because of who asked, and establishing that requests at that level succeed. Option A leaves a content designer refusing a director general by proxy. Option C treats a conversation as a compliance matter, which wins the point and costs the relationship you need for the next one.

**Question 2 (easy) — A strategy nobody uses**

You inherited a content strategy that teams do not refer to. It is thorough and two years old. What is the strongest approach?

- A) Republish it with a message from you.
- B) Find out why it is not used — most likely that it describes principles rather than decisions, or commits to things nobody resourced, or was written without the teams — and rewrite it around the decisions teams actually face, with the people who face them.
- C) Introduce compliance reporting against the strategy.
- D) Run sessions explaining the strategy.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A strategy nobody refers to has already failed, and republishing changes only the date. Option B does the diagnostic work first, and the candidates it names are the realistic ones: strategies go unused because they state principles everyone already agrees with rather than resolving the questions teams argue about, or because they commit to things nobody funded, or because they arrived without their authors. Rewriting around actual decisions — which channels get content resource, what happens when policy will not confirm a timescale, who arbitrates when a service and the strategy disagree — produces a document people open because it answers something. Option A is least effective, the purest form of appearing to act. Option C enforces a document people find useless. Option D explains rather than fixes.

**Question 3 (easy) — Conditions for the team**

Your content designers are embedded one per service across nine services, are pulled into delivery firefighting, have no time for critique, and rarely see each other. Three have left in eight months. What is the strongest response?

- A) Discuss workload with each delivery manager.
- B) Treat it as structural: establish a protected routine bringing content designers together, negotiate the time with delivery leadership by showing what the pattern costs in rework and attrition, and change how people are deployed if single embedding is the cause.
- C) Introduce a wellbeing initiative.
- D) Recruit replacements quickly.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Three departures in eight months alongside no critique and no professional contact is a structural pattern. Option B addresses it on three fronts, and the deployment clause matters particularly here: a content designer embedded alone has nobody to read their drafts, which removes the single most effective quality mechanism the discipline has — so single embedding damages the work as directly as the people, and your assurance burden rises to compensate. The negotiation detail matters too: protected time not paid for in delivery's currency gets cancelled by the third sprint, and you will have spent credibility introducing it. Option D is least effective, reproducing the attrition on a lag in a competitive market where your reputation as an employer travels quickly through a small professional community. Option A addresses symptoms individually. Option C treats a design-of-work problem as morale.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Assuring across a boundary**

A directorate you do not manage has published content whose central claim about eligibility is wrong. It is live. What is most effective?

- A) Correct it yourself.
- B) Contact the directorate's content lead immediately with the specific error and the evidence, agree who corrects it and by when, and check whether the claim appears elsewhere — while making clear a live eligibility error needs fixing today.
- C) Raise it at the content community.
- D) Report it to the director.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** A wrong eligibility claim may cause people to apply when they cannot or not apply when they can, so speed matters. Option B moves quickly while doing what distinguishes a head's response: going to the person who can fix it, agreeing ownership and a deadline rather than leaving a report, and checking where else the claim appears, because an eligibility statement is almost never made once. Option C is least effective by a distance — deferring a live factual error to a community forum mistakes what that forum is for, and it broadcasts a team's error before they have had a chance to fix it, which will cost you their cooperation for a year. Option A bypasses the people responsible and may exceed your permissions. Option D escalates before asking, which is slower and will be resented.

**Question 5 (moderate) — A stakeholder who will not accept evidence**

A director general dismisses research showing users do not understand a key term, saying the participants were unrepresentative and the term is standard in the sector. What is most effective?

- A) Concede and keep the term.
- B) Acknowledge the sample's limits honestly, point out that a term being standard among practitioners says nothing about the public and is if anything weak evidence the other way, and propose a check they will accept — the site search log, showing whether users search for that term or a different one.
- C) Insist on the change.
- D) Escalate.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Two claims of very different quality have been made, and separating them is the whole move. The sample objection is partly fair and worth conceding, which costs nothing and buys credibility for the part that matters. The sector-standard claim is a category error — terms become standard among specialists by being used among specialists, which is weak evidence the public shares them. Option B says both and then proposes evidence the director general is likely to accept, drawn from data the organisation already holds, converting a disagreement into a question with an answer. That last move is what makes it a head's answer rather than a practitioner's. Option A is least effective, conceding to an objection that does not survive examination and teaching a senior stakeholder that this works — which you will pay for repeatedly. Option C wins nothing without new evidence.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Capability you cannot buy**

You cannot recruit experienced content designers; posts stay vacant for months and services are covered by people working beyond their level. What is the strongest response?

- A) Keep advertising and escalate the recruitment difficulty.
- B) Treat it as capability design rather than recruitment: deploy experienced content designers across services rather than embedding singly, invest in structured development for the people you have, and build entry routes — apprenticeships, secondments, conversion from adjacent roles — while continuing to recruit.
- C) Lower the stated experience requirements.
- D) Use contractors.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** Months of unfilled posts in a competitive market is not a problem more advertising solves. Option B accepts the constraint and redesigns around it. Deploying experienced people across services rather than one-per-service makes scarce expertise go further and — the point specific to this discipline — gives less experienced designers someone to read their drafts, which is the mechanism that develops them. Developing the people you have is the only supply route you fully control. Entry routes address the pipeline rather than competing for the same pool, and content design is unusually well suited to conversion from adjacent roles, since people from operational, policy and communications backgrounds bring knowledge that is hard to teach. Option C is least effective: lowering the requirement without changing the support structure reproduces the current situation and sets people up to fail. Option D leaves when the contract ends and builds nothing.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Strategy meets reality**

Your strategy commits to "one voice across all channels". A review finds the letters, which reach the most excluded users, are produced separately and could not be aligned within two years. What is most effective?

- A) Restate the commitment and press for compliance.
- B) Revise the strategy: replace an unachievable universal with a sequenced commitment naming letters as the priority precisely because they reach the most excluded users, with a realistic horizon and the resource it would need.
- C) Exclude letters from scope.
- D) Escalate the letters team's non-alignment.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** A commitment that cannot be met within two years is not a stretch target; it is a statement that will be quietly ignored, and every commitment beside it loses force. Option B revises the strategy to say something true, and the sequencing rationale is what makes it strong — letters reach the most excluded users, so they are the highest priority rather than the awkward exception, and a strategy saying so is both more honest and more defensible. Naming the resource makes it a proposal rather than an aspiration. Option C is least effective: it solves the embarrassment by excluding the users who most need the commitment, which inverts the purpose of having one. Option A presses for the impossible. Option D treats a structural constraint as another team's compliance failure, which will fail and cost you the relationship you need.

**Question 8 (harder) — Content that may cause harm**

You learn that a page across four services omits that applying may reduce a benefit the user already receives. Correcting it needs policy sign-off. What is most effective?

- A) Add it to the content backlog for the four services.
- B) Raise it immediately with the relevant service owners, stating specifically that users may reduce their income without knowing, ask for an explicit recorded decision on whether the services continue unchanged meanwhile, and have your team draft the wording for policy so sign-off is as fast as possible.
- C) Instruct the four teams to add a sentence based on their understanding.
- D) Raise it at the next governance board.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** Content omitting a financial consequence across four services may cause substantial harm, and weighing that against the time to get sign-off is a decision for people with authority — though raising it precisely and now is entirely yours. Option B does everything in your remit, and three details make it a head's answer: stating the consequence in terms of income prompts action, asking for a recorded decision means the risk is knowingly held rather than drifting, and having your team draft the wording removes the commonest cause of delay, which is that policy has nothing to react to. Option C is least effective: instructing four teams to publish their own accounts of how benefits interact, unapproved, risks four different wrong statements about people's money, and authorising it makes it practice. Option A treats a live harm as backlog. Option D waits for a scheduled meeting.

**Question 9 (harder) — A recurring problem across teams**

Across six teams, content consistently omits timescales. Each has a plausible local reason. What is most effective?

- A) Add timescales to the content checklist.
- B) Establish the common cause — most likely that the standard is silent on what to publish when a timescale is uncertain — and fix it by agreeing a form of words such as "we usually reply within X and will tell you if it will take longer", then negotiating once, on all six teams' behalf, for the figures.
- C) Remind the six teams.
- D) Accept it; some timescales cannot be given.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** Six teams behaving identically for plausible reasons is one systemic cause with six rationalisations. Option B identifies the likely cause and does the two things only you can: supply the form of words that makes an uncertain timescale publishable, and negotiate once with policy rather than leaving six content designers to have the same losing conversation separately. That second element is the substance of leading a discipline, and it is the part that most often does not happen, because each conversation looks local until somebody counts them. Option C is least effective and is the weakest available compliance response. Option A adds a checklist item where teams already know the requirement and cannot meet it. Option D accepts the rationalisation untested — genuinely unknowable timescales are rarer than claimed, and where real, silence remains the wrong answer.

**Question 10 (harder) — Making the case at a board**

A board is considering halving content design resource, on the view that content is writing and can be absorbed by other roles. What is most effective?

- A) Explain the discipline and what content designers do.
- B) Lead with evidence in the board's currency — contact volume attributable to content gaps, calls avoided by specific past changes, the cost per call — then set out what halving the resource would predictably cost, tying each claim to a measurable outcome and naming what you would stop doing.
- C) Point to the service standard's requirements.
- D) Ask the director general to intervene.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A board that believes content is writing has already discounted the premise, and explaining the discipline to people who do not accept the problem exists reliably confirms their view that this is a craft preference. Option B inverts the order: establish the problem in numbers the board uses, then price the proposal. The final clause is what makes it credible — naming what you would stop doing, rather than claiming everything is essential, signals that you have made the trade-off honestly and gives the board a real choice rather than a defensive brief. Option A is least effective precisely because it feels natural. Option C invokes compliance, which wins a minimum and never wins resource. Option D forgoes the relationship and the standing that comes from making the case yourself, and a head of profession who needs rescuing at a board is unlikely to be asked to the next one.

**Question 11 (harder) — A failure in something you approved**

Six months ago you approved a form of words now used across six services. You realise it misstates a deadline in the two services with a different statutory period. What should you do?

- A) Note it for the next review.
- B) Notify all six now, scope precisely which two are affected and which four are not, correct the two immediately, and examine why the approval did not ask whether the deadline varied — then change what the approval process requires.
- C) Amend the wording quietly in the two affected services.
- D) Withdraw the agreed wording from all six.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** A shared form of words in six services means a flaw is potentially six errors, and scoping matters as much as disclosure — four services are correct, and telling them otherwise creates unnecessary work and erodes confidence in shared wording generally, a cost you pay for years. Option B does all four necessary things: notifies, scopes, corrects, and asks the question that prevents the next one, which is why the approval did not check whether the deadline varied by service. Option C is least effective: correcting quietly leaves four services unaware a shared asset had a defect and, more importantly, leaves your approval process unchanged, so the next shared wording will carry the same blind spot. Option A defers a live error about a deadline. Option D discards something working in four services.

**Question 12 (harder) — Cross-government collaboration**

Three departments each explain the same common benefit rule differently, and users move between all three. You have no authority over the other two. What is strongest?

- A) Fix your own department's version.
- B) Convene the three departments' heads of content, establish whether the rule is genuinely the same, and if so propose one tested form of words with the evidence behind it and an honest note on what was and was not tested — accepting that you are building consensus rather than exercising authority.
- C) Ask the cross-government profession board to mandate a common form of words.
- D) Publish your version and invite others to adopt it.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** One rule explained three ways, to users who encounter all three, is precisely the waste cross-government collaboration exists to prevent — and the argument is unusually strong here, because a user who reads two different explanations of the same rule cannot tell which is right, which is worse than either explanation alone. Option B is calibrated to the authority you have: convening is available, mandating is not, and establishing whether the rule is genuinely the same avoids the common failure in which a shared form of words is agreed and then fits nobody because the statutory detail differs. The honest note on what was tested prevents the wording acquiring unearned authority. Option A is least effective, fixing one third of a problem you can see whole. Option C reaches for a mandate before building the case, and boards asked without evidence decline. Option D publishes without the evidence or the caveats.

### Preparation tips

- **Absorb pressure downwards.** If a power differential is above the level of the person facing it, it is yours.
- **Ask what an option does to the system.** An intervention depending on your attention has not solved anything.
- **Lead with evidence in the other person's currency, and name what you would stop.** It makes a resourcing case credible.
- **Negotiate once on everyone's behalf.** Six designers having the same losing conversation is a leadership failure.
- **Supply the wording that makes compliance possible.**
- **Revise a strategy commitment that cannot be met.** An ignored commitment devalues the ones beside it.
- **Scope your own errors precisely.** Four correct services should not be told they are wrong.
- **Convene where you cannot mandate, and share with the caveats.**

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Republishing a strategy nobody uses.**
- **Explaining the discipline to people who have not accepted the problem exists.**
- **Reminding teams of rules they already know and cannot meet.**
- **Conceding to an objection that does not survive examination.** Senior stakeholders learn what works.
- **Excluding from a strategy the users who most need it.**
- **Deferring a live factual error to a scheduled forum.**
- **Authorising teams to publish their own account of something unconfirmed.**
- **Recruiting to fill a gap the conditions created.**

## Conclusion

You have reached the end, and it is worth registering the scale: forty-eight questions across four assessment types, with the reasoning worked through on each. That is a substantial piece of professional preparation and more than most people at your level undertake.

Look back at the ground. In the cognitive section you traced a compliant, well-written page's call volume to something outside the page, diagnosed six teams' missing timescales as one cause with six rationalisations, recognised a heavily used recovery page as a measure of a shared component's failure rate, revised a strategy commitment that could not be met rather than restating it, and treated two unusually strong teams as a source of transferable routine. In the numeric section you annualised before reporting, returned to counts before combining rates, reconstructed an implied cost per call, recognised that a fall in views can be success, and declined to endorse a comparison that would set a precedent against your teams. In the verbal section you separated a conditional prohibition from an absolute one, kept "materially" where others drop it, refused to convert a scope statement into an exemption, and declined to mark True on a claim you would have liked to be true. And in the situational judgement section you took a director general's pressure off a content designer, made a resourcing case in a board's own currency while naming what you would stop, negotiated once with policy on six teams' behalf, and convened three departments you could not compel.

Two threads run through it. The first is whether you know the limits of the evidence and will say so — including, and especially, when the claim would support something you want. The second is whether you act on mechanisms and conditions rather than instances. Between them those are most of what your role consists of. Organisations can write without you. What you supply is content that can be trusted at the strength it is stated, conditions in which that is the normal result, and a practice that outlasts you.

A word about the particular difficulty of your level. Content leadership is vulnerable to being treated as taste, which means your authority rests on evidence more than on craft judgement — and the errors that cost you most are no longer the ones you would notice. They are made fluently, in areas where your experience runs deep enough that checking feels unnecessary, and nobody in your organisation is positioned to catch them. The verbal reasoning section is the clearest demonstration, and the pattern generalises to every judgement you make alone.

If you want to build on this, a few things repay effort more than rereading. Read your services' site search logs for a month. Spend an hour listening to contact centre calls and find the pages that should have answered them. Read every letter and email one of your services sends, in the order a user receives them. Take a legal phrase your organisation has accepted for years and ask the legal team what the requirement actually is. Ask a head of content in another department to critique your strategy as harshly as they would a stranger's. And go and watch whatever your strongest team does that the others do not, before assuming you know what it is.

Finally, for the day itself. An assessment samples your reasoning on one morning under artificial conditions. It is not a verdict on your judgement or your career. Read the stem twice, find the denominator, trust Cannot Say, keep the qualifiers, and remember that at your level the strongest answer is almost always the one honest about uncertainty and aimed at the mechanism rather than the symptom.

Good luck. The profession is stronger for leaders who prepare like this, and so — which is the point of all of it — are the people who read these pages.
