## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive ability assessment measures the exact mental processes needed for your role as a lead digital evaluator. Unlike a generic intelligence test, it uses practical workplace scenarios and data formats drawn from real evaluation work to predict how effectively you will perform on the job. The questions mimic the everyday artefacts you handle — logic models, evaluation matrices, monitoring dashboards, quality assurance logs, and experimental designs — and ask you to reason about them quickly and accurately.

Typical characteristics of this assessment include:

- **Role-targeted content.** Questions replicate the tasks and data formats of senior evaluation work: checking a theory of change for logical gaps, sequencing evaluation activities across a product life cycle, diagnosing why two analytical outputs disagree, and prioritising competing demands across multiple evaluations.
- **Timed format.** You will usually have 15 to 30 minutes to complete the test online, often with around one to two minutes per question. Speed and accuracy are both scored.
- **Objective scoring.** Your results are compared against a benchmark or norm group, typically other senior analytical professionals. Employers often receive a breakdown showing speed versus accuracy rather than a single score.
- **Validated questions.** Items are designed and legally vetted to predict job success without bias, which is why they focus on genuine job content rather than trivia.
- **Practice items.** Most platforms give you short, ungraded practice questions first so you can get used to the interface. Some platforms are adaptive, adjusting difficulty based on your previous answers — so do not be discouraged if the questions seem to get harder; that usually means you are doing well.

For a lead digital evaluator, cognitive ability assessments matter because your role sits at the point where analytical rigour meets delivery pressure. You are the person who must notice that an outcome indicator does not actually measure the intended outcome, that a sampling approach will not support the inference a policy team wants to draw, or that two teams' quality assurance logs are inconsistent. These are cognitive tasks — pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and structured problem solving — performed on evaluation artefacts.

### What it measures for your role

The assessment dimensions map directly onto the skills in your role profile:

- **Logical deduction and pattern recognition** map to your **Evaluation delivery** skill: evaluating digital services using process evaluation, impact evaluation, theory of change, experimentation and economic evaluation all require you to reason about causal chains, spot confounds, and detect patterns in descriptive and inferential analysis.
- **Error checking** maps to your **Quality assurance of data and analysis** skill: setting up tools and systems for assuring data and analysis across multiple teams, and verifying and validating data, depend on rapid, accurate detection of inconsistencies.
- **Prioritisation and planning logic** map to your **Evaluation planning and strategy** and **Monitoring and evaluation across the product life cycle** skills: ensuring plans are appropriate and proportionate, recommending when to move forward and when to stop, and engaging stakeholders at the right stage all require you to sequence and weigh competing considerations under constraint.
- **Structured problem solving** maps to your **Product and service monitoring** skill: defining best practice for measuring indicators, including economic outcomes, and communicating limitations requires you to reason about what a measure can and cannot support.
- **Working with abstractions** maps to your work developing **theories of change**: you must hold a chain of activities, outputs, outcomes and unintended effects in mind and test it for coherence.

### Practice questions

Work through these under timed conditions if you can — aim for around 90 seconds to 2 minutes per question. Full explanations follow each answer; read them even when you answer correctly, because the reasoning method is what transfers to a live assessment.

**Question 1 (easy) — Theory of change sequencing.**
A junior evaluator drafts a theory of change for a new digital licensing service with these five elements, presented out of order:

- A. Applicants complete licence applications online without needing telephone support
- B. Build and launch an online application form with in-line guidance
- C. Reduced administrative cost per licence issued
- D. Increased public trust in the licensing regime
- E. Fewer incomplete applications submitted

Which ordering best represents the conventional logic-model chain from activity through output to intermediate and longer-term outcomes?

1. B → A → E → C → D
2. B → E → A → D → C
3. A → B → E → C → D
4. B → A → D → E → C

**Answer: Option 1 (B → A → E → C → D).**

**Explanation:** A theory of change runs from what you do, to what that produces, to what changes as a result. Building and launching the form (B) is the activity. Applicants completing applications online without telephone support (A) is the direct output of that activity — it describes use of the thing built. Fewer incomplete applications (E) is an intermediate outcome that follows from applicants using the in-line guidance. Reduced administrative cost per licence (C) follows from fewer incomplete applications and reduced telephone demand. Increased public trust (D) is the longest-range, most diffuse outcome and sits at the end of the chain. Option 3 fails because an outcome (A) cannot precede the activity that enables it. Options 2 and 4 place long-range outcomes before the intermediate outcomes that plausibly cause them.

**Question 2 (easy) — Error checking a monitoring table.**
You are quality-assuring a monitoring return before it goes to a programme board. The table reports, for each of four services, the number of completed user transactions and the completion rate:

| Service | Started transactions | Completed transactions | Completion rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alpha | 12,400 | 9,920 | 80% |
| Beta | 8,500 | 6,375 | 75% |
| Gamma | 15,000 | 12,300 | 82% |
| Delta | 9,600 | 7,970 | 87% |

Which row contains an internal inconsistency?

1. Alpha
2. Beta
3. Gamma
4. Delta

**Answer: Option 4 (Delta).**

**Explanation:** Check each rate against the underlying counts. Alpha: 9,920 ÷ 12,400 = 0.80, consistent. Beta: 6,375 ÷ 8,500 = 0.75, consistent. Gamma: 12,300 ÷ 15,000 = 0.82, consistent. Delta: 7,970 ÷ 9,600 = 0.8302…, approximately 83%, not the 87% reported. The reported rate is inconsistent with the counts. In a live assessment, do not calculate every row to full precision: scan for the row where a quick approximation fails. For Delta, 87% of 9,600 would be about 8,350, visibly above the 7,970 reported — a fast way to spot the error. This is exactly the verification-and-validation habit your quality assurance skill describes.

**Question 3 (moderate) — Logical deduction from evaluation findings.**
An impact evaluation of a digital benefits triage tool reports three validated findings:

- All local offices that adopted the triage tool saw reduced average waiting times.
- Some offices that saw reduced average waiting times also saw increased user satisfaction.
- No office with increased user satisfaction reported an increase in staff overtime.

Which one of the following conclusions must be true?

1. Some offices that adopted the triage tool saw increased user satisfaction.
2. All offices that adopted the triage tool avoided increases in staff overtime.
3. Some offices with reduced waiting times did not report an increase in staff overtime.
4. No office that adopted the triage tool reported an increase in staff overtime.

**Answer: Option 3.**

**Explanation:** Work syllogistically. From finding 2, some offices with reduced waiting times had increased satisfaction. From finding 3, every office with increased satisfaction avoided increased overtime. Therefore those particular offices — which have reduced waiting times — did not report increased overtime. That establishes option 3 with certainty. Option 1 is tempting but not guaranteed: the offices with both reduced waiting times and increased satisfaction are not stated to be adopters — reduced waiting times could also occur in non-adopting offices, and "some offices that saw reduced average waiting times" may refer entirely to those. Options 2 and 4 overreach: we know nothing about overtime in adopting offices that did not see increased satisfaction. This mirrors a real evaluator's discipline: conclusions must be no stronger than the evidence strictly supports, a core part of communicating limitations.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Prioritisation across a portfolio.**
You lead evaluation for a portfolio of digital services and manage three evaluators. On Monday morning you face four demands:

- A. A minister's office requests, by Thursday, a summary of the evidence on whether a live service reduced fraud.
- B. A service team entering private beta next month needs its evaluation plan agreed before its next gate review in three weeks.
- C. A junior evaluator has flagged that survey data collected last week may include personal data gathered without the correct privacy notice.
- D. A quarterly monitoring dashboard is due for routine refresh by Friday.

Which is the most defensible order in which to initiate action?

1. A, B, C, D
2. C, A, B, D
3. C, D, A, B
4. A, C, D, B

**Answer: Option 2 (C, A, B, D).**

**Explanation:** Prioritisation questions reward reasoning about severity, urgency, and reversibility. Item C is a potential data protection and ethics breach: it is severe (legal and public-trust consequences), it may worsen with delay (further processing of unlawfully collected data compounds the problem), and your role profile makes you responsible for overseeing compliance with data ethics standards and legislation. Initiating action on C first — pausing use of the affected data and notifying the right people — is quick and containment-focused; initiating action does not mean personally resolving it before doing anything else. Item A has a hard ministerial deadline of Thursday and high consequence, so it comes next. Item B matters but has three weeks of runway and can be scheduled and partly delegated. Item D is routine, lowest consequence, and can be delegated or, if necessary, briefly delayed. Option 1 fails because it defers a live compliance risk behind ordinary work. Options 3 and 4 misplace the routine dashboard ahead of higher-stakes items.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Spotting a flawed indicator.**
A service team proposes measuring the success of a new online appointment-booking service using this indicator: "Number of appointments booked through the online channel per month." The intended outcome in the theory of change is "users can access appointments more easily." What is the most important logical weakness in this indicator as a measure of the intended outcome?

1. The indicator is not expressed as a percentage.
2. Rising online bookings could reflect channel shift or population growth rather than easier access overall.
3. Monthly reporting is too infrequent for an agile team.
4. The indicator ignores staff satisfaction.

**Answer: Option 2.**

**Explanation:** The indicator counts activity in one channel, but the outcome concerns ease of access overall. Online bookings could rise while total access worsens — for example, if the telephone channel is withdrawn and users who cannot use digital services are excluded, online volume rises while access for some groups falls. Equally, volume can rise simply because the eligible population grew. A defensible measurement approach would triangulate: total bookings across channels, time-to-appointment, failure/abandonment rates, and evidence from qualitative research with users of assisted digital routes. Option 1 is cosmetic; a percentage of the wrong thing is still the wrong thing. Option 3 is a practical preference, not a validity flaw. Option 4 raises a different outcome, not a weakness in measuring this one. Defining best practice for measuring indicators and communicating their limitations is a named responsibility at your level.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Experimental logic.**
A delivery team wants to test whether a redesigned notification email increases the proportion of users who complete an identity verification step. They propose sending the new email to all users in March and comparing completion with February's rate. As the evaluation lead, you point out the design's central weakness. What is it?

1. The sample size will be too small.
2. Any February-to-March difference confounds the email change with seasonal and other time-varying factors.
3. Email is the wrong medium for notifications.
4. Completion rate is a binary measure and cannot be analysed statistically.

**Answer: Option 2.**

**Explanation:** A before-and-after comparison without a concurrent control group cannot separate the effect of the new email from anything else that changed between February and March: seasonal demand patterns, media coverage, policy changes, service performance, or the composition of users. The remedy is a concurrent randomised comparison — send the new email to a random subset in March and the old email to the rest, then compare like with like over the same period. Option 1 is unsupported: an all-user rollout typically has a large sample; the problem is bias, not variance. Option 3 is outside the evidence. Option 4 is simply false — binary outcomes are routinely analysed (proportions, confidence intervals, regression). Recognising that confounding, not sample size, is the fatal flaw in uncontrolled pre-post designs is bread-and-butter reasoning for the experimentation element of your evaluation delivery skill.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Pattern recognition in data quality logs.**
Your team maintains a weekly quality assurance log for a data pipeline feeding an evaluation. Over eight weeks, the number of records failing validation was: 120, 118, 124, 119, 240, 122, 121, 243. Which interpretation best fits this pattern?

1. Data quality is steadily deteriorating.
2. The failures are random noise around a stable level.
3. There is a recurring intermittent fault roughly every fourth week, superimposed on a stable baseline.
4. The validation rules were permanently tightened in week five.

**Answer: Option 3.**

**Explanation:** Separate the series into its structure: weeks 1–4 and 6–7 cluster tightly around 120, while weeks 5 and 8 jump to roughly double (240, 243). That is not steady deterioration (option 1) — the series returns to baseline after each spike. It is not pure noise (option 2) — the spikes are far outside the tight 118–124 band and are similar in size to each other. A permanent rule change in week five (option 4) would keep failures elevated in weeks six and seven, which it does not. The best-fitting hypothesis is an intermittent, recurring fault — for example, a monthly batch process that clashes with validation every fourth week. In practice you would test this hypothesis against pipeline schedules. This is the pattern-recognition discipline behind setting up tools and systems for effectively assuring data and analysis across multiple teams: good QA systems are designed to make exactly this kind of signature visible.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Deduction about evaluation scope.**
Government guidance adopted by your organisation states: "Every service with annual spend above £5 million must have an impact evaluation. Any service handling special category data must have a process evaluation, regardless of spend." You review four services:

- Service W: £7 million annual spend; no special category data.
- Service X: £3 million annual spend; handles special category data.
- Service Y: £6 million annual spend; handles special category data.
- Service Z: £2 million annual spend; no special category data.

Which services are required to have both an impact evaluation and a process evaluation?

1. W and Y
2. Y only
3. X and Y
4. W, X and Y

**Answer: Option 2 (Y only).**

**Explanation:** Apply each rule as a conditional. The impact-evaluation rule triggers on spend above £5 million: W (£7m) and Y (£6m) qualify; X and Z do not. The process-evaluation rule triggers on special category data: X and Y qualify; W and Z do not. The question asks which services require both, so take the intersection: only Y appears in both lists. A common error is to answer option 1 by anchoring on the spend rule and assuming the process requirement follows spend, or option 3 by anchoring on the data rule. Note also what the rules do not say: they set minimum requirements, so a proportionate evaluation plan might still choose to add a process evaluation to W — but the question asks what is required, and precision about requirement versus discretion is exactly what "ensuring plans are appropriate and proportionate" demands.

**Question 9 (hard) — Diagnosing conflicting findings.**
Two analyses of the same digital service reach different conclusions. Analysis One, using the service's management information covering all users, finds average task completion time fell by 15% after a redesign. Analysis Two, using a post-redesign survey of users who contacted the support desk, finds most respondents say the service became slower and harder to use. Both analyses are technically competent. What is the most likely explanation for the disagreement?

1. One team must have fabricated its results.
2. The survey respondents are a non-representative subgroup — people who contacted support are disproportionately those who struggled.
3. Management information can never be trusted over user opinion.
4. Task completion time and perceived ease of use are the same construct measured differently, so one dataset must be corrupted.

**Answer: Option 2.**

**Explanation:** The two analyses use different populations and different constructs. Analysis One measures actual completion time across all users. Analysis Two samples only users who contacted support — a group selected precisely because something went wrong for them — and measures perception. Selection bias fully explains the disagreement: the redesign may have made the service faster for the majority while creating genuine difficulty for a minority, and that minority dominates support contacts. As a lead evaluator you would treat both findings as valid about their own populations, and commission targeted research on the struggling subgroup. Option 1 leaps to misconduct without evidence. Option 3 replaces one bias with a prejudice. Option 4 is confused: completion time and perceived ease are related but distinct constructs, and difference between them does not imply corruption. Analysing and interpreting data you have collected alongside data from other sources — and reconciling them — is a named part of your evaluation delivery skill.

**Question 10 (hard) — Proportionality reasoning.**
You must advise on evaluation approaches for two initiatives. Initiative One is a £250,000 pilot of a chatbot on one low-risk information page, easily reversible, with results needed in three months. Initiative Two is a £40 million multi-year programme replacing the case-management system for a safety-critical inspection regime. Which pairing of evaluation approaches is most proportionate?

1. One: multi-year randomised controlled trial with economic evaluation. Two: quick before-and-after dashboard review.
2. One: light-touch monitoring with a rapid A/B test. Two: comprehensive multi-method evaluation combining process, impact and economic evaluation with staged reporting.
3. Both: identical full-scale impact evaluations, because standards must be uniform.
4. One: no evaluation, because the spend is small. Two: process evaluation only, because impact is hard to measure.

**Answer: Option 2.**

**Explanation:** Proportionality means matching evaluative effort to the scale, risk, irreversibility and evidence needs of the decision the evaluation supports. Initiative One is small, reversible and time-constrained: a rapid A/B test on the page plus light-touch monitoring answers the decision-relevant question ("does the chatbot help users?") within the window. Initiative Two is high-spend, high-risk and safety-relevant: it warrants the full toolkit — process evaluation (is it being implemented as intended?), impact evaluation (is it delivering outcomes, including safety outcomes?), and economic evaluation (is it value for money?) — with staged reporting so findings can influence the programme while it is still adaptable. Option 1 inverts proportionality. Option 3 confuses uniform standards (rigour appropriate to context) with uniform designs. Option 4 abandons evaluation exactly where learning is cheapest (One) and ducks the impact question exactly where it matters most (Two). This is the core judgement in "ensure plans for monitoring and evaluation of products and services are appropriate and proportionate."

**Question 11 (hard) — Error checking analytical logic.**
A report drafted by one of your evaluators states: "Users who opted in to email reminders were 30% more likely to renew their registration on time than users who did not opt in. Therefore, introducing email reminders for all users will increase on-time renewal by 30%." Identify the principal flaw in this inference.

1. The percentage should have been expressed in percentage points.
2. Self-selected opt-in users likely differ from non-opt-in users in motivation and organisation, so the comparison overstates the causal effect of reminders — and the effect on those who did not choose reminders may be quite different.
3. Email reminders are known to have no effect on behaviour.
4. The sample includes too many renewals to be statistically valid.

**Answer: Option 2.**

**Explanation:** The comparison is between self-selected groups. People who opt in to reminders are plausibly more organised, more engaged, or more motivated to renew — characteristics that independently raise on-time renewal. The observed 30% gap therefore bundles the causal effect of reminders with pre-existing differences (confounding by self-selection), and generalising it to users who chose not to opt in assumes they would respond the same way as volunteers, which is doubtful. The defensible next step is a randomised rollout to estimate the true effect. Option 1 is a presentational quibble, not the principal flaw. Option 3 asserts outside knowledge as fact. Option 4 is nonsense — larger samples do not invalidate analysis. Catching this class of error before publication is precisely why your role requires you to coach and mentor others in assuring data and analysis.

**Question 12 (hard) — Portfolio pattern recognition.**
Across six evaluations you oversee, you notice the following: evaluations with embedded evaluators (working inside the delivery team) delivered interim findings on time in 5 of 5 sprints reviewed; evaluations run at arm's length delivered interim findings on time in 2 of 6 sprints reviewed. However, the two arm's-length evaluations cover the most politically contested services, and their delayed findings were the only ones to report significant negative results. Which conclusion is the most defensible?

1. Embedded evaluation should be mandated for all future evaluations because it is faster.
2. Arm's-length evaluators are less competent.
3. Timeliness differs by model, but the pattern is confounded — contested services may cause both the arm's-length choice and the delay — so investigate the mechanism before changing policy.
4. Negative findings cause delays.

**Answer: Option 3.**

**Explanation:** The data show an association between evaluation model and timeliness, but the assignment of model to service was not random: contested, complex services were given arm's-length evaluation, presumably for independence. Contestedness could cause both the choice of model and the delays (longer clearance, more challenge from stakeholders, more careful analysis of negative results). Mandating embedded evaluation (option 1) on this evidence could sacrifice independence exactly where it matters most, and would generalise from five and six observations. Option 2 attributes to competence what the confound explains at least as well. Option 4 reverses plausible causation without evidence — although the co-occurrence of negative findings and delay is worth investigating as a possible sign of clearance pressure, which would be a serious standards issue. The lead evaluator's move is option 3: hold the pattern, hypothesise mechanisms, gather evidence, then act. This reflects both your quality assurance responsibilities and your duty to ensure high evaluation standards are set and met across the wider team.

**Question 13 (hard) — Problem solving under constraint.**
Ministers announce that a digital service you evaluate will be expanded nationally in six months. Your impact evaluation, designed around a 12-month stepped-wedge rollout, will now lose its comparison group halfway through. Your three evaluators propose different responses. Which proposal shows the strongest evaluative reasoning?

1. Abandon the impact evaluation, since the design is broken, and rely on user satisfaction scores.
2. Keep the original analysis plan and report as if the design had completed, noting the change in a footnote.
3. Re-plan now: bring forward comparative analysis to extract maximum causal evidence from the six months of preserved design, pre-specify the revised analysis, and supplement the post-expansion period with alternative methods such as interrupted time-series and synthetic comparison.
4. Delay the national expansion by lobbying ministers until the evaluation completes.

**Answer: Option 3.**

**Explanation:** Real evaluations rarely survive contact with political timetables intact, and lead-level skill lies in salvaging rigour rather than choosing between purity and surrender. Option 3 does three defensible things: it exploits the six months in which the stepped-wedge comparison remains valid; it pre-specifies the revised analysis (guarding against the suspicion of moving goalposts); and it lines up quasi-experimental methods — interrupted time-series, synthetic comparators — for the period after the comparison group disappears. Option 1 throws away attainable causal evidence and substitutes a weak proxy. Option 2 is worse than weak — reporting as though an interrupted design completed misrepresents the evidence and breaches the standards you are responsible for upholding. Option 4 mistakes the evaluator's role: you advise on evidence implications of the timetable and communicate the trade-offs; the decision on timing belongs to ministers. Considering wider policy and practical constraints that affect evaluation strategy is explicitly part of your planning and strategy skill.

### Preparation tips

- **Practise on your own artefacts.** Before the assessment, spend time critically reading a theory of change, a monitoring dashboard, and a QA log from your own portfolio. Ask of each: what would make this internally inconsistent? The assessment rewards exactly that habit.
- **Rehearse fast arithmetic checks.** Many error-checking items yield to approximation (as in Question 2). Practise estimating percentages of round numbers quickly so you can scan rather than compute.
- **Learn the classic inference traps.** Selection bias, confounding, pre-post designs without controls, and over-generalisation appear repeatedly because they appear repeatedly in real analytical work. If you can name the trap, you can usually spot the answer.
- **Manage the clock deliberately.** At senior level the temptation is to reason each item to certainty. Set a personal limit per question; flag and move on. An adaptive test in particular rewards steady progress over perfection on any single item.
- **Do the practice items.** Ungraded practice questions exist so the interface does not cost you marks. Use them to settle your nerves and check the calculator, flagging and navigation functions.
- **Arrive fresh.** Cognitive assessments are genuinely sensitive to fatigue. Treat the sitting as you would a high-stakes stakeholder presentation: rested, hydrated, and free of interruptions.

### Common pitfalls

- **Reading the artefact instead of the question.** Senior evaluators habitually critique everything they read. In a timed test, first identify precisely what is asked, then read the stimulus for that purpose only.
- **Importing outside knowledge.** You may know how a real booking service behaved; the question is about the one described. Reason strictly from the material given.
- **Time sink questions.** Getting stuck on one multi-stage item instead of banking easier marks elsewhere is the most common cause of underperformance, even among strong candidates. Skip and return.
- **Over-calculating.** Performing exact computation when an estimate distinguishes the options wastes your scarcest resource. Look at how far apart the answer options are before deciding how precisely to work.
- **Second-guessing adaptive difficulty.** If questions feel harder as you progress, that is often the platform responding to your success. Do not let perceived difficulty dent your confidence.
- **Assuming seniority exempts you from practice.** Familiarity with evaluation concepts does not automatically translate to speed under test conditions. A few timed practice sessions convert your expertise into assessment performance.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment evaluates your ability to analyse, interpret, and make sound decisions using numerical data of the kind your role genuinely handles. It mirrors the data-handling demands of leading evaluation work: reading tables of performance metrics, computing percentage changes and ratios, working with budgets and staffing figures, and interpreting the outputs of quantitative analysis. The emphasis is on applied business mathematics — percentages, ratios, rates, weighted averages, and cost-benefit style calculations — rather than abstract formulae.

Typical characteristics of this assessment include:

- **Real-world data formats.** Question inputs are tables, charts, budget lines, survey summaries and dashboards, presented much as they arrive on your desk.
- **Applied arithmetic.** Expect percentages and percentage-point differences, ratios and rates, proportions, growth calculations, unit costs, and simple economic comparisons — not calculus.
- **Calculator friendly.** Most assessments allow or provide an on-screen calculator; the test prioritises interpretation and method over mental arithmetic.
- **Strict time limits.** Typically around one to two minutes per question, requiring quick, accurate extraction of the right numbers from the stimulus.
- **Benchmarked scoring.** Your accuracy and speed are compared against a norm group of professionals at a similar level.

For a lead digital evaluator, numeric reasoning is not a peripheral skill — it is the substance of your credibility. When you influence senior stakeholders over data and analysis approaches, present findings to boards, or challenge a team's economic case, your ability to move fluently and correctly through numbers under pressure is on display. A misread percentage or a conflated rate-versus-count comparison in a live meeting can undo months of careful work; this assessment simulates exactly that pressure in a controlled setting.

### What it measures for your role

The assessment dimensions map onto your role profile skills as follows:

- **Interpreting tables, rates and percentages** maps to **Evaluation delivery**: collecting and analysing quantitative data, and using descriptive and inferential analysis, requires effortless fluency with completion rates, uptake percentages, response rates and effect sizes.
- **Budget and resource calculations** map to **Evaluation planning and strategy**: influencing stakeholders to secure resources for evaluation projects means being able to build, defend and stress-test costings at speed.
- **Economic measures and unit costs** map to **Product and service monitoring**: defining best practice for measuring indicators, including economic outcomes, requires comfort with cost per transaction, benefit-cost ratios and savings projections — and with communicating their limitations.
- **Cross-checking figures for consistency** maps to **Quality assurance of data and analysis**: verifying and validating data across multiple teams is, at its core, structured numerical cross-checking.
- **Translating numbers into decisions** maps to **Communicating analysis and insight** and **Monitoring and evaluation across the product life cycle**: recommending when to move forward and when to stop is often a numerical judgement — does the observed effect justify the cost of continuing?

### Practice questions

Use a calculator if you wish, as most platforms allow one. Work to about two minutes per question. Every explanation shows the arithmetic in full — study the method even where you got the right answer, because method is what survives time pressure.

**Question 1 (easy) — Percentage uptake.**
A digital renewals service was used by 43,700 of the 115,000 people who renewed a registration last quarter; the remainder renewed by post or telephone. What percentage renewed digitally?

1. 34%
2. 38%
3. 40%
4. 43.7%

**Answer: Option 2 (38%).**

**Explanation:** Divide the digital renewals by the total: 43,700 ÷ 115,000 = 0.38, which is 38%. A quick sanity check: 40% of 115,000 would be 46,000, which is above 43,700, so the answer must be a little under 40% — ruling out options 3 and 4 instantly. Option 4 is a distractor for candidates who read "43,700" and echo its leading digits. Building the habit of a bounding estimate ("what would 40% be?") before computing protects you against exactly this class of trap.

**Question 2 (easy) — Survey response rate and counts.**
Your team sent a post-transaction survey to 12,500 service users. The response rate was 16%. Of respondents, 65% rated the service "good" or "very good". How many respondents gave one of those two ratings?

1. 1,300
2. 2,000
3. 1,690
4. 8,125

**Answer: Option 1 (1,300).**

**Explanation:** Work in two steps. Respondents: 12,500 × 0.16 = 2,000. Positive ratings: 2,000 × 0.65 = 1,300. Option 2 stops after the first step. Option 4 applies 65% to all users surveyed rather than to respondents — the classic base error, and precisely the mistake you train your evaluators to catch, since only 2,000 people expressed any opinion at all. Option 3 (13.52% of 12,500) comes from multiplying the two percentages incorrectly (0.16 × 0.65 = 0.104; 12,500 × 0.104 = 1,300 — done correctly this shortcut also gives option 1). Note for interpretation, though never for the arithmetic itself: a 16% response rate raises non-response bias questions that a lead evaluator would flag when communicating these findings.

**Question 3 (easy) — Percentage change in a metric.**
Average time to complete an online application fell from 14 minutes 10 seconds to 11 minutes 20 seconds after a redesign. What is the percentage reduction, to the nearest whole per cent?

1. 15%
2. 20%
3. 25%
4. 27%

**Answer: Option 2 (20%).**

**Explanation:** Convert to consistent units first — seconds: 14 min 10 s = 850 s; 11 min 20 s = 680 s. Reduction: 850 − 680 = 170 s. Percentage reduction: 170 ÷ 850 = 0.20 = 20%. The unit conversion is the whole game here: treating "14.10" and "11.20" as decimal minutes gives 2.90 ÷ 14.10 ≈ 20.6%, which happens to be close in this case, but the habit is dangerous — with different figures it produces answers that are cleanly wrong. Mixed-unit time data appears constantly in digital service metrics, so make base-60 conversion automatic.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Evaluation budget shares.**
Your evaluation programme budget is £480,000 for the year, allocated as: impact evaluation 45%, process evaluation 25%, economic evaluation 15%, and the remainder to quality assurance and dissemination. Mid-year, the impact evaluation needs an extra £24,000, to be found by reducing the QA and dissemination allocation. What does QA and dissemination's share of the total budget become?

1. 10%
2. 12%
3. 7%
4. 15%

**Answer: Option 1 (10%).**

**Explanation:** First find the original remainder share: 100% − 45% − 25% − 15% = 15%, so QA and dissemination initially holds 0.15 × £480,000 = £72,000. Removing £24,000 leaves £72,000 − £24,000 = £48,000. As a share of the unchanged £480,000 total: 48,000 ÷ 480,000 = 0.10 = 10%. Option 4 is the original share for candidates who miss the reallocation; option 2 comes from subtracting £24,000 as if it were 3 percentage points of a £800,000 budget or from arithmetic slips. Note the substantive point a lead evaluator would raise alongside the arithmetic: quietly halving quality assurance funding to prop up one workstream is exactly the kind of trade-off that should be surfaced to stakeholders, not buried in a budget line.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Cost per transaction comparison.**
A monitoring dashboard shows the following for two channels of the same service last month:

| Channel | Transactions | Total operating cost |
|---|---|---|
| Digital | 62,000 | £186,000 |
| Telephone | 9,500 | £142,500 |

How many times more expensive is a telephone transaction than a digital transaction?

1. 3 times
2. 4 times
3. 5 times
4. 6 times

**Answer: Option 3 (5 times).**

**Explanation:** Compute unit costs. Digital: £186,000 ÷ 62,000 = £3.00 per transaction. Telephone: £142,500 ÷ 9,500 = £15.00 per transaction. Ratio: 15 ÷ 3 = 5. The distractors reward miscomputations such as comparing total costs (£142,500 vs £186,000, which would suggest telephone is cheaper) — a reminder that totals and unit rates answer different questions. When you define best practice for measuring economic outcomes, this distinction between aggregate spend and unit cost is one you will make repeatedly for stakeholders, along with its limitations: unit cost alone says nothing about who relies on the telephone channel and why, so a channel-shift recommendation needs equity analysis alongside the ratio.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Weighted average across services.**
You oversee evaluations for three services. Their user satisfaction scores and monthly user volumes are: Service A, 72% satisfaction, 40,000 users; Service B, 85% satisfaction, 10,000 users; Service C, 64% satisfaction, 50,000 users. What is the volume-weighted average satisfaction across the portfolio?

1. 73.7%
2. 69.3%
3. 70.5%
4. 74.0%

**Answer: Option 2 (69.3%).**

**Explanation:** Weight each score by its user volume. Weighted sum: (0.72 × 40,000) + (0.85 × 10,000) + (0.64 × 50,000) = 28,800 + 8,500 + 32,000 = 69,300. Total users: 40,000 + 10,000 + 50,000 = 100,000. Weighted average: 69,300 ÷ 100,000 = 69.3%. The main distractor is option 1: the simple (unweighted) mean, (72 + 85 + 64) ÷ 3 = 73.7%, which ignores the fact that the services differ five-fold in size. The key discriminating insight is that the weighted answer must sit well below the simple mean, because the largest service (C) has the lowest score — so options 1, 3 and 4 are all too high, and you can select option 2 from that reasoning alone before confirming with arithmetic. Portfolio-level reporting is a lead responsibility, and unweighted averages that flatter a portfolio are among the most common errors you will catch when assuring analysis across teams.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Sample size and subgroup counts.**
An impact evaluation plans a survey with a target of 1,200 completed responses, sampled proportionately to service usage: 55% working-age adults, 30% pensioners, 15% business users. Early fieldwork achieves only 900 completes, with the same proportions. How many more pensioner responses are still needed to hit the pensioner target?

1. 90
2. 135
3. 270
4. 360

**Answer: Option 1 (90).**

**Explanation:** Pensioner target: 0.30 × 1,200 = 360 responses. Achieved so far: 0.30 × 900 = 270. Shortfall: 360 − 270 = 90. Option 3 is the achieved count, option 4 the target — both are way-stations that a rushed candidate might select. The general method for shortfall questions is target minus achieved, with both computed on the correct bases. Note the evaluator's supplementary observation: because proportions have held at 55/30/15, the shortfall is spread evenly and no differential boost is needed; had pensioners under-responded relative to their 30% share, you would need disproportionate follow-up or weighting at analysis, a point worth making when you coach teams on fieldwork monitoring.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Ratios in resourcing.**
Your evaluation team's capacity model assumes a ratio of one senior evaluator to three evaluations, and one evaluator to two evaluations, with each evaluation needing exactly one senior evaluator (shared across three) and one dedicated share of an evaluator (shared across two). You currently run 12 evaluations. The portfolio will grow to 18 evaluations next year. How many additional staff (seniors plus evaluators) do you need, assuming current staff are exactly at capacity?

1. 2 seniors and 3 evaluators
2. 3 seniors and 2 evaluators
3. 2 seniors and 6 evaluators
4. 6 staff in any mix

**Answer: Option 1 (2 seniors and 3 evaluators).**

**Explanation:** Compute requirements at each scale. At 12 evaluations: seniors needed = 12 ÷ 3 = 4; evaluators needed = 12 ÷ 2 = 6. At 18 evaluations: seniors = 18 ÷ 3 = 6; evaluators = 18 ÷ 2 = 9. Additional need: 6 − 4 = 2 seniors and 9 − 6 = 3 evaluators. Option 3 doubles the evaluator increment by dividing by the wrong ratio; option 4 gets the total (5, not 6) wrong and ignores the mix, which matters — grade mix drives both cost and capability. This is the arithmetic core of "influence stakeholders to secure resources for evaluation": you cannot negotiate credibly for headcount without being fast and sure on the ratios behind your ask.

**Question 9 (hard) — Benefit-cost ratio.**
An economic evaluation of a digital service estimates total discounted benefits of £8.4 million over five years: £5.1 million in reduced processing costs, £2.2 million in reduced error correction, and £1.1 million in time savings to users. Total discounted costs are £2.4 million. The permanent secretary asks: "What is the benefit-cost ratio, and what does it become if we exclude user time savings, which the finance team regards as non-cashable?"

1. 3.5 including user time; 3.0 excluding it
2. 3.5 including user time; 2.9 excluding it
3. 2.9 including user time; 2.5 excluding it
4. 3.0 including user time; 2.6 excluding it

**Answer: Option 1 (3.5 including; 3.0 excluding).**

**Explanation:** BCR = total benefits ÷ total costs. Including user time: 8.4 ÷ 2.4 = 3.5. Excluding user time savings: benefits become 8.4 − 1.1 = £7.3 million; 7.3 ÷ 2.4 = 3.0417 ≈ 3.0. Option 2's second figure (2.9) comes from subtracting 1.1 from the ratio's numerator incorrectly or rounding harshly; options 3 and 4 mangle the base ratio. Two lead-level observations belong with the arithmetic. First, always present the cashable/non-cashable split transparently, as here — it is exactly the kind of limitation you are responsible for communicating. Second, note that the ratio remains comfortably above 1 on either basis, so the investment case is robust to the finance team's stricter convention; showing a conclusion is insensitive to a contested assumption is one of the most persuasive moves in stakeholder discussions.

**Question 10 (hard) — Interpreting an A/B test result.**
An experiment randomly assigned 40,000 users to a new appointment-reminder flow and 40,000 to the existing flow. In the new flow, 31,200 attended their appointment; in the existing flow, 30,000 attended. A colleague summarises: "Attendance improved by 3 percentage points, a 4% relative improvement, preventing about 1,200 missed appointments in the trial." Which parts of this summary are numerically correct?

1. All three claims
2. Only the percentage-point claim
3. The percentage-point and relative-improvement claims, but not the missed-appointments figure
4. None of the claims

**Answer: Option 1 (all three claims).**

**Explanation:** Check each claim. Attendance rates: new flow 31,200 ÷ 40,000 = 78%; existing flow 30,000 ÷ 40,000 = 75%. Difference: 78 − 75 = 3 percentage points — correct. Relative improvement: 3 ÷ 75 = 0.04 = 4% — correct. Missed appointments prevented: the new-flow group had 40,000 − 31,200 = 8,800 non-attenders; had they attended at the control rate, non-attenders would have numbered 40,000 − 30,000 = 10,000; the difference is 10,000 − 8,800 = 1,200 — correct. This question rewards candidates who understand that "percentage point" and "per cent" are different claims that can both be true, and who can move between rates and counts on the same base. Presenting all three framings — points, relative change, and absolute numbers affected — is best practice when you communicate analysis to mixed audiences, because each framing guards against misreading of the others.

**Question 11 (hard) — Unit cost with fixed and variable components.**
An evaluation costs £60,000 in fixed setup (design, ethics review, instruments) plus £45 per completed qualitative interview transcribed and analysed. The current plan is 200 interviews. A stakeholder proposes doubling to 400 interviews "since the cost per interview will fall". What is the all-in cost per interview under each plan, and is the stakeholder's reasoning arithmetically right?

1. £345 at 200; £195 at 400; yes, unit cost falls
2. £345 at 200; £345 at 400; no, unit cost is constant
3. £300 at 200; £150 at 400; yes, unit cost falls
4. £345 at 200; £195 at 400; no, unit cost rises

**Answer: Option 1 (£345 at 200; £195 at 400; yes).**

**Explanation:** At 200 interviews: total cost = £60,000 + (200 × £45) = £60,000 + £9,000 = £69,000; unit cost = 69,000 ÷ 200 = £345. At 400: total = £60,000 + (400 × £45) = £60,000 + £18,000 = £78,000; unit cost = 78,000 ÷ 400 = £195. The stakeholder is arithmetically right — spreading fixed costs over more units always lowers average cost — but a lead evaluator should immediately add the design point the arithmetic conceals: total spend still rises by £9,000, and the marginal analytical value of interviews 201–400 is usually far below that of the first 200, because qualitative research reaches thematic saturation. Cheaper per unit is not the same as worth doing. Being able to concede the arithmetic while contesting the inference is a hallmark of credible senior challenge, and it is the kind of proportionality judgement your planning and strategy skill demands.

**Question 12 (hard) — Reading a performance table under time pressure.**
Quarterly data for a digital service you evaluate:

| Quarter | Users (thousands) | Completions (thousands) | Support tickets (thousands) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Q1 | 180 | 126 | 27.0 |
| Q2 | 200 | 144 | 28.0 |
| Q3 | 220 | 165 | 26.4 |
| Q4 | 250 | 195 | 25.0 |

Which quarter had the highest completion rate, and which had the lowest support tickets per thousand users?

1. Q4 for both
2. Q4 highest completion; Q3 lowest tickets per thousand users
3. Q3 highest completion; Q4 lowest tickets per thousand users
4. Q2 for both

**Answer: Option 1 (Q4 for both).**

**Explanation:** Completion rates: Q1 = 126 ÷ 180 = 70%; Q2 = 144 ÷ 200 = 72%; Q3 = 165 ÷ 220 = 75%; Q4 = 195 ÷ 250 = 78%. Q4 is highest. Tickets per thousand users: Q1 = 27,000 ÷ 180 = 150; Q2 = 28,000 ÷ 200 = 140; Q3 = 26,400 ÷ 220 = 120; Q4 = 25,000 ÷ 250 = 100. Q4 is lowest. The trap is option 2, chosen by candidates who compare raw ticket counts (where Q3's 26.4 might look like it beats Q4's 25.0 only after checking — and Q4's is in fact lower anyway) or who compute one ratio and guess the other. Both quantities are ratios; neither can be read straight from the columns. Notice the strategic story the numbers tell — improving completion and falling per-user support demand as volume grows — the kind of compelling trend narrative your communicating-analysis skill asks you to construct, always with the caveat that association across four quarters is not proof the service improvements caused it.

**Question 13 (hard) — Compound change in an economic outcome.**
Error-correction costs for a service were £2.0 million in the year before a digital replacement launched. Evaluation evidence shows costs then fell by 20% in year one and a further 15% (from the year-one level) in year two. The business case had promised a 32% total reduction by end of year two. Did the service beat its target, and what were year-two costs?

1. Yes — costs fell 35% to £1.30 million
2. Yes — costs fell 32% exactly, to £1.36 million
3. No — costs fell 35% but the target was compound
4. Yes — costs fell 32% to £1.30 million

**Answer: Option 2 (Yes — costs fell 32% exactly, to £1.36 million).**

Check the arithmetic before accepting any option: year one: £2.0m × (1 − 0.20) = £1.60m. Year two: £1.60m × (1 − 0.15) = £1.36m. Total reduction: (2.00 − 1.36) ÷ 2.00 = 0.64 ÷ 2.00 = 32%. So costs fell exactly 32%, to £1.36 million, meeting the target precisely. Option 1's "35%" is the classic additive error (20% + 15% = 35%) with a wrong cost figure (£1.30m) to match.

**Explanation:** Sequential percentage changes compound; they do not add. A 20% fall followed by a 15% fall multiplies: (0.80 × 0.85) = 0.68 of the original, a 32% total reduction, and £2.0m × 0.68 = £1.36m. The additive shortcut (35%) overstates the reduction because the second percentage applies to an already-reduced base. This question is deliberately structured to punish candidates who pattern-match "the first option asserting success" without computing: in a live assessment, always verify the number as well as the direction. When you assure analysis across teams, compounding errors in savings trajectories are among the most frequent and most consequential mistakes in business-case monitoring, because they quietly inflate promised benefits year after year.

### Preparation tips

- **Drill the core moves.** Percentages of quantities, percentage change, percentage points versus per cent, ratios, unit costs, weighted averages, and compound change cover the overwhelming majority of items. Ten minutes of daily drill for a fortnight will make each move automatic.
- **Estimate before you calculate.** Bound the answer first ("it must be a bit under 40%") so distractors that violate the bound disappear. This is faster and it protects you from keying errors.
- **Read the column headers and units every time.** "Thousands", "per month", "per user" — scale qualifiers and rate bases are where test designers hide difficulty, just as real dashboards do.
- **Use the calculator for arithmetic, not for method.** Decide the operations on paper logic first, then compute. Candidates who explore with the calculator burn time.
- **Practise with your own dashboards.** Take a real monitoring pack from your portfolio and ask rapid-fire questions of it: unit costs, quarter-on-quarter changes, weighted rates. The content of this assessment is your day job at speed.
- **Keep perspective on nerves.** At lead level you interpret numbers in front of boards routinely; the test is a quieter version of the same task. Treat it as evidence-gathering about your own fluency, which is a very evaluator way to feel calm.

### Common pitfalls

- **Misreading chart and table labels.** Confusing monthly with annual data, or missing "in thousands", produces answers that are exactly a scale factor wrong — and test designers include those distractors deliberately.
- **Base errors in percentages.** Applying a percentage to the wrong denominator (all users instead of respondents; original instead of reduced cost) is the single most common numeric error in evaluation reporting and in tests alike.
- **Adding sequential percentages.** Compound changes multiply. If two changes are applied one after another, resist the sum.
- **Confusing counts with rates.** A channel or quarter can lead on raw volume while trailing on rate. Decide which the question asks before touching the data.
- **Over-calculating.** If the options are far apart, an estimate suffices; full-precision computation is a time sink. Match precision to the gap between options.
- **Getting stuck on one multi-stage item.** A hard budget or compounding question can eat five minutes. Flag it, bank the easier marks, return if time allows.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment evaluates how well you understand, analyse, and draw correct conclusions from written material of the kind your role genuinely demands: government evaluation guidance, data ethics policies, evaluation reports, stakeholder correspondence, and publication protocols. It mirrors the exact reading and comprehension demands of leading evaluation work, where a misread clause in guidance or a conclusion that overshoots the evidence can have real consequences.

Typical characteristics of this assessment include:

- **Context-specific text.** Passages use the terminology of your professional world — monitoring and evaluation guidance, theories of change, quality assurance standards, privacy notices, and departmental reports.
- **Fact-based logic.** You must answer based strictly on the text provided, setting aside everything you know from outside it. This is harder for experts than novices, because you know so much that the passage does not say.
- **Strict answer options.** Most items require a choice between **True** (the statement definitely follows from the passage), **False** (the statement definitely contradicts the passage), or **Cannot Say** (the passage provides insufficient information either way). Some items are multiple-choice comprehension.
- **Time pressure.** Tests typically allow less than a minute per question once the passage is read, simulating the pace of real briefing cycles.

For a lead digital evaluator, verbal reasoning is central rather than incidental. You follow established monitoring and evaluation methods and government guidance, which means reading dense guidance accurately. You publish reports, which means every sentence you sign off will be parsed by policy teams, journalists and the public — so you must be expert at noticing what a sentence does and does not claim. And you assure the analysis of multiple teams, which means detecting when a colleague's conclusion quietly exceeds their evidence. The True/False/Cannot Say discipline is, in essence, the evaluator's discipline: conclusions no stronger than the text supports.

### What it measures for your role

The assessment dimensions map onto your role profile skills as follows:

- **Precise comprehension of rules and guidance** maps to **Evaluation planning and strategy** and your duty to follow established monitoring and evaluation methods and government guidance: extracting exactly what guidance requires, permits and is silent on.
- **Distinguishing supported from unsupported conclusions** maps to **Quality assurance of data and analysis** and **Evaluation delivery**: verifying that stated findings follow from the evidence presented is a verbal skill as much as a numerical one.
- **Reading for obligation and compliance** maps to **Data ethics and privacy**: overseeing compliance with data ethics standards and legislation requires exact reading of what policies mandate versus recommend.
- **Extracting the core message from dense text** maps to **Communicating analysis and insight**: you cannot communicate a compelling, accurate story until you have accurately understood the source material — and you must brief upwards from documents you have had minutes, not hours, to read.
- **Interpreting stakeholder correspondence** maps to your lead-level duty to **influence stakeholders**: reading what a senior stakeholder's email actually commits them to, as opposed to what it gestures at, is a daily verbal reasoning task.

### Practice questions

For each passage, answer the questions using only the information given. For True/False/Cannot Say items: **True** means the statement must be true given the passage; **False** means it must be false; **Cannot Say** means the passage does not determine it either way.

**Passage A — Departmental evaluation guidance (for Questions 1–4).**

"All major programmes must have a proportionate evaluation plan agreed before public spending is approved. For digital services, the evaluation plan must be reviewed at each service assessment. Impact evaluation using experimental or quasi-experimental methods is the preferred approach where feasible; where such methods are not feasible, the plan must document the reasons and set out the strongest achievable alternative. Evaluation findings must be published within twelve months of the completion of analysis, except where publication would breach statistical disclosure rules. Departments are encouraged, but not required, to publish evaluation plans at the start of the programme."

**Question 1 (easy).** True, False, or Cannot Say: Under this guidance, a major programme's evaluation plan must be agreed before public spending is approved.

**Answer: True.**

**Explanation:** The first sentence states this directly: "All major programmes must have a proportionate evaluation plan agreed before public spending is approved." The statement is a near-verbatim restatement, so it is True. Easy items like this exist partly to check you are calibrated: answer from the text, quickly, and bank the mark.

**Question 2 (easy).** True, False, or Cannot Say: Departments must publish their evaluation plans at the start of a programme.

**Answer: False.**

**Explanation:** The final sentence states that departments are "encouraged, but not required," to publish evaluation plans at the start. "Must publish" directly contradicts "not required", so the statement is False — not Cannot Say. This is the classic obligation-versus-encouragement distinction: guidance documents draw it deliberately, and your data ethics oversight work requires you to read it precisely. Note the contrast with findings, which must be published (subject to the disclosure exception); conflating the two publication rules is the intended trap.

**Question 3 (moderate).** True, False, or Cannot Say: A department that completed its analysis fourteen months ago and has not published its findings has breached the guidance.

**Answer: Cannot Say.**

**Explanation:** The guidance requires publication within twelve months of completion of analysis, "except where publication would breach statistical disclosure rules." Fourteen months have passed, so the department has exceeded the twelve-month limit — but the passage gives us no information about whether the disclosure exception applies in this case. If it does, there is no breach; if it does not, there is. Because the passage cannot settle which, the answer is Cannot Say. Candidates who answer True have forgotten the exception; candidates who answer False have invented facts. Holding exceptions in mind while applying rules is precisely the skill of following government guidance accurately.

**Question 4 (moderate).** True, False, or Cannot Say: The guidance regards randomised controlled trials as the only acceptable method of impact evaluation.

**Answer: False.**

**Explanation:** The passage says experimental or quasi-experimental methods are "the preferred approach where feasible" and explicitly provides for cases where they are not feasible, requiring documentation of reasons and "the strongest achievable alternative." A preferred approach with a documented alternative route is not an only acceptable method, so the statement contradicts the passage and is False. Note also the statement narrows "experimental or quasi-experimental methods" to "randomised controlled trials", a subset — another reason it fails. Reading hierarchies of preference correctly matters when you agree evaluation approaches with diverse stakeholders, some of whom will claim guidance mandates more, or less, than it does.

**Passage B — Data ethics policy extract (for Questions 5–7).**

"Personal data collected for service monitoring may be reused for evaluation purposes only where the privacy notice provided at collection identified evaluation as a purpose, or where the data has been anonymised to the standard set out in the Information Commissioner's guidance. Pseudonymised data is not anonymised data and remains personal data. Evaluators must complete a data ethics assessment before any new linkage of administrative datasets. Where an ethics assessment identifies a high residual risk, the linkage must not proceed without approval from the Data Ethics Board. The Board meets monthly and its decisions are final."

**Question 5 (easy).** True, False, or Cannot Say: Pseudonymised data counts as anonymised data under this policy.

**Answer: False.**

**Explanation:** The policy states in terms: "Pseudonymised data is not anonymised data and remains personal data." The statement asserts the direct opposite, so it is False. This sentence exists in real policies precisely because the conflation is so common; as the person who oversees compliance with data ethics standards across the organisation, you would expect to correct this misreading in teams' data management plans regularly.

**Question 6 (moderate).** True, False, or Cannot Say: An evaluator may reuse monitoring data for evaluation if the original privacy notice identified evaluation as a purpose, even if the data is not anonymised.

**Answer: True.**

**Explanation:** The policy permits reuse "only where the privacy notice provided at collection identified evaluation as a purpose, **or** where the data has been anonymised…". The two conditions are alternatives joined by "or": satisfying either suffices. If the privacy notice identified evaluation, the first condition is met, and nothing in the passage adds an anonymisation requirement on top. So the statement follows and is True. Candidates who answer Cannot Say usually feel that data protection "surely" requires more — importing outside knowledge, however professionally admirable, is the error the test is designed to catch. Answer from the four corners of the text.

**Question 7 (hard).** True, False, or Cannot Say: A proposed data linkage assessed as carrying a high residual risk will be delayed by at least a month.

**Answer: Cannot Say.**

**Explanation:** The policy says a high-residual-risk linkage "must not proceed without approval from the Data Ethics Board" and that "the Board meets monthly." It is tempting to infer a delay of at least a month, but the passage does not say when the next Board meeting falls relative to the assessment — it could be the following day. Nor does it say approval must be sought at a meeting rather than, say, already scheduled business. Since the length of any delay is undetermined by the text, the answer is Cannot Say. This item rewards exactly the reading you apply when planning evaluation timelines around governance gates: the meeting cycle constrains the process, but the actual delay depends on facts the policy does not state.

**Passage C — Email from a director (for Questions 8–10).**

"Thanks for the interim evaluation findings on the eligibility checker. I note the reported fall in call-centre demand and the concerns about accessibility for users without smartphones. Before we discuss publication, I would like your team to double-check the demand analysis against the seasonal pattern we saw in the previous two years, since the reduction may partly reflect the usual spring dip. If the finding survives that check, I am content for the report to proceed to clearance in the normal way. I should add that the Minister has shown interest in citing the demand reduction in a speech next month, so an early steer on the robustness of that figure would be appreciated."

**Question 8 (easy).** True, False, or Cannot Say: The director has refused to allow publication of the interim findings.

**Answer: False.**

**Explanation:** The director says that if the finding survives the seasonal check, they are "content for the report to proceed to clearance in the normal way." That is a conditional green light, not a refusal. A refusal would contradict this expressed contentment, so the statement is False. Distinguish a request for further assurance (which this is) from suppression of findings (which it is not) — a distinction with real professional significance, and one this email is carefully drafted to sit on the right side of.

**Question 9 (moderate).** True, False, or Cannot Say: The director believes the fall in call-centre demand is entirely explained by the seasonal spring dip.

**Answer: False.**

**Explanation:** The director says the reduction "may partly reflect the usual spring dip." "May partly" asserts a possibility of partial explanation. The statement attributes to the director a belief in a complete explanation ("entirely explained") held with confidence ("believes"). The passage's hedged, partial phrasing is incompatible with that attribution — the director is explicitly not committing to the seasonal explanation, merely asking for it to be checked. So the statement misdescribes the director's expressed position and is False. Reading modality ("may", "partly", "usually") precisely is essential when you translate stakeholder commentary into analytical tasks for your team: "check for seasonality" is a different job from "remove the seasonal effect we know exists".

**Question 10 (moderate) — multiple choice.** Which of the following best characterises what the director is asking the evaluation team to do first?

1. Publish the interim report immediately to support the Minister's speech.
2. Re-run the demand analysis controlling for the seasonal pattern observed in the previous two years, and advise early on the robustness of the demand-reduction figure.
3. Remove the accessibility concerns from the report before clearance.
4. Delay all work on the evaluation until after the Minister's speech.

**Answer: Option 2.**

**Explanation:** The email's operative request is to "double-check the demand analysis against the seasonal pattern we saw in the previous two years," with a secondary request for "an early steer on the robustness of that figure" given the ministerial interest. Option 2 captures both elements. Option 1 inverts the sequence — publication is conditional on the check. Option 3 has no basis in the text; the director notes the accessibility concerns without asking for their removal, and inferring a suppression request would be reading in what is not there. Option 4 likewise appears nowhere. In a live test, the wrong options often dramatise fears or hopes about what senior stakeholders "really mean"; the discipline is to characterise exactly what was asked. That discipline also protects you in real life: it is the difference between responding to an email and responding to a rumour about it.

**Passage D — Extract from a published evaluation report (for Questions 11–13).**

"The process evaluation found that the digital grant application was implemented broadly as intended in five of the six pilot regions. In the sixth region, staff shortages meant that the assisted digital support offer was not available for the first three months. Application completion rates in the five fully implemented regions averaged 81%, compared with 66% in the sixth region. Qualitative interviews suggest that users valued the clarity of the eligibility questions, although several participants reported difficulty uploading supporting documents on older mobile devices. The evaluation cannot determine whether the completion-rate difference is attributable to the absence of assisted digital support, since the sixth region also differs in demographic profile and baseline digital inclusion."

**Question 11 (easy).** True, False, or Cannot Say: The assisted digital support offer was unavailable in one pilot region for the first three months.

**Answer: True.**

**Explanation:** Stated directly: "In the sixth region, staff shortages meant that the assisted digital support offer was not available for the first three months." The statement is a faithful paraphrase. Answer and move on — pacing is part of the test.

**Question 12 (moderate).** True, False, or Cannot Say: The absence of assisted digital support caused the lower completion rate in the sixth region.

**Answer: Cannot Say.**

**Explanation:** The passage explicitly declines to make this causal claim: "The evaluation cannot determine whether the completion-rate difference is attributable to the absence of assisted digital support," citing demographic and digital-inclusion differences. The causal statement is neither established (so not True) nor contradicted — the absence of support remains a possible cause among others (so not False). Cannot Say is the only defensible verdict. This is the purest overlap between test technique and professional practice: the report's own authors modelled the correct behaviour by refusing to over-claim, and the question checks you can match their restraint. When you coach evaluators on report drafting, this sentence pattern — a difference reported, candidate explanations listed, attribution withheld — is one you will teach.

**Question 13 (hard).** True, False, or Cannot Say: More than 80% of applications across all six pilot regions were completed.

**Answer: Cannot Say.**

**Explanation:** The passage gives an average completion rate of 81% for the five fully implemented regions and 66% for the sixth. The overall six-region figure depends on the number of applications in each region — a volume-weighted average — and the passage provides no volume information. If the sixth region were tiny, the overall rate would sit just under 81% (and note that even the five-region figure at exactly 81% does not guarantee "more than 80%" overall once the sixth region pulls it down); if the sixth region were large, the overall rate could fall far lower. Because the passage cannot settle whether the weighted figure exceeds 80%, the answer is Cannot Say. This item unites verbal and numerical discipline: the words tell you which numbers are missing. It is the same reasoning as the weighted-average question in the numeric section, arriving in verbal clothing — portfolio-level claims need volume weights.

**Question 14 (hard) — multiple choice.** Which one of the following conclusions is best supported by Passage D taken as a whole?

1. The pilot demonstrates that assisted digital support raises completion rates by 15 percentage points.
2. Implementation fidelity was high in most regions, users responded positively to the eligibility questions, and document upload on older devices is a candidate area for improvement.
3. The sixth region's demographic profile caused its lower completion rate.
4. The digital grant application should be rolled out nationally without modification.

**Answer: Option 2.**

**Explanation:** Option 2 gathers only claims the passage makes and hedges them at the passage's own strength: implementation "broadly as intended" in five of six regions (high fidelity in most), users "valued the clarity of the eligibility questions" (positive response), and "several participants reported difficulty uploading supporting documents on older mobile devices" (a candidate improvement area — note "candidate", matching the qualitative, suggestive nature of the evidence). Option 1 converts an explicitly non-attributed difference into a causal effect size, exactly what the final sentence forbids. Option 3 commits the same sin with a different cause: demographics are listed as a possible confounder, not an established cause. Option 4 is a policy recommendation the passage nowhere makes and which its findings (upload difficulties, unresolved regional difference) would not support unmodified. Summarising a report at exactly the strength of its evidence is the core of communicating analysis and insight: compelling stories, but never more compelling than the data.

**Passage E — Publication protocol note (for Questions 15–16).**

"Evaluation reports produced by or for the department are subject to the publication protocol. Reports must be released in an accessible format that meets the current accessibility regulations, and a plain-language summary must accompany any report longer than twenty pages. Pre-release access to findings is limited to named officials for quality assurance and factual accuracy checking; pre-release access must not be used to alter substantive findings or their presentation. Where an evaluation identifies risks to individuals or to national security, the responsible director may redact specific passages, and each redaction must be recorded in a published redaction log."

**Question 15 (moderate).** True, False, or Cannot Say: A fifteen-page evaluation report must be accompanied by a plain-language summary.

**Answer: False.**

**Explanation:** Read the governing sentence: "a plain-language summary must accompany any report longer than twenty pages." A fifteen-page report is not longer than twenty pages, so the protocol's mandatory summary requirement does not apply to it, and the claim that a summary "must" accompany it is contradicted within the protocol's own terms — False. It is worth pausing on why this is not Cannot Say. The statement uses "must", echoing the protocol's language, so its scope is the obligation the protocol creates; within that scope, the passage settles the matter, because the condition triggering the duty (longer than twenty pages) is definitively not met. Reserve Cannot Say for matters the passage genuinely leaves open — for example, whether the department chooses to publish a summary anyway, or whether some other policy elsewhere requires one; the passage says nothing about either, but the question did not ask about them. This item is included deliberately to show how threshold conditions decide borderline cases — and why, when you draft protocols and quality standards for your own team, you should state thresholds exactly and say both what is required and what is merely permitted.

**Question 16 (moderate).** True, False, or Cannot Say: Officials given pre-release access may correct factual errors but must not change what the report substantively finds.

**Answer: True.**

**Explanation:** The protocol limits pre-release access to "quality assurance and factual accuracy checking" and states it "must not be used to alter substantive findings or their presentation." The statement's two halves map directly onto these two clauses: factual correction is within the stated purpose; substantive change is expressly prohibited. So the statement follows from the passage and is True. The distinction it encodes — accuracy checking versus findings alteration — is the load-bearing wall of evaluation independence, and it is the standard you are responsible for holding when senior readers of a draft ask for "presentational adjustments" that would soften a conclusion. Knowing the exact words of your protocol is what lets you agree to the first and decline the second, courteously and immovably.

### Preparation tips

- **Train the Cannot Say reflex.** Most professionals under-use Cannot Say because it feels like giving up. It is not — it is the correct verdict whenever the passage leaves the matter open. Practise asking: "does the text determine this, either way?" If not, Cannot Say, with confidence.
- **Quarantine your expertise.** You know a great deal about evaluation guidance, data protection and service assessments. For the duration of the test, the passage is the entire universe. If a statement is true in real life but not established by the text, it is not True in the test.
- **Read the statement first, then hunt.** For True/False/Cannot Say items, read the statement before rereading the passage, then scan for the governing sentence. This is faster than absorbing the whole passage speculatively and mirrors how you check a claim against a source document at work.
- **Mark the modal verbs.** "Must", "may", "should", "encouraged", "required", "partly", "broadly" — these small words decide most answers. Reading them precisely is also the difference between correct and incorrect compliance advice in your data ethics role, so this practice pays twice.
- **Practise on real documents.** Take a page of central evaluation guidance or your organisation's data ethics policy and write five True/False/Cannot Say statements about it. Designing items teaches the distinctions faster than answering them.
- **Keep your pace.** Under a minute per question means first-pass judgements must usually stand. If you find yourself constructing elaborate scenarios to rescue an option, you have left the text — return to it.

### Common pitfalls

- **Importing outside knowledge.** The strongest candidates for this role are the most at risk: your professional knowledge will whisper answers the passage does not support. The test rewards the text, not the truth.
- **Confusing "not stated" with "false".** A passage's silence makes a statement Cannot Say, not False. False requires contradiction. Evaluators who mark absent evidence as negative evidence make the same error in literature reviews.
- **Confusing likelihood with fact.** "The reduction may partly reflect the spring dip" does not establish that it does. Treating hedged possibilities as established findings is the verbal equivalent of over-claiming from a small sample.
- **Misreading obligation strength.** "Encouraged", "preferred" and "required" create different duties. Flattening them all to "must" (or all to "optional") produces confident wrong answers in tests and in compliance advice alike.
- **Spending too long reading the whole text first.** Scan for the sentences that govern the statement at hand; dense passages are designed to punish exhaustive first readings.
- **Rescuing options with invented context.** If you need facts the passage does not contain to make an option work, the option is wrong. The passage is complete as given.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment evaluates your decision-making, interpersonal effectiveness, and professional conduct through realistic workplace dilemmas. Unlike logic or numerical tests, there is often no single objectively right answer: several responses may seem reasonable, and the assessment measures how well your behavioural choices align with the values of the organisation and the demands of the role. For a lead digital evaluator in the UK public sector, that means alignment with user needs, transparency, collaboration, accessibility and inclusion, evidence-based decision-making, and the integrity standards that underpin trustworthy analysis.

Typical characteristics of this assessment include:

- **Scenario-based items.** Short, realistic dilemmas: stakeholder conflicts, ethical tensions, resource pressures, team performance issues, and delivery clashes — all set in contexts a lead evaluator genuinely faces.
- **Response formats.** You will usually be asked either to select the **most effective** and **least effective** actions from a set, or to rank or rate all the options from best to worst.
- **Generous or no timing.** These assessments measure natural judgement rather than speed, so take the time to think each scenario through.
- **Values alignment.** Scoring keys are typically set by subject-matter experts and reflect the behaviours the organisation wants at your level: constructive, transparent, proportionate, and neither passive nor autocratic.

For a lead digital evaluator, situational judgement is arguably the most job-relevant assessment of the four. Your role profile is dense with situations rather than sums: influencing stakeholders to secure resources, agreeing evaluation approaches with diverse stakeholders, ensuring high standards across a wider team, overseeing data ethics compliance, recommending when to move forward and when to stop, and publishing findings that not everyone will welcome. Each of those is a judgement call made with incomplete information among people with legitimate but competing interests — exactly what this assessment simulates.

### What it measures for your role

The assessment dimensions map onto your role profile skills as follows:

- **Stakeholder influence and negotiation** map to **Evaluation planning and strategy**: influencing stakeholders to secure resources and agreeing approaches with diverse stakeholders are tested through scenarios of resistance, competing priorities and political pressure.
- **Integrity and ethical judgement** map to **Data ethics and privacy**: overseeing compliance, developing an ethical framework, and building a data ethics culture are tested through dilemmas where the convenient act and the right act diverge.
- **Standards leadership and coaching** map to **Quality assurance of data and analysis** and your duty to ensure high evaluation standards are set and met: scenarios probe how you handle substandard work, mentor struggling colleagues, and balance support with accountability.
- **Evidence-based candour** maps to **Communicating analysis and insight**: making complex recommendations at tactical and strategic level includes delivering unwelcome findings compellingly and honestly.
- **Delivery awareness** maps to **Monitoring and evaluation across the product life cycle**: working with agile delivery disciplines, engaging stakeholders at the right stage, and recommending when to stop are tested through scenarios of timeline clashes and go/no-go calls.

### Practice questions

For each scenario, decide your answer before reading the ratings. The explanations matter more than the "correct" letters: they show the reasoning pattern that scoring keys reward — act on root causes, preserve integrity and transparency, keep stakeholders engaged, and neither freeze nor bulldoze.

**Question 1 (moderate) — Pressure to soften a finding.**
Your team's impact evaluation of a flagship digital service finds no measurable effect on the primary outcome. A senior policy official, whose programme depends on a positive story, asks you to "reframe the headline finding to emphasise the positive user feedback and move the impact result into an annex." Publication is three weeks away. What are the most and least effective responses?

- A. Agree to the reframing; the user feedback is genuinely positive, and annexes are still published.
- B. Refuse outright by email, copying the official's director, and state that you will publish as drafted.
- C. Meet the official, explain that the impact finding must remain in the summary with its evidential weight intact, explore legitimate presentation improvements (clearer context, fair prominence for the user-experience findings), and escalate through your analytical leadership if pressure to distort persists.
- D. Delay publication until the disagreement resolves itself.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: A.**

**Explanation:** Option C holds the line that matters — findings keep their evidential weight and placement — while remaining collaborative: it distinguishes legitimate presentational input (which the publication protocol and good practice allow) from distortion (which they forbid), and it uses the proper escalation route only if needed. This is the "accuracy checking yes, findings alteration no" boundary in action. Option A is least effective because relocating a null primary result to an annex while headlining favourable secondary findings misrepresents the evidence in effect, even though every individual sentence stays true — it trades the credibility of the entire evaluation function for one comfortable meeting, and it teaches your team that standards bend under pressure. Option B protects integrity but sacrifices the relationship needlessly: instant public escalation before any conversation is disproportionate, and you will need this official's cooperation for years of future evaluations. Option D is passive: delay does not resolve the disagreement, it just moves it closer to the deadline while inviting suspicion that publication timing is being managed. Transparency and evidence-based candour, delivered with professional courtesy, is the pattern scoring keys reward.

**Question 2 (moderate) — Securing evaluation resources.**
A programme board is deciding next year's budget. The delivery director proposes cutting the evaluation budget by 60%, arguing: "The service is live and working; we know it works because complaints are low." You have five minutes on the agenda. What are the most and least effective responses?

- A. Accept the cut gracefully to preserve goodwill, and plan to do what you can with the remainder.
- B. Present, briefly and concretely, what the evaluation is due to answer next year (including the questions the board itself has asked), what decisions depend on those answers, the risks and costs of deciding blind, and a proportionate reduced-scope option with its trade-offs made explicit.
- C. Tell the board that low complaints prove nothing and that cutting evaluation would be analytically illiterate.
- D. Ask for the decision to be deferred and arrange to lobby each board member individually before the next meeting.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C.**

**Explanation:** Option B is influencing done properly: it reframes evaluation from overhead to decision-support by tying evidence to the board's own forthcoming choices, it treats the budget pressure as legitimate rather than an affront, and it offers a genuine proportionate alternative rather than all-or-nothing — exactly the behaviour "influence stakeholders to secure resources for evaluation" describes. It can also, briefly and respectfully, correct the inference from low complaints (non-complainers include the silently excluded — an accessibility and inclusion point). Option C is least effective: it may be analytically correct that low complaints are weak evidence, but delivering it as an insult loses the room, damages the relationship, and gets the cut confirmed. Option A is passive capitulation — accepting a 60% cut without articulating consequences fails your duty to the evidence base and to the users whose outcomes the evaluation protects. Option D is weaker than B but not disastrous: deferral plus individual engagement can be legitimate, yet as a first move it ducks the five-minute opportunity you have been given and can read as manoeuvring around the board rather than persuading it.

**Question 3 (hard) — A team member's substandard analysis.**
Reviewing outputs across your evaluations, you find that a capable but overstretched evaluator has produced an analysis with serious errors: mislabelled cohorts and an unjustified exclusion of outliers that flatters the result. The report is due with stakeholders on Friday. The evaluator is well liked and has recently returned from a period of sick leave. What are the most and least effective responses?

- A. Correct the analysis yourself overnight and say nothing, protecting the evaluator and the deadline.
- B. Tell the stakeholders the report is delayed, and use the extra time to have the evaluator rework the analysis alone, as a learning experience.
- C. Speak with the evaluator privately the same day: agree what went wrong and why, pair them with a senior colleague to correct the analysis before Friday if achievable (or communicate a short, honest delay if not), and separately review whether workload and your team's QA process should have caught this earlier.
- D. Raise the errors at the next team meeting as an anonymised case study so everyone learns from it.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: A.**

**Explanation:** Option C addresses all three layers of the problem: the immediate product (corrected properly, with an honest delay if needed — never a flattered result shipped on time), the person (a private, supportive conversation that takes the recent sick leave and workload seriously while still owning the standard), and the system (asking why your QA process let the errors get this far — which is your responsibility under "set up tools and systems for effectively assuring data and analysis"). Coaching and mentoring others in assuring analysis is a named duty at your level, and C is what it looks like under deadline. Option A is least effective despite its kindness: silently fixing the work deprives the evaluator of the feedback they need, hides a QA failure from the system that must learn from it, sets you up as a single point of failure, and — worst — leaves undisturbed the possibility that the flattering exclusion was judged acceptable. Option B protects quality but abandons the person: solo rework as "learning" after sick leave, under deadline, is closer to punishment than development. Option D is premature as a first response — the individual conversation must come first, and even anonymised, the team will likely identify the case, making the meeting a public shaming by another route.

**Question 4 (hard) — Ethics versus opportunity.**
A delivery team offers your evaluators access to a rich new dataset: detailed user journey logs that would transform your impact analysis. You discover the logs include user-level records that were collected for service operation, with a privacy notice that did not mention evaluation or research. The delivery team says: "Everyone uses this data; legal would slow us down by months." The analysis window closes in six weeks. What are the most and least effective responses?

- A. Use the data — the public benefit of a better evaluation outweighs a technicality, and other teams already use it.
- B. Decline the data outright and run the evaluation on the originally planned sources alone.
- C. Immediately engage your data protection and ethics colleagues with a specific, time-bound question: whether a lawful, ethical route exists within the window — such as anonymisation to the required standard, or aggregated outputs prepared by the delivery team — and plan the analysis both with and without the data while the answer is pending; also flag the "everyone uses this data" claim for separate attention.
- D. Ask the delivery team to strip the names out of the logs and send them over, which solves the privacy problem.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: A.**

**Explanation:** Option C refuses the false choice between ethics and evidence. It routes the compliance question to the right experts fast and in a decision-ready form (a specific question with a deadline, not an open-ended referral), it protects the evaluation timeline by planning both branches, and it acts on the systemic red flag — "everyone uses this data" describes a potential organisation-wide compliance failure that your role, which oversees data ethics compliance across the organisation, cannot ignore once heard. Option A is least effective: purpose limitation is not a technicality, "everyone does it" multiplies rather than mitigates the breach, and an evaluation built on unlawfully processed data is not just unethical — it is unpublishable and would poison trust in the whole function. Option D fails on knowledge you are expected to have: stripping names produces pseudonymised data, which remains personal data; detailed journey logs are notoriously re-identifiable, so the "fix" is illusory and delegating it to the data's enthusiasts compounds the risk. Option B is safe but needlessly defeatist — it forfeits potentially transformative evidence without spending even an email to learn whether a lawful route exists, and it lets the compliance red flag pass unexamined. Ethical leadership here is neither permissiveness (A, D) nor avoidance (B) but engineering a route that serves users and the law together.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Recommending a stop.**
Midway through a discovery-to-beta lifecycle, your monitoring and early evaluation evidence indicates a new digital service is very unlikely to meet its core user need: prototypes test poorly with exactly the user groups the service exists to serve, and the theory of change's central assumption has failed twice in testing. The delivery team is enthusiastic and wants to proceed to private beta; stopping now would be uncomfortable for the programme's sponsors. What are the most and least effective responses?

- A. Present the evidence to the service owner and sponsors with a clear recommendation not to proceed as planned, laying out the tested-and-failed assumption, the options (stop, pivot the design, or run a tightly scoped further test), and what evidence would change the picture.
- B. Let the beta proceed — momentum matters, the team is committed, and later evaluation stages will catch the problem if it is real.
- C. Share the findings with the delivery team only, softening the conclusion so as not to demoralise them, and continue monitoring.
- D. Escalate directly to the departmental board that the programme should be cancelled.

**Most effective: A. Least effective: B.**

**Explanation:** "Recommend when to move forward and when to stop" is written into your role, and option A performs it properly: the recommendation goes to the people empowered to decide (service owner and sponsors), it is evidence-led (the failed central assumption, the user-testing results), it is decision-shaped (options with consequences, not just a verdict), and it stays honest about uncertainty (what evidence would change the picture). Stopping or pivoting at this stage is exactly what agile lifecycles are for — failing cheaply and early is a success of the process, and spending public money on a beta the evidence already condemns fails both taxpayers and the excluded user groups. Option B is least effective: it spends the most money to learn what you already know, and "later stages will catch it" abdicates the early-warning role that justifies embedding evaluation across the lifecycle at all. Option C fails twice — softening findings is a mild form of distortion, and confining them to the delivery team keeps the decision away from those accountable for it. Option D overshoots: recommending cancellation to the departmental board without first engaging the service owner skips the governance chain, turns a evidence conversation into an ambush, and claims a decision (cancellation versus pivot) that the evidence alone does not settle and that is not yours to make.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Diverse stakeholders, contested approach.**
You are agreeing the evaluation approach for a cross-government service. The policy team wants a randomised trial for causal certainty; the operational director objects that randomising access to a public service is unfair; a charity representing service users worries the evaluation will burden vulnerable people with surveys; and the finance team wants economic results within a year. These positions appear incompatible, and the kick-off is next week. What are the most and least effective responses?

- A. Design the methodologically strongest evaluation yourself and present it at kick-off as a done deal, since you are the evaluation expert.
- B. Before kick-off, speak to each party to understand the interest beneath their position; then at kick-off propose a design that addresses the interests — for example, a phased-rollout (stepped-wedge) design that randomises timing rather than denying access, minimal-burden data collection reusing administrative sources, and staged economic reporting — and facilitate open discussion of the remaining trade-offs.
- C. Drop the experimental component entirely, since consensus matters more than causal rigour.
- D. Ask the four parties to resolve their differences among themselves and send you the agreed requirements.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D.**

**Explanation:** Option B is textbook "agree the evaluation approach with diverse stakeholders": it distinguishes positions (a randomised trial; no randomisation) from interests (causal certainty; fairness of access; user burden; timely economics), then finds design space where the interests are compatible — a stepped-wedge design gives everyone the service and preserves causal inference; administrative data cuts survey burden, which is also an inclusion win for vulnerable users; staged reporting serves finance. It also stays honest that some trade-offs remain and puts them on the table transparently. Option D is least effective: stakeholders cannot be expected to resolve a methodological negotiation without the methodologist — it delegates your core expertise, and the likely outcome is either deadlock or a lowest-common-denominator design you then inherit. Option A gets the method right and the influence wrong; imposed designs get relitigated for the life of the evaluation, and the objections (fairness, burden) deserved design responses, not a fait accompli. Option C treats consensus as the goal rather than the means; abandoning causal rigour without exploring designs that deliver both surrenders the evidence the public is paying for. Collaboration in the public sector means engineering agreement around user needs and evidence, not averaging opinions.

**Question 7 (hard) — A rushed timeline from the top.**
A director general announces that "evaluation evidence" on a high-profile digital programme must be presented to ministers in four weeks, to inform a spending decision. The impact evaluation you lead will not produce robust results for another five months; only early descriptive findings are available, and they are mixed. Your line management chain asks you to "pull together what we have and make it look as complete as possible." What are the most and least effective responses?

- A. Produce the strongest-looking pack possible from available material, presenting the descriptive findings with full analytical polish so the department is well represented.
- B. Provide a clearly labelled interim evidence pack: what is known now (the descriptive findings, with their mixed picture intact), what is not yet knowable and when it will be, the risks of deciding early, and — if the decision cannot wait — which elements of the eventual evidence the decision could be made conditional on.
- C. Decline to contribute, explaining that no robust evidence exists yet and that presenting anything would be premature.
- D. Ask your team to accelerate the impact analysis by relaxing the pre-agreed quality assurance steps, since a four-week deadline justifies exceptions.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D.**

**Explanation:** Option B serves the decision without corrupting the evidence: interim findings honestly labelled are legitimate and useful, and mapping what will be known when — plus the option of a conditional or staged decision — treats ministers as capable of handling uncertainty, which transparency requires and experience supports. Note what B refuses: "make it look as complete as possible" is a request to disguise uncertainty, and B answers it by managing the framing conversation rather than obeying or denouncing it. Option D is least effective because it degrades the one thing that gives your function its value: pre-agreed QA steps exist precisely for high-pressure moments, and relaxing them on the most politically exposed programme in your portfolio maximises the chance that a wrong number reaches ministers with your team's name on it — a worse outcome for everyone, including the director general, than a candid interim pack. Option A is polished distortion: presentation quality applied to conceal evidential immaturity misleads exactly in proportion to its polish. Option C mistakes purity for integrity — refusing to engage leaves ministers deciding with no analytical input at all, and someone less scrupulous may fill the vacuum. The lead evaluator's job in a timeline squeeze is to be maximally useful and maximally honest simultaneously; B is what that looks like.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Accessibility finding versus launch pressure.**
Two weeks before a service's public launch, your evaluation's user research shows that users relying on screen readers cannot complete a critical step, and users with low digital confidence abandon at three times the average rate. The service meets its performance targets for the majority. The service owner says accessibility fixes can come "in the next iteration, a month or two after launch." How do you rank these responses from most to least effective?

- A. Document the findings in the evaluation record and accept the service owner's sequencing — iteration is how agile works.
- B. Advise the service owner and, if unresolved, the relevant governance forum, that launching with a known barrier for disabled users is a legal and ethical exposure as well as a user-need failure; recommend either fixing the critical screen-reader step before launch or launching with an effective assisted-digital route in place, and offer evaluation support to verify the fix quickly.
- C. Report the finding to the service assessment team outside the service owner's line entirely, without telling the service owner you are doing so.
- D. Agree to defer the findings' publication until after the fixes ship, so the launch narrative is not complicated.

**Ranking, most to least effective: B, A, C, D.**

**Explanation:** Option B is strongest: it names the true weight of the finding — accessibility is a legal obligation and a core public sector value, not a nice-to-have — while staying inside constructive governance (service owner first, escalation only if unresolved) and offering practical routes (fix-before-launch or a verified assisted-digital alternative) plus evaluation support to keep the timeline realistic. Option A ranks second only because it is at least honest and recorded; but it under-weights the finding — "iteration" is a fine answer to cosmetic issues and a poor one to a completion barrier for disabled users, who are not an edge case but the reason accessibility regulations exist. Option C has the right instinct (assessment teams should know) and the wrong method: going around the service owner covertly destroys the trust your role depends on, and the same outcome is achieved transparently by B's escalation clause. Option D is worst: sitting on findings to protect a launch narrative is suppression by scheduling, incompatible with transparency, and the sort of act that, once discovered, discredits every future report your team publishes. Inclusion and candour outrank convenience — and the strongest option manages to be candid, inclusive and practically helpful at once.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Building ethics culture, not just compliance.**
You discover that evaluators across several teams treat the data ethics assessment as a form-filling exercise: sections copied between projects, risks rated low by default, assessments completed after analysis has already begun. No breach has occurred yet. What are the most and least effective responses?

- A. Issue a directive that any assessment found to be copied or retrospective will be escalated to senior management, with spot checks monthly.
- B. Redesign the process with the people who use it: shorten and sharpen the assessment, embed it at the natural planning stage, share concrete stories of where ethics review changed a project for the better, make good practice visible and praised, and keep proportionate checks as a backstop.
- C. Accept it — no harm has occurred, the teams are busy, and the forms are recording low risk anyway.
- D. Take over all ethics assessments yourself so they are done properly.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C.**

**Explanation:** Your role profile is explicit: develop a data ethics culture, influencing others to act on it — and culture is precisely what option B builds. It diagnoses why capable professionals game a control (usually because it arrives too late to matter and costs effort without visible value), fixes the process defect (embedding assessment at planning stage, where it can actually change decisions), supplies meaning (stories of ethics review improving projects), and rewards the behaviour it wants, while keeping proportionate verification — trust and check, not trust or check. Option C is least effective: "no harm yet" describes every control failure before its incident; assessments rated low by default are not evidence of low risk, they are the absence of evidence, and accepting that normalises it permanently. Option A treats a systems problem as a discipline problem — it will produce beautifully completed forms and buried honesty, the opposite of an ethics culture, because people punished for candour stop being candid. Option D does not scale, deskills the teams whose judgement you are supposed to be developing, makes you the bottleneck for the whole organisation, and confirms the belief that ethics is someone else's job — the belief that caused the problem.

**Question 10 (hard) — Conflicting evidence on your own portfolio's flagship.**
Your team's flagship evaluation, already cited twice by ministers, reported strong positive effects a year ago. A respected external research group now publishes a study, using a different method on newer data, finding much weaker effects. Journalists have noticed. Your team is defensive; one evaluator suggests issuing a rebuttal highlighting the external study's limitations. What are the most and least effective responses?

- A. Issue the rebuttal — your study was sound, and public confidence in government evidence must be defended.
- B. Say nothing publicly and hope attention moves on.
- C. Commission a genuine reconciliation: examine both studies' methods, data vintages and populations to understand why they differ; engage the external group directly and collegially; state publicly that the department is reviewing the new evidence seriously; and update your published evaluation's status if the reconciliation warrants it.
- D. Quietly withdraw your evaluation from the departmental website to limit exposure.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: D.**

**Explanation:** Option C is what evidence-based government actually means when the evidence turns awkward. Divergent findings between sound studies are normal science — different methods, periods and populations frequently explain the gap, and finding out which is a job your evaluation delivery skill equips you for (analysing and interpreting data from other sources is on the list). Engaging the external group collegially converts a public confrontation into a professional collaboration; the public statement models transparency; and willingness to update your own published position is the behaviour that makes government evidence credible over decades, not news cycles. Option D is least effective: a quiet withdrawal is the worst of every world — it concedes the finding without examining it, it will be noticed (removals always are), and stealth converts an ordinary scientific disagreement into a cover-up story. Option A entrenches: a rebuttal drafted in defensiveness, highlighting only the other study's limitations while exempting your own from scrutiny, is advocacy rather than analysis — and if the external group turns out to be right, the rebuttal becomes a monument to it. Option B forfeits the chance to lead the story with honesty and leaves journalists to frame it; silence reads as having nothing to say. Your standards leadership extends to this: how your team behaves when challenged is the standard you are really setting.

**Question 11 (hard) — The overcommitted portfolio.**
You lead evaluation across nine services with a team of six. A new minister's priorities add two urgent evaluation demands. Honest assessment: your team can deliver seven high-quality evaluations, or eleven mediocre ones. Your own manager suggests "spreading the team thinner — everyone does it." What are the most and least effective responses?

- A. Spread the team thinner as suggested; delivering something on everything keeps all stakeholders satisfied.
- B. Prepare a prioritisation proposal: assess all eleven demands against decision-value criteria (what decisions each evaluation informs, when, at what stake), recommend a portfolio — including which existing evaluations to descope to light-touch monitoring or pause, so the new priorities are properly resourced — and put the choice, with its trade-offs explicit, to the governance level that owns the priorities.
- C. Protect your existing nine commitments and decline the two new demands, since they arrived last.
- D. Accept all eleven and quietly let quality find its own level, flagging problems if stakeholders notice them.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D.**

**Explanation:** Option B does the two things a lead does that a senior evaluator does not: it converts an impossible workload into an explicit portfolio choice, and it moves that choice to the governance level that owns the priorities, equipped with decision-value analysis — which evaluations inform which decisions, when, at what stake. Note that B does not simply defend the status quo: descoping or pausing existing work to fund the minister's genuine priorities may well be the right answer, and proposing it yourself demonstrates responsiveness rather than obstruction. It is also the honest version of proportionality: light-touch monitoring chosen deliberately is a standard; mediocrity distributed evenly is not. Option D is least effective because it is dishonesty by inertia — accepting commitments you know cannot be met at quality, then waiting for stakeholders to discover it, guarantees the failures happen on the least important work as much as the most, and destroys the reliability that makes your function worth funding. Option A is D with better intentions: "everyone does it" is how whole evaluation functions become ornamental. Option C uses arrival order as a priority criterion, which no stakeholder should accept: the new demands come from the elected government's priorities and may outrank half the existing portfolio. Order of arrival is administrative accident; decision-value is strategy.

### Preparation tips

- **Anchor on the role's values, not your instincts alone.** Scoring keys for UK public sector roles reward user needs, transparency, collaboration, accessibility and inclusion, and evidence integrity. Before the assessment, reread your organisation's values and the analytical standards you work to; let them be the lens for every scenario.
- **Look for the option that acts on the root cause.** Across scenarios, the strongest options share a shape: they address the immediate issue, the person or relationship, and the system that allowed the problem — without waiting, and without steamrolling.
- **Watch for the passive options.** Delaying, hoping, deferring to whoever spoke last, quietly absorbing the problem — these are consistently scored as least effective, because they leave the root cause untouched. If an option's verb is essentially "wait", be suspicious of it.
- **Watch equally for the autocratic options.** At lead level, imposing the technically correct answer without engagement scores poorly too; your role is influence, coaching and standards leadership, not command.
- **Read the question format carefully.** "Most and least effective" asks for two judgements; ranking asks for a full ordering. In ranking formats, the middle positions matter — think about why the second-best option falls short of the best.
- **Rehearse with your own history.** Recall real dilemmas from your leadership of evaluations — the pressured finding, the resource negotiation, the struggling colleague — and articulate why what worked worked. The assessment measures exactly that articulated judgement.

### Common pitfalls

- **Answering idealistically rather than effectively.** The perfect-world answer ("simply explain the evidence and everyone will agree") is often not the effective one. Choose options that would actually work with real, busy, interested people.
- **Choosing conflict-avoidant options.** Softening findings, deferring hard conversations, or accepting cuts to keep the peace read as agreeable but score as weak leadership. Courteous firmness beats comfortable silence.
- **Escalating too early or too publicly.** Options that leap past the direct conversation to formal or public escalation usually score below options that try constructive engagement first and keep escalation in reserve.
- **Ignoring your specific duties.** Your role carries explicit responsibilities — data ethics oversight, evaluation standards, honest publication. An option acceptable for a generic manager may be wrong for you because it neglects a duty your role profile names.
- **Passing the problem along.** Options that hand the dilemma to legal, HR, the team, or the stakeholders without your own contribution score poorly; involving experts is right, abdicating to them is not.
- **Forgetting the least-effective judgement.** Candidates often deliberate the best option carefully and pick the "least effective" hastily. The least-effective choice is scored too — apply the same care, and remember it is usually either the passive option or the one that quietly compromises integrity.

The cognitive section was about reasoning from evidence to conclusions that hold — separating what an evaluation design can establish from what it merely suggests, and recognising when a difference between groups is a finding about the groups rather than about the intervention.

The numeric section was the arithmetic that decides whether an evaluation says anything: base rates, confidence intervals against targets, the difference between a metric moving and an outcome changing, and the seasonal or compositional explanations that account for a great many apparent effects.

The verbal section asked for a discipline that runs against your expertise. You know a great deal about evaluation guidance, data protection and what these documents usually require, and the format asks only what this passage establishes. The situational judgement section circled the particular position of a lead: enough authority to be listened to, not enough to compel, with explicit duties around data ethics and quality assurance that occasionally require you to be the difficult person in the room. The credibility to be believed when a finding is unwelcome is built slowly on evaluations that turned out to be sound, and spent quickly by a single conclusion softened to keep the peace.
