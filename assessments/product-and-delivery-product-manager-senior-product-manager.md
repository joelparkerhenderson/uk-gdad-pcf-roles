# Senior Product Manager - Psychometric Assessment Resources

## Introduction

Welcome. This guide has been created for you as a senior product manager working within the UK Government Digital and Data profession. Whether you are preparing for an internal assessment, applying for a new post, benchmarking your capability against the role framework, or maintaining your professional edge, this document offers structured, role-specific practice with full worked reasoning.

Psychometric assessments are standardised exercises measuring the cognitive capabilities and behavioural tendencies that predict performance in a role. At your level, the assessments worth practising are not abstract puzzles; they are job-specific instruments built from the materials you actually handle — multi-team roadmaps, business cases, portfolio-level KPI sets, life cycle decision papers, stakeholder communications strategies — and the decisions you actually make: whether a team should continue, change direction, or stop; how to plan prioritised work across multiple teams while minimising dependencies and risk; when to challenge a strategy with evidence; and how to develop the product managers you manage.

Why do these assessments matter for a senior product manager in particular? Your role level is defined by complexity and consequence. You work on a group of products sharing a value proposition, or on a single product that is complex, high risk, or sensitive. Your reasoning errors no longer cost a sprint; they cost programmes. Your reading errors no longer confuse a stand-up; they misinform boards. And your judgement is exercised increasingly through others — the product managers and associates you manage, coach, and recruit — which multiplies both the value of getting it right and the cost of getting it wrong. Cognitive, numeric, verbal, and situational assessments map directly onto those demands: reasoning through multi-team dependency structures, interrogating benefit models before boards do, extracting exact meaning from strategy and governance documents, and choosing effective courses of action amid ambiguity, politics, and incomplete evidence.

There is a second reason this practice earns its place in a senior diary: you now sit on the other side of the table. You are involved in recruiting associate product managers, product managers, and contractors, and assessment results may be among the evidence in front of you. Practising these instruments sharpens your ability to interpret them — to know what a numeric reasoning score does and does not say about a candidate, and where structured assessment genuinely predicts performance versus where it merely reflects familiarity.

This document is organised into four main assessment sections, each following the same pattern: an overview of what the assessment measures and why employers use it at this level; a mapping of its dimensions to the named skills in your role summary, from Agile and Lean practices to Strategic ownership; a substantial set of practice questions progressing from easy to challenging, each with a complete worked explanation tying the technique back to senior product work; then preparation tips and common pitfalls.

To get the most from it: commit to an answer before reading any explanation; treat every explanation as a mini-lesson even when you answered correctly; and use the harder questions — which involve genuine ambiguity, competing goods, and organisational politics — as prompts for reflection on your own recent decisions. Where a scenario resembles something you handled last quarter, ask honestly whether you chose the option you are about to endorse.

Take your time, and enjoy the practice. Let's begin.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive ability assessment measures the mental processes your role demands, using realistic workplace material rather than abstract items. For a senior product manager, that material is distinctive: multi-team roadmaps with interlocking dependencies, portfolio KPI sets whose signals conflict, life cycle decision papers, benefit models, and governance artefacts where a single overlooked condition changes the correct decision.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 15 to 30 minutes with 15 to 30 questions spanning pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective against a norm group — at senior levels, often a managerial or professional norm group, which means the comparison bar is higher — and adaptive platforms will escalate difficulty as you perform. Employers typically receive speed and accuracy separately, so measured pace beats haste. Ungraded practice items are usually offered first.

At your level, the assessment's relevance is less about raw processing and more about disciplined reasoning under load. You plan prioritised work across multiple teams, minimising dependencies and risks — a constraint-propagation problem. You use evidence to decide whether a team should continue, change direction, or stop — a deduction problem with asymmetric costs of error. You lead teams to create effective measurement methods — an error-checking problem, because badly defined measures corrupt every decision downstream. And you do all of this while stakeholders supply confident, plausible, and sometimes contradictory claims that must be tested rather than absorbed. The questions below simulate exactly those demands, and the explanations articulate the reasoning patterns so you can teach them onward — because at senior level, your cognitive habits become your teams' cognitive habits through coaching, review, and example.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The assessment dimensions map directly to the named skills in your role summary:

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **Managing product outcomes** and **Agile and Lean practices**: reading portfolio-level data — outcome trends across products, recurring failure themes across teams — and distinguishing structural signal from local noise is how you drive continuous improvement at scale.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Life cycle management** and **Strategic ownership**: using evidence to decide whether teams continue, change direction, or stop, and using evidence to challenge a strategy when needed, both demand valid inference from stated conditions — and explicit acknowledgement of what remains unknown.
- **Error checking** maps to **Managing product outcomes** and your duty to **ensure teams comply with appropriate standards**: leading teams to create effective measurement methods means catching definitional flaws, inconsistent baselines, and gamed metrics before they reach a board.
- **Prioritisation** maps to **Product management** and **Creating value for money**: planning prioritised work across multiple teams, and helping others make value-based decisions, are ranking problems under interacting constraints.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Product leadership** and **Stakeholder relationship management**: decomposing a politically loaded, evidence-poor situation into testable parts — then sequencing action — is the senior product craft in miniature.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern recognition across a product group**

You oversee four related products. Their quarterly completion rates over the last three quarters:

- Product A: 81%, 82%, 81%
- Product B: 78%, 74%, 69%
- Product C: 85%, 84%, 86%
- Product D: 62%, 71%, 79%

Which single sentence best characterises the portfolio pattern?

- A) All four products are performing comparably.
- B) A and C are stable, D is improving strongly, and B is in sustained decline — B needs diagnostic attention first.
- C) D is the weakest product because its average is lowest.
- D) B and D are both volatile and should be treated identically.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Read trajectories, not snapshots. A oscillates within a point — stable. C likewise. D has climbed 17 points over three quarters — the portfolio's success story, worth studying for transferable causes. B has fallen steadily, 78 → 74 → 69, nine points with no sign of arrest — the pattern demanding intervention, because sustained monotonic decline is rarely noise. Option C anchors on the level (D's average of ~71 is indeed lowest) while ignoring direction; a product climbing through 79% is in better health than one falling through 69%, and next quarter their order likely inverts. Option D calls two opposite trends "volatile", collapsing exactly the distinction a portfolio owner is paid to make. At your level this is the routine discipline of outcome review across products: rank attention by trajectory and momentum, not by league-table position — and mine your improvers as deliberately as you triage your decliners, since D's 17-point gain may contain the fix B needs.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction from a continue/stop rule**

Your governance framework states: "A product in public beta receives continued funding only if it meets at least three of its four KPI targets, or presents an approved recovery plan." Product X met two of its four KPI targets this quarter. Which one of the following must be true?

- A) Product X's funding will stop.
- B) Product X requires an approved recovery plan to receive continued funding.
- C) Product X's team has failed.
- D) Product X will meet three targets next quarter if funded.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The rule offers two routes to funding: three-of-four KPIs, or an approved recovery plan. X has closed the first route (two of four). Therefore the second route is now necessary: continued funding requires the approved recovery plan — precisely option B. Option A overreaches: funding stops only if the second route also fails, and nothing says it will; the recovery plan may well be approved. Options C and D leave the logic entirely — "failed" is a judgement the rule does not issue (two strong KPIs plus two narrow misses can be a healthy product with stretch targets), and D is prophecy. The professional pattern: when a disjunctive gate loses one arm, the correct statement is "the other arm is now decisive", never "the gate has closed". Senior product managers who master this phrasing conduct materially better governance conversations — telling a team "we now need the recovery-plan route, so let's make it approvable" mobilises; telling them "funding will stop" misinforms and demoralises in one move.

**Question 3 (easy) — Error checking a measurement method**

A product manager you manage proposes this KPI set for a new casework product. Which proposed measure is most likely to distort behaviour if adopted as a target?

- A) "Median end-to-end case processing time, reported monthly."
- B) "Number of cases closed per caseworker per week."
- C) "Percentage of decisions overturned on review, reported quarterly."
- D) "User satisfaction among applicants, sampled monthly."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Test each measure with the question senior reviewers should always ask: "what is the cheapest way to make this number look good, and does that cheap way serve users?" For A, C, and D the cheap ways are hard to find and mostly virtuous. For B — raw closure throughput per individual — the cheap route is obvious and harmful: prioritise easy cases, rush complex ones, close prematurely (reopens may even count twice), and compete rather than collaborate. Volume-per-person targets are the canonical Goodhart case: the measure may be fine as diagnostic context, but as a target it manufactures exactly the behaviour C exists to catch — and note the set's internal safeguard, since B's distortions would eventually surface in C's overturn rate and D's satisfaction, a lagged and expensive detector. Leading product teams to create effective methods for measuring outcomes — your named skill — means running this distortion test before adoption, and coaching your product managers to run it themselves. In the assessment, "which measure distorts" questions yield fastest to the cheapest-gaming heuristic.

**Question 4 (easy) — Prioritising across teams, not within one**

Monday, 9am. Four items compete for your attention across your product group:

1. Team One's product manager reports their release this Thursday may breach a data standard; they want a decision on delaying.
2. Team Two requests you attend their sprint review on Wednesday.
3. A director asks for your product group's quarterly narrative "by end of week".
4. An associate product manager you manage has asked for urgent career advice after being approached by another department.

Which sequencing is most defensible?

- A) 1, 3, 4, 2 — the possible standards breach first, then the director's Friday deadline, then the retention conversation, then the optional ceremony
- B) 3, 1, 2, 4 — directors first
- C) 4, 1, 3, 2 — people first, always
- D) 1, 2, 3, 4 — strict order of arrival

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Item 1 combines urgency (Thursday), impact (a standards breach in a live release), and role centrality — ensuring teams comply with appropriate standards is your named duty, and a product manager is asking for exactly the decision support seniors exist to give. It leads. Item 3 has a real Friday deadline and material audience; it follows. Item 4 is genuinely important — retention of someone you manage — and "urgent" to the person, but a same-week conversation scheduled today serves it fully; note that scheduling it promptly is itself the retention signal. Item 2 is valuable presence, not decision work; attend if the week allows. Option B ranks the director's Friday request above a Thursday compliance risk — seniority-weighted rather than impact-weighted. Option C honours a real principle ("people first") in the wrong currency: people-first means the associate gets protected time this week, not that a compliance breach waits behind a careers chat. Option D abdicates judgement entirely. The senior wrinkle worth noticing: three of these four items are best served not by doing them first but by a two-minute scheduling response sent immediately — triage communication is itself a prioritisation act.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Dependency reasoning across three teams**

Three of your teams plan next quarter. Constraints: (i) Team Alpha's payments API must be live before Team Beta can start integration work; (ii) Beta's integration takes four weeks; (iii) Team Gamma's citizen-facing launch requires Beta's integration complete plus two weeks of end-to-end testing; (iv) Alpha will make the API live at the end of week 2; (v) Gamma's launch is publicly committed for the end of week 9. Assuming work starts as early as dependencies allow, what is the position?

- A) The plan meets the commitment exactly, with no slack.
- B) The launch lands at the end of week 8, one week early.
- C) The launch cannot happen before the end of week 8, leaving one week of slack against the commitment.
- D) The launch will miss the commitment by one week.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Propagate forward. API live: end of week 2. Beta's four-week integration: weeks 3–6, complete end of week 6. Two weeks of end-to-end testing: weeks 7–8, complete end of week 8. Earliest launch: end of week 8, against a commitment of end of week 9 — so the critical path allows the date with exactly one week of slack. Option C states this precisely; option B confuses "earliest possible" with "planned" (you may well choose week 9), and options A and D miscount. The senior judgement is what you do with the finding: one week of slack across a three-team critical path with a public commitment is thin — a single slipped week anywhere consumes it. Planning prioritised work across multiple teams, minimising dependencies and risks — your named skill — means asking next: can Beta start any integration scaffolding before the API is live (breaking dependency i into parts)? Can testing overlap integration's final week? De-risking by restructuring dependencies, not just measuring them, is the difference between reading a plan and owning one. In the assessment, though, answer only what is asked: earliest completion and slack, computed cleanly.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Error checking a business case claim**

A business case you are reviewing states: "The new digital grant service will save £2.4m over three years. Current processing costs £30 per application across 40,000 annual applications (£1.2m per year). The digital service will halve processing cost per application, saving £600,000 per year, which over three years with volume growth of 10% per year gives £2.4m." Which is the most serious flaw in the arithmetic as presented?

- A) £30 × 40,000 is not £1.2m.
- B) Halving £30 does not give a £600,000 annual saving.
- C) Three years of £600,000 is £1.8m; reaching £2.4m requires the volume growth to apply, but growing volumes would also raise the counterfactual baseline — the case double-counts growth on the saving while holding the stated annual saving fixed, and presents £2.4m without showing the year-by-year arithmetic.
- D) The case fails to discount future savings to present value.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Verify each step. £30 × 40,000 = £1.2m — correct, so A fails. Halving to £15 saves £15 × 40,000 = £600,000 per year — correct, so B fails. Now the total: £600,000 × 3 = £1.8m, not £2.4m. The gap is bridged by the phrase "with volume growth of 10% per year" — but check what growth legitimately contributes: if volumes grow 10% annually, savings become £600k, £660k, £726k ≈ £1.99m, still short of £2.4m; reaching £2.4m requires more aggressive assumptions the case never shows. The deeper methodological sin, which option C names, is presenting a headline reconciled by an unmodelled adjustment — the reader cannot audit £2.4m because no year-by-year table exists, and growth is applied to the saving while the narrative holds "£600,000 per year" fixed. Option D is a real refinement (Green Book appraisal does discount), but a secondary one beside arithmetic that does not reconcile at all. Writing or contributing to business cases is your named skill; the reviewing counterpart is this exact habit — recompute the headline from the stated components, and when it does not reconcile, the explanation is usually an assumption someone preferred not to surface.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Conflicting signals across data sources**

For your flagship product this quarter: digital uptake rose from 64% to 71%; average satisfaction rose from 79% to 83%; support contact volume rose 22%; and complaint themes shifted from "cannot find the service" toward "cannot complete the identity step". Which synthesis best explains the pattern?

- A) The data is contradictory — satisfaction and complaints cannot both rise — so at least one source is wrong.
- B) Successful uptake growth has pulled in a larger and broader user population; most fare well (satisfaction up), but the identity step is failing a growing minority, driving contact volume — the next investigation is who is failing identity and why.
- C) The support contact rise proves the product got worse, and the satisfaction rise reflects survey bias.
- D) All metrics moving means the quarter was a success; no investigation is needed.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The senior skill here is holding four signals simultaneously and finding the single mechanism that explains all of them — not averaging them into a mood. Uptake rose seven points: the user base grew and, critically, broadened, now including people previously on paper or phone channels, plausibly with more varied digital confidence and document access. Satisfaction (a mean across succeeders) can rise while an absolute minority struggles more: both facts fit a bigger, mostly-happier population with a localised failure point. The complaint-theme shift is the diagnostic gem: "cannot find" fading means awareness and routing improved; "cannot complete identity" growing localises the new binding constraint. Option A declares contradiction where composition effects are the obvious reconciliation — averages and volumes routinely diverge when populations change. Option C discards a data source by convenience; option D discards curiosity entirely, and misses that a 22% contact rise has a per-transaction cost. B also does what your **Managing product outcomes** skill requires: it converts synthesis into a targeted question — who fails identity, and why — which is where user research now points. Portfolio reading in one line: when metrics conflict, suspect composition change before data error.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Prioritisation with explicit value reasoning**

Your product group can staff exactly one of three initiatives next quarter:

1. Initiative P: relieves a legal compliance risk assessed as "likely within 12 months; high impact" — no new user value.
2. Initiative Q: projected £900k over three years in verified-methodology savings; benefits start in month 8.
3. Initiative R: the top user-researched pain point; would improve satisfaction for ~200,000 users; benefits immediate but unquantified in money terms.

Your director says "just pick the biggest number". What is the strongest reasoning?

- A) Q — £900k is the biggest number, and the director has spoken.
- B) R — user needs always outrank money and risk.
- C) P — a likely-within-12-months, high-impact legal risk is an expected cost that plausibly dwarfs both alternatives and can forfeit permission to operate; then make the case to the director in exactly those value terms, while scoping whether Q or R can proceed in reduced form.
- D) Split the quarter three ways so nothing is neglected.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** "Pick the biggest number" fails because only one option arrives with a number attached — the comparison must be constructed, which is precisely the senior product manager's job. Convert P to expected-value terms: a high-impact legal breach rated likely within the year carries expected costs — remediation under enforcement, service suspension, litigation, and the harder-to-price loss of licence to operate — that credibly exceed £900k of efficiency and an unmonetised satisfaction gain; and unlike Q and R, its cost profile worsens with delay. Option C also does the two things the distractors omit: it manages upward in the director's own currency (helping others make value-based decisions and communicating business-value propositions are your named skills — the answer to "biggest number" is a bigger, better-constructed number, not defiance), and it refuses the false totality of the choice by scoping reduced versions of Q or R. Option A obeys an arithmetic error; option B replaces one absolutism with another — user needs are weighed, not enthroned, and users are poorly served by a product forced offline by enforcement action. Option D spreads three initiatives across capacity for one, the classic route to zero completions. The durable pattern: monetise or bound what resists quantification, compare expected values including time profiles, decide, and communicate in the decider's language.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Applied problem solving: the stalled discovery**

A discovery you commissioned into caseworker tooling is in week 5 of 8 and drifting: the team has interviewed 14 caseworkers but cannot articulate the problem they are solving; the researcher and the lead developer disagree about scope; and the policy stakeholder is pressing for "the solution options paper" a discovery was never going to produce. As the senior product manager, what is the most effective intervention?

- A) Extend the discovery by four weeks to allow more interviews.
- B) Run a mid-discovery checkpoint: require the team to state candidate problem framings from the evidence so far, test each against the original discovery questions, reset scope explicitly with researcher and developer in the room, and re-contract with the policy stakeholder on what a discovery output is and when decisions follow.
- C) Cancel the discovery and commission an options paper directly, since that is what the stakeholder wants.
- D) Replace the researcher, since problem articulation is a research output.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Diagnose before treating. Fourteen interviews without a problem statement is rarely an evidence shortage — it is a synthesis and alignment failure, so option A treats the wrong disease and burns four weeks doing it (more data often deepens unsynthesised drift). Option B is the intervention pattern experienced practitioners converge on: force early articulation ("state the candidate framings now, however rough") because framing attempts expose both the synthesis gap and the real shape of the scope disagreement; anchor the reset to the original discovery questions, the instrument the drift abandoned; resolve the researcher–developer scope dispute as a decision in the room rather than a simmering ambiguity; and separately re-contract the stakeholder's expectations — their pressure for premature solutions is a misunderstanding of the life cycle phase, and guiding colleagues and stakeholders through phases is your named **Life cycle management** skill. Option C capitulates to that misunderstanding, producing an options paper unmoored from a defined problem — the costliest document in government digital, because it launches delivery at the wrong target. Option D personalises a structural failure; synthesis is a team act, the disagreement has two parties, and mid-engagement replacement resets the clock while teaching your teams that drift gets someone fired rather than fixed. Senior intervention style in one line: tighten the frame, force the synthesis, fix the contract — change the people only on evidence that the frame and contract were not the problem.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Multi-constraint sequencing under a fixed window**

You must sequence five pieces of work for one team over six sprints (S1–S6): security remediation (X, 2 sprints, must complete before any public-facing release), feature launch (L, 1 sprint, publicly committed no later than S4), accessibility fixes (F, 1 sprint, must complete before L), data migration (M, 2 sprints, cannot overlap X because both need the same platform engineer), and a service assessment preparation sprint (A, 1 sprint, must be the sprint immediately before the assessment in S6, i.e. S5). One piece of work per sprint except where a two-sprint item occupies consecutive sprints. Which schedule works?

- A) X: S1–S2, F: S3, L: S4, A: S5, M: S5–S6
- B) M: S1–S2, X: S3–S4, F: S5, L: S6
- C) X: S1–S2, F: S3, L: S4, M: impossible this window — it must move out; A: S5
- D) F: S1, L: S2, X: S3–S4, M: S5–S6

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Anchor the rigid items. A must occupy S5. L must land by S4, needs F before it, and — being a public release — needs X complete before it. X takes two sprints, so earliest completion is end of S2 (X: S1–S2). Then F: S3, L: S4. That fills S1–S5 (X, X, F, L, A). M needs two consecutive sprints, cannot overlap X (S1–S2 are gone anyway), and only S6 remains — one sprint, not two. So M cannot fit: option C is the true statement. Check the others: A puts M in S5–S6 while A occupies S5 — double-booked. B launches L in S6, breaching the S4 commitment. D releases L in S2 before X completes — a public release ahead of security remediation, breaching the precedence rule. The uncomfortable structure of this question is deliberate: sometimes the correct answer to "which schedule works?" is "the constraint set is over-committed, and something must move". Recognising infeasibility is a senior skill with a governance consequence — the honest output is an escalation ("M moves to next quarter, or we add platform capacity"), decided openly, not a schedule that silently double-books an engineer and fails in week one. Assessments test whether you will force a fit; portfolios punish it.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Spotting the flaw in a scaling argument**

A product manager argues: "Our assisted-digital pilot in Newcastle cut failed applications by 40%. Rolling it out nationally will therefore cut national failed applications by 40%, and since the pilot cost £50k for a city of 300,000, national rollout for 56 million people will cost about £9.3m — the business case writes itself." Which critique identifies the most serious flaws?

- A) The percentages are computed incorrectly.
- B) The argument assumes the pilot effect transfers uniformly (Newcastle's users, channels, and delivery conditions may be unrepresentative; pilots also enjoy novelty and hand-picked staff), and the linear cost scaling ignores both economies and diseconomies of scale — the 40% and the £9.3m are each extrapolations presented as facts.
- C) The pilot should have run longer before any conclusion.
- D) National rollout decisions are for ministers, not product managers, so the argument is out of scope.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The arithmetic itself survives checking — 56m ÷ 300k ≈ 187 × £50k ≈ £9.3m — so option A fails, and the flaws live in the inferential structure, where option B locates both of them. First, effect transfer: a single-site pilot result carries site-specific composition (Newcastle's demographic and channel mix), delivery conditions (pilot teams are typically enthusiast-staffed and closely supported), and novelty effects; the honest national estimate is a range anchored by pilot evidence, tested through staged rollout, not a copied point estimate. Second, cost scaling: linear per-capita extrapolation ignores fixed costs already sunk in the pilot (economies) and coordination, recruitment, and estate costs that grow super-linearly (diseconomies) — the direction of net error is unknown, which is exactly why £9.3m is a guess in a suit. Option C gestures at rigour but names duration as the flaw when representativeness is; a longer unrepresentative pilot is a longer wrong answer. Option D mistakes decision rights for analytical duty: ministers may decide, but determining the benefit of a product and persuading others with defensible numbers — your **Creating value for money** skill — is precisely your scope, and passing flawed arithmetic upward because "the decision is theirs" is how flawed arithmetic becomes policy. The reviewing habit to bank: when a case "writes itself", the assumptions are doing the writing — find them, and make the next step a staged test of the extrapolation, not a national commitment to it.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Reconciling strategy, evidence, and a sunk commitment**

Eighteen months ago, your directorate's strategy committed to "a single unified portal for all business licensing services", and £1.8m has been spent building it. Evidence has accumulated since: research across three rounds shows licensees overwhelmingly arrive via search and task-specific journeys, and struggle to navigate the portal's unified structure; analytics show 78% of successful transactions now bypass the portal's homepage entirely; and two major licensing services have quietly built direct entry points because their users could not find them through the portal. The programme board meets next month expecting a "continue" recommendation for portal phase 3 (£600k). Your strategy skill says you should "use evidence to challenge the vision and strategy when needed". What is the strongest position to take to the board?

- A) Recommend continuing phase 3 as planned — £1.8m is spent, the strategy is agreed, and boards dislike reversals.
- B) Recommend stopping all portal work immediately and declaring the strategy failed.
- C) Present the accumulated evidence that the portal's unifying assumption no longer holds, recommend redirecting phase 3 funding toward the pattern users have chosen — strong task-based entry points with shared components (accounts, payments, common lookups) behind them — and propose the strategy be formally revised to "unified underlying services, multiple entry points", with the portal's useful components retained.
- D) Present the evidence but make no recommendation, letting the board decide unguided.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The evidence triangulates decisively — behaviour (78% bypass), research (three rounds of navigation struggle), and organisational revealed preference (services building their own doors) all point the same way: users want unified plumbing, not a unified porch. Option C converts that finding into the recommendation structure boards can act on: name the falsified assumption precisely (not "the portal failed" but "the single-front-door assumption failed"), redirect rather than merely cancel (the £600k buys the shared components that deliver the strategy's actual intent — coherence, reuse, reduced duplication — through the entry pattern users have already chosen), and formally revise the strategy so the change is governed, not drifted into. This is your **Strategic ownership** skill executed whole: ensure the strategy delivers the intended benefits and policy intent, and use evidence to challenge it when needed — the intent survives; the mechanism changes. Option A is escalation of commitment wearing governance clothes: the £1.8m is sunk and irrelevant to the marginal £600k decision, and "boards dislike reversals" is managing the board's comfort instead of the public's money. Option B overcorrects into theatre — "declare the strategy failed" discards salvageable components and the strategy's legitimate intent, and immediate cessation strands the services mid-transition. Option D abdicates: boards fund recommendations, and a senior product manager who presents evidence without a position has done half the job and charged for all of it. Note, finally, the sunk-cost framing for the room: the question is never "was £1.8m wasted?" but "what does the next £600k best buy?" — teaching boards to ask the second question is itself senior product leadership.

### Preparation tips

- Practise on your own portfolio artefacts. Recompute the headline of the last business case you approved; trace your current cross-team plan's critical path and name its slack; run the "cheapest way to game it" test over your KPI set. Each is five minutes of genuine assessment practice with a live payoff.
- For deduction questions, classify every condition as satisfied, failed, or unknown before concluding — and when a disjunctive rule loses one arm, phrase the conclusion as "the other arm is now decisive", never as a verdict the rule has not issued.
- Write out dependency chains and schedules; never juggle them mentally. Anchor fixed items first, propagate constraints, then feasibility-test each option — and stay alert for the option that says "this is infeasible", because at senior level it is sometimes the correct one.
- When data sources conflict, reach for composition effects before data error: changed populations make averages and volumes diverge legitimately. The reconciling mechanism usually explains every signal at once; the wrong answers explain some by discarding others.
- Convert unquantified considerations — legal risk, satisfaction, licence to operate — into expected-value reasoning or explicit bounds before comparing them with quantified ones. "It has no number" must never silently mean "it weighs nothing".
- Pace yourself as you would a board paper review: bank the quick verifications accurately, time-box the deep ones, and leave two minutes to revisit flagged items. Rested, measured performance beats caffeinated heroics on adaptive tests.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- Ranking by snapshot when the question gives you trajectory. Levels mislead; slopes and momentum decide where attention goes — in test data and portfolio reviews alike.
- Forcing a fit on over-constrained problems. If every schedule option breaches something, the answer acknowledging infeasibility is not defeatism; it is the only one that survives contact with delivery.
- Accepting headline figures that a components check cannot reproduce. When stated parts do not sum to the stated whole, an unsurfaced assumption is bridging the gap — find it before the audit does.
- Treating sunk costs as arguments. £1.8m spent purchases no validity for the next £600k; assessments and boards both reward marginal reasoning.
- Extrapolating pilots linearly — in effect or in cost. Single-site results transfer as ranges to be tested, not point estimates to be multiplied by 187.
- Answering with your authority instead of your reasoning. Senior-level distractors flatter decisiveness — cancel it, replace them, overrule the room. The scored answer usually tightens the frame, forces the synthesis, and fixes the contract first.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures accuracy and judgement with the numbers your role genuinely trades in. The underlying mathematics remains deliberately accessible — percentages, ratios, weighted averages, rates, trends — but at senior level the data carries realistic complications: multi-product tables, business-case arithmetic with embedded assumptions, benefit models that must be audited rather than merely computed, and figures whose correct interpretation depends on choosing the right denominator, period, or comparison.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 20 to 40 minutes with 15 to 25 questions, calculator permitted, scored objectively and often adaptively against a professional or managerial norm group. Employers see speed and accuracy separately.

For a senior product manager, numeric fluency operates in two directions, and the assessment probes both. Producing: you write or contribute to business cases, determine the benefit of products, and communicate business-value propositions — work that collapses if the arithmetic under it does not reconcile. Consuming: teams, suppliers, and stakeholders bring you numbers engineered to persuade — pilot extrapolations, unweighted averages, totals masquerading as performance — and your role is the quality gate before those numbers reach boards. Helping others make value-based decisions, advocating for data-driven decision making, and using evidence to decide whether teams continue, change direction, or stop all reduce, at the moment of decision, to reading figures correctly under time pressure and knowing which computation settles the question. The questions below rehearse exactly that dual competence, with every calculation shown step by step.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Percentages, rates, and funnel arithmetic** map to **Managing product outcomes**: leading teams to create effective measurement methods requires fluency in the computations those methods produce — and in the distortions they can hide.
- **Weighted averages and mixed-population reasoning** map to **Applying user-centred insights** and portfolio reporting: blending satisfaction, completion, or cost figures across products and user groups without weighting errors is a weekly senior task.
- **Cost-benefit and unit-economics arithmetic** maps to **Creating value for money**: writing and reviewing business cases, determining product benefit, and communicating value propositions are exercises in auditable arithmetic with stated assumptions.
- **Trend reading and projection discipline** maps to **Strategic ownership** and **Life cycle management**: deciding from evidence whether to continue, change direction, or stop requires separating trend from noise, and projection from promise.
- **Table and chart interrogation** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management** and **Product leadership**: representing product management in senior conversations means extracting, verifying, and — where necessary — correcting figures in the meeting where they are presented.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Portfolio completion rates**

Across your product group last month: Product A had 24,000 starts and 18,000 completions; Product B had 6,000 starts and 5,100 completions. What are the individual completion rates, and the group rate?

- A) A: 75%; B: 85%; group: 80%
- B) A: 75%; B: 85%; group: 77%
- C) A: 80%; B: 85%; group: 82.5%
- D) A: 75%; B: 82%; group: 77%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Individual rates: A: 18,000 ÷ 24,000 = 0.75 = 75%. B: 5,100 ÷ 6,000 = 0.85 = 85%. Group rate: total completions ÷ total starts = (18,000 + 5,100) ÷ (24,000 + 6,000) = 23,100 ÷ 30,000 = 0.77 = 77%. Option B. Option A commits the classic blend error — averaging the two rates (75 + 85) ÷ 2 = 80% — which overweights the smaller product fourfold; B's 6,000 starts are a fifth of A's 24,000, so the group figure sits much nearer A's rate. At portfolio level this is the difference between reporting 80% and 77% to a board, and the habit generalises: whenever you aggregate across products, teams, or channels, aggregate the raw numerators and denominators, never the percentages. It is also the error to check first in any group-level figure a team hands you — unweighted blending flatters portfolios whose small products outperform their large ones, which is exactly when the flattering number gets waved through.

**Question 2 (easy) — Percentage change with the right anchor**

Your product group's monthly support cost fell from £84,000 in April to £63,000 in July. What is the percentage reduction, and what monthly cost would represent a further 20% reduction from July?

- A) 25% reduction; further 20% takes it to £50,400
- B) 33% reduction; further 20% takes it to £50,400
- C) 25% reduction; further 20% takes it to £42,000
- D) 21% reduction; further 20% takes it to £50,400

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Reduction: (84,000 − 63,000) ÷ 84,000 = 21,000 ÷ 84,000 = 0.25 = 25%. Further 20% from July's base: 63,000 × 0.80 = £50,400. Option A. Option B computes the change against the new value (21,000 ÷ 63,000 ≈ 33%) — the anchor error; option C applies the further 20% to the original £84,000 base (84,000 × 0.20 = £16,800; 63,000 − 16,800 = £46,200 — and note even that is not option C's £42,000, which is 20% of 84,000 doubled-counted differently; either way, wrong base). The compounding lesson matters at your level because savings narratives chain: "25% down, then a further 20%" is a combined reduction of 1 − (0.75 × 0.80) = 40%, not 45% — and a business case or supplier deck that adds sequential percentage reductions instead of compounding them overstates the total. When you review chained-savings claims, multiply the survival factors; never add the cuts.

**Question 3 (easy) — Cost per outcome, not cost per activity**

Two teams tackle failed applications. Team One spent £120,000 on interventions that prevented 4,000 failures. Team Two spent £45,000 preventing 1,200 failures. Which statement is correct?

- A) Team Two is more efficient: it spent far less.
- B) Team One is more efficient at £30 per prevented failure versus Team Two's £37.50 — though Team Two's smaller programme may face different conditions worth understanding before reallocating.
- C) Team One is more efficient because it prevented more failures.
- D) Efficiency cannot be compared across teams.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Unit costs: Team One: £120,000 ÷ 4,000 = £30 per prevented failure. Team Two: £45,000 ÷ 1,200 = £37.50. Team One achieves the outcome 20% cheaper per unit — option B, which also carries the senior-grade caveat: before reallocating budget on this comparison, check comparability (Team Two may serve harder cases, a costlier region, or be earlier on its learning curve; marginal costs may differ from average costs as programmes scale). Option A judges by spend alone — spending less is not efficiency if outcomes fall faster; option C judges by volume alone — preventing more at any cost is not efficiency either. Both distractors are live failure modes in real budget rounds. The named skill exercised here is helping others make value-based decisions: converting activity-and-spend claims into cost-per-outcome, then interrogating comparability, is the two-step that turns a shouting match between teams into an evidence conversation — and the caveat step is what stops cost-per-outcome itself becoming a blunt instrument.

**Question 4 (easy) — Reading a KPI table with a defined threshold**

Your governance rule funds products meeting at least three of four KPI targets. This quarter's table:

| KPI | Target | Product X actual | Product Y actual |
|---|---|---|---|
| Completion rate | ≥ 78% | 81% | 77% |
| Satisfaction | ≥ 80% | 79% | 84% |
| Unit cost | ≤ £3.20 | £2.95 | £3.18 |
| Digital uptake | ≥ 65% | 68% | 71% |

Which products meet the funding threshold?

- A) Both X and Y
- B) X only
- C) Y only
- D) Neither

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Score each KPI against its threshold, watching the inequality directions — unit cost is "at most", the others "at least". Product X: completion 81 ≥ 78 ✓; satisfaction 79 < 80 ✗; unit cost £2.95 ≤ £3.20 ✓; uptake 68 ≥ 65 ✓ — three of four, meets. Product Y: completion 77 < 78 ✗; satisfaction 84 ≥ 80 ✓; unit cost £3.18 ≤ £3.20 ✓; uptake 71 ≥ 65 ✓ — three of four, meets. Both qualify: option A. The traps are mechanical but common under time pressure: misreading the cost inequality (a lower actual is better), or letting one prominent miss (X's satisfaction, one point short) colour the whole row into a fail when the rule is three-of-four. Two professional footnotes: near-misses by one point (X's 79, Y's 77) deserve trend attention even when the gate passes — gates are floors, not goals; and thresholds sitting this close to actuals invite gaming, which is why your outcome-measurement leadership includes watching how near the herd clusters to the fence.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Weighted satisfaction across a product group**

Your three products surveyed satisfaction last quarter: Product A: 82% satisfied from 1,500 respondents; Product B: 74% from 500 respondents; Product C: 90% from 2,000 respondents. What is the respondent-weighted group satisfaction?

- A) 82.0%
- B) 85.0%
- C) 84.5%
- D) 86.5%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Weight each rate by its respondent count. Satisfied respondents: A: 1,500 × 0.82 = 1,230. B: 500 × 0.74 = 370. C: 2,000 × 0.90 = 1,800. Total satisfied: 1,230 + 370 + 1,800 = 3,400. Total respondents: 1,500 + 500 + 2,000 = 4,000. Group rate: 3,400 ÷ 4,000 = 0.85 = 85% — option B. Option A is the unweighted mean ((82 + 74 + 90) ÷ 3 = 82%), understating the blend because the best-performing product has the most respondents. Two senior caveats belong with the correct arithmetic. First, respondent counts are not user counts: if Product B's 500 respondents represent 40% of actual users, the usage-weighted figure — usually the honest one for a board — differs again; always ask what the weights should be before computing with the weights you have. Second, the 85% headline conceals a 16-point spread (74% to 90%); portfolio reporting that surfaces the spread alongside the blend — "85% overall; Product B at 74% is the outlier we are investigating" — informs decisions, while the blend alone anaesthetises them.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Business case payback with phased benefits**

A proposed feature costs £180,000 to build (all in year 0) and £20,000 per year to run. Benefits: £60,000 in year 1 (partial rollout), then £150,000 in each of years 2 and 3. What is the cumulative net position at the end of year 3, and in which year does cumulative net first turn positive?

- A) Net +£120,000; turns positive during year 3
- B) Net +£120,000; turns positive during year 2
- C) Net +£180,000; turns positive during year 2
- D) Net +£60,000; turns positive during year 3

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Build the year-by-year table — the discipline this question exists to rehearse. Year 0: −£180,000 (build). Year 1: benefit £60,000 − running £20,000 = +£40,000; cumulative −£140,000. Year 2: £150,000 − £20,000 = +£130,000; cumulative −£10,000. Year 3: +£130,000; cumulative +£120,000. End-of-year-3 net: +£120,000, and the cumulative line crosses zero early in year 3 (needing only £10,000 of year 3's £130,000). Option A. Option B misses that year 2 ends still £10,000 underwater — the exact kind of near-miss that matters when a spending review asks "does it pay back within two years?" and the honest answer is "no, by £10,000, on these assumptions". Option C forgets the running costs entirely — a £60,000 error and the single most common omission in draft cases you will review; option D nets only one benefit year. Contributing to business cases, a named element of your **Creating value for money** skill, means insisting every case shows this cumulative table rather than a headline "net benefit £120k over three years" — because the shape of the line (how deep, how long underwater) is what boards are actually deciding about, and phased benefits with flat headline totals is where optimism hides.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Detecting the misleading average**

A supplier reports: "Average response time across your product group's services improved from 3.0 to 2.6 seconds." Your own data shows Service One (90% of traffic) went from 2.8 to 2.9 seconds, while Service Two (10% of traffic) went from 4.8 to 0.5 seconds after being moved to a static page. What does the traffic-weighted picture show?

- A) The supplier is right: performance improved meaningfully across the group.
- B) Traffic-weighted response went from 3.0 to 2.66 seconds, so the supplier's claim is broadly fair for the group and for users.
- C) The unweighted improvement is driven entirely by a small service's architectural change; for 90% of traffic, response worsened from 2.8 to 2.9 seconds — the supplier's average conceals a mild degradation for the overwhelming majority of users.
- D) Both datasets are wrong since they disagree.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Reconstruct both views. Unweighted mean before: (2.8 + 4.8) ÷ 2 = 3.8 — hmm, that is not the supplier's 3.0, so check traffic-weighted: 0.9 × 2.8 + 0.1 × 4.8 = 2.52 + 0.48 = 3.0 ✓; after: 0.9 × 2.9 + 0.1 × 0.5 = 2.61 + 0.05 = 2.66. So the supplier's figures are the traffic-weighted means, and 3.0 → 2.66 is arithmetically genuine — which is what makes this question senior-grade: the deception is not in the calculation but in the composition. Decompose the change: Service Two's improvement contributes 0.1 × (4.8 − 0.5) = 0.43 seconds of the 0.34-second gain, more than all of it, while Service One — nine-tenths of every user's experience — degraded by 0.1 seconds, contributing −0.09. One architectural change on a minor service is subsidising the average while the flagship quietly worsens. Option B accepts the blend as "fair for users" when no actual user experiences the blend: 90% of traffic got slower. Option C is the reading that protects your users and your contract conversations. The transferable audit: whenever an average improves, decompose by segment weight before celebrating — averages are where minority miracles launder majority decay. This is advocating for data-driven decision making in its real sense: not more data, but decomposed data.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Capacity arithmetic across teams**

Next quarter (13 weeks), your three teams have these effective capacities after leave and support duties: Team One: 4.2 developer-weeks per week; Team Two: 3.5; Team Three: 5.0. Committed work: Team One: 41 developer-weeks; Team Two: 52 developer-weeks; Team Three: 58 developer-weeks. Which teams are over-committed, and by how much?

- A) Only Team Two, by 6.5 developer-weeks
- B) Teams Two and Three; Two by 6.5 and Three by 7 developer-weeks
- C) Only Team Three, by 7 developer-weeks
- D) Teams One and Two; One by 13.6 and Two by 6.5 developer-weeks

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Quarterly capacity = weekly rate × 13. Team One: 4.2 × 13 = 54.6 against 41 committed — headroom of 13.6. Team Two: 3.5 × 13 = 45.5 against 52 — over-committed by 6.5. Team Three: 5.0 × 13 = 65 against 58 — headroom of 7. Only Team Two is over: option A. Option B misreads Team Three's 7 weeks of headroom as overload (58 < 65); option D inverts Team One's position. Note how the wrong options mirror real planning-meeting errors: eyeballing the biggest committed number (Three's 58) as the biggest problem, when commitment only means anything relative to capacity. The senior moves that follow the arithmetic are the point of doing it: Team Two's 6.5-week excess is close to Team Three's 7-week headroom, so the plan-level options are transferring suitable work (if skills and product knowledge allow — developer-weeks are imperfectly fungible), descoping Team Two by 6.5 weeks' worth, or explicitly accepting schedule risk. Planning prioritised work across multiple teams while minimising dependencies and risks starts with exactly this table — and with refusing to let a 14% over-commitment hide inside optimistic velocity assumptions until week 10 reveals it.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Benefit realisation against profile**

Your benefits realisation profile promised savings ramping as automation coverage grew: £30k (Q1), £60k (Q2), £90k (Q3), £120k (Q4) — £300k for the year. Actuals so far: Q1: £28k, Q2: £51k, Q3: £66k. What is the cumulative variance at the end of Q3, and — if the Q3 shortfall ratio persists into Q4 — the projected full-year total?

- A) −£35k cumulative; projected year ≈ £233k
- B) −£35k cumulative; projected year ≈ £265k
- C) −£24k cumulative; projected year ≈ £276k
- D) −£35k cumulative; projected year ≈ £240k

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Cumulative profile to Q3: 30 + 60 + 90 = £180k. Cumulative actual: 28 + 51 + 66 = £145k. Variance: −£35k. For the projection, the question specifies persisting the Q3 shortfall ratio: Q3 delivered 66 ÷ 90 = 73.3% of profile; applying that to Q4's £120k gives 120 × 0.733 = £88k; projected year = 145 + 88 = £233k. Option A. Option B persists the cumulative ratio (145 ÷ 180 ≈ 80.6% × 120 ≈ £96.7k; year ≈ £241.7k — close to D) rather than the specified Q3 ratio; the distinction matters because the shortfall is widening (93% in Q1, 85% in Q2, 73% in Q3), so the most recent ratio is the defensible persistence assumption, and the cumulative ratio flatters. That widening pattern is the real finding for your governance conversation: this is not a static 12% haircut but a divergence growing as the ramp steepens — the automation coverage assumption underlying the profile is failing progressively, and the honest board message is "trajectory says ~£233k against £300k, cause under diagnosis, options to follow", delivered now rather than discovered in Q4's actuals. Tracking benefit against profile, diagnosing variance direction, and choosing the defensible projection assumption is the quarterly grammar of determining the benefit of a product — and of the trust your numbers command.

**Question 10 (challenging) — The denominator shift**

A team reports: "Complaint rate improved from 4.0% to 3.2% of contacts this quarter." You check the underlying figures: last quarter, 800 complaints among 20,000 total contacts; this quarter, 960 complaints among 30,000 total contacts — the contact rise driven by a new webchat channel answering simple queries. Transactions were stable at 100,000 per quarter. What is the sharper reading?

- A) The team is right: 3.2% is better than 4.0%, so complaints improved.
- B) Complaints per contact fell only because the denominator was inflated by new low-stakes webchat contacts; per 1,000 transactions — the stable base — complaints rose from 8.0 to 9.6, a 20% worsening the chosen metric conceals.
- C) The webchat channel caused the extra complaints.
- D) Both metrics are equally valid, so the team may choose either.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Verify the team's arithmetic: 800 ÷ 20,000 = 4.0%; 960 ÷ 30,000 = 3.2% ✓ — the computation is honest; the metric is not. The denominator grew 50% via a new channel of simple, low-complaint-propensity contacts, mechanically diluting the rate, while the numerator — actual complaints — rose 20% (800 → 960). Against the stable base of transactions: 800 ÷ 100 = 8.0 and 960 ÷ 100 = 9.6 complaints per 1,000 transactions — users are complaining 20% more per unit of actual service delivered. Option B. Option C over-reads: webchat inflated the denominator; nothing shown attributes the numerator's rise to it — that is the next diagnostic question, not a conclusion. Option D's metric relativism is the trap engineered for senior sign-off: metrics are not equally valid when their denominators move for reasons unrelated to the quantity being tracked; the principle is to normalise by the most stable base semantically connected to the outcome (transactions, here), and to treat any metric whose denominator a team can grow at will as a self-gradeable exam. This is leading teams to create effective measurement methods in its enforcement mode — and the coaching conversation with this team ("walk me through why contacts is the right denominator") teaches more than the correction itself.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Comparing options with different risk and time profiles**

Two mutually exclusive proposals for the same team-quarter:

- Option One: £90k cost; benefit £200k per year starting year 1, with delivery confidence 90%.
- Option Two: £90k cost; benefit £520k per year starting year 2 (nothing in year 1), with delivery confidence 50%.

Using expected value over a three-year horizon (benefits × confidence − cost, no discounting), what does the comparison show, and what judgement should accompany it?

- A) Option One: EV +£450k; Option Two: EV +£430k — One leads on EV and certainty, but the £20k gap is within assumption noise, so the decision should turn on confidence in the 50% estimate, appetite for a zero-benefit year, and whether Two's upside can be de-risked before full commitment.
- B) Option Two: its £520k benefit is much larger, and product managers should back ambition.
- C) Option One: EV +£540k; Option Two: EV +£520k.
- D) The options are identical since both cost £90k.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Compute each expected value over three years. Option One: benefits in years 1, 2, 3 = £200k × 3 = £600k; × 0.9 confidence = £540k; − £90k cost = +£450k. Option Two: benefits in years 2, 3 only = £520k × 2 = £1,040k; × 0.5 = £520k; − £90k = +£430k. One leads by £20k — but the senior content of option A is what surrounds the arithmetic. A £20k gap on figures built from a round 50% confidence estimate is not a decision; it is a coin toss dressed as analysis, and pretending otherwise is false precision. The real decision inputs are: how was 50% estimated, and can cheap work (a spike, a supplier test) move it? Can the organisation tolerate Option Two's zero-benefit year 1? Is there a staged version of Two that buys information before the full £90k? Option C computes gross expected benefit but forgets the cost subtraction on One (540 − 90 = 450, not 540) — check it against the stem's own formula. Option B abandons arithmetic for vibes; ambition is a portfolio property (carry some high-variance bets), not a license to ignore expected value on a single forced choice. Option D confuses equal inputs with equal outcomes. The pattern for your business-case work: compute EV honestly, then say aloud where the model is weakest and what would strengthen it — helping others make value-based decisions means showing both the number and its error bars.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Auditing a percentage story before it reaches the board**

A draft board paper about your product group states: "Digital uptake rose 12% this year (from 60% to 67.2%). Assisted digital demand fell 25% (from 8,000 to 6,000 sessions), confirming reduced need for the assisted route. Cost per digital transaction fell 10% (from £2.00 to £1.80), so the digital estate saved £480,000 across our 2.4 million transactions." Total transactions were stable year on year. Which audit finding is most important?

- A) All three claims are arithmetically consistent and the paper can proceed.
- B) The uptake claim confuses percentage points with percent (60 to 67.2 is 7.2 points, a 12% relative rise — the wording "rose 12%" will be misread); the savings figure applies the 20p unit saving to all 2.4m transactions instead of the ~1.61m digital ones (true saving ≈ £322k, overstated by ~£158k); and falling assisted sessions amid rising uptake does not by itself confirm reduced need — need may be unmet rather than absent.
- C) The assisted digital claim is wrong because demand never falls.
- D) The cost figure is wrong because £2.00 minus 10% is £1.90.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Audit each claim. Uptake: 60% → 67.2% is +7.2 percentage points; as a relative change, 7.2 ÷ 60 = 12% — so "rose 12%" is defensible only with "relative" attached, and boards reliably misread it as 12 points; the paper should say "7.2 percentage points (a 12% relative increase)". Savings: the 20p per-transaction saving applies only to digital transactions: 67.2% × 2.4m ≈ 1.613m × £0.20 ≈ £322,600 — the £480,000 figure taxes the paper's credibility by applying digital savings to paper transactions too, overstating by roughly £158k (and a fuller audit would ask whether last year's £2.00 base should apply to last year's digital volume for a true year-on-year comparison — the direction of error stands either way). Assisted digital: sessions falling 25% while uptake rises is consistent with genuinely reduced need, but equally with degraded awareness or access to the assisted route — usage is not need, and "confirming" is the word an assessment of the evidence cannot support; the users who most need assistance are precisely those least visible when a route quietly withers. Option D miscomputes (£2.00 × 0.90 = £1.80; the paper's cost arithmetic is actually fine); option C asserts a fact about the world instead of auditing the inference. Option B is the finding that protects the board, the budget, and the assisted digital users at once — and this triple audit (units, denominators, inference-from-absence) is very close to a complete checklist for reviewing any percentage-rich paper your teams produce. Representing product management in senior conversations means being the person who catches all three before the meeting does.

### Preparation tips

- Audit, don't just compute. At senior level the assessment's hardest questions hand you arithmetic that is technically correct and semantically misleading — practise asking "right formula, right base, right base-period, right interpretation?" as four separate checks.
- Aggregate raw numbers, never percentages: group rates come from summed numerators over summed denominators; chained reductions come from multiplied survival factors; blended satisfaction comes from weighted counts. If you catch yourself averaging percentages, stop and rebuild.
- Build the year-by-year table for any cost-benefit question — build cost, running cost, phased benefit, cumulative line. The crossing point and the depth of the trough answer more board questions than the headline net figure.
- Decompose every improved average by segment and weight before trusting it, and check every rate whose denominator can grow independently of its numerator. These two audits — composition and denominator — catch most engineered good news.
- Keep expected-value discipline for risk-weighted comparisons: probability × payoff − cost, computed per option, then an honest statement of where the probabilities came from. Practise saying "within assumption noise" about small EV gaps.
- Use your real artefacts as a gym: this quarter's benefits profile versus actuals, your teams' capacity versus commitments, the last supplier performance report. Fifteen minutes of genuine audit is worth an hour of synthetic drills — and occasionally finds real money.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- Averaging percentages across unequal groups, or accepting a blend without its spread. The board hears 85%; the decision lives in the 74%.
- Confusing percentage points with percent — in both directions. "Rose 12%" from a 60% base is ambiguous to the point of negligence in a governance paper; write points and relative change explicitly.
- Forgetting running costs, or netting benefits against build cost only. Cumulative tables prevent this; headlines invite it.
- Applying unit savings to the wrong volume — all transactions instead of affected transactions, respondents instead of users, contacts instead of cases. Match every rate to the population it actually touches.
- Treating a favourable metric with a self-inflating denominator as improvement. Complaints per contact, cost per session, errors per deployment — each can be "improved" by manufacturing the denominator. Normalise to the stable base.
- Letting arithmetic settle questions that are really about assumptions. A £20k expected-value gap, a persisted shortfall ratio, a 50% confidence figure — the number is the start of the judgement, not its substitute; say so in the answer you give and the papers you sign.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment measures the precision with which you read, interpret, and draw inference from written material — at your level, the material of consequence: strategy documents, governance minutes, legal and policy extracts, supplier contracts and correspondence, research syntheses, and the board papers your own teams draft for your signature.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 15 to 30 minutes: short passages followed by questions, most commonly true / false / cannot say (True: the passage guarantees it; False: the passage contradicts it; Cannot say: neither), plus best-summary selection, assumption identification, inference, and clear-writing judgement. Scoring is objective, and the discipline is absolute: answer from the passage alone, however much your experience whispers otherwise — senior assessments in particular weaponise professional plausibility as a distractor.

Verbal precision at senior level is not a support skill; it is a control function. You are frequently the last reader before words acquire force: the board paper that commits your programme, the supplier letter that concedes or preserves a contractual position, the strategy sentence that teams will quote at each other for two years, the research synthesis whose over-claim would misdirect three teams' roadmaps. You also produce words with force — representing product management in senior conversations, communicating value propositions, getting stakeholder agreement to strategic approaches — where the difference between "committed" and "anticipated", between "approved" and "noted", between "need has fallen" and "usage has fallen", is the difference between positions you can defend and positions that unravel. The questions below train exactly those distinctions, and the explanations model the close-reading you should expect — and teach — in every document review you run with the product managers you manage.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **True/false/cannot-say discipline** maps to **Managing product outcomes** and **Applying user-centred insights**: advocating for data-driven decisions requires stating exactly what evidence supports, and representing users means never claiming more than the research shows — nor less.

**Question 1 (easy) — A spend control extract**

Passage: "Digital spending proposals above £100,000 require spend approval before contracts are signed. Proposals below this threshold do not require approval but must be recorded in the spend register within 30 days of contract signature. Approval decisions are normally issued within 15 working days."

Statement: "A £90,000 digital spending proposal must be recorded in the spend register within 30 days of contract signature."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, and it also requires approval

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** £90,000 falls below the £100,000 threshold, and the passage assigns below-threshold proposals exactly one obligation: registration within 30 days of signature. The statement reproduces that obligation for a qualifying case — True. Option D bolts the above-threshold requirement onto a below-threshold case, contradicting the express exemption ("do not require approval"). Note also what the passage does not guarantee, because assessments probe it next: "normally issued within 15 working days" is a norm, not a commitment — a follow-up statement claiming approval "will be issued within 15 working days" would be Cannot say. Threshold-and-obligation structures recur throughout the governance you operate — spend controls, assessment triggers, security review gates — and the reading habit is always the same: place the case on the correct side of the threshold first, then apply only the obligations attached to that side. Coaching your product managers to read controls this way saves more governance friction than any process change.

**Question 2 (easy) — Governance minutes and decision status**

Passage (programme board minutes): "The board considered the product group's proposal to consolidate three case-handling tools. The board endorsed the direction of travel, requested a full business case including workforce impacts by the November meeting, and delegated approval of discovery spend up to £75,000 to the SRO. The SRO indicated support."

Statement: "The board approved the consolidation of the three case-handling tools."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, up to £75,000

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Track the governance verbs, each of which has a precise weight: "considered" (took the item), "endorsed the direction of travel" (support in principle, no commitment), "requested a full business case" (the approval decision is explicitly downstream, at November's meeting), "delegated approval of discovery spend up to £75,000" (a narrow, bounded authority for one phase's spend — not the consolidation), "indicated support" (the SRO's disposition, not a decision). The consolidation itself has therefore not been approved — the minutes' own structure contradicts the statement: False. Option D confuses the delegated discovery-spend ceiling with a partial approval of the programme. The professional stakes are concrete: a team that reads these minutes as "approved" starts consolidation work, prejudices the November business case, and spends legitimacy along with money; a senior product manager who briefs their director that the board "approved consolidation" will be corrected in public. Endorsement of direction plus a bounded delegation is a common and useful governance position — the skill is neither inflating it nor undervaluing it: you have permission to spend £75k learning, and an evidenced case to make by November. That is what you tell your teams, in exactly those words.

**Question 3 (easy) — Research synthesis and claim scope**

Passage: "Across two rounds of research (26 participants), small-business users consistently described the licensing renewal reminder as arriving too late to act on. Participants who employed accountants reported that reminders were typically forwarded to the accountant unread. No sole traders in the sample reported using an accountant for renewals."

Statement: "Small-business users would prefer reminders to be sent directly to their accountants."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True for users who employ accountants

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage establishes two behaviours: reminders arrive too late to act on, and — among accountant-employing participants — reminders get forwarded unread. Neither is a preference statement about where reminders should be sent. The forwarding behaviour makes direct-to-accountant delivery a plausible design hypothesis, but plausible-and-untested is the definition of Cannot say: no participant expressed the preference, and rival hypotheses fit the same evidence (users may forward unread precisely because timing makes the reminder useless to them, and might act on a better-timed one themselves; some may object to government contacting their accountant directly). Option D is the sophisticated trap — narrowing the scope makes the inference feel safer, but a narrower unsupported claim is still unsupported. The senior consequence: research syntheses that quietly convert behaviour into preference are how roadmaps acquire features nobody asked for; when you review your teams' syntheses, circle every "users want" and ask whether the fieldwork shows wanting or merely doing. The legitimate next step is exactly what the evidence licenses: a design hypothesis for the next round to test — which is how "work with others to understand and recommend tools to get the appropriate user insight" cashes out in practice.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Supplier correspondence under contract**

Passage (supplier letter): "We acknowledge the service credits triggered by October's availability breach and will apply them to the December invoice. Regarding the November performance figures, we note the measurement methodology remains under joint review, and we therefore reserve our position on any credits for that period. We remain committed to the remediation plan agreed on 4 November and expect completion by 31 January."

Statement: "The supplier has accepted liability for service credits in respect of November."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, because the remediation plan covers November

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The letter draws a careful line the statement erases. October: acknowledged and to be applied — accepted. November: "we reserve our position on any credits for that period" — an express refusal to accept liability at this time, hooked to the methodology dispute. Reserving a position is the contractual language of non-acceptance; the statement's claim of acceptance is therefore contradicted by the letter's own words: False. Option D imports the remediation commitment as if operational remedy implied financial liability — suppliers routinely commit to fixing problems precisely while disputing the figures that would monetise them, and conflating the two surrenders your negotiating position. Read the letter as its author drafted it: one concession (October, already crystallised), one contested space held open (November, pending methodology), one relationship-preserving commitment (remediation). Your response should mirror that structure — bank October in writing, progress the methodology review with your own evidence, and avoid any reply that could be quoted as accepting the November uncertainty as legitimate. Managing supplier relationships at senior level is substantially this: reading reservations as reservations, and never paraphrasing a reserved position as an agreed one in your board reporting — because your paraphrase, minuted, can become the concession.

**Question 5 (moderate) — A policy exception with stacked conditions**

Passage: "Services must not collect information the department already holds, unless re-collection is necessary to verify identity, or the existing information is more than two years old and the user confirms it is no longer accurate. Where re-collection occurs under either exception, the service must record the justification and review it at each service assessment."

Statement: "A service that re-collects a user's address because the held record is three years old has complied with the policy, provided it records the justification."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, because three years exceeds the two-year threshold

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Unstack the second exception: it requires two conjoined conditions — information more than two years old AND the user confirms it is no longer accurate. The scenario establishes the first (three years) but is silent on the second: we are not told whether the user confirmed inaccuracy. If they did, the re-collection is within the exception and the recorded justification completes compliance (True); if they did not, the exception fails and re-collection breaches the prohibition regardless of any recording (False). Both worlds are consistent with the stated facts: Cannot say. Option D isolates the age condition as if it were sufficient — the precise misreading the "and" exists to prevent, and the most common real-world failure with stacked conditions: teams satisfy the measurable condition (age is in the database) and skip the interactive one (confirmation requires asking the user). Notice also the recording clause is a duty attached to lawful re-collection, not a cure for unlawful re-collection — paperwork does not convert a breach into an exception. When you review your teams' data-collection justifications at assessment time, this is the audit: every condition of the claimed exception evidenced, not just the convenient ones. Conditional compliance questions reward one mechanical habit: circle every "and", and demand a fact for each side of it.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Strategy text and what it actually mandates**

Passage (from your directorate's digital strategy): "By 2027 we expect the majority of our transactional services to be built on shared platform components. New services should adopt shared components by default; teams proposing bespoke builds must document why shared components cannot meet their needs. Existing services will be assessed for migration opportunistically, prioritising those approaching major re-procurement."

Statement: "The strategy requires all existing transactional services to migrate to shared components by 2027."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True for services approaching re-procurement

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Three graduated postures live in this passage, and the statement flattens them into the strongest. "We expect the majority... by 2027" is an aspiration about most services, not a mandate about all. "New services should adopt... by default" plus a documented-exception route is a rebuttable default for new builds only. "Existing services will be assessed... opportunistically, prioritising those approaching re-procurement" is the weakest posture of the three — case-by-case assessment on an opportunistic trigger, with priority (not obligation) for the re-procurement cohort. A universal migration requirement for existing services with a 2027 deadline is contradicted by that opportunistic language: False. Option D upgrades "prioritising" into "requiring" for the re-procurement subset — a strength error one notch smaller but the same species. The senior reading skill is mapping mandate gradients: expect/should/must, majority/default/all, opportunistic/prioritised/required — because strategies deliberately mix strengths, and both over-reading (forcing a struggling legacy service into unfunded migration "because the strategy requires it") and under-reading (waving a bespoke new build through without the documented exception) misuse the document. When you challenge or defend work using strategy text — a named element of your **Strategic ownership** skill — quote the actual modal verbs; the argument usually ends there.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Best summary of a decision paper**

Passage: "We assessed three options for the failing appointments module: remediate in place (£250k, retains known architecture, high confidence), replace with the cross-government booking component (£180k integration, resolves accessibility backlog, medium confidence pending a technical spike), and full bespoke rebuild (£600k, maximum flexibility, low confidence in the timeline). We recommend option two, subject to a two-week technical spike confirming API compatibility; if the spike fails, we recommend option one as fallback. Option three is not recommended at current funding levels."

Which sentence best summarises the paper?

- A) The paper recommends replacing the module with the cross-government component, conditional on a two-week compatibility spike, with in-place remediation as the agreed fallback and the bespoke rebuild ruled out at current funding.
- B) The paper recommends the cheapest option because budgets are constrained.
- C) The paper recommends remediating in place, with replacement as a future aspiration.
- D) The paper finds all three options viable and defers the decision pending a spike.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** A faithful summary must preserve four structural elements: the primary recommendation (option two), its condition (spike confirms API compatibility), the contingent fallback (option one if the spike fails), and the bounded rejection (option three "at current funding levels" — not forever). Option A carries all four at correct strength. Option B is true-ish on price (£180k is cheapest) but false on reasoning — the paper recommends on accessibility resolution and confidence, with cost one factor; summarising motive wrongly poisons later debate ("we only chose it because it was cheap"). Option C promotes the fallback to the recommendation. Option D misreads a conditional recommendation as no recommendation: the paper decides now, with a defined trigger for the alternative — materially different from deferral, because teams can mobilise for option two immediately. The senior relevance is double. You sign papers like this: the conditional-recommendation-with-fallback structure is the strongest form for uncertain technical choices, and you should coach your product managers toward it. And you summarise papers like this upward: the fidelity test — every recommendation, condition, fallback, and bounded rejection preserved at strength — is exactly what "create a prioritised roadmap and use it to discuss product strategy at all levels" demands of your compression skills. Most summary damage is strength damage, not content damage.

**Question 8 (moderate) — The assumption a business case stands on**

Passage (business case extract): "Consolidating our two licensing front-ends into one will reduce user confusion, because applicants currently start in the wrong service 18% of the time, and each misdirected start costs an average of 11 minutes before users find the correct route. A single front-end eliminates wrong starts, saving applicants approximately 40,000 hours annually."

Which unstated assumption is most critical to the claimed saving?

- A) That users prefer single front-ends aesthetically.
- B) That the consolidated front-end's internal routing will not simply relocate the confusion — users who today pick the wrong service must, in the new design, still be routed to the correct journey, and the case assumes that routing costs them approximately zero time.
- C) That the two licensing services have similar transaction volumes.
- D) That 11 minutes is measured accurately.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Skeletonise the argument: wrong starts exist (18%, 11 minutes each) → single front-end "eliminates wrong starts" → therefore all wrong-start time is saved. The pivot is the word "eliminates". Consolidation removes the choice between two services, but the underlying user task — being routed to the correct licensing journey — does not vanish; it moves inside the single front-end, where the same users who misjudged the front door must now navigate triage questions, and the saving equals old wrong-start time minus new internal-routing time. The case books the entire 40,000 hours, which silently assumes internal routing costs ~nothing — the load-bearing, unstated, and empirically testable premise option B names. Option D is a genuine but secondary audit point (mismeasured minutes scale the saving; they do not threaten its logic); option C affects distribution of the saving, not its existence; option A is decoration. This is the verbal-reasoning form of a review you will conduct dozens of times: confusion, contact, or failure demand rarely "eliminates" — it relocates, and honest cases estimate the residual at the new location. The strongest version of this case would cite triage completion times from a comparable single front-end, converting the assumption into evidence — which is precisely the improvement you should require before it proceeds. Determining benefit and persuading others, per your **Creating value for money** skill, is only as strong as the weakest unstated premise you failed to surface.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Two passages, one apparent contradiction**

Passage 1 (performance report, May): "Median processing time for standard applications fell from 9 days to 6 days following the March automation release."

Passage 2 (caseworker forum notes, May): "Caseworkers report that applications now take longer to process than before March, with complex cases requiring extensive manual rework of automated pre-checks."

Statement: "The two passages cannot both be accurate."

- A) True
- B) False — both can be accurate if automation accelerated standard applications (moving the median) while adding rework to complex cases, which the median for standard applications would not register.
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, because caseworker perception is subjective

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Test for a world in which both passages hold. Passage 1's metric is scoped twice: median and standard applications. Passage 2's experience is scoped to complex cases and to caseworker effort. Construct the reconciling world: automation pre-checks standard applications successfully (their processing time falls; the median for that population drops 9 → 6), while the same pre-checks misfire on complex cases, generating manual rework that makes those slower — a change invisible to a median computed over standard applications only, and largely invisible to any median if complex cases are a minority. Both passages are accurate; they measure different populations with different instruments. So the statement is False, option B. Option D dismisses frontline testimony as "subjective" — the reflex that delays operational discovery by months; caseworker reports are data about a population the headline metric was never scoped to see. The senior lesson operates at two depths. Analytically: apparent contradictions between metrics and testimony usually dissolve at the scope boundary — read the population, statistic, and unit of each claim before declaring conflict. Managerially: this exact pattern (headline improves, edge cases degrade, the metric cannot see it) is among the most common failure modes of automation programmes, and the reconciliation is not the end of the inquiry but the start — the next question is "what share of total effort and user outcomes do complex cases represent, and what is rework costing?" Advocating for data-driven decisions includes advocating for the data your current metrics structurally exclude.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Clear-writing judgement: the escalation paragraph**

You must escalate a supplier performance issue to your director, who has five minutes and no context. Which paragraph serves best?

- A) "Further to ongoing dialogue with our delivery partner regarding performance variances against contractual baselines, a range of remediation levers are being explored in the context of the broader relationship, and we will revert with a fuller position in due course."
- B) "Our booking supplier missed the 99.5% availability target for the third consecutive month (October: 97.1%), causing an estimated 4,200 failed citizen bookings. Service credits are applied, but the remediation plan has now slipped twice. I recommend we trigger the contract's formal improvement notice this week; if November also breaches, we begin exit planning. I need your agreement to issue the notice."
- C) "The supplier situation has deteriorated significantly and urgent senior intervention is now required as a matter of priority."
- D) "October availability was 97.1% against a 99.5% target; service credits were applied per clause 14.2; the remediation plan baseline was re-agreed on 4 November; a further review is scheduled for 30 November; the joint service board meets quarterly; minutes are attached."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** An escalation paragraph has one job with four parts: the fact pattern (what, how bad, trend), the user consequence, the recommendation, and the specific decision requested — all sized to the reader's five minutes. Option B delivers each: third consecutive breach with the number, 4,200 failed citizen bookings (the consequence that converts contract administration into public-service urgency), a recommendation with a trigger-based next step (improvement notice now; exit planning if November breaches), and an unambiguous ask ("your agreement to issue the notice"). A director can act on it in ninety seconds. Option A is the house style of concealment — no figure, no consequence, no ask, and "in due course" invites the deterioration to continue unescalated; its true failing is that it protects its author, not its reader. Option C has urgency without content: the director now knows to worry but not what about, which generates a meeting rather than a decision. Option D is B's evil twin and the subtle trap: every fact is present, accurate, and dated — and no judgement, consequence, or request exists; it outsources the actual escalation to a busy reader, which is abdication formatted as thoroughness. The senior writing principle, worth teaching to every product manager you coach: facts select themselves around the decision you are asking for — a paragraph with all the facts and no ask is not neutral, it is unfinished. Identifying and resolving issues through effective senior communication is precisely this craft.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Inference from an evaluation report**

Passage: "The 12-week proactive-contact trial called applicants whose applications stalled for 10+ days. Called applicants completed at 64%, against 41% for stalled applicants in the comparison group. However, the trial required callers to attempt contact within two working days of the stall, a standard met in 71% of cases; completion among applicants actually reached within two days was 78%. Trial staffing used experienced caseworkers volunteering from other duties, and the evaluation notes that stalled applications rose 8% service-wide during the trial period for unrelated seasonal reasons."

Statement: "If the proactive-contact approach is rolled out with the same two-day contact standard met at the same rate, completion among called applicants of around 64% is a better-supported expectation than 78%."

- A) True
- B) False — 78% is the demonstrated effect of the intervention working properly
- C) Cannot say
- D) False — the seasonal rise invalidates all the trial's figures

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Distinguish the estimands. The 64% is the intention-to-treat result: completion across everyone the process attempted to help, including the 29% of cases where the two-day standard was missed — it measures the intervention as actually operated. The 78% is a per-protocol figure: completion among the subset reached in time — it measures the intervention when everything works, and is inflated both by excluding operational failure and, plausibly, by selection (applicants easy to reach within two days may be more engaged and likelier to complete anyway). The statement's rollout scenario explicitly holds the operational rate constant ("the same two-day standard met at the same rate"), which is the intention-to-treat world: 64% is the matching, better-supported expectation — True. And a disciplined forecaster would shade even that downward: trial delivery used volunteer experienced caseworkers, a staffing quality rollout rarely replicates. Option B is the optimism error that inflates business cases — quoting the per-protocol number for an at-scale decision; the honest use of 78% is as an upper bound showing what improving the contact-timeliness rate might buy. Option D overcorrects: a service-wide seasonal rise affects both trial and comparison groups and is noted as unrelated; it complicates but does not "invalidate all figures". Reading evaluation reports at exactly this grain — which number answers which question, what the trial's delivery conditions were, which direction each bias points — is how "use evidence to decide when a team should continue, change direction or stop" avoids becoming "use the most flattering number available".

**Question 12 (challenging) — What the strategy sentence commits you to**

Passage (from a strategy you co-authored, now quoted back at you): "We will prioritise reducing failure demand across the licensing estate, and will not commence major new feature programmes until the top five drivers of avoidable contact have been addressed." A programme director emails: "Your own strategy says no new features until failure demand is fixed. The renewals redesign my team wants is therefore blocked, and so is your API programme. Please confirm both are paused."

Statement: "The strategy text, as quoted, blocks the API programme only if the API programme is a major new feature programme and the top five avoidable-contact drivers have not been addressed."

- A) True
- B) False — the strategy blocks all new work until failure demand is fixed
- C) Cannot say
- D) False — strategies cannot block programmes

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Parse the commitment's actual structure: the prohibition applies to a defined class ("major new feature programmes") until a defined condition ("the top five drivers of avoidable contact have been addressed") is met. Whether any particular programme is blocked therefore turns on exactly two questions — class membership and condition status — which is precisely the biconditional the statement articulates: True. The director's email, by contrast, performs two silent expansions: "major new feature programmes" becomes "new features" then "all new work" (an API programme may well be infrastructure, or itself a failure-demand remedy — class membership is arguable and must be argued, not assumed), and "until the top five drivers have been addressed" becomes "until failure demand is fixed" (a far higher bar than the strategy set — "addressed" and the bounded "top five" were chosen, presumably deliberately, to make the condition satisfiable). Option B adopts both expansions. The senior craft here runs in two directions. Reading: when your own strategy is quoted against you, return to the exact text — authors are haunted by their precise words, and rescued by them too. Writing: this is why strategy sentences deserve drafting care — "major", "top five", "addressed" are load-bearing terms that will be litigated in emails like this one, and the co-author who can say "the renewals redesign is squarely in the blocked class; here is our evidence that the API programme reduces avoidable contact and so serves the condition rather than breaching it" is doing **Strategic ownership** as the framework defines it: getting agreement on the strategic approach, adapting from feedback, and using the strategy's own evidence-logic to arbitrate what it governs.

### Preparation tips

- Apply the three-way test mechanically, at senior speed: could the statement be false while the passage stays true? Not True. Does the passage's own content rule it out? Only then False. Everything else — however professionally plausible — is Cannot say.
- Read governance and correspondence for verb weight: considered / noted / endorsed / requested / delegated / approved; acknowledge / reserve / commit / expect. Build the habit of quoting the verb back in your answer — and in your board reporting.
- Circle conjunctions and modal gradients: every "and" in an exception demands a fact per side; every should/must/expect/prioritising marks a different mandate strength. Most hard questions — and most misquoted strategies — turn on one such word.
- For evaluation and research passages, identify the population, statistic, and delivery conditions behind each number before comparing numbers. Intention-to-treat versus per-protocol, standard versus complex cases, respondents versus users: scope first, arithmetic second.
- Practise the assumption hunt on real cases: skeletonise the argument (evidence → mechanism → benefit), then name what must be true at the mechanism step that the text never states. One surfaced premise per review meeting will change your teams' drafting within a quarter.
- Train your writing eye with the four-part escalation test — facts, consequence, recommendation, ask — and its converse: a paragraph with all facts and no ask is unfinished. Edit one of your own recent emails against it.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- Letting professional plausibility masquerade as textual support. At senior level you will recognise every scenario; the test still scores only what the passage licenses — as does the record, when your paraphrase is minuted.
- Flattening mandate gradients: reading "endorsed the direction" as approval, "prioritising" as requiring, "expect the majority" as mandating all. Strength errors, in either direction, are the senior misreading.
- Converting behaviour into preference, usage into need, or remediation commitments into liability acceptance. Each is an inference the evidence may invite but does not license — and each has a real cost when it reaches a roadmap, an assisted-digital decision, or a supplier negotiation.
- Satisfying one limb of a conjoined condition and calling it compliance. "Two years old AND user confirms" is two facts; paperwork attached to a breach is still a breach.
- Quoting per-protocol numbers for intention-to-treat decisions. The 78% is what the intervention can do; the 64% is what your rollout will do at current operational fidelity — business cases built on the former fail by the difference.
- Treating fact-complete, judgement-free prose as neutral. Whether reading option D or writing your own updates, remember: escalations without asks, summaries without strengths, and averages without spreads all transfer the real work to the reader — and the reader may not do it.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment presents realistic workplace dilemmas and asks you to evaluate the effectiveness of possible responses — most effective, most and least effective, full ranking, or independent rating formats. Scoring keys derive from experienced practitioners' consensus and the profession's behavioural frameworks; there is no formula, but there are stable principles, which the explanations below surface explicitly.

The typical format is an online exercise of 20 to 40 minutes with 10 to 20 scenarios, usually untimed or generously timed. At senior level the distractors are engineered with more care: responses that were correct one level down (personally absorbing work you should now route through others), responses that pattern-match to leadership clichés (decisive unilateralism, unconditional team backing), and responses whose flaw is visible only at organisational timescale (quiet fixes that corrupt precedent, kindnesses that entrench unfairness).

For a senior product manager, situational judgement is the closest of the four instruments to the actual job. Your role summary is a behavioural specification: manage and coach product managers and associates; recruit; protect teams from distraction and direction churn; represent product management with senior stakeholders; use evidence to decide whether teams continue, change direction, or stop; challenge strategy when the evidence demands it. Each verb hides dilemmas — between the person and the team, the team and the programme, this quarter's delivery and next year's capability, loyalty upward and honesty outward. The scenarios below sit deliberately at those joints. As you practise, attend to the recurring senior fingerprint in the effective responses: they act through structures rather than around them, develop people rather than replace their judgement, escalate by making decisions visible rather than by making them louder, and treat trust — of teams, stakeholders, and the public — as the asset every other outcome depends on.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **People management scenarios** map to your level's duties to **manage associate product managers and product managers** and be involved in **recruiting**: performance conversations, development risk, fairness under pressure, and the difference between supporting people and shielding them from growth.
- **Team protection scenarios** map to **Product leadership**: absorbing organisational turbulence so teams keep delivering — without becoming an information filter that leaves teams naive or stakeholders blind.
- **Evidence and governance scenarios** map to **Life cycle management** and **Managing product outcomes**: continue/pivot/stop recommendations, honest metrics, and the courage to bring unwelcome data to welcoming audiences.
- **Strategy and challenge scenarios** map to **Strategic ownership**: using evidence to challenge vision and strategy when needed — including strategies you authored and sponsors you respect.
- **Stakeholder and mediation scenarios** map to **Stakeholder relationship management** and **Creating value for money**: influencing without authority, holding value arguments against seniority, and building relationships that survive disagreement.
- **Standards and integrity scenarios** map to your duty to **ensure teams comply with appropriate standards**: accessibility, data protection, and evidential honesty as non-negotiables that trade against nothing.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — A product manager's first big mistake**

A product manager you manage misread a governance condition and told their team to begin a public beta launch sequence without the required assessment report being cleared. You catch it a day in; the launch can be unwound with about two days of lost work and some stakeholder embarrassment. The product manager is capable, mortified, and this is their first serious error. What is the most effective response?

- A) Unwind the launch quietly yourself and tell the product manager you have handled it — no need to dwell.
- B) Have the product manager lead the unwinding — correcting the sequence, informing their stakeholders, and briefing the governance team — while you support from behind, then run a blame-free debrief on how the condition was misread and what check would have caught it.
- C) Take over the situation, inform stakeholders yourself, and note the error formally for the product manager's next performance review.
- D) Let the launch continue since unwinding causes embarrassment; retrofit the assessment clearance as fast as possible.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Two tests run simultaneously here: governance integrity and people development, and option B passes both. The error must be corrected visibly and properly (option D fails the first test outright — proceeding without clearance converts an honest mistake into a knowing breach, and "retrofit" governance is the precedent that eventually produces the launch nobody can unwind). But who corrects it matters as much as whether: having the product manager lead their own recovery — with you visibly behind them — builds the exact capability the error revealed missing, preserves their standing with their team and stakeholders (people respect a well-led recovery more than an absence of error), and signals to your whole group that mistakes surface safely here. Option A is kindness that stunts: the product manager learns that errors vanish upward, stakeholders learn nothing, and you have taught your group's most capable person that recovery is above their grade. Option C is the inverse failure — taking the recovery and formalising the blame — which teaches everyone watching to hide the next error; note the review-note move especially: consequences for first honest mistakes, handled well, should be developmental, or your group's error-reporting rate will tell you what people concluded. The debrief's design detail — "what check would have caught it" — converts the incident into system improvement, which is what separates managing errors from merely surviving them. Coach and manage others in product management, per your named skill, means exactly this: the person grows, the system improves, the standard holds.

**Question 2 (easy) — The direction change request, third time this quarter**

A policy deputy director — senior to you, and a key long-term relationship — asks you to redirect one of your teams for the third time this quarter, this time toward a ministerial interest that "needs something visible in six weeks". The previous two redirections produced work that was demonstrated once and shelved. Your team is mid-way through a committed outcome improvement with measurable user value. What is the most effective response?

- A) Redirect the team again — ministerial interest is the weather; teams adapt.
- B) Refuse on the team's behalf and inform the deputy director that the roadmap is fixed for the quarter.
- C) Meet the deputy director: acknowledge the ministerial need, surface the pattern (two prior redirections, both shelved, at a stated cost to committed outcomes), propose meeting the visibility need without derailing the team — an existing demo, a lightweight prototype from your discovery capacity, or a genuinely scoped six-week slice if the need is real — and agree a decision rule for future redirections.
- D) Accept the redirection but have the team quietly continue the committed work at reduced pace, showing the deputy director only the ministerial workstream.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Protecting teams from requests to change direction — your named skill — is not a portcullis; it is a negotiation conducted with evidence at the right level, which is exactly what option C stages. Its components are each load-bearing: acknowledging the ministerial need keeps the relationship and recognises that visibility demands are sometimes legitimate strategy; surfacing the pattern with costs converts your resistance from preference to evidence ("two redirections, both shelved, N weeks of outcome delay" is a value-for-money argument the deputy director can defend upward); the graded alternatives distinguish the need's real size (most "something visible" needs are satisfiable far more cheaply than a team redirection — and offering the cheap route tests whether the need is visibility or control); and the decision rule converts a recurring ambush into governed process, which is the only durable fix for a third-time pattern. Option A treats churn as weather when the record shows it is waste — absorbing it silently makes you the mechanism of the waste. Option B protects this quarter and spends the relationship: flat refusal to a senior stakeholder without an alternative teaches them to route around you, and "the roadmap is fixed" is a posture, not an argument. Option D is the seductive senior failure — the hidden double-book: it deceives the stakeholder, halves both workstreams, and when discovered (it is always discovered) converts a defensible prioritisation dispute into a trust breach that follows you. The fingerprint to bank: acknowledge the need, evidence the pattern, price the options, govern the future.

**Question 3 (easy) — Recruitment shortcut pressure**

You are recruiting two product managers. After a strong first sift, your director — under pressure to show the programme is staffed — suggests: "Skip the second interviews and take the top two on paper; we can performance-manage later if needed. HR won't object if I clear it." One of the two paper-leaders interviewed unevenly at sift; a third candidate interviewed exceptionally but scores lower on written criteria. What is the most effective response?

- A) Take the director's route — speed matters, clearance is offered, and performance management exists for a reason.
- B) Run the full process as designed: explain to the director that the second interview exists precisely to resolve cases like the uneven paper-leader and the exceptional lower-scorer, offer to compress the timeline (days, not weeks) rather than delete the stage, and hold the panel to the published criteria.
- C) Skip the interviews but substitute your personal judgement of the candidates for the missing stage.
- D) Escalate the director's suggestion to HR as an attempted breach of recruitment rules.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Being involved in recruiting product professionals — named at your level — carries the fairness obligations of civil service recruitment: published criteria, consistent process, appointment on merit. Option B holds that line while treating the director's underlying need (speed) as legitimate and solvable: compressing the timeline attacks the actual problem; deleting the assessment stage attacks the process's validity — and the scenario is constructed so the deleted stage is exactly where the decision lives, since the evidence in hand is contradictory (paper-leader interviewing unevenly; strong interviewer scoring lower on paper). Resolving that contradiction by fuller evidence is what good selection is; resolving it by paper rank alone (option A) predictably mis-hires, and "performance-manage later" prices a mis-hire as an HR routine when it is actually a year of team damage, a person in the wrong job, and a manager's diary consumed — the most expensive shortcut in management. Option C replaces a structured stage with private judgement, which is both less valid and less defensible than either running the stage or trusting the paper; unstructured senior instinct is precisely what fair process exists to discipline. Option D mistakes a poor suggestion for misconduct: the director proposed, with offered clearance, a bad idea — the proportionate response is to decline it with reasons and an alternative, not to file it; escalation is for pressure that persists after refusal. Note the quiet inclusion stake: process shortcuts systematically favour candidates who look like previous hires, and the "exceptional interviewer, lower paper score" candidate is often exactly who structured full process exists to see fairly.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Two teams claim the same engineer**

Your platform engineer — the only person with deep knowledge of the legacy integration layer — is claimed by two of your teams for the same six-week window: Team One needs them to complete a security remediation with a hard external deadline; Team Two needs them for the data migration underpinning a ministerial commitment two quarters out. Both product managers have escalated to you, each convinced their need is absolute. What is the most effective response?

- A) Give the engineer to Team One — security deadlines are hard, and decide the rest later.
- B) Split the engineer 50/50 across both teams for the six weeks.
- C) Convene both product managers with the engineer: sequence rather than split (security remediation's hard external deadline first), identify what migration preparation genuinely requires the engineer versus what others can advance meanwhile, put knowledge transfer into the plan so the single point of dependency shrinks, and reset Team Two's schedule openly with the stakeholders of the ministerial commitment.
- D) Tell the two product managers to resolve it between themselves — you hired them to make decisions.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The surface question is allocation; the senior question is system design, and option C answers both. Sequencing beats splitting because scarce-expertise work is precisely where context-switching costs bite hardest — a 50/50 split (option B) predictably delivers neither the remediation by its hard deadline nor meaningful migration progress, while feeling fair to everyone except the outcomes. The sequence itself follows from constraint structure: an external hard deadline in-window outranks an internal milestone two quarters out — but option C's remaining moves are what distinguish it from option A's half-answer: decomposing Team Two's need (most "we need the engineer for six weeks" claims contain two weeks of genuinely expert work and four weeks others can do with direction), scheduling the knowledge transfer that shrinks the single point of failure this collision has just exposed (the third collision is prevented now or never), and — critically — resetting the ministerial-commitment schedule in the open, because absorbing the slip silently converts today's manageable adjustment into next quarter's surprise. Option D abdicates a decision that structurally cannot be resolved a level down: each product manager is correctly advocating their product's need; only the portfolio owner holds the frame in which the trade-off exists. Deciding it is not distrust of your product managers — it is the job that justifies your level, done in front of them, with reasons, which is also how they learn to run the same meeting someday. Planning across teams while minimising dependencies and risks, per your named skill, cashes out here as: sequence the scarce, decompose the claimed, transfer the knowledge, and surface the slip.

**Question 5 (moderate) — The metric your own dashboard contradicts**

Preparing your quarterly review, you notice the programme dashboard shows your product group's flagship KPI (renewal completion) at 84%, comfortably green. Your own team-level data shows 84% is produced by a definition change made two quarters ago — abandoned renewals restarted within 7 days are now counted as single successful journeys — and on the old definition the figure would be 76%, below the 80% amber threshold. The definition change was properly approved at the time; the dashboard does not annotate it. Nobody has asked. What is the most effective response?

- A) Report 84% — the definition was properly approved, and reopening it invites confusion.
- B) Report 76% instead, since it reflects the "real" performance.
- C) Report 84% with the definition change annotated, show the old-basis 76% as a comparator with the trend on both bases, and propose the dashboard permanently footnotes definitional breaks — separately assessing whether the new definition is actually the better measure of user success.
- D) Quietly ask the analytics team whether the definition could be revisited next year.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The trap is constructed from legitimacy: the definition change was approved, so option A is procedurally defensible — and substantively misleading, because a green KPI crossing a governance threshold on definitional rather than behavioural change is exactly what boards need to see annotated, and "nobody has asked" is not a disclosure standard. Option C is transparency executed at senior grade: report the official number (unilateral reversion, option B, substitutes your judgement for governance and creates a discontinuity in the other direction — two wrongs, symmetrically), but surface the break, the comparator, and both trends, so the review sees what is actually happening: performance around 76-and-trending-whatever on a consistent basis. The systemic move — footnoting definitional breaks as standard — is what distinguishes handling this instance from fixing this class; measurement systems accumulate approved changes whose combined effect is unaccountable optimism unless breaks are visible by default. And the final clause matters: the new definition may genuinely be better (a user who restarts within a week and completes has, arguably, succeeded) — assessing that on user-success grounds, separately from its convenient effect on the RAG status, is the honest sequence; adopting-because-it-helps and rejecting-because-it-helps are the same corruption with different signs. Option D defers the question precisely as long as the convenient number is in service. Leading teams to create effective measurement methods and being open about decision making meet here: the senior product manager's dashboards should be hardest to game in the direction their own interests point.

**Question 6 (moderate) — The star performer with a pattern**

Your strongest product manager delivers exceptional outcomes, and you have now heard, through three separate informal channels over two months, that they are abrasive in cross-team settings: interrupting a junior researcher repeatedly, dismissing a delivery manager's risk flag with visible contempt, and being "someone people prepare for" before meetings. No formal complaint exists. Their products carry your group's hardest deliverables. What is the most effective response?

- A) Wait for a formal complaint — informal reports are hearsay, and the delivery record speaks for itself.
- B) Raise it directly and specifically with them now: name the reported behaviours and their impact, listen genuinely for context and pressures, make the expectation unambiguous, agree observable changes, and follow up — while separately watching the settings where it occurs and making clear to your group, through your own behaviour, what the standard is.
- C) Restructure meetings so the product manager attends fewer cross-team settings.
- D) Mention it lightly at their next scheduled review in two months, sandwiched between praise for delivery.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Three independent channels over two months is a pattern, not hearsay, and the absence of formal complaint is information about the reporters' safety, not the behaviour's acceptability — juniors do not file complaints against star performers whose deliverables the leadership visibly depends on; they prepare for meetings, then they leave. Option B is the management conversation done properly: specific (named behaviours and impacts, not "some feedback about style"), immediate (option D's two-month deferral prices the researcher's next two months at zero, and the praise-sandwich dilutes the one message that must land), genuinely two-directional (context and pressures are worth hearing — overload and unclear expectations produce abrasion, and fixing causes beats punishing symptoms — but hearing context is not accepting it as license), and structured for follow-through (observable changes, follow-up, your own attention to the settings). Option A is the star-performer exemption in its classic form, and its arithmetic is worse than it looks: the delivery record does not "speak for itself" — it currently prices in the discretionary effort of everyone who has stopped contributing candidly around this person, the risk flags now going unraised (one was already dismissed with contempt — that is a delivery risk, not a style issue), and the eventual departure of the people being interrupted. Option C treats the exposure, not the behaviour: it shrinks the star's development, signals to observers that the organisation routes around its problems, and leaves every setting the star does attend unimproved. Coaching and managing others includes your best people — especially your best people, because your group calibrates its norms on what you tolerate from them.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Continue, pivot, or stop, with the sponsor in the room**

A two-year-old product in your group — sponsored personally and enthusiastically by a director who champions your wider portfolio — shows: usage flat at one-third of the business case projection; unit costs double the target; two pivots already attempted with minor effect; and user research indicating the underlying need is real but largely met through an adjacent channel users prefer. The quarterly review, which the director chairs, is next week. Your evidence-based view is that the product should stop and its residual need be folded into the adjacent channel. What is the most effective response?

- A) Recommend another pivot — stopping a director's sponsored product at their own review risks the portfolio relationship.
- B) Brief the director before the review: walk through the evidence and your stop-and-fold recommendation, acknowledge what the product taught, give them room to test the reasoning and shape the narrative, then take the recommendation to the review with the analysis in full.
- C) Take the stop recommendation straight to the review without warning — surprise prevents lobbying.
- D) Present the evidence neutrally at the review and let the director draw their own conclusion.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The evidence assessment is the easy half: flat usage at a third of case, doubled unit costs, two failed pivots, and — decisively — the need being met by a preferred adjacent channel is the profile where "use evidence to decide when a team should continue, change direction or stop" answers stop; a third pivot (option A) is escalation of commitment with the added corruption that its true purpose is relationship management, spending public money as social lubricant. The hard half is the choreography, and option B gets it exactly right: the pre-brief is not softening, it is sound governance practice with sponsors — surprising a chair with the death of their sponsored product at their own review (option C) converts an evidence conversation into a status confrontation, makes defensiveness the rational response, and burns the relationship for the theatre of ambush; "surprise prevents lobbying" mistakes where lobbying happens (it happens after, in worse fora). The pre-brief gives the director what sponsors legitimately need: time to test your reasoning while changing it is still possible, credit for what the product taught (real, and cheap to give), and agency in the narrative — directors can champion a well-evidenced stop as portfolio discipline, but only if they are not learning of it live. Option D is the abdication dressed as neutrality: you hold an evidence-based view; presenting data without recommendation transfers your accountability to the room and usually yields the sponsor's preferred conclusion by default. Note what B refuses to trade: the recommendation itself, and the full analysis at the review. Pre-briefing shapes how truth arrives, never whether. That distinction — manage the choreography, never the conclusion — is the whole craft of challenging strategy while building trust, and it is why sponsors end up trusting the product people who have stopped things on them.

**Question 8 (moderate) — The associate's flawed analysis, already circulating**

An associate product manager you manage produced a benefits analysis for a stakeholder working group. You discover — after it has circulated — that it contains a significant methodological error: benefits were computed against total users rather than affected users, overstating the headline roughly threefold. The associate is talented, early-career, and visibly proud of the work; the working group meets in four days; one stakeholder has already quoted the headline in an email upward. What is the most effective response?

- A) Correct the document yourself, reissue it quietly, and tell the associate at their next one-to-one.
- B) Tell the associate the error privately, then work through the correction with them and have them reissue it with a clear note before the meeting — including direct notice to the stakeholder who quoted it — and debrief afterwards on both the method and the experience of correcting in public.
- C) Let the meeting proceed on the circulated figures — the error favours your programme, and the methodology is arguable.
- D) Email the working group flagging the error and noting the analysis was produced by a junior colleague and should have been checked.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Strip the options to what each teaches and what each costs. Option C fails first and worst: "the error favours us" is precisely why it must be corrected fast — a threefold overstatement now travelling upward in someone else's email becomes your programme's credibility debt, compounding daily, and "the methodology is arguable" is what people say about errors they have decided to keep. Option D corrects the record while spending the associate — naming a junior colleague's fault to a stakeholder group is a trust breach dressed as transparency, and every early-career person watching learns that visible work is dangerous work. Option A corrects competently and wastes the entire developmental event: the associate learns of their public error after the fact, from its absence — and learns that when things get serious, work is taken away. Option B sequences it correctly: privately first (dignity preserved, facts established — and note the correction is done with them, not for them or merely by them: the method error is a teaching opportunity about denominators that this associate will never forget); reissue under their name with a clear note (owning a correction publicly, with a supportive manager behind you, is how professionals learn that errors are survivable — arguably the single most valuable lesson an early career can bank); direct notice to the quoting stakeholder (the error has propagated; the correction must chase it along the same path, or the meeting reconvenes on two versions of the truth); and the double debrief (method and experience — the second is where resilience is built). Being involved in developing product people, per your role, means engineering exactly this: the record corrected at the speed the error travelled, and the person larger, not smaller, on the other side.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Most and least effective: the accessibility non-negotiable at programme scale**

A programme board — needing your group's flagship service live before a legislative deadline eight weeks out — is told by the delivery workstream that meeting the date requires deferring the remaining accessibility fixes (screen reader failures on two of seven journey steps) into a post-launch backlog "with a committed fix-by date". The board chair turns to you: "Product view?" Rank the responses: which is MOST effective and which is LEAST effective?

1. State that launching with known screen reader failures excludes disabled users from a statutory service and carries legal risk under accessibility regulations; propose the scope alternative — launch on the deadline with the five compliant steps plus an assisted route covering the two failing steps while they are fixed — and if the board still prefers full launch, require the risk to be formally accepted at board level with legal advice minuted.
2. Support the deferral with the committed fix-by date — legislative deadlines outrank all other considerations, and a backlog commitment is a real plan.
3. Refuse: state that you will not allow launch until every accessibility fix is complete, whatever the legislative consequence.
4. Suggest the board seek further analysis of the options and return to the question at its next monthly meeting.

- A) Most effective: 1; least effective: 4
- B) Most effective: 1; least effective: 2
- C) Most effective: 3; least effective: 4
- D) Most effective: 3; least effective: 2

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Response 1 is the senior template for a values-versus-deadline collision, and each of its three moves earns its place: name the stakes accurately (exclusion from a statutory service and legal risk — facts, not positions); engineer the third option (the binary "full launch versus miss the deadline" is almost always false, and the compliant-steps-plus-assisted-route design meets the legislative date and the access obligation — dissolving a dilemma beats winning it); and, if overruled, force the risk to its rightful owner, formally, minuted, with legal advice — because a board that accepts a legal and exclusion risk knowingly and on the record has at least performed governance, and boards asked to minute such acceptance frequently discover their appetite for it was smaller than their appetite for the deadline. For least effective, weigh 2, 3, and 4. Response 3 is wrong but legibly principled — and note it exceeds your authority ("will not allow" is the board's call, not yours) and abandons the disabled users its purism claims to serve if the legislative consequence lands on them; it fails, but visibly and honestly. Response 2 endorses knowingly excluding disabled users from a statutory service on the strength of a backlog promise — post-launch accessibility backlogs are where fixes go to be reprioritised, and "committed fix-by date" has no enforcement once the launch pressure that produced it has dissipated. Response 4, though, is the least effective: eight weeks from a legislative deadline, "return next month" is not caution, it is running out the clock — it guarantees the decision will be made later, worse, under more pressure, with fewer options (the assisted-route build needs the weeks response 4 burns), while dressing abstention as diligence. So most: 1, least: 4 — option A. The senior scoring insight: at board level, the deferral that forecloses the good options is more damaging than the wrong answer honestly given — because the wrong answer can be challenged in the room, while the deferral quietly decides by default. And the durable rule beneath it: non-negotiables are held by engineering compliant paths and forcing formal ownership of deviations — not by volume, and never by delay.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Your strategy, their evidence**

Two years ago you authored your product group's channel strategy: consolidate five entry routes into two, on evidence that fragmentation drove failure demand. The consolidation is 70% complete and has delivered measurable gains. Now a product manager in your group — junior to you, newer to the domain — brings analysis suggesting the final consolidation phase will harm a significant minority: 60,000 annual users of the remaining specialist route show completion rates 30 points higher there than migrated users show on the consolidated route, and their support contacts triple post-migration in the pilot cohort. They are visibly nervous about challenging your strategy. What is the most effective response?

- A) Thank them, but proceed as planned — the strategy's overall gains are proven, the final phase completes the vision, and exceptions erode strategic coherence.
- B) Examine their analysis seriously and visibly: test the data with them (cohort comparability, pilot representativeness), and if it holds, adapt the strategy — retain or redesign the specialist route for that population within the two-route architecture — crediting their analysis in the change, and telling your group why the strategy moved.
- C) Hand the analysis to your own analyst to quietly re-derive before responding at all.
- D) Ask the product manager to present it to the programme board and let governance decide.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This scenario tests the sentence in your framework most people fail in practice: "ensure that the vision and strategy deliver the intended benefits... and use evidence to challenge this when needed" — where the framework does not exempt strategies you wrote. Option B's structure honours both the evidence and the system that produced it. Testing the analysis with them is not hedging — a 30-point completion gap and tripled contacts are exactly the effect sizes that deserve scrutiny of cohort comparability (specialist-route users may differ in ways that travel with them), and rigorous joint examination respects the work more than uncritical acceptance would. But the conditional is real: if it holds, the strategy adapts — and note the adaptation preserves the strategy's intent (reduced failure demand) while amending its mechanism, which is what strategy iteration means; a strategy that cannot metabolise evidence is a monument. The two visibility clauses are the senior craft: crediting the analysis publicly, and narrating the change to your group, convert one nervous junior's courage into your group's standing norm — the next person with inconvenient evidence about your decisions will bring it a month earlier, and that latency is worth more than any single strategy. Option A is authorship bias in strategic dress: "coherence" that requires harming 60,000 identifiable users is aesthetics, not strategy, and the gains of phases one-to-four are sunk — they justify nothing about phase five. Option C's quiet re-derivation, as a first response, tells the product manager their work enters via the tradesman's entrance; verification is legitimate, but with them, not around them. Option D launders your decision through governance: the board should eventually ratify a material strategy change, but sending the junior alone to propose amending your strategy — before you have engaged with it — outsources both the analytical risk and the political risk to the person with least cover. The fingerprint: strategies are owned hardest by those willing to amend them in public, on evidence, with credit flowing to the challenger.

**Question 11 (challenging) — The confidential heads-up**

A trusted peer in the finance directorate tells you, informally and in confidence, that next month's spending review will likely cut your product group's budget by around 25%, and that decisions are still fluid for roughly two weeks. Nothing is announced; your teams are mid-quarter with committed roadmaps; one team is about to sign a 12-month contractor extension that would consume much of the flexibility a cut would demand. What is the most effective response?

- A) Tell your product managers everything now — they deserve the truth, and planning needs lead time.
- B) Do nothing differently — unannounced decisions are not actionable, and acting on leaks rewards leaking.
- C) Act on the substance without breaching the confidence: pause or shorten the contractor extension on defensible portfolio grounds, quietly stress-test your group's plans against a 25% scenario, prepare the value evidence that could inform the still-fluid decision through legitimate channels, and say nothing that identifies the source or the specifics until announcement.
- D) Use the two-week window to lobby the finance director directly, citing what you have heard.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The scenario tests whether you can hold three duties simultaneously: to the confidence (a trusted channel that dies the first time you burn it), to your teams (who bear the consequences of decisions you could have shaped), and to the organisation (whose decision is legitimately still forming). Option C is the only response that serves all three, and its components repay study. The contractor extension is the live wire — signing a 12-month commitment days before a probable 25% cut is exactly the flexibility-destroying move the heads-up exists to prevent — and pausing it is achievable on grounds you can state openly (portfolio prudence ahead of a known spending review cycle; reviews are public knowledge even when their outcomes are not), which is the craft: act where action is defensible without the leak. The stress-test and the value evidence are the same principle extended: scenario planning against cuts is ordinary senior hygiene, and "decisions are still fluid" means the two weeks are precisely when well-marshalled benefit evidence, routed through legitimate channels — your director, formal review submissions — can still inform the outcome; that is not gaming the process, it is the process. Option A discharges your candour instinct at your source's expense and your teams' too: "everything now" spreads unactionable anxiety mid-quarter, identifies the leak by its specificity, and — when the number lands at 15% or 35% — costs you the credibility the real announcement will need. Option B is principled-sounding abdication: "not actionable" is false (the extension proves it), and treating foreknowledge as contamination rather than stewardship confuses your comfort with your duty. Option D burns everything at once — source, channel, and your standing — by converting a confidence into a lobbying credential; the finance director's first question ("who told you?") has no good answer. The senior pattern: metabolise privileged information into defensible actions, never into quotable claims — protect the channel, position the group, and let the announcement arrive to a portfolio already standing where the wave will land.

**Question 12 (challenging) — The supplier demo that doesn't add up**

At a market-engagement day, a supplier demonstrates an AI triage tool claiming "94% accuracy, deployed with three government departments". Your director is enthusiastic and asks you to "get a pilot moving this quarter". Your observations: the demo ran on the supplier's curated data; "accuracy" was never defined (against what baseline, which error types, whose labels?); the three departments are unnamed; and your own casework's error tolerance is asymmetric — a false "routine" label on a safeguarding-relevant case is far costlier than the reverse. Procurement rules apply; your teams are already at capacity. What is the most effective response?

- A) Decline to engage — AI triage vendors overclaim routinely, and the observations confirm this one is not credible.
- B) Channel the director's enthusiasm into a structured evaluation: define your own success measures first (accuracy by error type on your data, with the safeguarding asymmetry weighted; baseline against current triage), require the reference-department evidence, run any pilot through proper procurement as a time-boxed trial against those pre-registered measures, and give the director a realistic quarter-shaped first step — the evaluation design, not the deployment.
- C) Start the pilot as asked — the director's instruction is clear, procurement can be handled as a small-value trial, and concerns can be tested inside the pilot itself.
- D) Ask the supplier to re-run the demo on a sample of your data before deciding anything.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The scenario braids four senior duties — evidence discipline, stakeholder management upward, procurement propriety, and duty of care to users the tool would touch — and option B is the only response that serves all four. Its decisive move is sequencing: define success measures before engaging further, because measures defined after a vendor relationship forms are negotiated with the vendor's numbers in the room; "pre-registered" is doing real work — a pilot whose success criteria are written first cannot be retro-fitted to whatever the pilot happens to show. The safeguarding asymmetry is the domain judgement that generic enthusiasm misses: a single accuracy figure is meaningless where error costs are asymmetric, and weighting error types by consequence is precisely the product judgement your role exists to inject — note this concern alone could disqualify a tool that is genuinely "94% accurate" on a symmetric measure. The upward move is equally deliberate: the director's enthusiasm is an asset mishandled by refusal (option A converts plausible scepticism into a closed door — the observations justify rigour, not verdict; "vendors overclaim routinely" is a prior, not a finding, and the underlying need for better triage is real), so B gives the director a genuine quarter-shaped deliverable — the evaluation design and procurement route — which respects both the instruction's intent and its impossibility as stated. Option C fails on three counts at once: "handled as a small-value trial" is the procurement phrase that precedes findings of improper disaggregation; concerns "tested inside the pilot" means measures defined after commitment; and capacity-less teams absorb the pilot by degrading committed work — the honest version of C requires naming what stops. Option D sounds empirical but hands the supplier your data under no framework — no procurement basis, no data-sharing agreement, no protection against the demo simply being re-curated on your sample; a supplier-run test on your data without your measures is their demo with your props. The durable senior pattern: meet enthusiasm with structure, meet claims with your own definitions, meet vendors through the process that keeps you free to say no — and always locate the asymmetric error before admiring the average.

### Preparation tips

- Extract the fingerprints, not the answers. Across these scenarios the effective responses: act through structures and make deviations formally owned; correct records at the speed errors travelled; give recoveries to the people who need the growth, with visible backing; engineer third options before accepting binaries; pre-brief sponsors on choreography while never negotiating conclusions; and metabolise privileged or inconvenient information into defensible action rather than quotable claims.
- Audit your instincts against your level. Many wrong options here were right one level down — doing the recovery yourself, absorbing the redirection, deferring to the process owner. Senior scoring rewards routing action through the people and structures you now hold, and reserving your direct intervention for what only you can decide.
- For most/least formats, locate the least effective by asking "which response decides by default?" — deferrals that foreclose options, silences that let errors compound, neutrality that yields to the strongest voice in the room. At senior level, abdication usually outdamages honest error.
- Hold the non-negotiables list consciously: user safety, accessibility, evidential honesty, fair process, data protection, procurement propriety. Effective responses trade dates, scope, and comfort freely — and these never; the craft is engineering the path that makes the non-negotiable affordable.
- Rehearse the people patterns until they are reflexes: specific-immediate-private for behaviour conversations; correction-with-them for junior errors; credit-flowing-to-challengers for evidence against your own positions. Your group's culture is the compound interest on these reflexes.
- Use your real diary as the practice set: after each significant judgement call, write the four options you actually had and rank them honestly. Reflective practice is named in your Agile and Lean skill — this is what it looks like applied to yourself.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- The star-performer exemption and the sponsor exemption: adjusting standards for people whose delivery or patronage you depend on. Assessments probe these directly, and so does your group — which calibrates its norms on what you tolerate.
- Kindness that stunts: quiet fixes, absorbed recoveries, deferred feedback, praise-sandwiched corrections. The developmental cost of protecting people from their own growth is a senior-level scoring dimension.
- Managing truth's arrival instead of truth: filtered dashboards, unannotated definition changes, evidence without recommendations, neutrality in rooms where you hold a view. Choreography is legitimate; conclusion-shaping is not — know the line.
- Escalation of commitment in strategic dress: the third pivot, the completing phase, the "coherence" that requires harming identifiable users. Sunk gains justify nothing about the marginal decision.
- Binary acceptance: full launch versus missed deadline, this team versus that team, engage versus refuse the vendor. The highest-scoring responses usually contain an engineered third option — practise generating one before choosing between two.
- Spending trust for convenience: hidden double-books, laundered confidences, juniors sent alone to carry your risk, suppliers handed data outside process. Every scenario's least effective response converts a relationship or a safeguard into short-term ease; the pattern is recognisable, and recognised.
