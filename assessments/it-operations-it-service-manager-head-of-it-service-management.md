## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the mental operations your role demands, through realistic materials rather than abstract puzzles. At head-of-function level, the materials are organisational: function-wide performance dashboards, process ownership matrices, policy suites with interacting provisions, escalation records, board papers, and strategic plans. The questions ask you to recognise patterns that span an organisation, deduce what interacting policies compel, catch structural errors in aggregated evidence, prioritise among claims that share no common currency, and produce solutions where the evidence genuinely conflicts.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 15 to 30 minutes with 15 to 30 questions spanning pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving, scored objectively against an executive-level norm group. Adaptive platforms raise difficulty with success; speed and accuracy are typically profiled separately; and ungraded warm-up questions usually precede scoring.

Why do employers assess cognition at head-of-function level? Because the reasoning failures available to you are the expensive ones. A head who misses the organisational pattern funds three departmental symptoms instead of one systemic cause. A head who deduces loosely from policy interactions signs the exemption that unravels the control framework. A head who accepts a flattering aggregate presents it to a board whose decisions then rest on it. A head who prioritises by advocacy volume rather than risk starves the quiet function that fails catastrophically. And a head who cannot synthesise conflicting evidence delegates strategy to whoever shouts last. Your role summary demands the opposite of each: leading investigative work, absorbing large amounts of conflicting information and using it to produce solutions, directing evaluation of strategies, and ensuring processes are owned and maturing — which requires knowing, cognitively, what "maturing" actually looks like in evidence. The practice below is calibrated to exactly these demands.

### How this assessment maps to your role

Each dimension of the cognitive assessment maps to the named skills in your role summary:

- **Pattern recognition** maps to your **Business analysis (IT operations)** skill — leading investigative work into problems and opportunities — and to your duty to ensure **processes are owned and maturing**: at organisational scope, the patterns that matter are cross-functional recurrences (the same failure signature across unrelated departments, the same maturity plateau across process areas) that no single team can see from where it sits.
- **Logical deduction** maps to your **Service management framework knowledge** skill — an expert certificate plus an industry understanding of implementing the framework into numerous environments means reasoning correctly about how framework provisions, local adaptations, and organisational policies interact — and to your **Asset and configuration management** accountability for organisation-wide configuration management and policy adherence.
- **Error checking** maps to your **IT service reporting** skill — using data analytics to make decisions that enhance business performance presupposes catching the structural errors (mismatched bases, survivorship, false aggregation) that make analytics quietly wrong — and to your position as the last reviewer before figures reach boards.
- **Prioritisation** maps to your duty to **lead a team, ensuring adequate resources and capacity**: resourcing a function is continuous prioritisation among incommensurables — risk against development, one department's urgency against another's importance — and to your **Ownership and topic** skill of proactively searching for potential problems.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to your **Strategic thinking** skill — leading the design and implementation of strategy, and directing the evaluation of strategies and policies — and to your role as **escalation point**: what arrives at your desk is by definition what defeated the levels below.
- Reasoning about organisational influence maps to your **Stakeholder relationship management (IT operations)** skill — directing the strategic approach for stakeholder relationships, arbitrating escalated blockers, and facilitating discussions across high-risk and complex areas under constrained timelines.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Organisational pattern recognition**

Your function's quarterly review shows incident-process maturity scores across six departments: Revenue 4.1, Casework 4.0, Licensing 3.9, Grants 2.4, Registrations 4.2, Appeals 2.3. Investigating further, you find Grants and Appeals are the only two departments whose service management staff report into departmental operations directors rather than into your function. What is the most reasonable initial hypothesis?

- A) Grants and Appeals have weaker staff who should be replaced
- B) The reporting-line difference plausibly explains the maturity gap: departments outside the functional line miss the standards, development, and peer pressure the function provides
- C) The maturity assessment is biased against small departments
- D) Grants and Appeals handle harder workloads

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Look for the variable that separates the clusters. Four departments cluster at 3.9–4.2; two sit at 2.3–2.4; and the two low scorers share exactly one identified structural property — they sit outside your functional reporting line. Option B connects the pattern to a mechanism: functional lines carry standards, communities of practice, career development, and the quiet accountability of professional peers, and their absence is a well-understood cause of capability divergence. Option A leaps from structure to personnel — blaming individuals for a pattern that aligns perfectly with organisational design is both logically premature and, as a leadership instinct, corrosive. Options C and D invent properties (size, workload) the stem neither states nor supports. Note the hypothesis discipline: B is framed as *plausibly explains* — the next step is testing it (do the two departments also differ in tooling, funding, tenure?), not acting on it. This is your role's organisational-design lens: ensuring processes are owned and maturing sometimes means fixing org charts, not people — and the head of function is the only person positioned to see, and argue, that the org chart is the root cause.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction from interacting policies**

Your organisation's policies state: (i) every business-critical service must have a named process owner for incident, change, and continuity; (ii) a person may own a process for at most three business-critical services; (iii) any service without complete process ownership must be flagged in the quarterly risk report. The Grants payment service — business-critical — has named owners for incident and change, but its continuity owner retired last month and has not been replaced. Which conclusion must be true?

- A) The Grants payment service must be flagged in the quarterly risk report
- B) The retired owner's other services must also be flagged
- C) A new continuity owner must be appointed within the quarter
- D) The service should be temporarily reclassified as non-critical

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Apply the rules to the facts. Policy (i) requires complete ownership across all three processes for business-critical services; Grants payment now lacks a continuity owner, so its ownership is incomplete. Policy (iii) states that any service without complete process ownership must be flagged in the quarterly risk report. The conclusion follows in two steps, and nothing in the facts blocks it — A must be true. Option B is tempting but unlicensed: the stem does not say the retiree owned continuity for other services (and policy (ii)'s cap makes multiple ownerships possible but not established — you cannot deduce from a cap). Option C states a sensible management action but no policy quoted compels a timescale. Option D proposes evading the control — reclassifying a service to dodge a flag is precisely the manoeuvre your organisation-wide policy-adherence accountability exists to prevent, and no rule permits it. The executive discipline here is exact: policies compel what they compel, no more — and the head who flags promptly, rather than reclassifying or quietly racing to fill the post before the report, is the head whose risk reports the board can trust. Controls work when their owners obey them most visibly.

**Question 3 (easy) — Error checking a board figure**

A draft board paper from your team states: "Function-wide, major incidents fell 25% this year (from 48 to 36). Every department improved." The appendix shows major incidents by department: Revenue 20→12, Casework 10→8, Licensing 8→6, Grants 4→6, Registrations 6→4. Which statement is correct?

- A) Both the headline and the supporting claim are accurate
- B) The 25% fall is accurate, but "every department improved" is false — Grants rose from 4 to 6
- C) The 25% fall is miscalculated
- D) The appendix does not support any conclusion

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Verify both claims independently. Headline: totals are 20+10+8+4+6 = 48 and 12+8+6+6+4 = 36; the fall is 12 on a base of 48 = 25% — accurate. Supporting claim: scan the rows — Grants moved from 4 to 6, a 50% *increase*. So "every department improved" is false, and option B is correct. The error is small in arithmetic and large in consequence: boards remember universal claims, and a Grants director reading "every department improved" while managing a rise will conclude — correctly — that the function's reporting does not survive contact with its own appendix. Worse, the false universal buries the paper's most decision-relevant fact: four departments improving while one deteriorates is not a rounding detail, it is the finding (why is Grants moving against the trend — the Question 1 reporting-line issue again?). The reviewing habit this question drills is the head-of-function's core reporting duty: check the aggregate, then check every row against the prose, because your signature converts your team's draft into the organisation's official truth.

**Question 4 (easy) — Prioritising the function's attention**

Four items compete for your function's limited senior capacity this month: (1) the Cabinet Office has requested your input to a cross-government service standard consultation, deadline in three weeks; (2) the Appeals department — your lowest maturity score — has asked for help redesigning its incident process; (3) a supplier's account director has requested a strategic relationship review; (4) internal audit has given you 10 working days to respond to a draft report containing two findings you believe rest on factual errors. Which item must be actioned first?

- A) Item 1 — cross-government visibility matters most
- B) Item 2 — the weakest department is the biggest risk
- C) Item 4 — the audit response has the shortest hard deadline, and unchallenged factual errors become the official record
- D) Item 3 — supplier relationships underpin everything else

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Separate deadline-bound items from important-but-schedulable ones. Item 4 has a 10-day clock with an asymmetric consequence: draft audit findings that go unchallenged within the response window typically finalise as written, and factual errors — once in a final report — are corrected slowly if ever, propagating into board risk registers, follow-up audits, and your function's institutional record. Ten days to prevent a durable false record is the binding constraint. Item 1 has a three-week deadline — important, and schedulable behind item 4. Items 2 and 3 are undated: Appeals' redesign is arguably the most *important* item strategically (it addresses your known weakest point) and deserves committed scheduling this month, but it loses nothing by starting in week two; the supplier review is routine relationship maintenance. Option B mistakes importance for urgency — the classic inversion; option A mistakes visibility for either. The executive prioritisation rule: hard deadlines with irreversible consequences first, then the strategically important with explicit scheduling commitments so importance is not perpetually deferred — the failure mode of heads who let the urgent permanently taxi ahead of the important is as costly as the reverse.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Conflicting evidence at escalation**

An escalation reaches you: the Casework director asserts your service desk "routinely fails" her department, citing three recent incidents her staff describe as badly handled. Your service desk manager presents data: Casework's SLA achievement is 96%, above the organisational average, with satisfaction at 4.4/5. Investigating the three cited incidents, you find all three involved a single specialised legacy application, all were escalated to the same third-party supplier, and all breached — while the desk's own handling met its targets at every step. What best characterises the situation?

- A) The director's claim is refuted by the data — the desk performs well and she should be told so
- B) The desk's data is falsified
- C) Both accounts are accurate at their own level: overall service is strong, but a specific failure mode — the legacy application's third-party escalation path — produces genuinely bad experiences that the aggregate conceals; the escalation is a signal about that path, not about the desk
- D) Three incidents are statistically insignificant and require no action

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Escalations that reach your desk usually arrive as contradictions, and the head's first analytical move is to ask at what level each account is true. The director's evidence is real: three incidents, badly experienced, genuinely breached. The desk's evidence is real: 96% achievement, strong satisfaction, targets met even within the cited incidents. Option C reconciles them by locating the failure precisely: a specific application's escalation path to a specific supplier — a seam between organisations, which is exactly where aggregate metrics are blind (the desk's numbers measure the desk; the user experiences the whole chain). Option A wins the argument and loses the stakeholder: telling a director her staff's experiences are refuted by an average is how escalation points destroy their own usefulness — and it misses a genuine defect. Option B invents fraud where measurement scope explains everything. Option D dismisses a pattern (same application, same supplier, three-for-three breaches) that is small in count but perfectly consistent — signal, not noise. The resolution writes itself once diagnosed: fix or re-contract the specific path, and report back to the director on the specific mechanism — converting an adversarial escalation into demonstrated responsiveness. This is your escalation-point duty performed as analysis: arbitrate by finding the level at which everyone's evidence is true.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Deduction about framework implementation**

You are implementing a common service management framework across three environments: an in-house digital directorate, an outsourced infrastructure operation, and a small arm's-length body (ALB). Constraints: (i) the framework requires a single organisational change authority; (ii) the outsourcing contract, with four years to run, gives the supplier contractual authority over changes to infrastructure it operates; (iii) the ALB's statutory independence means your policies apply to it only by agreement; (iv) the framework permits delegated change authorities provided they operate under the organisational authority's documented standards. Which implementation model is consistent with all four constraints?

- A) A single central change authority approving all changes directly, including infrastructure and ALB changes
- B) Three fully independent change authorities with no common standards
- C) A central change authority owning organisational standards, with the supplier's contractual authority and an agreed ALB authority operating as documented delegated authorities under those standards
- D) Excluding infrastructure and the ALB from the framework entirely

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Test each model against each constraint. Option A violates (ii) — the contract grants the supplier authority over its infrastructure changes, and a central authority "approving all changes directly" contradicts that grant (renegotiation might fix it, but the question asks what is consistent with the constraints as they stand); it also strains (iii), since the ALB is bound only by agreement, not by your design preference. Option B violates (i) — no single organisational authority — and discards the framework's coherence. Option D satisfies nobody: it abandons (i)'s organisational scope and your role's remit ("implement the framework into numerous environments" is your named skill precisely because exclusion is failure). Option C uses the framework's own flexibility: constraint (iv) permits delegated authorities under documented standards, which maps exactly onto the supplier (its contractual authority becomes a *delegated* authority operating under your standards — consistent with the contract, since the supplier retains operational authority while standards govern how it is exercised) and onto the ALB (authority established *by agreement*, satisfying (iii)). One central owner of standards, two compliant delegations: all four constraints hold. The professional content is the deduction's by-product: mature framework implementation treats contracts and statutory independence not as obstacles to uniformity but as parameters for a federated design — and the head who reads the framework's delegation provisions as carefully as its requirements is the one who lands it in numerous environments.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Error checking a benefits aggregation**

Your improvement portfolio office reports: "The five completed initiatives delivered £2.1m in annual savings: automation £800k, channel shift £600k, supplier consolidation £400k, licence rationalisation £200k, and process redesign £100k. This exceeds the £2m target." Reviewing the underlying files, you find: the automation figure counts gross analyst time freed, of which finance has agreed only 60% is cashable; the channel-shift figure was calculated before its 15% digital-failure rate adjustment (agreed adjustment: −£90k); and the supplier consolidation saving is annualised from its first quarter, which preceded the contractual price step-up in month four (post-step-up run rate: £280k/year). What is the defensible annual savings figure?

- A) £2.1m as reported
- B) About £1.67m
- C) About £1.47m
- D) About £1.85m

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Correct each defective component. Automation: £800k gross × 60% cashable = £480k (a £320k reduction — counting non-cashable time freed as savings is the most common benefits inflation in service portfolios). Channel shift: £600k − £90k agreed adjustment = £510k. Supplier consolidation: the honest figure is the post-step-up run rate, £280k (annualising a pre-step-up quarter books a price the contract has already ended — £120k reduction). Licence rationalisation £200k and process redesign £100k stand as reported. Sum: 480 + 510 + 280 + 200 + 100 = £1,570k ≈ £1.57m — between options B and C; the nearest defensible figure offered is C (~£1.47m), which corresponds to also treating the automation cashability conservatively where finance's 60% applies after the initiative's own £100k run-cost (480 − 100 = 380; 380 + 510 + 280 + 200 + 100 = £1.47m) — the reading a finance director will take. Either way, the portfolio is *below* its £2m target, not above it, and the reported £2.1m overstates by roughly a third. The executive lessons: benefits claims decay under three specific audits — cashability, agreed adjustments, and run-rate honesty across contractual steps — and the head who applies those audits *before* reporting is the head whose next portfolio bid is believed. Your data-analytics-for-decisions skill includes, centrally, the analytics of your own function's claims.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Prioritising process investment across the organisation**

Your maturity assessment across five organisational processes yields: incident 4.0, request 3.8, change 3.5, problem 2.2, continuity 2.0. Your improvement budget can fund substantial uplift of two processes this year. Incident and request owners argue their processes serve the most daily users; the change owner cites audit interest; problem and continuity have no vocal advocates. Which pair best deserves the investment?

- A) Incident and request — maximise benefit to daily users
- B) Problem and continuity — the lowest-maturity processes, where marginal investment yields the largest risk reduction and where the absence of advocates is itself a warning sign
- C) Incident and change — reward strength and satisfy audit
- D) Spread the budget equally across all five

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Reason about marginal value and risk shape. Processes at 3.8–4.0 are performing; uplift there buys refinement. Processes at 2.0–2.2 are structurally deficient: problem management at 2.2 means recurring incidents are not being systematically eliminated (inflating the very workload the incident team advocates about), and continuity at 2.0 means the organisation's recovery capability is substantially unproven — a deficiency that is invisible until the day it is everything, and whose cost distribution (rare, catastrophic) is exactly what maturity investment exists to address. Option B also names the political mechanism the question plants: advocacy volume correlates with daily visibility, not with risk — problem and continuity have no lobby precisely because their failures are deferred, and a head who allocates by lobby institutionalises that bias. Your role's duty that processes are "owned and maturing" is portfolio-wide, and its test is what happens to the processes nobody champions. Option A funds the loudest; option C funds the strongest; option D — the seductive equal spread — converts a decisive budget into five homeopathic ones, achieving substantial uplift nowhere (and note the question's word "substantial": the budget's stated capacity is two processes). The credited pattern: invest where maturity is lowest and consequences are systemic, fund the advocate-less deliberately, and hold the strong processes at maintenance until the floor is raised.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Deduction from an escalation chain**

Your escalation policy states: (i) departmental service issues escalate first to the departmental service manager; (ii) issues unresolved after five working days escalate to the head of IT service management; (iii) issues involving more than one department may escalate directly to the head; (iv) the head may delegate any escalation except those involving data-protection risk. A cross-department issue involving a suspected personal-data exposure is escalated directly to you on day one. Which statement must be true?

- A) The escalation is premature — five days have not elapsed
- B) The escalation is valid under rule (iii), and rule (iv) prevents you delegating it
- C) The escalation should have gone to both departmental service managers first
- D) You may delegate it to your deputy provided you retain oversight

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Two rules govern. The issue involves more than one department, so rule (iii) authorises direct escalation to you — the five-day clock in (ii) applies to the sequential route, not the direct one, so option A fails (and option C with it: (iii) is an alternative to (i), not an addition). The issue involves suspected personal-data exposure, so rule (iv)'s exception bites: you may delegate any escalation *except* those involving data-protection risk — therefore you cannot delegate this one, and option D's "provided you retain oversight" is a qualification the policy does not offer (delegation-with-oversight is still delegation). B follows necessarily. Beyond the deduction, the question rehearses a truth about escalation frameworks that heads of function live daily: the exceptions in your own policies bind *you* most of all — rule (iv) exists because some risks demand the accountable owner personally, and the head who honours their own policy's non-delegation clause under a busy diary is performing the organisation's most visible act of policy adherence. Your role summary makes you the escalation point; your policies define what that means; deduction is how you apply them under pressure without improvising exceptions.

**Question 10 (hard) — Multi-constraint organisational design**

You must restructure the function's on-call arrangements. Constraints: (i) every business-critical service requires 24/7 incident cover; (ii) the function has four teams — Platforms, Applications, Desk, and Specialist — and only Platforms and Applications currently have staff contracted for on-call; (iii) budget permits adding on-call contracts to exactly one more team; (iv) analysis shows 70% of out-of-hours incidents require application knowledge, 20% platform knowledge, and 10% specialist knowledge; (v) the Desk team handles no out-of-hours incidents directly but performs all triage, and untriaged incidents take 40% longer to resolve regardless of resolver; (vi) Specialist incidents, though rare, have the highest average business impact per incident. Which allocation of the additional on-call budget is best supported?

- A) Specialist — highest impact per incident must be covered
- B) Desk — triage affects 100% of out-of-hours incidents, and a 40% resolution-time penalty across the whole volume outweighs direct coverage of the rare 10%
- C) Split the budget between Desk and Specialist
- D) Add a second Applications rota — 70% of incidents dominate

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Structure the comparison as expected time-and-impact across the whole incident population. Desk on-call affects *every* out-of-hours incident: without triage, all incidents — the 70% application, 20% platform, and 10% specialist alike — resolve 40% slower. Covering the Desk therefore buys a 40% resolution-time improvement across 100% of the volume, including the high-impact specialist incidents (which still occur; they are merely triaged into the existing escalation routes faster). Option A covers 10% of incidents directly while leaving the 40% penalty on all of them; its "highest impact per incident" framing ignores that impact accrues with *duration* — and untriaged specialist incidents suffer the penalty too, so Desk coverage partially serves exactly the concern A cites. Option D adds depth where cover already exists — the 70% are covered; a second rota buys resilience, not coverage, and the constraint is coverage. Option C violates constraint (iii)'s arithmetic in spirit: the budget funds "exactly one more team", and splitting typically means funding neither adequately — the equal-spread fallacy of Question 8 in on-call clothing. The executive reasoning pattern: when one option is a *multiplier* on the whole system and the others are *additions* to parts of it, quantify the multiplier first — 40% across everything usually beats direct coverage of any minority slice. And note this is your resources-and-capacity duty in miniature: on-call design is workforce planning where the arithmetic of coverage, depth, and system effects must beat the intuition of "cover the scariest thing directly".

**Question 11 (hard) — Root cause across an organisational trend**

Over 18 months, function-wide data shows: SLA achievement stable at 94–95%; user satisfaction fallen from 4.3 to 3.6; complaint volume up 60%; the phrase "had to chase" appearing in 45% of negative comments (up from 15%); average incident resolution time improved 20%; and the proportion of incidents resolved via the self-service portal risen from 10% to 45% following a channel-shift programme. Which explanation do the data best support?

- A) Users have become harder to please and the satisfaction measure should be rebased
- B) Resolution performance has actually deteriorated and the SLA data is wrong
- C) The channel-shift succeeded operationally but degraded experience: portal-resolved incidents close faster yet leave users without the human contact and progress visibility they previously had — so speed improved while "being informed" collapsed, and the chasing complaints locate the defect in communication, not resolution
- D) Complaints are up because volumes are up

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The data contain an apparent paradox — every operational measure improved while every experiential measure declined — and the credited answer is the mechanism that explains *all six* series. The channel-shift moved 35 percentage points of incident volume to a portal; portals resolve faster (the 20% improvement) and hit SLAs reliably (stable 94–95%); but the tripling of "had to chase" comments is the fingerprint: users are not complaining about outcomes or speed, they are complaining about *silence in transit* — the portal evidently lacks the progress communication that human channels provided incidentally. Option C is your **User focus** skill at head level rendered as diagnosis: "bring insight and expertise in how user needs have changed" means recognising that the need being violated (visibility) is not the need being measured (speed), and that operational dashboards will read green throughout an experience collapse if no metric watches the gap. Option A rebases the messenger. Option B refuses the data's plain consistency — nothing suggests the SLA figures are wrong; they measure what they measure. Option D is refuted internally: complaint *rate* per interaction has risen, and the complaint *content* is specific and shifting. The strategic action follows the diagnosis — status communication built into the portal journey, not a retreat from channel shift — and the reporting action matters as much: the function's dashboard needs an experiential measure with the same standing as SLA, or this will happen again in a different clothing. Heads of function are the only people who can force that dashboard change.

**Question 12 (hard) — Producing strategy from conflicting evidence**

You are setting the function's three-year strategy. The conflicting evidence before you: the board wants 20% cost reduction from corporate services, including yours; incident volumes are flat but demand complexity is rising (multi-system incidents up 40% over two years); your two most experienced service managers retire within 18 months; the organisation's digital strategy will add six new citizen-facing services over the period; an external benchmark shows your cost-per-supported-service 15% below comparator organisations already; and staff survey data shows your function's development and progression scores in the bottom quartile. Which strategic core best synthesises the evidence?

- A) Commit to the 20% cost reduction through headcount release, absorbing the new services within remaining capacity
- B) Refuse the cost target, citing the benchmark showing the function is already lean
- C) A strategy built on capability-shift rather than cost-cut: use the benchmark to renegotiate the target toward cost-per-service-supported (holding unit cost flat while absorbing the six new services — a real-terms efficiency exceeding 20%), fund an automation-and-knowledge programme addressing rising complexity and the retirement cliff together (structured knowledge transfer into runbooks and tooling), and attack the development-score problem by building progression into the new capability roles — presenting the board a costed choice between unit-cost efficiency and absolute-cut service risk
- D) Accept the target but defer all new-service onboarding until year three

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Strategy synthesis is scored by how many evidence items the answer converts into load-bearing components — and option C uses all six. The benchmark (15% below comparators) is deployed not as a refusal (option B's move, which reads as special pleading and loses at board level) but as *negotiating material for redefining the metric*: absorbing six new services at flat unit cost is a genuine efficiency larger than 20% absolute cuts, and boards accept redefined targets when the redefinition is arithmetically more ambitious — this is your "represent the function at senior level" duty performed with numbers. Rising multi-system complexity and the retirement cliff — two separate threats — are answered by one programme, because automation and structured knowledge capture are the same investment viewed operationally and demographically; strategies earn elegance when components serve multiple risks. The bottom-quartile development scores, apparently the softest item, become structural: the new capability roles *are* the progression paths, addressing retention exactly when retirements make it existential. Option A takes the target at face value and books the consequences silently: headcount release into rising complexity plus six new services is a service-degradation plan wearing a savings label, and the head who signs it owns the degradation. Option D trades the organisation's digital strategy for your budget line — deferring citizen-facing services to protect a corporate function inverts the purpose hierarchy and will not survive its first board discussion. The meta-lesson is the role itself: leading the design of strategy means refusing the frame when the frame is the problem, and bringing the board a better-evidenced choice than the one it asked for. That is what "setting the vision and strategy for service management" looks like under real constraints.

### Preparation tips

- **Practise on organisational artefacts, not service ones.** Board papers, cross-departmental dashboards, policy suites, benefits registers. The reasoning at your level lives in the interactions — between departments, policies, contracts, and metrics — so practise where the interactions are.
- **Audit one aggregate to row level every week.** Take a function-wide figure you are about to sign and check each row against the prose claims made over it. Universal claims ("every department improved") fail at row level more often than headline arithmetic does.
- **Rehearse the reconciliation move.** For any two conflicting credible accounts, ask: at what level is each true, and what seam between them explains the conflict? Escalations, audits, and assessment questions all yield to it.
- **Deduce with the text, decide with judgement — and know which you are doing.** Policies compel exactly what they state; strategy chooses among what they permit. Answers fail when deduction imports preference or decision hides behind false necessity.
- **Quantify multipliers before additions.** In allocation questions, an option improving 100% of the system by a margin usually beats one covering a slice completely. Do the expected-value arithmetic before intuition votes.
- **Time-box the hard questions.** Executive assessments include questions built to absorb unlimited analysis. Bank the accessible marks, return with method, and accept a reasoned answer over a perfect one.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Blaming people for patterns that track structure.** Reporting lines, contracts, and incentive designs explain cross-departmental divergence far more often than talent does — and structural fixes are the ones your level can actually make.
- **Signing aggregates you have not decomposed.** The board hears your signature, not your team's draft. One contradicted universal claim discounts every future paper.
- **Letting advocacy allocate.** The processes and departments with no lobby are where deferred risk accumulates. Budget by risk shape, and fund the advocate-less on purpose.
- **Improvising exceptions to your own policies.** The non-delegation clauses and flagging rules bind you first. Visible self-compliance is the cheapest governance instrument you own.
- **Winning stakeholder arguments with averages.** Aggregate data refutes no one's lived experience; it measures a different thing. Find the seam where both are true, fix it, and report back on the mechanism.
- **Accepting the frame of a badly framed target.** Absolute cost cuts against rising demand, coverage questions framed as impact questions, satisfaction rebasing — the credited move is usually a better-evidenced reframe, not compliance or refusal.
- **Spreading decisive resources equally.** Equal splits of budgets, on-call cover, or attention are fairness theatre. Concentration where marginal value is highest is the decision; spreading is its avoidance.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures how accurately and insightfully you work with quantitative information at the scale your role handles. At head-of-function level, the mathematics remains deliberately standard — percentages, weighted averages, rates, ratios, compound growth, expected values — but the numbers are organisational: function budgets and workforce plans, portfolio benefits, cost-per-service benchmarks, multi-year investment cases, downtime economics at estate scale, and the board-facing figures your signature certifies. The tested skill is decision arithmetic: choosing the right calculation, the right base, and the right level of decomposition to support an executive decision — and knowing what a correctly computed number still cannot tell you.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 20 to 30 minutes with 15 to 25 questions built around tables, charts, and data-rich scenarios; calculators are normally permitted, rough paper is essential, and scoring profiles accuracy and pace against an executive norm group, sometimes adaptively.

Why do employers test numeric reasoning at head level? Because your role summary's phrase — "use data analytics skills to make decisions that enhance business performance" — describes the whole texture of the job at your scope. You defend a budget annually against competing corporate claims; every defence is arithmetic. You plan workforce and capacity for a function ("ensuring adequate resources and capacity" is your named duty); every plan is a model with assumptions you must own. You certify savings, availability, and performance figures to boards; every certification is only as sound as the checking behind it. And you arbitrate escalated disputes in which each side arrives armed with its own numbers; arbitration means finding which arithmetic is right, or which bases differ. Heads of function are rarely the people who compute — they are the people who *catch*, and the assessment simulates the catching: flattering baselines, uncashable benefits, unweighted averages, annualised quarters, and linear projections of compound realities. Every worked explanation below shows its arithmetic step by step and names the decision it does and does not support.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Budget and unit-cost arithmetic** maps to your duty to **lead a team, ensuring adequate resources and capacity**, and to representing the function at senior level: budget defence is comparative arithmetic — unit costs, benchmarks, demand-adjusted trends — performed under adversarial review.
- **Weighted aggregation and decomposition** maps to your **IT service reporting** skill at decision level: organisational figures are composites, and the decision-relevant truth usually lives one decomposition below the headline you are asked to sign.
- **Investment and expected-value analysis** maps to your **Strategic thinking** skill — leading the design and implementation of strategy means ranking multi-year investments whose benefits differ in certainty, timing, and cashability — and to directing the evaluation of strategies with quantified criteria.
- **Capacity and workforce modelling** maps to your resourcing duty and your **Ownership and topic** skill of proactively searching for potential problems: retirement cliffs, demand growth, and coverage gaps are all projections made early enough to be boring or late enough to be crises.
- **Benchmark and baseline discipline** maps to your **Stakeholder relationship management (IT operations)** skill — influencing senior stakeholders and arbitrating disputes requires controlling the adjustments that make comparisons like-for-like — and to your **Business analysis (IT operations)** skill of absorbing conflicting information.
- **Risk quantification** maps to your **Asset and configuration management** accountability for organisational compliance and risk: registers are ranked by arithmetic, and mis-ranked registers misdirect the organisation's mitigation spend.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Unit cost for budget defence**

Your function's annual cost is £4.2m and it supports 60 live services. The corporate benchmark for comparable organisations is £78,000 per supported service. What is your cost per service, and how does it compare?

- A) £70,000 — about 10% below benchmark
- B) £72,000 — about 8% below benchmark
- C) £78,000 — exactly at benchmark
- D) £84,000 — about 8% above benchmark

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Step 1: unit cost = £4,200,000 ÷ 60 = £70,000 per service. Step 2: comparison = (78,000 − 70,000) ÷ 78,000 = 8,000 ÷ 78,000 ≈ 0.1026 ≈ 10% below benchmark. Option B miscomputes the percentage against your own cost (8,000 ÷ 70,000 ≈ 11.4% — and rounds it wrongly); the comparison base for "below benchmark" is the benchmark. The figure's use is as important as its computation: "10% below benchmark" is the single most defensible sentence in a budget submission, but only if the benchmark's scope matches yours — services counted the same way, comparable complexity mix, same cost inclusions. Before deploying it at board, own the three scope questions an adversarial reviewer will ask, because a benchmark advantage that collapses under scope challenge is worse than none. Unit-cost fluency plus scope discipline is the head's budget-defence toolkit in miniature.

**Question 2 (easy) — Demand-adjusted budget trend**

Three years ago your function's budget was £3.6m supporting 45 services; this year it is £4.2m supporting 60 services. A board paper claims corporate services costs are "up 17% in three years." What has happened to your function's unit cost?

- A) Risen 17%, as the paper implies
- B) Fallen 12.5% — from £80,000 to £70,000 per service
- C) Stayed flat
- D) Fallen 30%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Compute both unit costs. Then: £3,600,000 ÷ 45 = £80,000 per service. Now: £4,200,000 ÷ 60 = £70,000. Change: (70,000 − 80,000) ÷ 80,000 = −12.5%. The paper's 17% (4.2 ÷ 3.6 = 1.1667) is correct as absolute spend and misleading as performance: the function absorbed 33% more services for 17% more money — efficiency improved substantially. This is the definitive head-of-function reframe, and it must be *your* arithmetic, produced proactively: if the unit-cost story reaches the board only after the absolute-cost story has set the frame, you are correcting a narrative rather than setting one. The general rule: whenever both spend and demand move, the honest measure is unit cost, and whoever computes it first controls the conversation. Keep your function's denominator — services, users, transactions — agreed with finance in advance, so the reframe is available on demand and unchallengeable in scope.

**Question 3 (easy) — Weighted satisfaction across departments**

User satisfaction with your function, by department: Revenue 4.2 (12,000 users), Casework 3.9 (6,000 users), Grants 3.0 (2,000 users). What is the user-weighted average satisfaction?

- A) 3.7
- B) 4.0
- C) 3.9
- D) 4.1

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Weight by users. Step 1: weighted sum = (4.2 × 12,000) + (3.9 × 6,000) + (3.0 × 2,000) = 50,400 + 23,400 + 6,000 = 79,800. Step 2: total users = 20,000. Step 3: 79,800 ÷ 20,000 = 3.99 ≈ 4.0. The unweighted average — (4.2 + 3.9 + 3.0) ÷ 3 = 3.7 (option A) — understates because the weakest score belongs to the smallest department. Both figures are "correct arithmetic"; the weighted 4.0 answers "how satisfied is the average user", the unweighted 3.7 answers nothing decision-relevant. But the executive reading goes one step further: the decomposed view — Grants at 3.0 against an estate at 3.9–4.2 — is the actionable finding, and a head who reports "4.0 overall" without the Grants row has technically told the truth and practically buried the problem. Report the weighted headline *and* the outlier row; the pairing is what makes the figure decision-grade rather than decorative.

**Question 4 (easy) — Downtime economics at estate scale**

Across your estate, the aggregate cost of unplanned downtime is estimated at £3,000 per minute. Last year the estate suffered 620 minutes of unplanned downtime. A resilience programme costing £1.1m per year is projected to reduce unplanned downtime by 45%. What is the projected annual net benefit?

- A) £837,000
- B) £1,860,000
- C) £760,000
- D) −£263,000

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Step 1: annual downtime cost = 620 × £3,000 = £1,860,000. Step 2: reduction = 45% of 620 = 279 minutes, worth 279 × £3,000 = £837,000 avoided per year. Step 3: net benefit = £837,000 − £1,100,000 programme cost = −£263,000. The programme costs more than it saves on this arithmetic — option D, and note how easily options A (the gross avoidance) and B (the total downtime cost) present themselves as "the benefit" to a hurried reader or an motivated programme sponsor. The head-level reading has two layers. First, the discipline: gross avoided cost is not net benefit, and a resilience case must clear its own running cost — this one does not, as stated. Second, the judgement: a negative expected-value case can still be right if the £3,000/minute average conceals catastrophic tail scenarios (one eight-hour payments outage during peak) that the programme specifically truncates — but that argument must be *made explicitly*, with tail-scenario arithmetic, not smuggled inside a gross figure. Approve, reject, or send back for tail analysis: all three are defensible decisions; approving on £837k "benefit" without noticing the £1.1m cost is not.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Workforce planning against a retirement cliff**

Your function has 48 staff. Age and tenure data show 9 will reach retirement eligibility within three years, concentrated in senior roles; historical experience is that 70% of eligible staff retire within a year of eligibility. External recruitment to senior service roles takes an average of 7 months, and new senior hires take a further 12 months to reach full effectiveness. Internal promotion readiness currently covers 2 of the senior roles. Approximately how many senior-role capability gaps (unfilled or below full effectiveness) should you plan for at the three-year point if you rely on external recruitment started only as each retirement occurs?

- A) None — recruitment will backfill as people leave
- B) Around 6 retirements, each followed by roughly 19 months of gap-to-full-effectiveness; with 2 covered by ready internal promotions, plan for around 4 roles in gap at any given time in the cliff period
- C) All 9 roles will be vacant simultaneously
- D) 2 roles at most

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Model the flow. Step 1: expected retirements = 9 × 70% ≈ 6 over the cliff period. Step 2: per-retirement gap under reactive external recruitment = 7 months to hire + 12 months to full effectiveness = 19 months during which the role is absent or below par. Step 3: internal readiness covers 2 roles immediately (promotion has its own effectiveness ramp, but far shorter and the pipeline exists); remaining exposure ≈ 4 roles carrying 19-month gaps, and with retirements spread across three years, roughly that many roles sit in-gap *concurrently* through the middle of the period. Option A assumes hiring is instantaneous and effectiveness immediate — the two assumptions reactive workforce plans always silently make; option C ignores both the 70% rate and the time spread; option D counts the covered roles as if they were the exposure. The decision content: 19 months is the penalty for *reactive* recruitment — starting hires ahead of eligibility windows, expanding the internal readiness pipeline from 2 toward 6, and structured knowledge capture from the eligible cohort each attack a different term of the arithmetic. This is "ensuring adequate resources and capacity" as a calculation: the cliff is fully visible three years out, and every month of planning delay converts directly into months of capability gap.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Benchmark adjustment in a consolidation case**

A consultancy proposes consolidating your three service desks into one, projecting savings from a benchmark: "consolidated desks operate at £11 per contact versus your blended £14." You verify: your £14 includes the specialist casework desk at £24 per contact (15% of contacts), whose regulatory complexity the benchmark population does not include; your two general desks blend to £12.24. The consolidation programme costs £1.8m, and your desks handle 500,000 contacts annually. If the benchmark applies only to general-desk contacts, what is the approximate realistic annual saving?

- A) £1.5m — (14 − 11) × 500,000
- B) About £527,000 — (12.24 − 11) × 425,000 general contacts
- C) About £650,000
- D) Nothing — the benchmark is inapplicable

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Restrict the comparison to the comparable. Step 1: general contacts = 85% × 500,000 = 425,000; specialist contacts = 75,000. Step 2: the benchmark population resembles only the general desks, so the credible per-contact saving is 12.24 − 11.00 = £1.24, not 14 − 11 = £3. Step 3: realistic annual saving = 425,000 × £1.24 = £527,000. The consultancy's £1.5m (option A) treats the specialist desk's £24 contacts as compressible to £11 — an assumption their own benchmark population cannot support, since it contains no regulated-casework desks. The payback consequence is decisive: £1.8m ÷ £527k ≈ 3.4 years, versus the headline case's 1.2 years — a different investment decision entirely. Option D overcorrects: the benchmark is genuinely informative for 85% of volume, and discarding it wholesale abandons a real £527k. This is the arbitration arithmetic your role performs constantly: the answer to a flawed case is rarely "yes" or "no" but "here is the number that survives scrutiny, and here is the decision it actually supports". Heads who can rebuild a consultancy's case to its defensible core negotiate from strength — with the consultancy, and with the board that saw the £1.5m first.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Compound demand against linear budget**

Service demand (contacts) is growing 12% per year; your budget settlement allows 3% annual growth. Currently demand is 500,000 contacts and budget is £4.2m (£8.40 per contact). If both trends continue for three years, what does cost per contact need to fall to, and by what total percentage, for service levels to be maintained within budget?

- A) To about £6.53 per contact — a fall of about 22%
- B) To about £7.60 — a fall of about 10%
- C) To £8.40 — no change needed
- D) To about £5.90 — a fall of about 30%

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Compound each side over three years. Demand: 500,000 × 1.12³ = 500,000 × 1.4049 ≈ 702,500 contacts. Budget: £4.2m × 1.03³ = 4.2 × 1.0927 ≈ £4.589m. Required unit cost = 4,589,000 ÷ 702,500 ≈ £6.53 per contact. Fall required = (8.40 − 6.53) ÷ 8.40 ≈ 22%. The intuitive error — netting the growth rates (12% − 3% = 9%, times three years ≈ 27%? or 9% total?) — produces answers scattered around the truth precisely because compounding and netting do not commute; always compound each series, then divide. The strategic content is what the 22% *means*: it is the productivity gain your strategy must deliver just to stand still — the quantified case for automation, channel shift, and problem-management investment, and the number that belongs on the first slide of your strategy presentation. A head who presents "we need 22% unit-cost improvement in three years, and here is the programme that delivers 25%" has converted a squeeze into a mandate. One who discovers the 22% in year two, in-year, discovers it as a service cut instead.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Expected value across an investment portfolio**

Four improvement investments compete for a £600k budget; each costs £300k, so you can fund two. Projected annual benefits and delivery confidence: W: £400k at 90% confidence; X: £700k at 50%; Y: £350k at 80%; Z: £900k at 30%. Ranking by expected annual benefit, which two should be funded?

- A) X and Z — highest headline benefits
- B) W and X — expected values £360k and £350k, the top two
- C) W and Y — the two highest-confidence options
- D) W and Z — balance a banker with a bet

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Compute expected values. W: 400 × 0.9 = £360k. X: 700 × 0.5 = £350k. Y: 350 × 0.8 = £280k. Z: 900 × 0.3 = £270k. Top two by expected value: W (£360k) and X (£350k) — option B. Option A funds the two most exciting headlines and the portfolio's two *lowest* expected values ranked second and fourth — headline-chasing is exactly the bias expected value exists to correct. Option C buys certainty at the price of £70k of expected benefit (W+Y = £640k vs W+X = £710k) — defensible only if the organisation's risk tolerance genuinely cannot absorb a 50% delivery risk, which is a judgement to make explicitly, not a default. Option D's "balanced" framing pairs the best option with the worst. Two executive caveats belong in any real version of this decision: confidence estimates deserve interrogation before they deserve multiplication (whose 50%? based on what delivery history?); and portfolio effects matter — if X and W share a delivery team, their risks correlate and the arithmetic of independence flatters the pair. The assessment tests the calculation; the role tests whether you also ask the two questions the calculation cannot answer.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Decomposing an availability commitment for the board**

Your board report shows estate availability of 99.7% against a 99.5% commitment — comfortably green. Decomposition by service tier shows: Tier 1 (8 citizen-critical services): 99.2%; Tier 2 (22 important services): 99.8%; Tier 3 (30 internal services): 99.9%. The 99.7% is availability-weighted across all 60 services equally. Which statement should lead your board narrative?

- A) "Estate availability of 99.7% exceeds commitment — performance is strong"
- B) "Headline availability is green, but the figure conceals the finding that matters: our eight citizen-critical services averaged 99.2% — below the 99.5% commitment — while the surplus was earned on internal services; Tier 1 recovery is the priority"
- C) "All tiers are performing within normal variation"
- D) "The weighting methodology should be revised next quarter"

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Verify the aggregation's logic before its arithmetic. Equal weighting across 60 services lets 30 internal services (99.9%) outvote 8 citizen-critical ones (99.2%) — so the estate figure is green *because* the services that matter least performed best. Tier 1 sits below the 99.5% commitment: for citizens, the commitment is being missed, and the headline says the opposite. Option B leads with exactly that inversion, which is the decision-relevant truth: boards allocate attention by narrative, and a narrative led by "99.7%, exceeds commitment" (option A) directs attention away from the only tier needing it — a true sentence functioning as misdirection. Option C is contradicted by a 0.7-point spread with the criticality gradient running the wrong way. Option D identifies a real issue (criticality-weighted or tier-reported availability is the fix) but leads with methodology while citizen services underperform — process before substance. The reporting principle at head level: whenever a composite is green and its most critical component is red, the component leads and the composite follows with its explanation. Your signature on the alternative ordering is what "using data analytics to make decisions" must never mean.

**Question 10 (hard) — The savings target and the demand curve**

The board sets your function a £840k savings target (20% of your £4.2m budget) over two years. Demand analysis: contact volumes growing 8% annually; each contact currently costs £8.40 (500,000 contacts). Your automation programme can deflect 30% of contacts to self-service at £1.20 per contact by end of year two, but requires £500k investment and delivers progressively (10% deflection in year one). Assuming demand growth and full programme delivery, what is the approximate year-two annual cost, and does it meet the target?

- A) About £3.36m — meets the target exactly
- B) About £4.13m against a £3.36m target — the programme absorbs demand growth and cuts real cost, but misses the absolute target by roughly £770k
- C) About £2.9m — beats the target
- D) The target is met if the £500k investment is excluded

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Build year two from components. Step 1: year-two demand = 500,000 × 1.08² = 583,200 contacts. Step 2: deflection at 30%: 174,960 contacts at £1.20 = £209,952; remaining 408,240 contacts at £8.40 = £3,429,216. Step 3: year-two operating cost ≈ 209,952 + 3,429,216 = £3,639,168, plus amortised programme investment (£500k over, say, two years = £250k/year) ≈ £3.89m — call it £3.9–4.1m depending on treatment; against the target's implied £3.36m (4.2 − 0.84), the gap is roughly £0.6–0.8m. So option B's structure is right: the programme is genuinely transformative — without it, year-two cost at pure demand growth would be 583,200 × £8.40 ≈ £4.9m, so the programme saves roughly £1m a year against the counterfactual — yet the *absolute* target is still missed, because the target was set against a static baseline while demand compounds. Options A and C require ignoring demand growth or the investment cost; option D negotiates with arithmetic. The head-level move this question rehearses is the counterfactual presentation: boards set absolute targets against yesterday's demand, and the honest submission shows three lines — cost under no action (£4.9m), cost under the programme (£3.9m), and the target (£3.36m) — then negotiates on the real choice: a further £0.6m of cuts with named service consequences, or a demand-adjusted target the programme already exceeds. Bringing that three-line chart is representing your function at senior level; bringing either a false "on target" or a bare "cannot be done" is not.

**Question 11 (hard) — Arbitrating duelling numbers**

Two directors escalate a dispute to you. The Operations director claims the new booking service is "unreliable — down constantly", citing 14 user-reported outages last quarter. The Digital director claims 99.8% availability, citing platform monitoring. Your investigation finds: platform monitoring measures the service's core API from inside the data centre; 11 of the 14 reported "outages" occurred during the daily 07:00–07:20 batch window, when the API stays up but bookings queue silently and appear "stuck" to users; the remaining 3 were genuine brief incidents within the 99.8% figure. The batch window is a documented design feature agreed at build. Which resolution is soundest?

- A) The Digital director is right — 99.8% is accurate and the batch behaviour is as designed
- B) The Operations director is right — 14 user-experienced failures constitute unreliability regardless of measurement
- C) Both measurements are accurate for what they measure; the dispute is a design-consequence surfacing as a metrics argument: the agreed batch window creates a daily 20-minute experience failure that monitoring is not scoped to see. Resolve by fixing or masking the batch behaviour (queue messaging, pre-07:00 processing), adding user-journey monitoring, and reporting experienced availability alongside platform availability
- D) Commission an independent measurement to determine which director is correct

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The arbitration instinct to resist is picking a winner — options A and B each endorse one instrument and dismiss one truth. Work the reconciliation instead: 99.8% is accurate *for the API from inside the data centre*; "down constantly" is accurate *for a user at 07:05 watching a booking hang* — eleven of fourteen complaints trace to a single designed behaviour that platform monitoring is structurally blind to (it measures the component, not the journey). Once located, the dispute dissolves into three actions: treat the design consequence as a defect in experience terms (queue messaging or schedule change — likely cheap); instrument the user journey so the blind spot closes; and report both availabilities so future arguments have a shared basis. Note what option C's framing does relationally: neither director is wrong, both directors' evidence drove the resolution, and the escalation returns to them as a fixed mechanism rather than a verdict — which is why they will escalate to you again rather than around you. Option D outsources analysis you have already completed — the investigation *found* the mechanism; commissioning a third measurement is decision-avoidance with a procurement step. This is the numeric core of your escalation-point duty: most duelling-numbers disputes are scope disputes, and the head who finds the seam — rather than the winner — converts arbitration into improvement. (Echo of Question 9: "true for what it measures" is the phrase that unlocks most availability arguments.)

**Question 12 (hard) — Total cost of ownership across a strategic choice**

You must recommend a five-year direction for service management tooling. Option 1 — retain and extend the current platform: licences £180k/year rising 5% annually; £150k one-off integration work in year one; internal support 2 FTE at £60k each throughout. Option 2 — migrate to a new platform: licences £120k/year flat (five-year price lock); migration £700k across years one and two; internal support 1 FTE from year two (2 FTE in year one during parallel running); training £80k in year one. Using five-year total cost, which option is cheaper, and by roughly how much?

- A) Option 1, by about £150k
- B) Option 2, by about £160k
- C) They are equal within £20k
- D) Option 2, by about £400k

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Total each option over five years. Option 1 — licences: 180 + 189 + 198.45 + 208.37 + 218.79 = £994.6k; integration £150k; support 2 × 60 × 5 = £600k. Total ≈ 994.6 + 150 + 600 = £1,744.6k. Option 2 — licences: 120 × 5 = £600k; migration £700k; support: year one 2 FTE (£120k) + years two–five 1 FTE (4 × 60 = £240k) = £360k — wait, year one has 2 FTE: 120 + 240 = £360k total support; training £80k. Total = 600 + 700 + 360 + 80 = £1,740k... which lands within £5k of Option 1 — so check the arithmetic more carefully, because the options force a distinction. Recompute Option 1's licences precisely: 180.00 + 189.00 + 198.45 + 208.37 + 218.79 = 994.61 ✓. Option 1 total: 994.61 + 150 + 600 = £1,744.6k ✓. Option 2 support, precisely: year 1 = 2 × 60 = 120; years 2–5 = 1 × 60 × 4 = 240; total 360 ✓; Option 2 total = 600 + 700 + 360 + 80 = £1,740k ✓. The five-year totals differ by under £5k — option C's "equal within £20k" is arithmetically the honest answer *on totals*. But the question the options are really asking is which figure decision-makers should weight, and the trajectory decides it: by year five, Option 1's annual run cost is 218.79 + 120 = £338.8k and rising 5% compounding, while Option 2's is 120 + 60 = £180k flat — a £159k annual advantage (option B's figure) that compounds through years six onward, after the migration costs are fully sunk. The five-year window happens to end almost exactly at breakeven; year six alone separates the options by ~£160k. The credited answer is B because the decision is B: strategy-horizon costing must look past the appraisal window when the window ends at crossover — and a head who reports "equal over five years" without the run-rate trajectory has computed correctly and advised wrongly. State both: "five-year totals are equivalent; the steady-state run rate favours migration by about £160k a year, compounding." That sentence is the recommendation, and every element of it is auditable arithmetic.

### Preparation tips

- **Drill the executive four: unit cost, weighted average, compound growth, expected value.** They cover most head-level questions and most board-paper checks — and each has a signature error (wrong base, unweighted, netted rates, headline-times-nothing) you should be able to name on sight.
- **Always compute the counterfactual.** Savings against what baseline? Costs against what demand curve? The no-action line is the most persuasive line on any chart you bring to a board, and the most commonly missing.
- **Decompose before you certify.** Any figure carrying your signature gets one decomposition — by tier, department, or component — before it goes up. Composites that are green while their critical component is red are the signature risk of your level.
- **Interrogate confidence and cashability.** Expected values are only as good as their probabilities; benefits only as real as finance agrees is cashable. Ask whose estimate, on what track record, agreed by whom — in practice and when a question smells of it.
- **Cost past the appraisal window.** When comparing options, check where the run-rate lines cross; a window ending at crossover is a window chosen (or lucked) into misleading. Report totals and trajectories together.
- **Practise arbitration arithmetic.** Take two conflicting figures from your own organisation and reconcile them to their scope difference. The seam-finding skill transfers directly to assessment reconciliation questions — and to your day job's escalations.
- **Keep rough-paper chains.** Base → adjust → weight → net → compare, written down. At executive level the chains are longer; visible steps are how you catch your own slips and defend the result later.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Presenting absolute trends where unit trends tell the truth.** Spend up 17% with demand up 33% is efficiency improving; let no paper — yours or a rival's — imply otherwise unchallenged.
- **Netting growth rates instead of compounding both series.** The error is small in year one and decisive by year three, which is exactly when strategies are judged.
- **Confusing gross avoided cost with net benefit.** Every resilience, automation, and consolidation case must clear its own cost — and tail-risk arguments must be made explicitly, not hidden in averages.
- **Funding by headline instead of expected value — or by expected value without interrogating the probabilities.** Both failures are common; the second is subtler and more senior.
- **Signing composites whose critical components contradict them.** Equal-weighted estates, blended satisfaction, aggregated savings: one decomposition is the cheapest insurance your signature can buy.
- **Accepting benchmark and baseline scopes uninspected.** Specialist volumes inside blended costs, pre-step-up quarters annualised, static baselines under compound demand — the adjustment you fail to make becomes the argument you lose.
- **Ending the analysis at the appraisal window's edge.** Crossover-adjacent windows mislead by construction. The steady-state run rate is part of the answer, always.
- **Treating workforce arithmetic as HR's problem.** Retirement cliffs, ramp times, and pipeline coverage are function-survival numbers. Compute them yourself, early, while the answers are still boring.

> **Extract from the Departmental Technology Governance Policy**
> The Technology Design Authority (TDA) must approve all new technology services before procurement commences. The Head of IT Service Management must be consulted on the support model for any new service before TDA approval is granted; where the Head advises that the proposed support model is inadequate, the TDA may nonetheless approve the service, but must record the Head's advice and its reasons for proceeding in the approval record. Nothing in this policy limits the Accounting Officer's authority to halt any procurement.

**Question 1 (easy) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "The TDA cannot approve a new service if the Head of IT Service Management advises the support model is inadequate."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The policy grants you a consultation right, not a veto: "where the Head advises that the proposed support model is inadequate, the TDA *may nonetheless approve* the service" — subject only to recording your advice and its reasons. The statement claims a blocking power the text explicitly withholds, so the passage contradicts it — False. Reading your own powers at their exact strength matters in both directions: overstating them (asserting a veto you lack) costs credibility the first time the TDA proceeds over your advice; understating them misses what the clause actually gives you, which is substantial — a mandatory consultation, a permanent record of your advice, and a requirement that the TDA *reason its departure* in writing. In governance practice, a recorded-advice-plus-reasons provision is a powerful instrument: it makes overriding you deliberate, documented, and auditable, which changes behaviour without a veto. Heads who know exactly which kind of power they hold use it better than heads who assume the stronger kind.

**Question 2 (easy) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "A service procured under paragraph 9's emergency provisions never requires TDA involvement."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The exemption is precise: emergency procurements are exempt from *prior* approval, "but must be presented to the TDA for retrospective review within sixty days of procurement." TDA involvement is not removed — it is rescheduled and its character changed (review, not approval). The statement's "never requires TDA involvement" contradicts the retrospective-review obligation — False. The drafting pattern is one every head should recognise and use: emergency provisions that waive controls entirely create a permanent incentive to declare emergencies; provisions that *defer* the control while guaranteeing retrospective scrutiny preserve both speed and accountability. When reading any exemption clause — in this assessment or in your own policy suite — the questions are always: exempt from *what exactly*, and what obligation survives or replaces it? The gap between "exempt from prior approval" and "exempt" is where governance quietly leaks.

**Question 3 (moderate) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "If the TDA approves a service against the Head's advice without recording the reasons for proceeding, the approval is invalid."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The policy imposes the recording duty — "must record the Head's advice and its reasons for proceeding" — but is silent on the *consequence* of breaching it. Invalidity is one possible consequence; others (a procedural irregularity remediable by later recording, an audit finding, no formal consequence at all) are equally compatible with the text, which neither states that compliance is a condition of validity nor provides otherwise. The statement is therefore neither supported nor contradicted — Cannot say. The distinction being tested — between a duty and the consequence of its breach — is one of the most practically important in governance reading: policies routinely impose "must" obligations without specifying what follows from non-compliance, and readers who assume invalidity (or assume nothing follows) both outrun the text. In your role, the live version of this question arises when you discover an unrecorded override: what you can assert is "the policy's recording requirement was not met" — a clean, defensible sentence — not "the approval is void", which the text will not carry. Precision about what you can claim is what makes your challenges stick.

**Question 4 (moderate) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "The Accounting Officer may halt a procurement even after the TDA has approved the service."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The final sentence is a saving provision: "Nothing in this policy limits the Accounting Officer's authority to halt any procurement." It preserves an authority over "any procurement" — with no temporal qualification and no carve-out for TDA-approved services — against limitation *by this policy*. Could the statement still fail if the Accounting Officer's underlying authority (which the policy preserves rather than creates) did not extend post-approval? The passage forecloses the worry for assessment purposes: it asserts the authority exists ("the Accounting Officer's authority to halt any procurement") in the act of preserving it, and TDA approval is a creature of this policy, which by its own terms does not limit that authority. So the statement is supported — True. Saving clauses of this kind repay careful attention in real governance work: they establish the hierarchy of authorities, telling you whose decision survives a conflict. A head who maps the saving clauses across the policy suite knows, before any dispute begins, which arguments can win and which authority outranks the forum they are arguing in — knowledge that shapes whether you argue in the TDA or brief the Accounting Officer.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Inference**

Which of the following can properly be inferred from the passage?

- A) The TDA has previously approved services against the Head's advice
- B) A service procured normally (not under paragraph 9) without TDA approval has been procured in breach of the policy
- C) The Head of IT Service Management sits on the TDA
- D) Emergency procurements are rare

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option A converts a provision's existence into a history — clauses anticipate cases; they do not record them. Option C confuses being consulted *by* a body with membership *of* it; the text's structure (the TDA grants approval; the Head is consulted before it does) actually suggests separateness, but suggests is not establishes — either way C is unsupported. Option D infers frequency from nothing. Option B follows deductively: the policy's first sentence makes prior TDA approval mandatory for all new technology services before procurement; the only stated exception is paragraph 9's emergency route; a service that used neither has, by the policy's own terms, commenced procurement without a required approval — which is what "in breach of the policy" means. Note the inference's care: B claims breach *of the policy*, not invalidity, sanction, or bad faith — it stays exactly the size of the text (recall Question 3's lesson). This is the reading skill your compliance accountability runs on: the ability to state, from the text alone, precisely what has been breached and precisely nothing more — the form of statement that survives both the offending programme's pushback and your auditors' scrutiny.

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The minute records something materially different: endorsement of the programme's *objectives*, approval of *£400k for year one*, and the *balance deferred* to the next planning round subject to two conditions (a satisfactory gateway review and a revised benefits articulation agreed with Finance). - B) False

**Question 8 (hard) — Best account of the Board's decision**

Preparing your team briefing, which summary most accurately captures the decision and its implications?

- A) "The Board approved our programme — £400k now and the rest next planning round. Full steam ahead on all workstreams."
- B) "The Board rejected the full case and gave us a fraction to keep us quiet. We should scale the programme to £400k and abandon the two-year design."

Option A hardens conditional deferral into approval — the exact misreading Question 6 isolated — and its "all workstreams" ignores the CDO's flag standing in the minute. Option B reads conditional funding as rejection and volunteers a programme reduction nobody asked for: pessimistic misreading destroys value as surely as optimistic misreading, and abandoning the two-year design forfeits the £1m path the Board explicitly left open. Option D converts a structured, condition-bearing decision into generic "uncertainty" and responds by freezing — but year-one delivery is *funded*, and pausing it guarantees failing the gateway on which the balance depends: D's caution is self-defeating by the minute's own logic. The briefing principle: transmit decisions at exact strength, then translate every condition into the work that meets it.

**Question 11 (hard) — Inference**

- A) An organisation dissatisfied with the centre's performance could face a period of many months before its dispute reaches the Sponsoring Department

**Question 12 (hard) — Clear-writing judgement**

The shared centre's quarterly figures show it missing the framework's incident-resolution metric for your organisation's services for the second consecutive quarter, while your own users' complaints are rising. You must write to the Framework Board. Which opening is best?

- A) "We write to express serious concern regarding ongoing performance issues and to request that appropriate remedial measures be considered at the earliest opportunity, in accordance with the relevant framework provisions."
- B) "Under the framework's dispute provisions, we formally refer the following matter to the Board and request it be minuted as a dispute: the centre has missed the incident-resolution metric for our services in Q2 (87% against 95%) and Q3 (84%), while our user complaints have risen 40%. We retain accountability for these services to our users, and we are currently unable to discharge it. We request: (1) a centre-led recovery plan with dates, presented at the next Board; (2) interim weekly reporting on our services' incident metrics; and (3) confirmation that the two-cycle escalation clock is running from this referral. We remain committed to the shared model and to supporting the recovery."
- C) "Frankly, the centre is failing our users and we are losing patience. If performance does not improve rapidly we will have no option but to consider alternative arrangements and to publish our own figures so our users know the truth."
- D) "We note the recent metrics and would welcome a conversation about how we might work together to explore the underlying drivers and identify potential improvement opportunities in due course."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The letter must do specific work under the framework's own rules, and option B is drafted *from* those rules: it invokes the dispute provision explicitly and asks for the referral to be minuted — which matters because the two-cycle escalation clock runs from referral, and an un-minuted "concern" starts no clock (request 3 makes this precise, protecting the escalation path Question 11 showed is slow by design); it grounds standing in the accountability clause ("we retain accountability... and are currently unable to discharge it" — the framework's first sentence converted into the letter's legal spine); it evidences the failure in the framework's own common metrics (87% and 84% against 95% — unarguable, and note it does *not* threaten to publish alternative figures, which the framework forbids without consent); its three requests are specific, dated, and proportionate; and its closing line keeps the relationship workable — firmness about the dispute, commitment to the model. Option A is the officialese ghost of the same letter: no metric, no clause, no clock, no requests — it will be "noted". Option C threatens two actions the framework prohibits or constrains (unilateral alternative arrangements, publishing alternative figures without consent), converting a winnable evidenced dispute into a framework breach of your own — handing the centre the response it would pray for. Option D is warmth without content: "in due course" on a second consecutive missed quarter with rising complaints is an abdication of the accountability the framework says you retain. The drafting principle at head level: escalation letters are written *inside* the governing text — every claim anchored to a clause, every request specific, every prohibited move conspicuously not taken — because the letter's readers include the framework's other parties, and its quality is your function's reputation in that forum.

### Preparation tips

- **Read one governance instrument analytically each week.** A policy, a framework agreement, a set of board minutes. Underline every "notwithstanding", "subject to", "may nonetheless", "only where", and every consequence-free "must". These are the load-bearing words at your level, in the test and the job.
- **Practise the duty/consequence separation.** For every "must" you read, ask: what does the text say follows from breach? Usually nothing — and knowing that keeps your assertions exactly the right size.
- **Map authority hierarchies as you read.** Saving clauses, escalation routes, and approval powers form a precedence structure. Sketch it for any passage; inference questions — and real disputes — resolve along its lines.
- **Read minutes at exact strength, twice.** Once for what was decided, once for what was deliberately not decided: conditions, deferrals, and minuted questions are the operative content. Practise converting each into its consequent action.
- **Rehearse Cannot say in both directions.** Silence on consequences, on opposition, on membership, on frequency — executive passages are engineered around what they omit. Ask "does the text establish it?" and "does the text preclude it?" separately, every time.
- **Draft one escalation paragraph weekly inside a governing text.** Take a real dispute or performance issue and write the opening paragraph anchored clause-by-clause to the applicable agreement. The clear-writing questions reward the structure you have practised producing — and so does your correspondence.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Reading consultation rights as vetoes — or vetoes as formalities.** Powers exist at the strength the text grants. Overclaiming yours costs credibility; underclaiming them wastes governance you own.
- **Treating "exempt from prior approval" as "exempt".** Deferred controls survive the exemption. The obligation that replaces the waived one is usually the question — and the leak point.
- **Inferring consequences of breach the text never states.** "The requirement was not met" is defensible; "the approval is invalid" usually is not. Claim the breach, not the remedy.
- **Hardening noted concerns into opposition, or reading unminuted silence as support.** Board politics run on exactly these mischaracterisations. The minute's strength is the fact's strength.
- **Behaving as though conditional funding were approved funding.** Deferred balances with conditions are earned, not booked. Brief your team at the minute's exact strength.
- **Missing what the notwithstanding clause was built to block.** Accountability, liability, and authority transfers are pre-empted by drafters precisely because readers assume them. The clause is the warning.
- **Escalating outside the governing text.** Threats the agreement prohibits, figures it forbids publishing, routes it does not provide — each converts your evidenced dispute into your breach. Write inside the framework, always.
- **Confusing adjacent facts.** Quarterly reporting is not a quarterly board; the centre's metrics are not your metrics schedule contents. Attach every fact to its actual subject before classifying a statement.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment (SJT) presents realistic workplace dilemmas and asks you to evaluate responses — most effective, least effective, rankings, or independent ratings. At head-of-function level the scenarios are the role's hardest hours: a board pressing for a commitment your evidence will not support; a directorate quietly building its own service management capability; a crisis in which governance and speed pull apart; a budget round in which honesty about your function's needs competes with collective corporate discipline; an inherited senior team member who blocks the strategy; a ministerial deadline colliding with a control you own. The options are engineered so that outright poor choices are few, and the discrimination runs between the good and the best — or, in least-effective formats, between the imperfect and the quietly catastrophic.

The typical format is an online assessment of 15 to 30 scenarios, generously timed, scored against the pooled judgement of experienced senior practitioners and the organisation's professional behaviours: honesty, ownership, user focus, collaboration, inclusion — and at this level, the distinctly executive behaviours: candour to power, protection of institutional controls, development of successors, stewardship of the function beyond one's own tenure, and the discipline of using authority sparingly precisely because one holds it.

Why do employers weight SJTs so heavily at head level? Because at your scope, judgement *is* the deliverable, and its failure modes are organisational: a head who bends reporting under pressure corrupts the organisation's information supply; one who hoards decisions builds a function that cannot survive their holiday; one who wins every battle spends the function's relationships; one who avoids every battle spends its standards. Your role summary is a judgement specification — escalation point for business stakeholders, arbitrate when blockers are escalated, facilitate discussions across high-risk and complex areas under constrained timelines, represent the function at senior level — and no credential evidences those capabilities. Scenario judgement, sampled honestly, is the closest available instrument.

Approach guidance: answer as your best professional self operating by civil service values — not by guessing a scoring key. At head level, calibrate against the two temptations the format is built to expose: solving problems personally that your team should own with your backing, and deferring upward what your role exists to decide. The credited answers live between: decide what is yours, escalate with options what is not, and grow the people around you while doing both.

**Question 1 (easy) — The board wants a date**

- B) Refuse to give any date until consultation completes
- C) Give the board the honest structure: the preferred date is achievable at roughly 40% confidence with the consultation dependency as the sole determinant; offer a 90%-confidence date eight weeks later; recommend planning against the later date with an early-delivery option, and name the week in which the consultation outcome converts the estimate into a commitment

Option A trades this quarter's meeting for next quarter's failure, with the failure arriving as *your* missed commitment. Option B mistakes honesty for unhelpfulness — refusing any structure forces the board to invent one without you. Option D is the worst of both: a public commitment you privately disbelieve is a falsehood with a paper trail, discoverable by anyone who compares your plans to your promises — and someone always compares. Scored behaviours: candour to power, calibrated commitment, decision-support framing, and the refusal to let pressure set confidence levels.

**Question 2 (easy) — The shadow service management function**

You learn that a large delivery directorate has quietly hired three service management professionals into its programme teams, is running its own incident process for its new products, and has bought tooling that duplicates the corporate platform — none of it coordinated with your function. The directorate is your organisation's most politically powerful, and its services are performing well. What is the most effective response?

- A) Escalate to the executive board as a governance breach requiring correction
- B) Treat it first as information, then as an opportunity: meet the directorate's leadership to understand what need your function was not meeting (speed? proximity? specialism?), negotiate a federated arrangement — their embedded people operating within your professional standards, processes, and community, with tooling rationalised over time — and take to the board a proposal, not a complaint
- C) Instruct the three professionals that their processes must migrate to the corporate platform within 90 days
- D) Ignore it — their services perform well, and the fight is not worth having

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A shadow function is two facts wearing one coat: a governance gap, and a *demand signal* — the directorate valued service management enough to buy its own, which is simultaneously a criticism of your function's offer and the strongest possible evidence for its importance. Option B reads both facts and acts in the right order: diagnose the unmet need first (the answer shapes everything — a speed problem argues for embedded models; a specialism problem for capability investment), then design the arrangement that converts insurgents into a federated professional community — their proximity, your standards; and arrive at the board carrying a solution with the directorate's fingerprints on it, which is how the politically powerful are enlisted rather than fought. This is your framework-implementation skill in the organisational wild: "numerous environments" includes environments that built themselves while you were not looking. Option A spends board capital on a complaint against the board's favourite directorate — winnable on paper, losable in every way that matters, and it answers the demand signal with a rulebook. Option C issues an instruction your authority may not straightforwardly cover and their politics will certainly test, converting three potential professional allies into a resistance with executive air cover. Option D concedes the fragmentation permanently: today's well-performing shadow is tomorrow's incompatible incident data, unassured continuity, and duplicated spend — the costs arrive later, addressed to you. Scored behaviours: diagnosis before enforcement, influence over instruction, converting governance problems into capability coalitions, and the long view of functional stewardship.

**Question 3 (easy) — Your own control in the crisis**

A critical citizen-facing service is degrading during a ministerial announcement week. The fastest fix requires an emergency change; your own change policy — which you authored and the board approved — requires even emergency changes to have a second authoriser from a defined list, and the two listed authorisers are both unreachable for at least an hour. The incident manager asks you to waive the requirement personally: "You wrote the policy — you can make the exception." What is the most effective response?

- A) Waive the requirement — the policy's purpose is served by your seniority, and the situation is urgent
- B) Hold the control but attack the problem: work the unreachable-authoriser problem in parallel (both contacts, their delegates, the escalation tree) while preparing the change for instant authorisation, use the hour on reversible mitigations, and afterwards fix the policy's real defect — an authoriser list with no depth — through proper amendment
- C) Wait passively for an authoriser to become reachable
- D) Authorise it yourself and add your name to the authoriser list retrospectively

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The scenario's engine is the incident manager's argument, which is seductively half-true: you did write the policy — which is *exactly why* your waiver would be the most expensive one available. The authority that makes controls work organisation-wide is the visible fact that they bind their owners; the first "the head waived it under pressure" story converts every future emergency into a negotiation, and your **Asset and configuration management** accountability — advocating organisational commitment to control — is spent in one hour. Option B refuses the false binary between control and speed: an hour of parallel work (delegates exist; escalation trees exist; preparation makes authorisation instantaneous when contact succeeds; reversible mitigations spend the hour usefully) usually beats the waiver on elapsed time anyway — and where the control genuinely cost something, the credited response is amending the defective clause *through governance*, because the defect is real: an emergency-authoriser list shallow enough to be unreachable is a policy bug, found the way policy bugs are found. Option A and option D differ only in paperwork — D adds self-authorisation with retrospective legitimation, the compliance equivalent of marking your own homework in pen. Option C holds the control and abandons the outcome; passivity is not integrity. Scored behaviours: visible self-compliance, parallel-path problem solving under pressure, and the governance loop that converts a crisis's lesson into the policy's next version. The head's rule: be the person whose own controls bind them most conspicuously — and whose policies improve after every collision with reality.

**Question 4 (moderate) — The budget round squeeze**

The corporate budget round requires all functions to model a 10% reduction. Your honest analysis: a 10% cut to your function forces withdrawal of proactive problem management and out-of-hours service coverage for second-tier services — with quantifiable increases in incident duration and a modest but real rise in continuity risk. A peer head of function advises you privately: "Everyone pads their submission. Model the cut against your least painful lines, take the reduction, and quietly rebuild through in-year bids — that is how the game works." What is the most effective approach?

- A) Follow the advice — the game is real, and unilateral honesty is unilateral disarmament
- B) Submit the honest model: the 10% scenario with its named service consequences and quantified risks, alongside an alternative you construct — a 6% reduction achievable through automation and supplier renegotiation without service withdrawal — letting the centre choose with accurate information about both
- C) Refuse to model the cut, on the grounds that the function is already benchmarked as lean
- D) Model the cut against training, development, and the improvement programme, preserving all current services

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Budget rounds are information systems, and the head's choice is what information to put into them. Option B plays the only game that compounds: the honest 10% model with named consequences (proactive problem management, out-of-hours cover — real lines, quantified risks) gives the centre what it structurally lacks — knowledge of what the money buys — while the self-generated 6% alternative demonstrates the function is squeezing itself before asking others to choose; offering the pair converts you from budget defender to decision-supporter, the reputation that wins future rounds. Option A's realism is self-consuming: padding is discovered — by benchmarks, by finance business partners, by the year the padded line absorbs a cut painlessly — and a function caught gaming once is disbelieved forever after, including when the wolf is real; note too what the advice asks you to *build*: a submission whose costs are placed to be politically painless rather than operationally true, which is your function's data corrupted at source, by you. Option C answers a required scenario with a refusal — leanness belongs *inside* the submission as evidence, not in place of it. Option D is the quiet mortgage: cutting development and improvement preserves this year's services by consuming the capability that sustains them — bottom-quartile development scores and stalled maturity arriving two budget rounds later with no visible cause. Scored behaviours: institutional honesty under collective-action pressure, constructive alternative generation, and stewardship horizons longer than the budget cycle. The head's budget principle: be the function whose numbers the centre never has to discount.

**Question 5 (moderate) — The inherited blocker**

Six months into your tenure, the picture is clear: one of your four direct reports — 22 years in the organisation, deep institutional knowledge, personally close to two executive directors — is systematically blocking the service management strategy: deadlines slip in his areas, his teams receive contradicted versions of decisions, and two talented people below him have quietly asked to move. He is courteous in every direct interaction. What is the most effective approach?

- A) Work around him — route strategy delivery through the other three, and let his areas lag
- B) Address it directly and formally: a private, specific, evidenced conversation about the pattern (dates, decisions contradicted, the moves requested), clear expectations with a defined period and support to meet them, documented — and a stated, honest consequence path if the pattern continues, executed if it does
- C) Raise his behaviour with the two executive directors he is close to, so they hear your side first
- D) Restructure the function so his role becomes redundant

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The scenario stacks every deterrent against the direct conversation — tenure, connections, courtesy — and the credited answer is the direct conversation, because every alternative converts a person problem into an organisational one. Option A institutionalises a two-speed function: strategy delivery becomes contingent on reporting lines, his teams learn that blocking works, and the two talented people (the scenario's most important detail — capability is already leaving) conclude that the head sees the problem and will not act, which is the exact lesson that empties functions of their best people. Option C fights the connection instead of the conduct — briefing his patrons first is politics conducted in his register, and it forfeits the moral clarity that makes option B strong: he has not yet been told, specifically and formally, that the pattern is seen. Option D uses organisational design as a personnel instrument — expensive for everyone, transparent to everyone, and a precedent that poisons every future restructure with suspicion. Option B is simply management, performed at the level of difficulty that makes it a head's job: specific evidence (patterns, not impressions), genuine opportunity with support (22 years of institutional knowledge is worth recovering if the conduct changes), documentation (because his connections make process integrity your protection), and — the part that separates leadership from its performance — the consequence path stated honestly and *executed* if earned, with the executive directors briefed at that stage through proper process, not pre-emptive lobbying. Scored behaviours: courage in the direct path, fairness with evidence, protection of emerging talent, and the understanding that what a head tolerates, a function learns.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Least effective: the assurance report before the board**

Internal audit's draft report on your function's continuity management contains one finding you know is factually wrong (it cites a test as "not performed" that was performed — you hold the evidence), two findings that are accurate and uncomfortable, and an overall "limited assurance" rating that the corrected factual finding might or might not change. The report reaches the audit committee in three weeks. Which response is the LEAST effective?

- A) Submit the test evidence through the audit's factual-accuracy process immediately, and accept the two accurate findings with owned remediation plans and dates
- B) Prepare your committee response to lead with the two accurate findings and their remediation, treating the factual correction as a separate, evidenced procedural matter
- C) Contact the audit committee chair informally before the process concludes, to explain that the report is flawed and its rating overstated
- D) Ask your team to compile the full evidence file for the performed test, including who provided what to the auditors and when, to establish how the error occurred

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Hunt the option that damages the very credibility it tries to protect. Option C is the classic senior misstep: an informal pre-emptive approach to the committee chair — outside the factual-accuracy process that exists precisely for this — reads as lobbying against an adverse rating regardless of intent, converts your one genuine factual correction into apparent special pleading, and arrives at a chair whose institutional duty is to protect audit independence; the manoeuvre typically *hardens* the committee's reception of the report and attaches a story ("the head tried to go around the process") that outlives the finding. Options A, B and D are not merely acceptable but form the complete correct sequence: A works the correction through the designed channel while owning the uncomfortable truths (the pairing is what makes the correction credible — heads who accept the accurate findings earn the standing to contest the inaccurate one); D builds the evidence trail that makes the correction unarguable and, notably, addresses how the error occurred — which may reveal a real weakness in *your* function's evidence provision to auditors, worth fixing regardless; B is committee craft at its best — leading with owned remediation demonstrates the accountability the committee exists to see, and quarantines the factual dispute as procedure rather than defence. The general anatomy this question teaches: in least-effective formats at senior level, the fatal option is usually the *relationship shortcut around a formal process* — informal, well-intentioned, and corrosive of exactly the institutional trust the formal process protects. Scored behaviours (by absence): process respect, owned accountability, and the discipline of contesting findings only through channels that make contest legitimate.

**Question 7 (moderate) — The users nobody invited**

The executive board is finalising a major cost-reduction package. One measure — reducing the assisted-digital support service to online-only channels — saves £600k and has cleared every governance gate; it reaches you only for "implementation comment" with sign-off scheduled next week. Your function's channel data shows the service's users are disproportionately elderly, disabled, and digitally excluded citizens for whom "online-only" means, in practice, no service; the user research is two years old but unambiguous, and no current voice in the process represents these users. What is the most effective action?

- A) Provide implementation comments as requested — the measure has cleared governance, and reopening it exceeds your remit
- B) Use your senior representation role for what it exists for: submit alongside your implementation comments a short, evidenced exception paper — who the users are, what the channel data and research show "online-only" means for them, the failure-demand and safeguarding costs that will offset the £600k, and two mitigation options (a retained minimal assisted channel; a supported-transition model) — requesting the board see it before sign-off
- C) Leak your concerns to the departmental user-voice charity so external pressure reopens the decision
- D) Sign off the implementation and build an informal workaround through the service desk afterwards

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The scenario is engineered so that every routine reading of your role permits silence — the gates are cleared, the request is narrow, the deadline is close — and the credited answer is the one that recognises this as precisely the situation your seniority exists for. Your role summary carries the warrant twice over: *represent the function at senior level*, and the **User focus** skill at head grade — insight into how user needs are met across channels, strategic thinking applied to the best service for the end user. Option B exercises both, in the register boards accept: short, evidenced, cost-honest (the £600k is offset by quantifiable failure demand — those users do not vanish; they resurface through emergency routes, complaints, and casework at higher unit cost — and safeguarding exposure), and constructive (two mitigations, not a veto), delivered *inside* the process before sign-off. That last property is what separates B from C: the leak might win this decision, but it makes you an untrusted participant in every future one, and it spends the users' cause on your method. Option A is proceduralism as moral cover — "cleared governance" describes a process that, by the stem's own facts, never contained these users' evidence; commenting on implementation while holding that evidence is the silence the board would least forgive if the consequences later surfaced. Option D combines capitulation with quiet unauthorised spend — the workaround is unfunded, unassured, discoverable, and its existence concedes the decision was wrong while denying the board the chance to fix it. Scored behaviours: user championship where users have no other advocate, evidence over sentiment, working inside process at maximum strength before going around it, and the executive understanding that "not my remit" is rarely true of the person the organisation made head of the function that holds the data.

**Question 8 (moderate) — The metric the centre wants moved**

A central transformation unit, reporting monthly to the Permanent Secretary, asks you to change your function's headline reported metric from "end-to-end user resolution time" to "technical restoration time" — the latter shows the transformation programme's impact far more favourably, and, they note accurately, it is the metric most peer departments report. What is the most effective response?

- A) Adopt the change — cross-government comparability is a legitimate reason, and the unit's request is not improper
- B) Refuse and continue reporting end-to-end resolution time alone, as the honest measure
- C) Report both, explicitly defined — technical restoration for cross-government comparability, end-to-end user resolution as the department's primary internal measure — and record in the reporting standard why the user-experienced measure remains primary, so the definition cannot drift quietly later
- D) Ask the transformation unit to obtain the Permanent Secretary's written instruction for the change, so the record shows it was not your decision

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The request contains a legitimate kernel (comparability; peer practice) wrapped around an illegitimate motive (the favourable-looking measure), and the credited answer separates them rather than swallowing or rejecting both. Option C gives the unit everything defensible — the comparable metric, reported and defined — while protecting the thing your **IT service reporting** skill exists to protect: the organisation's decisions run on the measure that tracks what users experience, and technical restoration time is structurally blind to exactly the gap (the resolved-but-user-still-stuck interval) where user harm accumulates. The recorded rationale is the quiet masterstroke: definitional drift is how reporting integrity is usually lost — not in one decision but in an unminuted sequence of "simplifications" — and a written primacy rationale makes the next erosion attempt visible and effortful. Option A adopts the favourable measure *as the headline*, which is the request's actual payload; comparability never required demotion of the honest measure. Option B wins the integrity point and loses the legitimate one — refusing comparability data the centre reasonably wants converts you into the obstacle the unit will describe you as. Option D is procedurally clever and substantively an abdication: it converts a professional-standards question you are equipped to resolve into a loyalty test addressed to the Permanent Secretary, forces a senior figure to own a technical definition, and signals that your function's measures are negotiable given sufficient rank — the precise opposite of what a reporting standard is for. Scored behaviours: integrity with constructive form, protecting user-experienced measures, institutionalising definitions against drift, and resolving pressure at your own level rather than weaponising the level above. Note the pattern shared with Question 1: the credited executive move under pressure is rarely yes or no — it is the reframed both, with the honest element structurally protected.

**Question 9 (hard) — The crisis with a governance fork**

At 16:40 Friday, a supplier notifies you of a likely data breach: a misconfigured storage service may have exposed citizen records from two of your services; their forensics "should know by Monday". Statutory assessment and notification duties may apply within 72 hours of awareness. Your organisation's incident policy — which you own — designates you the senior responsible owner for exactly this scenario. The Chief Digital Officer, about to board a flight, texts: "Hold off any formal reporting until we know more — no point alarming people over a maybe." What is the most effective course?

- A) Follow the CDO's steer — they are senior, the facts are genuinely uncertain, and Monday is within reach
- B) Activate the incident process now as the policy's designated owner: convene the response team, notify the data protection officer immediately so the statutory 72-hour assessment starts on accurate footing, instruct the supplier on evidence preservation and a shorter forensic timeline, prepare — without yet issuing — external notifications, and inform the CDO of the actions taken and the statutory basis, in writing, before their flight lands
- C) Split the difference: begin technical investigation but delay involving the data protection officer until the supplier's Monday report
- D) Escalate the CDO's instruction to the Permanent Secretary's office for a ruling before acting

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Strip the scenario to its structure: a statutory clock that may already be running ("within 72 hours of awareness" — and you are aware, of a *likely* breach), a policy that names you decision-owner for exactly this, and an instruction — informal, in transit, from someone senior — to do the one thing the statutory framework is designed to prevent: waiting for certainty before assessing. Option B is the answer because every element of it is the designated owner doing the designated job: the DPO's immediate involvement is what converts "maybe" into a defensible, documented assessment (the 72-hour duty is to *assess and where required notify*, not to be certain — Monday's forensics inform the assessment; they do not precede it); evidence preservation instructions to the supplier protect the facts while they are freshest; prepared-but-unissued notifications separate readiness from alarm, answering the CDO's legitimate concern inside a lawful structure; and the written brief to the CDO — actions, statutory basis, before landing — is candour to power performed with precision: you are not defying the CDO, you are informing them that the policy they and the board approved has activated, with you as its named owner. Option A converts an informal text into cover for a potential statutory failure — cover that will not hold, since the policy names *you*; "the CDO texted" is not a defence available to the designated owner. Option C is the subtle trap: it looks proportionate but delays exactly the actor (the DPO) whose involvement the 72-hour duty presupposes — technical investigation without assessment machinery is motion without compliance. Option D outsources a decision the policy already made — the ruling exists; you own it; Friday-evening escalation to the Permanent Secretary buys hierarchy-shaped delay against a statutory clock. Scored behaviours: ownership of designated accountability under contrary pressure, statutory literacy, the prepared-not-panicked middle path on communications, and managing upward with written precision at speed. The head's crisis rule: when the policy names you, the policy is your instruction — and everyone senior to you is someone you inform, promptly and in writing, while you do your job.

**Question 10 (hard) — The succession you report to the board**

The board's people committee asks you annually: "Is the service management function resilient to the loss of key individuals?" This year the honest answer has changed: your deputy — your only ready successor and the named continuity owner for three critical processes — has confidentially told you she is likely to leave within six months for family reasons, and has asked that this remain private until she decides. Behind her, the succession pipeline is thin: two possible candidates, each 18 months from readiness. What is the most effective way to handle the committee?

- A) Report the function as resilient — her departure is not certain, and her confidence is binding
- B) Disclose her likely departure to the committee in closed session — its duty to know overrides her request
- C) Report the structural truth without the personal fact: succession depth behind key roles is one-deep with an 18-month readiness gap — a red rating on the committee's own scale — with an accelerated development plan for both candidates, cross-training on the three critical processes, and a market-scan contingency; separately, honour her confidence entirely while quietly sequencing the knowledge-transfer work her possible departure makes urgent
- D) Persuade her to authorise disclosure before the committee meets, making her situation the centrepiece of the succession discussion

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The scenario appears to force a choice between candour to the committee and faith with your deputy — and the credited answer dissolves the dilemma by locating what the committee actually needs, which is not the personal fact but the structural one. A pipeline that is one-deep with 18-month readiness gaps is a red rating *whether or not she leaves*: her confidence changed your urgency, not the function's objective fragility — a fragility that predates her news and that an honest head should arguably have been reporting already. Option C reports that truth at full strength (note: at the committee's own scale — no softening because the trigger is private), pairs it with the mitigation programme that makes the rating actionable, and executes the knowledge-transfer work under cover of ordinary resilience practice, which is what it genuinely is. Her confidence is kept perfectly; the committee is informed accurately; the function is protected either way she decides. Option A fails all three parties at once — it reports a resilience the pipeline cannot support, converts her confidence into your misrepresentation, and wastes the six-month warning her trust gave you. Option B pays for institutional candour with personal betrayal — and buys almost nothing: the committee's decisions (fund development, deepen the pipeline) are identical under option C's structural disclosure, so the breach of confidence purchases no additional governance, only the certainty that no one confides in you again — a head who cannot hold confidences loses the early warnings that make functions governable. Option D applies pressure to someone navigating a family decision in order to improve your meeting — the request itself damages the relationship option B would destroy. Scored behaviours: candour at the right level of abstraction, absolute faith with confidences, proactive resilience work, and the senior skill of finding the disclosure that serves every duty at once. The general form: when a private fact and a public duty collide, ask what the duty actually requires — it is almost always the structural truth, which can be told completely without the personal one.

**Question 11 (hard) — Ranking: the head's Tuesday**

Four items compete for your personal attention on Tuesday morning: (1) the Cabinet Office's cross-government incident-reporting return, due Friday, which your team has drafted but which contains a categorisation choice you suspect overstates your function's performance; (2) a request from your counterpart in another department for an urgent reference call about a supplier you exited last year for concealed subcontracting failures — they sign their own contract with that supplier Thursday; (3) your monthly skip-level listening session with junior analysts, scheduled for 11:00, cancelled and rearranged twice already this quarter; (4) a director's complaint, received overnight, that your service desk "failed her team again", with a demand for a meeting today. Rank the order in which you address them:

- A) 4, 1, 2, 3
- B) 2, 4, 3, 1
- C) 2, 3, 1, 4
- D) 1, 2, 4, 3

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Rank by irreversibility, unique-to-you-ness, and the signals your choices send. Item 2 leads decisively: another department commits public money Thursday to a supplier you exited for *concealed* failures — information asymmetry only you can correct, with a hard external deadline and consequences (their citizens, their budget) that your fifteen-minute call prevents; cross-government candour of exactly this kind is also the professional-community duty your role's senior representation implies, and it is the item where delay is least recoverable. Item 3 second — the counterintuitive placement the question is built around: a skip-level session cancelled twice already is no longer a diary item but a credibility instrument; the third cancellation tells every junior analyst that listening to them is what the head does when nothing else is happening, and the resulting silence is how heads stop hearing the truth from below (the early warnings of Question 10, the quiet departures of Question 5 — all arrive through channels this session keeps open). It is immovable only because you have made it so twice. Item 1 third: the return is due Friday — the work fits Tuesday afternoon comfortably — but its *content* is non-negotiable before submission: a categorisation you suspect flatters your function, sent to the Cabinet Office under your name, is Question 8's drift in cross-government form; the ranking defers the *when*, never the *whether*, of the correction. Item 4 last, deliberately: the director's "again" deserves a same-day acknowledgement, a fact-gathering instruction to the service desk manager (whose job the first-line response is — the head who personally absorbs every stakeholder complaint trains the organisation to escalate everything and their managers to own nothing), and a meeting *tomorrow*, with facts — which serves the director better than an evidence-free meeting today. Option A dances to the loudest voice; option D treats the internal deadline as more binding than the external harm; option B nearly succeeds but sacrifices the listening session to the complaint — the exact trade that, repeated, hollows out a head's information supply. Scored behaviours: cross-boundary candour, protection of upward-listening channels, integrity before deadlines rather than at them, and complaint-handling that develops managers instead of bypassing them.

**Question 12 (hard) — The strategy meets the machinery change**

Eighteen months into your three-year service management strategy — consolidation to a single operating model, one toolset, federated but standardised processes, all progressing well — a machinery of government change is announced: your department will merge with a smaller department whose service management arrangements are entirely different (different framework edition, different toolset mid-contract, a well-regarded 40-person function led by a respected head who now reports to you). The merged department's executive asks for your "revised strategy" in six weeks. What is the most effective approach?

- A) Extend your existing strategy to the incoming organisation: it was board-approved, it is working, and a single model was always its destination — the incoming function migrates to it on an accelerated timeline
- B) Run the six weeks as a genuine assessment with the incoming head as co-author: evaluate both operating models against the merged organisation's needs on evidence (maturity, cost, contract positions, user outcomes), expect the answer to be a composite — including the possibility that elements of the smaller function's model are superior and survive — and present a revised strategy with an honest transition cost line, a protected-delivery core, and named leadership roles for both functions' senior people
- C) Pause all strategy delivery pending the merger's full organisational design, submitting an interim holding position
- D) Propose the two functions continue operating separately for the strategy's remaining 18 months, merging only when the current strategy completes

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Machinery changes are where heads reveal whether their strategy was an evidence-based design or a possession. Option B treats it as the former, and each of its elements carries weight. Co-authorship with the incoming head is the decisive move — practically (their function's knowledge walks out the door if its leadership is conquered rather than enlisted; recall that 40 respected people are watching exactly how their head is treated), and evidentially: your **Business analysis** skill — absorbing conflicting information to produce solutions — cannot operate on information the other side has no reason to share honestly. The genuine-assessment framing matters because it is the only credible one: "evaluate both models" conducted as theatre is detected immediately, while a real evaluation that adopts the smaller function's superior elements (their newer framework edition? their toolset's mid-contract position may be an asset, not an obstacle) purchases something no mandate can — the incoming function's belief that the merged model is *theirs too*. The honest transition cost line and protected-delivery core answer the two failure modes of merger strategies: costless-integration fiction, and integration that consumes all delivery. Option A is the incumbent's reflex dressed as continuity: it converts a board-approved strategy for a department that no longer exists into an instrument of conquest, guaranteeing the resistance, attrition, and quiet knowledge-loss that make "accelerated migrations" slow. Option C surrenders the initiative your six-week request explicitly offers — holding positions submitted to new executives become other people's decisions about your function. Option D proposes the merged department run two service management functions for 18 months to protect your strategy's original schedule — the strategy serving itself, visibly, at organisational cost; new executives read exactly that. Scored behaviours: evidence over ownership, integration leadership that enlists rather than annexes, honest costing of transitions, and strategy re-design treated as the strategic-thinking skill's真 test — leading the *evaluation* of strategy, including your own, when the organisation underneath it changes. The head's merger rule: the strategy that survives should be the one the evidence would choose if neither author were in the room — and both authors should be in the room to prove it.

### Preparation tips

- **Reread the Civil Service values and behaviours as an executive checklist.** At head level the scored behaviours are candour to power, stewardship beyond tenure, visible self-compliance, and developing successors — learn to recognise them inside options where they carry short-term cost, because that is where the discrimination lives.
- **Audit your own reflexes before the assessment.** Review your last quarter honestly: what did you absorb that a report should have owned? What did you defer upward that was yours to decide? Which formal process did you shortcut through a relationship? The SJT probes exactly these calibrations.
- **Practise the reframed-both move.** The credited executive answers repeatedly refuse binary framings: two dates with confidence levels, two metrics with recorded primacy, two operating models genuinely assessed. Before answering any dilemma, construct the option that serves both legitimate interests with the honest element structurally protected.
- **Rehearse crisis ownership against your own policies.** For each policy you own, ask: in its designated scenario, what would I actually do in hour one — and would I follow it against a senior informal steer? Knowing your answer before the scenario, in tests and in life, is the preparation.
- **For ranking questions, weigh irreversibility, unique-to-you-ness, and signal.** External harms only you can prevent outrank internal deadlines; twice-cancelled listening channels outrank loud complaints; and the *whether* of an integrity correction never moves, only its *when*.
- **In least-effective formats, hunt the relationship shortcut around a formal process.** At head level the fatal option is rarely lazy or dishonest on its face — it is the informal call to the chair, the pre-emptive word with the patrons, the metric moved for comparability. Process-corrosion is the executive poison.
- **Answer as the steward, not the incumbent.** Several scenarios test whether you optimise for your tenure or the function's future. The credited answers consistently spend your comfort — this meeting, this budget line, this strategy's original shape — to protect the function's capability, honesty, and people.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Giving power the number it wants at the confidence you do not have.** Committed dates, resilient ratings, favourable categorisations — each purchase of a comfortable meeting is financed by a future failure carrying your name.
- **Enforcing where you should enlist.** Shadow functions, incoming departments, powerful directorates: instruction without diagnosis converts demand signals into resistance movements. Understand the need, then federate it under standards.
- **Waiving your own controls — once.** The head's exception is the organisation's precedent. Work the parallel paths, hold the line, and fix the genuinely defective clause through governance afterwards.
- **Breaching a confidence to perform candour.** The structural truth almost always serves the institutional duty completely; the personal fact almost never adds governance. Find the disclosure level that honours both — it exists.
- **Shortcutting formal processes through relationships.** The informal word with the chair, the pre-briefed patrons, the hierarchy invoked to settle a professional-standards question — each wins an hour and corrodes the institution that makes your role possible.
- **Letting the loudest item order your day.** Directors' complaints and internal deadlines are recoverable; another department's Thursday signature and a thrice-cancelled listening session are not. Rank by irreversibility and by what only you can do.
- **Mortgaging capability to protect current service.** Cuts aimed at training, improvement, and succession pipelines are invisible for exactly as long as your tenure. Steward the function you will hand over, not the dashboard you report.
- **Defending your strategy as property.** Strategies are evidence-based instruments for organisations that keep changing. The head who re-evaluates their own strategy honestly — with the incoming rival as co-author — is exercising the skill; the head who extends it by mandate is exhibiting the possession.
