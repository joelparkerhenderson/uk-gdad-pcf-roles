# Senior User Researcher - Psychometric Assessment Resources

## Introduction

Welcome. This guide is written for you as a senior user researcher in the UK Government Digital and Data profession — an experienced practitioner who plans and leads research in larger teams and on more complex services, builds user-centred practice where it does not yet exist, aligns research with the wider plans that shape a service proposition, and supervises and develops other researchers. That is a role defined as much by judgement and influence as by technique, and the assessments in this guide are pitched accordingly.

You may be preparing for a formal assessment as part of a selection or promotion process, or you may be here because the reasoning these tests sample is the reasoning your job runs on. Either way, it is worth being precise about what psychometric assessment is at this level. It is not an intelligence test and it is not a knowledge quiz. It is a standardised sample of the thinking a role demands, and for a senior practitioner that thinking has a distinctive character: you are rarely choosing between a right and a wrong answer, and almost always choosing between defensible options under incomplete information, competing pressures and real consequences.

Three features of your role shape everything that follows. First, you are accountable for research quality beyond your own hands — you critique colleagues' findings, evaluate work against professional standards, and give feedback that has to be both honest and usable. That means the assessments will test whether you can spot a flaw in someone else's reasoning, not only avoid one in your own. Second, you work with sceptical people. Your role description names engaging sceptical colleagues in analysis and advocating for user research with sceptical stakeholders, which is a polite way of saying that a substantial part of your job is being disbelieved by people with more organisational power than you. Third, you operate strategically: understanding the decisions a team needs to make so you can design research that informs them, and aligning research plans to service priorities rather than to your own curiosity.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the reasoning your role demands, presented through practical materials rather than abstract puzzles. At senior level the materials are messier and the reasoning less mechanical. You will meet evidence that supports two incompatible readings, research designs with a flaw that compromises some conclusions but not others, prioritisation problems with no dominant option, and claims made by other people that need diagnosing rather than merely accepting or rejecting.

The format is typically an online test of fifteen to thirty minutes covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective against a norm group at a comparable level, which means the comparison set is other experienced practitioners. Adaptive platforms raise difficulty after correct answers, so a hard test is usually a good sign. Employers generally receive an acuity report distinguishing speed from accuracy, which rewards precision over pace.

What distinguishes this section at your level is the shift from producing sound reasoning to auditing it. Your role names evaluating the quality of research against accepted professional standards, critiquing colleagues' findings to assure best practice, and advising on the choice and application of techniques. Each of those is a diagnostic act performed on somebody else's work, often under time pressure and often in a setting where saying "this does not support that conclusion" carries a social cost. Several items below put you in exactly that position.

A second distinguishing feature is strategic reasoning. Understanding the decisions the team needs to make so you can design appropriate research is a named skill, and it turns prioritisation from an administrative question into a methodological one. The strongest prioritisation answers at this level do not rank tasks by importance; they identify which research could still change which decision, and within what window.

A third is comfort with irreducible uncertainty. Several questions have no clean answer, and the discriminating factor is whether you can identify the strongest reading of ambiguous evidence and state its limits, rather than collapsing prematurely into confidence. Holding "this is worth acting on" and "this is not yet established" simultaneously is uncomfortable, and it is what senior research judgement consists of.

You need to memorise nothing. Everything you need appears in the question. What you need to bring is the discipline not to let fluency substitute for care.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Auditing a research plan**

Your organisation's standard requires research plans to state: research questions, method, recruitment approach, sample composition including access needs, analysis approach, ethical considerations, and dissemination. A plan you are assuring states the research questions, the method, the recruitment approach, the analysis approach, and notes that "findings will go to the sprint review and the repository". How many required elements are absent?

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Work through all seven. Research questions, method, recruitment approach and analysis approach are present. The dissemination requirement is satisfied by the note about the sprint review and repository. That leaves sample composition including access needs, and ethical considerations — two absences, so the answer is B. Option C typically comes from treating the dissemination note as insufficient, and option A from overlooking one of the two genuine gaps. Note which elements are missing, because it is not random: sample composition and ethics are the two requirements most often skipped, and they are the two whose absence most reliably produces research that either fails excluded users or harms participants. When assuring plans, those are the fields to check first rather than last.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction from a governance rule**

Your standard states: "Research programmes spanning more than one service require sign-off from the head of user research, unless every constituent study has already been individually approved through the departmental ethics process." A programme lead tells you: "Our three-service programme started last month without sign-off from the head of user research." Which must be true?

- A) The standard was breached.
- B) Every constituent study had individual ethics approval.
- C) Either every constituent study had individual ethics approval, or the standard was breached.
- D) The programme should be paused.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A conditional rule with a stated exception, plus facts silent on the exception, yields only the disjunction. Options A and B each assume a branch without evidence. Option D prescribes an action rather than stating what must be true, and the question asks the latter — a distinction worth noticing, because assessments at this level frequently offer a sensible action as a distractor in a logic item. Only C holds in every case. The professional analogue is direct and matters more at your level than at a junior one: as the person likely to be assuring this programme, your opening move is to establish which route was taken, and framing that as an enquiry rather than a finding preserves both accuracy and the relationship you will need if the answer is bad.

**Question 3 (moderate) — Diagnosing a colleague's conclusion**

A researcher you supervise reports: "Users trust the new identity verification flow — 11 of 14 participants said they were comfortable with it." The sessions were conducted in a government building, with participants who had been recruited through the departmental panel, and the comfort question was asked by the facilitator at the end of the session. What is the most important methodological concern?

- A) Fourteen participants is too small a sample.
- B) The setting, the recruitment source and the direct question from the facilitator all push in the same direction — towards socially desirable responses about trusting a government process — so the finding is unusually vulnerable to acquiescence bias and cannot support a claim about trust.
- C) Comfort is not the same as trust.
- D) The question should have been asked at the start.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Several concerns are legitimate here, and the strongest answer is the one that identifies the compounding structure rather than a single weakness. Each factor alone would warrant caution: a government building primes deference, a departmental panel selects for people already well disposed to the department, and a direct question from the facilitator invites the answer the participant thinks is wanted. Together they all bias in the same direction, which is what makes the finding untrustworthy rather than merely imperfect. Option C is a real and good point — comfort and trust are distinct constructs — but it is a subset of the problem and does not explain why the number is inflated. Option A misidentifies the issue; fourteen is a reasonable qualitative sample, and the problem is validity, not size. Option D would help marginally but does not address the setting or the recruitment source. When you supervise, the useful feedback names the compounding, because fixing one factor while leaving the other two produces the same result.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Strategic prioritisation across teams**

You lead research across three teams and have capacity to support one substantial study this quarter. The candidates:

1. Team A is choosing between two service models next month; the decision commits three years of build.
2. Team B has a live service with a persistent 30% drop-off at one step.
3. Team C is about to launch and wants pre-launch usability testing on screens already signed off.
4. A director wants research to support a business case due in six months.

Which is strongest, and why?

- A) Option 2, because it affects the most users now.
- B) Option 1, because the decision is imminent, irreversible for three years, and research is the only thing that could change it — making the value of information dramatically higher than in the other cases.
- C) Option 4, because it has the most senior sponsor.
- D) Option 3, because launching with untested screens is a risk.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The right frame at this level is the value of information: how much does a decision change as a result of what research tells you, and is there still time for it to do so? Option 1 scores highest on both. The decision is live, the commitment is enormous, and a service-model choice is precisely the kind of question where research can shift the answer rather than merely decorate it. Option 2 matters greatly but has persisted for months and will persist for three more; deferring it costs comparatively little, and it may also be better addressed with analytics first. Option 3 is the weakest despite sounding responsible: screens already signed off with a launch imminent means findings arrive with nowhere to go, which is the classic pattern of research performed for reassurance rather than for decisions. Option 4 has six months and a senior sponsor, neither of which makes it urgent. Note that C is the seniority trap, and at your level it is a genuine risk, because directors ask directly.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Competing explanations**

Analytics show that completion of an application is 71% for users who arrive from a GOV.UK guidance page and 43% for users who arrive from a search engine result linking directly to the form. A product manager proposes improving the form. What is the most rigorous analysis?

- A) Agree; the form is clearly the problem for direct arrivals.
- B) The two groups differ in preparation, not necessarily in what they encounter: users arriving via guidance have typically read what is required and gathered documents, while direct arrivals may not know what the form needs — so the intervention with the highest expected value may be at the point of entry rather than in the form itself.
- C) The difference is too large to be explained by anything other than the form.
- D) Recommend blocking direct entry to the form.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The referral route is a marker for what the user has already done before arriving, and this is a common and consequential confound in service analytics. Someone who read a guidance page has usually learned what evidence is required, decided they are eligible, and assembled what they need. Someone who lands on the form from a search result may be discovering all of that mid-application. That difference in preparation can account for much of a 28 percentage point gap without the form itself being worse for either group. Option B also draws the practical implication, which is what makes it a rigorous analysis rather than merely a sceptical one: the highest-value fix may be a lightweight "what you'll need" interstitial for direct arrivals rather than a form redesign. Option A acts on an unexamined correlation. Option C mistakes effect size for causal certainty, which is a surprisingly common error among people comfortable with data. Option D would harm users who legitimately arrive prepared.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Feedback on a colleague's synthesis**

A researcher presents an affinity map with six themes drawn from twelve sessions. One theme, "users want more automation", contains four quotes; three come from the same participant. What is the most useful feedback?

- A) The theme is invalid and should be removed.
- B) Note that the theme currently rests on two participants, one of whom dominates it, and suggest either re-examining whether other sessions contain related material or re-labelling it as an observation from a small number of participants rather than a theme.
- C) Suggest running more sessions.
- D) Approve it; four quotes is enough evidence.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The issue is that quote count has been mistaken for participant breadth, which is one of the most common flaws in affinity mapping and one of the easiest to miss when the wall looks full. Two participants out of twelve is a weak base for a theme presented alongside five others, and a reader of the output will assume otherwise. Option B offers two proportionate routes: look again, because related material may have been coded elsewhere and genuinely belongs here, or re-label honestly. Both preserve the observation while correcting the claim. Option A discards something potentially real and is disproportionate feedback for a fixable problem. Option D approves a misleading output. Option C is not wrong in principle but is expensive and premature before the existing data has been re-examined. Your role names critiquing colleagues' findings to assure best practice, and this is the texture of it: specific, fixable, and framed so the researcher can act without feeling dismantled.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Method choice under constraint**

You need to understand why a complex, multi-week benefits application is abandoned midway. Users complete it over several sessions, often waiting for documents from third parties. You have six weeks. What design is strongest?

- A) Moderated usability sessions on the form.
- B) A diary study with participants who are currently mid-application, supplemented by interviews at the point of abandonment or completion, because the phenomenon unfolds over weeks and includes events outside the service that a single session cannot observe.
- C) A survey of users who abandoned.
- D) Analytics analysis of drop-off points.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Match the design to the shape of the phenomenon. Abandonment here is not a moment at a screen; it is a process spread over weeks and heavily influenced by things happening outside the service, such as an employer failing to send a letter or a document arriving after a deadline the user did not know about. A moderated session captures a snapshot and will systematically miss the between-session decay where most abandonment actually occurs. Option B observes the process as it happens and catches the moment of abandonment while the reasons are still retrievable. Option C reaches only people willing to respond afterwards and relies on recall of a decision often made passively rather than deliberately. Option D locates where users stop but not why, though it is a valuable input for recruiting the diary study. Planning research for services with challenging user needs and complex journeys is a named skill, and the discriminating judgement is recognising when the unit of analysis is a journey rather than an interface.

**Question 8 (harder) — A flaw that compromises some claims but not others**

A study you are assuring used a prototype that, unknown to the researcher at the time, had a broken back button throughout. The report contains four claims: (i) participants could not find the eligibility criteria; (ii) participants abandoned the journey at the evidence step; (iii) the task took longer than expected; (iv) participants found the language of the declaration confusing. Which claims are most compromised?

- A) All four.
- B) None; the back button is a minor issue.
- C) Claims (ii) and (iii) are directly compromised, because inability to go back plausibly caused both abandonment and extended task times, while (i) and (iv) concern comprehension of content and are largely unaffected.
- D) Only claim (ii).

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The discipline being tested is scoping a flaw precisely rather than treating it as total or trivial. A broken back button prevents recovery from a wrong turn, which directly manufactures both abandonment and inflated task times — so claims (ii) and (iii) cannot be separated from the artefact. Claims (i) and (iv) concern whether participants could find and understand content, which the back button does not affect; a participant who cannot locate the eligibility criteria could not locate them regardless of navigation history. Option A over-corrects and discards two sound findings. Option B under-corrects and leaves two manufactured findings in circulation. Option D catches the obvious compromise and misses the task-time one. This is exactly the judgement your assurance role requires: an audit that condemns everything is as unhelpful as one that condemns nothing, because neither tells the team what it can rely on.

**Question 9 (harder) — Aligning research with a proposition**

Your team is developing a service proposition. The product manager wants research on which of three interface designs users prefer. You believe the more consequential open question is whether users understand the service exists and what it is for. How should you proceed?

- A) Run the interface research as requested; it is the team's decision.
- B) Run the research on comprehension and awareness instead, since it is more important.
- C) Set out both questions explicitly, show what each would tell the team and what decision each would inform, and let the team see that a preference between three designs is a low-stakes decision while whether users can find and understand the service determines whether any design matters — then agree the sequence together.
- D) Propose splitting the study to cover both.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** You have a genuine strategic disagreement, and the strongest move is to make the reasoning visible rather than to comply or to override. Option C reframes the choice in terms the product manager can evaluate: what decision does each question inform, and what happens if we get it wrong? A preference test between three designs has a bounded downside — pick the wrong one and the service is slightly worse. A service nobody finds or understands fails regardless of interface. Presenting it this way usually resolves the disagreement without anyone having to lose an argument, and it also builds the team's capacity to prioritise research themselves, which is part of building user-centred practice. Option A defers on a question where your expertise is the reason you were hired. Option B overrides unilaterally, which wins this round and costs you the relationship. Option D dilutes both and is the characteristic failure of compromise under constraint.

**Question 10 (harder) — Building practice in a sceptical team**

You have joined a team that has never worked with a user researcher. The delivery manager says research is welcome "as long as it doesn't slow us down", and the team has a two-week sprint cadence. What is the strongest approach for the first month?

- A) Run a comprehensive discovery to establish the user needs properly before anything else.
- B) Start with something small, fast and visibly useful that fits inside a sprint — for example testing what the team is about to build, with the whole team observing — so that the value is demonstrated in their terms before you ask for more; then use what surfaces to make the case for deeper work.
- C) Explain the service standard's research requirements and the risk of non-compliance.
- D) Ask the delivery manager to allocate research time in every sprint.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A team that has never worked with a researcher does not yet have a reason to trust that research repays its cost, and no amount of explanation substitutes for the experience of watching a user struggle with something you built. Option B is calibrated to that: it fits the existing cadence rather than demanding a change to it, it produces something the team can act on within their sprint, and — crucially — it gets the team into the sessions as observers, which converts research from a report they receive into an experience they had. Option A is right in principle and wrong in sequence; a month-long discovery in a team that is sceptical about slowing down confirms their fear before you have earned any credit. Option C wins compliance and loses goodwill, and compliance-driven research is performed rather than used. Option D asks for the resource before demonstrating the return. Your role names helping inexperienced teams adopt user-centred practices, and the reliable pattern is demonstrate, then expand.

**Question 11 (harder) — Contradictory evidence across sources**

For the same service, you have: analytics showing 84% task completion; a satisfaction survey at 79% positive; your own fourteen sessions in which nine participants experienced significant difficulty; and a support desk reporting the service as their second-highest call driver. How should you synthesise this?

- A) The qualitative evidence is outweighed by the quantitative.
- B) The sources measure different populations and constructs, and taken together they suggest a service that most people complete, many find hard, and a substantial minority need help with — the support desk volume being the strongest corroboration of the session findings, since it reflects real users seeking help at scale.
- C) The sources conflict and no synthesis is possible.
- D) The service is performing well with minor usability issues.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The apparent conflict resolves once you note who each source sees and what it measures. Completion analytics count people who got through, including those who struggled or phoned for help. Satisfaction surveys reach people who reached the end and often ask about outcome rather than process. Your sessions observed the process. The support desk data is the pivot: high call volume is behavioural evidence, at scale, that people cannot complete unaided — which corroborates your sessions using precisely the kind of data that sceptics find credible. Option B assembles all four into a coherent picture without discarding any. Option A defers to scale over relevance. Option C gives up on a synthesis that is available. Option D is the comfortable reading and it ignores the support desk entirely, which is the single most telling number in the set. Recognising which available evidence will persuade a sceptical audience — and leading with it — is a senior skill in itself.

**Question 12 (harder) — Assuring research you did not commission**

A team presents findings from research conducted by an agency: 400 survey responses showing 88% satisfaction, used to justify closing a telephone channel. You notice the survey was served only within the online service. What is the most important observation?

- A) 400 responses is a small sample for a national service.
- B) The survey could only reach people already using the online channel, so it structurally excludes the people who use the telephone channel — the exact population whose loss is at issue — and therefore cannot support a decision to close it.
- C) Satisfaction is not a good measure of channel need.
- D) The agency should have used a larger sample.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The sampling frame makes the evidence not merely weak for this purpose but structurally incapable of addressing it. Everyone who answered was, by definition, transacting online successfully enough to reach a survey; people who cannot or will not use the online service could not have responded. Using their satisfaction to justify removing the telephone channel is close to a formal error — it is asking the people unaffected by a decision whether the decision is acceptable. Option B names this precisely. Option C is a fair point and secondary; even a perfect measure of the wrong population would not support the conclusion. Options A and D treat it as a sample size problem, which is the comfortable technical objection and the wrong one — 4,000 responses from the same frame would be equally useless. This is the kind of observation your assurance role exists to produce, and it is worth noticing how much organisational courage it takes to make it when a decision is already moving.

### Preparation tips

- **Practise scoping flaws precisely.** For any methodological problem, identify which specific claims it compromises and which survive. Total condemnation and total dismissal are both weak audits.
- **Ask who could not have appeared in this data.** Sampling frame exclusions are the highest-yield question you can ask of somebody else's evidence.
- **Frame prioritisation as value of information.** Which decision changes, how much is at stake, and is there still time?
- **Look for compounding bias.** Several small biases pointing the same way are qualitatively worse than several pointing different ways.
- **Rehearse the strength-of-claim ladder.** Observation, pattern, supported finding, measured rate — and what each licenses.
- **Practise auditing real outputs.** Take a report from another team and write three specific, usable critiques. This is your role's core skill and it improves with deliberate practice.
- **Read the stem's final sentence twice.** At this level items often ask something narrower than the scenario implies, and a sensible action is frequently offered as a distractor in a logic question.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Treating effect size as causal certainty.** A 28 point gap can still be entirely confounded.
- **Mistaking quote count for participant breadth.** Four quotes from two people is not a theme.
- **Ranking by sponsor seniority.** Directors ask directly at your level, which makes this a live risk rather than a theoretical one.
- **Reaching for sample size as the objection.** When the sampling frame is wrong, more of the same data is no better.
- **Overriding a team rather than making reasoning visible.** You win the decision and lose the capability you were meant to build.
- **Compromising by splitting a constrained resource.** Half a strategic study plus half a tactical one is usually two useless studies.
- **Condemning a flawed study wholesale.** Teams need to know what they can still rely on.
- **Letting fluency substitute for care.** Experience makes overreach feel like insight.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A numeric reasoning assessment measures how accurately you handle quantitative material. At senior level the emphasis shifts decisively from calculating to interrogating. You will still need to compute rates and averages correctly, but the harder items ask whether a figure means what somebody claims it means — which is, not coincidentally, the question you are most often the only person in the room able to ask.

The format is typically a timed online test of fifteen to thirty minutes, each item presenting a table, chart or paragraph of figures with a multiple-choice question. Time per item is tight, so the operative skill is locating what matters and disregarding what does not. Calculators are usually provided; the arithmetic is deliberately tractable.

There is a professional stake here worth naming plainly. Quantitative evidence carries unearned authority in most organisations: a number in a slide is treated as fact, a qualitative finding as an opinion, regardless of the rigour behind either. Senior researchers who cannot interrogate numbers confidently lose arguments they should win, and occasionally win arguments they should lose. Being the person who asks "what is the denominator, and who could not have appeared in this data?" is a substantial part of the value you add — and your role's requirement to influence stakeholders and resolve issues frequently comes down to exactly that question, asked calmly, in a room where a decision is already moving.

Four habits carry most of the load. Establish what the question asks before looking at the data. Identify the denominator, and the population it represents, before calculating. Estimate, calculate, then compare the two. And sanity-check against the world: a figure implying that 40% of users complete a form in under three seconds is measuring something other than what it claims.

A caution about distractors. They are constructed from specific, predictable errors — wrong denominator, percentage confused with percentage point, new value used as the base in a percentage change, averages averaged. Finding your answer among the options is not evidence that you are right. But knowing the catalogue means you can often see the trap being set.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The numerical skills tested map onto your named skills:

- **Interrogating quantitative claims** maps to **Research management, leadership and assurance**, where you evaluate the quality of research against professional standards.
- **Comparing rates between user groups** maps to **Inclusive research**, where gaps between groups are frequently the first visible trace of an accessibility barrier.
- **Judging sampling adequacy** maps to **User research methods**, where you advise colleagues on the choice and application of methods.
- **Combining quantitative and qualitative evidence** maps to **Analysis and synthesis**, where you help teams apply a range of methods and critique findings.
- **Presenting figures at defensible strength** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**, where you influence stakeholders and use evidence to resolve issues.
- **Detecting misleading metrics** maps to **User-centred practice and advocacy**, where advocacy survives only if the evidence behind it does.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Rate from a table**

Of 32 participants across a research programme, 24 completed the core task unaided. What percentage is that?

- A) 66%
- B) 72%
- C) 75%
- D) 80%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 24 ÷ 32 = 0.75, so 75%, and the answer is C. Estimate first: 24 of 32 is three quarters, since 16 would be half. Options A and B come from dividing by 36 and 33 respectively — the sort of mis-keyed entry that time pressure produces. Worth noting even here that you would not report this as "75% of users"; it is 24 of 32 participants across a programme, and if those 32 came from three studies with different recruitment approaches, aggregating them into a single rate may itself be questionable.

**Question 2 (easy) — Over-recruitment**

Your no-show rate runs at 30%. You need 21 completed sessions. How many bookings?

- A) 27
- B) 28
- C) 30
- D) 32

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** If 30% do not attend, 70% do, so you need bookings whose 70% is 21: 21 ÷ 0.7 = 30, giving C. The common error is adding 30% to 21, which gives 27.3 and points at A — an underestimate, because the percentage applies to the larger booking number. Verify: 30% of 30 is 9, and 30 − 9 = 21. At programme scale this matters more than it sounds; across five studies, a systematic under-recruitment of ten per cent leaves you consistently short of the sample composition your plans committed to, and it is the access-need and minority-group quotas that fail first.

**Question 3 (easy) — Comparing group rates**

| Group | Participants | Completed |
|---|---|---|
| Digitally confident | 20 | 18 |
| Low digital confidence | 12 | 6 |
| Assistive technology users | 8 | 2 |

What is the gap in percentage points between the highest and lowest completion rates?

- A) 40
- B) 50
- C) 65
- D) 75

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Calculate each rate: 18 ÷ 20 = 90%; 6 ÷ 12 = 50%; 2 ÷ 8 = 25%. The gap between highest and lowest is 90 − 25 = 65 percentage points, so C. Option A is the gap between the top two, option B is the middle group's rate mistaken for a gap. Beyond the arithmetic, note what a 65 point spread means for how findings should be reported: a single overall completion figure for these 40 participants would be 26 ÷ 40 = 65%, a number that describes no group in the study and conceals the fact that assistive technology users are failing three times out of four. Whenever you can disaggregate, do, and say so.

**Question 4 (easy) — Median and spread**

Nine sessions produced task times in minutes of: 5, 6, 7, 7, 8, 9, 10, 12, 54. Which summary is most defensible?

- A) Mean 13.1 minutes
- B) Median 8 minutes, range 5 to 54, with the 54-minute case described separately
- C) Median 8 minutes
- D) Mean 13.1 minutes, range 5 to 54

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The mean of 13.1 exceeds all but one observation, so it describes nobody. The median, the fifth value in the ordered list, is 8, which represents the group well. But reporting the median alone conceals a session that took nearly seven times the typical duration, and that session is almost certainly the most informative thing in the dataset — something went badly wrong for that person. Option B gives the central figure, the spread, and explicit treatment of the outlier. Option D pairs the range with a misleading centre. Option C hides the outlier. The general rule at your level, and one worth enforcing in the work you assure: a central figure never travels alone.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Weighted combination**

Three studies: Study 1 had 10 participants with a 40% completion rate; Study 2 had 25 participants at 60%; Study 3 had 15 participants at 80%. What is the overall completion rate across all 50 participants?

- A) 60%
- B) 62%
- C) 63%
- D) 65%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Convert to counts before combining. Study 1: 40% of 10 = 4 completions. Study 2: 60% of 25 = 15. Study 3: 80% of 15 = 12. Total completions 4 + 15 + 12 = 31, across 10 + 25 + 15 = 50 participants, so 31 ÷ 50 = 0.62, which is 62% and the answer is B. Option A is the trap: the unweighted mean of 40, 60 and 80 is exactly 60, which looks plausible and silently over-weights the smallest study. Always return to counts when combining rates from groups of different sizes. One caution beyond the arithmetic, and it is the more important half at your level: aggregating completion rates across three studies is only meaningful if the task and the population were genuinely comparable. If Study 1 tested a harder task, or recruited a different user group, the combined figure is a number without a referent — and part of assuring research quality is asking whether the aggregation should have been performed at all.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Funnel denominators**

Funnel: 50,000 start, 38,000 pass identity, 22,800 upload evidence, 20,520 submit. What proportion of those who uploaded evidence went on to submit?

- A) 41%
- B) 54%
- C) 60%
- D) 90%

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** The stem names users who uploaded evidence, so the denominator is 22,800 and the part is 20,520: 20,520 ÷ 22,800 = 0.9, which is 90%, giving D. Option A uses 50,000, option B uses 38,000, and option C is the upload rate from identity. All four are true statements about this funnel; only one answers the question. Note also what the funnel tells you strategically: the largest single loss is identity to upload, where 15,200 users are lost, which is where research effort belongs — not at the submit step, where 90% of those who get there succeed.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Percentage points and relative change**

Completion rose from 35% to 49% after a change. Which statement is accurate?

- A) A 14% increase.
- B) A 14 percentage point increase, equivalent to a 40% relative increase.
- C) A 40 percentage point increase.
- D) A 28.6% increase.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The arithmetic gap is 49 − 35 = 14 percentage points. The relative increase is 14 ÷ 35 = 0.4, or 40%. Option B labels both correctly. Option A is ambiguous and typically read as relative, making it wrong. Option C misapplies the percentage-point label. Option D divides by the new value. The reason this matters at your level is not the arithmetic but the governance: "completion rose 40%" will appear in a business case, and it describes the same change as "completion rose 14 percentage points". Both are true; one is chosen. Being the person who notices which was chosen, and asks for both, is part of assuring the organisation's evidence.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Small bases**

A survey of 1,200 users includes 15 who identify as using a screen reader; 9 of them report being unable to complete a task. A report states: "60% of screen reader users cannot complete the task." What is the strongest objection?

- A) The arithmetic is wrong.
- B) A base of 15 cannot support a percentage stated to this precision, and the 15 are self-selected survey respondents already using the online service — so the figure both overstates its own reliability and generalises from a group that excludes those who never got far enough to answer.
- C) Screen reader users should not be reported separately.
- D) The survey needed more respondents.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The arithmetic is fine, which rules out A. The problems are precision and frame. A base of 15 means one respondent moves the figure by nearly seven percentage points, so "60%" implies a stability the data does not have. And the 15 are people who reached and completed a survey inside the online service, which excludes screen reader users who abandoned earlier — likely the ones experiencing the worst barriers. Option B names both. Option C is wrong in principle; disaggregation is how accessibility failures become visible, and the answer to a small base is honest reporting, not silence. Option D is a recommendation, not an objection. The defensible phrasing is "9 of the 15 screen reader users who responded reported being unable to complete the task", followed immediately by a recommendation for dedicated research — which reports the signal without manufacturing a rate.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Working back from a claim**

A stakeholder states that a 3 percentage point improvement in completion would be worth £2.1 million annually, and that current completion is 62% on 900,000 annual starts. What is the implied value per additional completed application?

- A) £26
- B) £78
- C) £2.33
- D) £39

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A 3 percentage point improvement on 900,000 starts means 3% of 900,000 = 27,000 additional completions. Dividing the stated benefit by that: £2,100,000 ÷ 27,000 = £77.78, so approximately £78, giving B. Option C divides by 900,000, valuing every start rather than every additional completion. Option A and option D come from using 62% somewhere it does not belong — the current rate is a distractor here, since the improvement is expressed in percentage points of the total, not of the current completers. The wider point is that business cases rest on figures like this, and being able to reconstruct the implied unit value in thirty seconds lets you ask whether it is plausible, which is often the most useful contribution available in the meeting.

**Question 10 (harder) — A metric that conflates populations**

A dashboard reports "average time to complete: 9 minutes" for a form your sessions showed taking most participants over twenty. What is the most likely explanation and the right response?

- A) The dashboard is wrong.
- B) The mean is probably pulled down by sessions that were abandoned early or by returning users completing a partly filled form, and it may exclude multi-session completions entirely — so the right response is to ask for the distribution and for the definition of a completed session, rather than to dispute the number.
- C) The participants in your sessions were atypical.
- D) The two figures cannot be compared.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A mean completion time typically mixes several populations: people who abandoned quickly and were nonetheless counted, returning users who finished a form started earlier and are recorded only for the final short visit, and genuine single-sitting completions. Many implementations also drop sessions that span days, which removes precisely the slowest and most difficult journeys from the calculation. The response that gets somewhere is to ask two questions — what does the distribution look like, and how is a completed session defined — rather than to challenge the figure head-on, because the figure is probably correctly calculated and wrongly interpreted. Option A picks a fight you will lose. Option C blames participants without evidence. Option D forecloses a reconciliation that is available and instructive.

**Question 11 (harder) — Sampling adequacy for a comparison**

A team proposes measuring whether a redesign improved completion by running 30 moderated sessions before and 30 after. Baseline completion is 55%; a 7 percentage point improvement would be considered meaningful. What is the strongest response?

- A) Agree; 60 sessions is a substantial study.
- B) Explain that a 7 point difference on 30 per group is about two participants and well inside ordinary variation, so the comparison cannot detect it — recommend measuring the rate with live analytics across thousands of users, and spending the 60 sessions on understanding mechanisms, which is what moderated research does uniquely well.
- C) Increase to 50 per group.
- D) Run it and report whatever difference appears.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Thirty per group cannot distinguish a seven point difference from noise; you would need several hundred per group. Option B says so and, importantly, redirects rather than merely objecting: analytics can measure the rate at negligible marginal cost across the whole user base, while moderated sessions are the only method that explains why the rate is what it is. Splitting the work along that line is the substantive recommendation. Option A endorses a design that cannot answer its own question. Option C moves towards adequacy without reaching it, and still misallocates scarce moderated capacity. Option D is the most damaging: reporting an observed difference from an underpowered comparison invites the organisation to act on noise, and — worse — establishes a precedent that small moderated studies can measure rates, which will be cited back at you for years. Advising colleagues on method choice to assure best practice is a named skill, and this is where it earns its keep.

**Question 12 (harder) — Interrogating a claim in a business case**

A business case states: "User research shows 92% of users can complete the service unaided." You trace the figure to a study of 25 participants, 23 of whom completed, recruited from the departmental panel. What is the most important thing to say?

- A) The figure should be 92% only if rounded correctly.
- B) A study of 25 panel participants cannot support a population rate at all; the honest statement is that 23 of 25 panel-recruited participants completed unaided, and the business case needs either analytics or a properly sampled survey if it requires a rate.
- C) The sample should have been larger.
- D) The panel is not representative.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The error is a category error, not a precision error: a small qualitative sample from a self-selected panel has been converted into a population statistic and embedded in a document that will commit money. Option B names what the evidence actually supports and specifies what would be needed instead, which is the constructive half — objecting without providing a route forward tends to get overruled. Option A treats it as a rounding question. Option C implies that a bigger sample from the same frame would fix it, which it would not. Option D is true and incomplete; even a perfectly representative sample of 25 could not support a rate stated to the nearest percentage point. This is among the highest-value interventions a senior researcher makes, and it is also among the least welcome, since the figure is usually load-bearing by the time you see it. Saying it anyway is the job.

### Preparation tips

- **Ask who could not have appeared in this data.** Sampling frame is the highest-yield question you can put to any figure.
- **Return to counts before combining rates.** Unweighted averages over-weight the smallest group.
- **Learn to reconstruct implied unit values.** Business cases are full of figures whose plausibility collapses under a thirty-second division.
- **Ask for the distribution and the definition.** Most misleading metrics are correctly calculated and wrongly defined.
- **Know what sample sizes can detect.** You need no formal power calculation to know that 30 per group cannot see seven points.
- **Practise on live business cases and dashboards in your organisation.** Trace three figures back to their source; you will learn more than any drill provides.
- **Keep the percentage point sentence automatic.** It appears constantly and decides items.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Averaging rates without weighting.** The single most common real-world error in aggregated reporting.
- **Objecting on sample size when the frame is the problem.** More of the same data is no better.
- **Converting qualitative samples into population rates.** This is the error most likely to end up in a business case.
- **Accepting a mean without a spread or a definition.** Bimodal data hides entirely behind an average.
- **Adding a percentage instead of dividing when over-recruiting.** For a 30% no-show rate, divide by 0.7.
- **Reporting percentages from tiny bases.** Nine of fifteen is not "60%" in any load-bearing sense.
- **Endorsing an underpowered comparison.** Acting on noise is worse than acting on nothing.
- **Trusting an answer because it appears among the options.** Distractors are built from exactly the errors candidates make.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately you read. In a senior role that runs on documents — service standards, ethics frameworks, evaluation reports, ministerial submissions, business cases, contracts and the transcribed words of participants — reading accuracy is the substrate everything else rests on. A misread exception clause in a governance document produces a compliance failure; a misread finding in a colleague's report produces bad advice given confidently.

The classic format presents a short passage of dense workplace prose, typically three to six sentences, followed by statements to classify as True, False or Cannot Say. True means the statement follows necessarily. False means the passage contradicts it. Cannot Say means the passage neither establishes nor excludes it. Alongside these come comprehension items, main-point items, and questions asking which of four summaries is most accurate.

The governing rule is that you answer from the passage alone. Your professional knowledge must be set aside entirely — and here is the uncomfortable part, which is specific to people at your level: expertise actively hurts you in this section. The more you know about research ethics or data protection or the Service Manual, the more readily you supply a connection the text does not make, and the more confident you feel while doing it. Senior candidates lose marks on verbal reasoning at rates that surprise them, and the mechanism is almost always this one.

Cannot Say deserves particular attention. Candidates avoid it because it feels like a failure to commit, and senior candidates avoid it more than most, because the professional habit of forming a view is deeply ingrained. In practice Cannot Say is frequently correct. The reliable diagnostic: if you are filling a gap with knowledge or inference, however reasonable, the answer is Cannot Say.

Small words carry the load. "All" is not "most". "May" is not "must". "Should" is not "will". "Recommends considering" is not "recommends". "Solely", "entirely", "unless", "provided that", "subject to" and "where practicable" govern everything around them. Wrong answers are constructed almost entirely from swaps of these terms, so a statement that reads like a faithful paraphrase may still be False.

For a senior user researcher there is an additional payoff. The discipline this section demands — separating what a text states from what it implies — is identical to the discipline you enforce when critiquing colleagues' findings. Every time you catch a report writing "users want X" where the evidence supports "three participants said Y", you are exercising precisely this muscle.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The verbal skills tested map onto your named skills:

- **Reading precisely** maps to **Research management, leadership and assurance**, where you evaluate research against accepted professional standards and explain what good practice involves.
- **Separating statement from inference** maps to **Analysis and synthesis**, where you critique colleagues' findings to assure best practice.
- **Identifying the main point** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**, where you tailor communication and develop stakeholder communications strategies.
- **Handling qualified language** maps to **User-centred practice and advocacy**, where advocacy that overstates its evidence loses the credibility that makes it work with sceptics.
- **Reading for absence** maps to **Inclusive research**, where noticing that a document never mentions access needs is a reading skill with an ethical consequence.
- **Reading governance prose accurately** maps to **Research management, leadership and assurance**, where you set and apply standards including ethics.

### Practice questions

*Passage A — for Questions 1 to 4*

"Research programmes must be registered with the profession's central register before fieldwork begins, unless the programme consists entirely of studies already registered individually. Registration requires a named senior user researcher accountable for the programme's ethical conduct. Where a programme involves participants who may be considered vulnerable, the accountable researcher must additionally confirm that a safeguarding protocol is in place; the protocol itself is not submitted. Programmes may be amended after registration provided amendments are recorded within five working days."

**Question 1 (easy)** — Statement: "Every research programme must be registered before fieldwork begins." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The passage attaches an exception: programmes consisting entirely of individually registered studies are exempt. The statement's "every" contradicts that, so it is False. The pattern — absolute term in the statement, exception in the passage — is the most reliable in the section. Note also the word "entirely" in the exception, which narrows it considerably: a programme where most but not all studies are individually registered does not qualify, and that distinction supplies Question 3 below.

**Question 2 (easy)** — Statement: "The safeguarding protocol must be submitted to the central register." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The passage states directly that the protocol itself is not submitted; what is required is the accountable researcher's confirmation that one is in place. The statement contradicts this, making it False. The item tests whether you read to the end of a sentence that appeared to be finished — the semicolon clause carries the operative fact, and a reader who stopped after "safeguarding protocol is in place" would plausibly answer True. Governance prose routinely places the qualification after the semicolon.

**Question 3 (moderate)** — Statement: "A programme of four studies, three of which are individually registered, must be registered as a programme." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The exemption applies only where a programme consists *entirely* of individually registered studies. Three of four does not meet that condition, so the exemption does not apply and the general requirement stands — the statement follows, making it True. This item rewards holding "entirely" in mind from the first sentence through to a scenario presented several lines later. It also mirrors something real: partial compliance with an exception condition is non-compliance, and teams frequently assume the reverse, reasoning that they are "mostly covered".

**Question 4 (moderate)** — Statement: "An amendment recorded eight working days after it was made complies with the requirements." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** Amendments are permitted provided they are recorded within five working days. Eight exceeds five, so the condition fails and the amendment does not comply — the statement is contradicted, making it False. Note the structure carefully, because it differs from a Cannot Say: here the passage supplies a specific threshold and the scenario gives you a specific figure that breaches it, so the facts are complete. Contrast Question 3 in Passage B below, where the passage is silent rather than conditional. The distinction between a failed condition and an unaddressed matter is what separates False from Cannot Say throughout this section.

*Passage B — for Questions 5 to 8*

"The cross-government review of research practice found considerable variation in how departments assure research quality. Four departments operate formal peer review; the remainder rely on line management oversight. The review notes that departments with formal peer review reported higher confidence in their findings, though it did not assess whether findings were in fact more accurate. The review recommends that the profession consider developing a common assurance standard."

**Question 5 (easy)** — Statement: "Some departments do not operate formal peer review." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The passage states that four departments operate formal peer review and the remainder rely on line management oversight, which establishes directly that some do not. The statement follows and is True. Straightforward — answer it fast and bank the time. The only trap available here would be reading "the remainder" as possibly empty, but the passage's own phrasing rules that out by describing what the remainder does.

**Question 6 (moderate)** — Statement: "Formal peer review produces more accurate findings." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The passage reports that departments with formal peer review had higher *confidence* in their findings, and then states explicitly that the review did not assess whether findings were more accurate. Confidence and accuracy are different constructs, and the passage flags its own silence on the second. So the answer is Cannot Say. This is a doubly-signposted item: the passage distinguishes the constructs and then declares the gap, and a candidate who still answers True has substituted a professional intuition — that peer review probably does improve accuracy — for what the text supports. That intuition may well be correct, and it is not evidence.

**Question 7 (moderate)** — Statement: "The review recommends developing a common assurance standard." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The review recommends that the profession *consider developing* a common assurance standard. That is a recommendation to evaluate an option, not a recommendation to adopt it. The statement collapses the two and asserts a recommendation the review did not make, so it is False rather than Cannot Say — the passage tells you what was recommended, and it was something else. This distinction pervades real governance writing, and misreading it in the workplace produces the familiar spectacle of a team implementing something nobody asked for, or a review author objecting that their recommendation has been overstated.

**Question 8 (harder)** — Statement: "Line management oversight is an inadequate form of assurance." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The passage describes two approaches and reports a difference in confidence between them. It makes no judgement about adequacy, and it explicitly declines to assess accuracy. A reader may reasonably infer that line management oversight is weaker, and the passage neither supports nor rules that out, so the answer is Cannot Say. What makes this item harder than Question 6 is that the passage's overall tone leans mildly towards peer review, and tone is not content. Detecting where you are responding to a text's implied stance rather than its statements is one of the most useful things this section trains.

*Passage C — for Questions 9 to 12*

"Departments should design research so that findings reach decision-makers before decisions are taken. Where this is not possible, the reason must be recorded. Research findings must not be amended to reflect the preferences of stakeholders; presentation may be adapted to the audience provided the substance is unchanged. Researchers who are asked to alter findings should raise the matter with the head of user research, who is accountable for the integrity of research outputs across the department."

**Question 9 (easy)** — Statement: "Findings may be presented differently for different audiences." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The passage states that presentation may be adapted to the audience provided the substance is unchanged. The statement, which claims only that different presentation is permitted, follows and is True. Note that the statement is weaker than the passage's full provision — it omits the substance condition — but a weaker statement that the passage supports is still True. Candidates sometimes mark such statements False for being incomplete, which confuses "does not state everything" with "states something wrong".

**Question 10 (moderate)** — Statement: "A researcher who is asked to alter findings must refuse." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The passage says two related things: findings must not be amended to reflect stakeholder preferences, and researchers asked to alter findings should raise the matter with the head of user research. Neither states what the individual researcher must personally do at the moment of the request. A requirement to escalate is not the same as a requirement to refuse — indeed the escalation route exists partly so that the individual is not left to refuse alone. Since the passage neither imposes nor excludes a duty to refuse, the answer is Cannot Say. This is a genuinely subtle item, and it rewards noticing that "must not be amended" is a rule about outcomes while "should raise the matter" is a rule about conduct.

**Question 11 (harder)** — Statement: "Where findings cannot reach decision-makers before a decision, the department has breached the requirement." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The passage anticipates exactly this situation and provides for it: where reaching decision-makers in time is not possible, the reason must be recorded. So the timing failure is not itself a breach; the breach would consist in failing to record the reason. The statement asserts a breach from the timing alone, contradicting the passage, so it is False. Well-drafted standards commonly work this way — they set an expectation and then define what honesty looks like when it cannot be met — and reading them as absolute produces both unnecessary alarm and, more damagingly, a culture where teams conceal timing failures rather than recording them.

**Question 12 (harder) — Main point**

Which best captures the main point of Passage C?

- A) Research findings must never be changed.
- B) Research should inform decisions before they are made, and its substance must remain independent of stakeholder preference — with adaptation permitted in form, honesty required where timing fails, and accountability for integrity resting with the head of user research.
- C) The head of user research is accountable for research integrity.
- D) Departments should record reasons when research is late.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The main point must span the passage while adding nothing. Option A overstates: the passage permits adapting presentation and prohibits only substantive amendment driven by stakeholder preference. Options C and D are each accurate about one clause and silent on the rest, making them details rather than main points. Option B captures the four elements and, more importantly, the logic connecting them: research is useful only if it arrives in time and is believed, and both timing and integrity therefore need explicit protection. Main-point items reward the option broad enough to cover everything and disciplined enough to invent nothing, and the characteristic wrong answer is a true detail promoted beyond its scope — which three of these four are.

### Preparation tips

- **Set expertise aside deliberately.** Before marking True, point at the words. This is the senior candidate's specific failure mode and it needs a specific counter-habit.
- **Treat Cannot Say as decisive.** If you are supplying a link, that is the diagnostic.
- **Circle every qualifier.** All, every, entirely, solely, some, may, must, should, unless, provided that, subject to, where practicable, considers, predominantly.
- **Separate "recommends considering X" from "recommends X".** Governance prose runs on this distinction.
- **Distinguish a failed condition from declared silence.** The first gives False; the second gives Cannot Say.
- **Watch for qualifications after semicolons and at sentence ends.** They carry the operative fact more often than not.
- **Notice when you are reading tone.** A passage that leans a direction has not stated a conclusion.
- **Practise on real governance documents.** Take a page of your own department's research standard and write three statements — one True, one False, one Cannot Say.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Answering from professional knowledge.** The commonest senior error by a wide margin.
- **Avoiding Cannot Say to appear decisive.** Forming a view is a professional habit that costs marks here.
- **Missing "entirely", "solely" and "only".** These narrow exceptions dramatically.
- **Reading confidence as accuracy, or association as causation.** Passages frequently distinguish these deliberately.
- **Treating a substitution as an exemption.** "Where X, do Y instead" changes the form of an obligation, not its existence.
- **Marking a weaker-but-supported statement False.** Incomplete is not incorrect.
- **Responding to implied stance.** Tone is not content.
- **Rushing the second half of a passage.** Exception clauses cluster there because attention fades.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment presents realistic workplace scenarios and asks what you would do. There is no passage to parse and no arithmetic. You get a dilemma of the kind your role produces, four or five candidate responses, and a question asking which is most effective and often which is least.

This section measures whether your judgement matches what the profession considers good practice at your level — and level is the whole calibration. Your role description places you as an experienced practitioner who leads research in larger teams and on more complex services, builds user-centred practice in new teams, supervises and develops other researchers, engages sceptical colleagues, evaluates work against professional standards, gives constructive feedback, and influences stakeholders to resolve issues. The strongest answers therefore involve exercising independent professional judgement, taking responsibility for other people's practice as well as your own, holding positions under genuine organisational pressure, and developing capability rather than simply solving problems yourself.

That last point separates your level from an independent practitioner's more sharply than anything else. A user researcher who fixes a colleague's flawed analysis has solved a problem. A senior user researcher who fixes it without the colleague learning anything has failed at half the job. Several items below hinge on that distinction.

Four principles carry most scenarios.

Participants and users come first. When an option protects wellbeing, consent, dignity or access, it almost always outranks one protecting a schedule, a budget or somebody's comfort — including your own.

Protect the integrity of the evidence, and protect other people's ability to protect it. You are the person whose claims are supposed to be trustworthy, and increasingly the person other researchers look to when they are pressed to say something they should not. Options that inflate, soften, bury or mislabel findings are close to automatically wrong, and options that leave a junior colleague alone with that pressure are nearly as bad.

Develop capability while solving the problem. The best answers at your level frequently teach, delegate with support, or change a process — not because that is nicer, but because a senior researcher who only produces outputs does not scale.

Escalate what genuinely requires authority you do not hold, and handle everything else. Safeguarding, ethics approvals, data incidents and serious professional misconduct go up. Methodological disagreements, stakeholder pressure, scope conflicts and peer feedback you handle.

One trap: options that are pleasant, and options that are decisive, are both attractive for reasons unconnected to effectiveness.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The judgement dimensions map onto your named skills:

- **Ethical judgement and participant care** map to **Inclusive research** and the ethical core of **User research methods**.
- **Assurance and constructive feedback** map to **Research management, leadership and assurance**, where you evaluate quality, explain good practice and give feedback to other researchers.
- **Engaging sceptics** maps to **Analysis and synthesis**, where you effectively engage sceptical colleagues, and to **User-centred practice and advocacy**, where you advocate with sceptical stakeholders.
- **Influence and negotiation** map to **Stakeholder relationship management**, where you identify and resolve issues and build long-term strategic relationships.
- **Building practice in new teams** maps to **User-centred practice and advocacy**, where you help inexperienced teams adopt user-centred practices.
- **Strategic alignment** maps to **Agile research practices**, where you understand the strategic decisions the team needs to make and influence priorities.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — A junior researcher is under pressure**

A user researcher you supervise tells you a product manager has asked them to remove a finding about accessibility failures from a summary deck, saying it will "distract from the main story". They are unsure what to do. What is the most effective response?

- A) Tell them to leave the finding in and to say you said so.
- B) Talk through why the finding matters and what is at stake, agree with them how it should be presented — including framing it constructively with a proposed next step — and offer to be in the meeting; then, separately, speak to the product manager yourself.
- C) Contact the product manager and instruct them not to ask researchers to alter findings.
- D) Escalate to the head of user research.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Two things need to happen: the finding must survive, and the researcher must come out of this more capable of handling the next one. Option B does both. Talking it through builds their judgement rather than substituting yours; agreeing the presentation together gives them something constructive to bring rather than a refusal; offering to attend provides backing without taking over; and speaking to the product manager separately addresses the behaviour at the right level, which is yours, not theirs. Option A is least effective because "say your senior said so" leaves them holding a position they cannot defend and teaches them nothing, while also framing research integrity as a matter of hierarchy rather than principle. Option C addresses the product manager without preparing the researcher, and an instruction from you may make their working relationship harder. Option D escalates something you are equipped to handle, though you would reasonably mention it if it recurs.

**Question 2 (easy) — A new team resists research**

You have joined a programme with three teams, none of which has worked with a researcher. The programme director is supportive; the delivery managers are polite and unenthusiastic. What is the strongest first move?

- A) Present the service standard's research requirements to all three teams.
- B) Ask the director to mandate research participation.
- C) Find the team with the nearest consequential decision, offer to answer their specific question inside their sprint, and get the whole team observing sessions — then let that team's experience do the persuading with the other two.
- D) Run a discovery across all three services simultaneously.

**Correct answer: C. Least effective: B**

**Explanation:** Scepticism about research rarely yields to argument or to mandate; it yields to the experience of watching a real user fail at something you built. Option C is calibrated for that: it starts where the appetite is highest, answers a question the team actually has, fits their existing cadence, and gets them into sessions as observers rather than recipients of a report. The second-order effect matters most — a delivery manager telling another delivery manager that research saved them a fortnight is worth more than anything you can say. Option B is least effective because mandated research is performed rather than used, and it makes you the director's enforcement mechanism, which poisons the relationships you need. Option A wins compliance and loses goodwill. Option D is right in ambition and wrong in sequence; a simultaneous three-service discovery confirms every fear about research slowing things down before you have any credit.

**Question 3 (easy) — Findings arrive after the decision, repeatedly**

Across a programme, research consistently arrives after decisions are taken. Teams are appreciative but nothing changes as a result. What is the most effective response?

- A) Work faster.
- B) Gather specific examples across the programme showing what each late finding would have changed, take them to the programme's governance forum with a proposed change to how decisions are sequenced, and secure the director's support for the change.
- C) Raise it in each team's retrospective.
- D) Reduce the depth of research so it can be delivered sooner.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** This is a systemic problem at programme level, so it needs a programme-level forum, evidence and a proposal. Option B assembles all three and adds the element that distinguishes senior from independent practice: securing sponsorship from someone with the authority to change how decisions are sequenced. Specific examples are essential, because "research arrives too late" is a complaint while "these four decisions would have gone differently" is a case. Option A treats a sequencing problem as a speed problem and will exhaust you without fixing anything. Option C is a reasonable component and insufficient alone, since three separate retrospectives will produce three local accommodations rather than a structural change. Option D trades the value of the research for its timeliness, which delivers findings on time that are not worth having.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Assuring work that is not good enough**

A researcher in another team, who does not report to you, has produced a report whose central claim is not supported by the evidence. It has been circulated to stakeholders. What is the most effective response?

- A) Correct the record with the stakeholders directly.
- B) Speak to the researcher first, privately and specifically, and agree how the claim should be corrected and communicated — supporting them to make the correction themselves; if they decline, then escalate.
- C) Say nothing; it is not your team.
- D) Raise it in the research community of practice as a general point about evidence standards.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** The claim needs correcting and the researcher needs to remain a functioning professional afterwards, and option B is the only option that achieves both. Going to them first is not merely courteous; they may have context you lack, and if they do not, the correction lands far better coming from them. The conditional escalation matters too — B is not naive, it has a next step if the researcher refuses. Option A corrects the record and destroys a working relationship you will need, and it also bypasses the possibility that you have misread something. Option C is least effective: stakeholders are currently acting on an unsupported claim, and departmental boundaries are not a reason to let that stand. Option D is passive-aggressive in effect if not intent — a general point about standards, made publicly, immediately after a specific failure, is understood by everyone as being about that failure and gives the person no way to respond.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Being asked to predetermine a conclusion**

A director tells you, before a programme of research begins, that it "needs to support the case for the new digital-only channel, because that decision is effectively made". What is the most effective response?

- A) Design the research to explore the benefits of the channel.
- B) State plainly that the research will report what users experience, and reframe the conversation around what the director actually needs — if the decision is made, the useful questions become who will struggle, what support they will need, and what would signal the change is failing.
- C) Agree, then report honestly and deal with the fallout later.
- D) Refuse and report the conversation to the head of user research.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Option B declines the premise without a confrontation and then does something genuinely useful with the situation. If the channel decision really is made, research aimed at relitigating it wastes everyone's time — but research identifying who will be excluded, what assisted digital provision they need, and what early indicators would show the change failing is both honest and directly valuable to the director. That reframe converts an integrity problem into a scoping conversation, which is a far better outcome than winning an argument. Option A is least effective: designing to find benefits is a rigged study, and rigged studies surface eventually, usually at the worst moment. Option C leaves the director's expectation uncorrected, guaranteeing a worse conflict when findings land. Option D may become necessary if the pressure persists, and telling your head of profession for support is sensible, but the first move is a professional conversation you are entirely equipped to have.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Developing someone whose practice is weak**

A researcher you supervise consistently writes findings that overreach — small samples described as "users", preferences reported as needs. You have mentioned it twice and it continues. What is the most effective next step?

- A) Rewrite their findings before they circulate.
- B) Move from general feedback to something structured: work through one of their reports together, line by line, agreeing what each claim can support; then agree they will apply that test themselves before their next report, and review it together.
- C) Raise it in their performance review.
- D) Reduce their responsibility for reporting.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Twice-repeated general feedback has not worked, which usually means the person agrees in principle and cannot yet see the problem in their own writing. Option B addresses that directly: working line by line makes the abstract concrete, agreeing a test gives them something portable, and reviewing the next one closes the loop. This is deliberate capability building rather than repeated correction. Option A is least effective because it removes the problem from view while guaranteeing it persists, and it quietly signals that you do not think they can learn. Option C is not wrong as a component — persistent unaddressed issues do belong in a performance conversation — but reaching for it before trying a structured intervention is premature and will read as an escalation rather than support. Option D protects the outputs at the cost of the person's development and their standing in the team.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Sceptical colleague, repeated publicly**

A lead engineer repeatedly characterises research findings as "anecdotes" in front of the team, and it is affecting how findings are received. What is the most effective response?

- A) Explain the methodological basis of qualitative research at the next team meeting.
- B) Invite them to observe two sessions and discuss afterwards what they saw; separately, find a question where analytics located a problem and sessions explained it, and present that jointly with whoever owns the analytics — so complementarity is demonstrated rather than argued.
- C) Ask the delivery manager to address the behaviour.
- D) Present findings with quantitative framing to satisfy them.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Methodological explanations almost never shift this position, because the objection is not really methodological — watching a dashboard feels like evidence and hearing about a stranger's frustration does not. Observation changes that more reliably than any argument, because watching someone fail at something you built is hard to dismiss as an anecdote. The second half of option B is what makes it senior rather than merely sensible: presenting jointly with the analytics owner recruits the sceptic's preferred evidence to your side and makes the point structurally rather than rhetorically. Option A rarely works alone. Option C converts a professional disagreement into a management matter and will entrench the position. Option D is least effective because dressing qualitative findings in quantitative clothing concedes the false premise and manufactures precisely the overreach that would justify the scepticism.

**Question 8 (harder) — An ethical problem in another team's research**

You learn that a team is recruiting for research on a domestic abuse support service by approaching users through the service's own contact list, with sessions conducted by video and no protocol for what to do if a participant is not alone. What should you do?

- A) Raise it with the researcher and offer to help them design a safer approach.
- B) Raise it immediately with both the researcher and the head of user research, set out the specific risks — that contact via the service's list may reach people whose devices are monitored, and that a video session with no aloneness protocol could expose a participant to danger — and press for fieldwork to pause until safeguards are in place.
- C) Report it to the data protection officer.
- D) Raise it at the next community of practice session.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** The potential harm here is severe and immediate: contacting someone through a domestic abuse service's list can alert a perpetrator who monitors their device, and a video call where the researcher does not know whether the participant is alone can put a person in physical danger during the session. That severity is what makes this different from the peer-feedback scenarios above, and it justifies going to the head of user research simultaneously rather than sequentially, and pressing for a pause rather than offering to help improve things over time. Option A is the right instinct at the wrong severity — it treats a safety risk as a design problem and leaves fieldwork running while you help. Option C addresses a real but secondary concern. Option D is least effective by a wide margin: deferring a live physical-safety risk to a scheduled meeting is indefensible, and the community of practice is the right forum for the lesson afterwards, not for the intervention now.

**Question 9 (harder) — Evidence that contradicts a commitment already made**

Your research shows that a service change announced publicly by a minister will substantially disadvantage users with limited digital access. Colleagues suggest the findings be framed as "areas for future improvement". What is the most effective response?

- A) Agree; the commitment is public and cannot be reversed.
- B) Report the findings accurately, and separately do the constructive work: quantify who is affected, set out what mitigation would look like, and present the mitigation alongside the finding so the organisation has a route that is neither reversal nor denial.
- C) Report the findings starkly and let the organisation deal with it.
- D) Escalate to the head of user research before doing anything.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** The instinct behind "areas for future improvement" is to protect the organisation from an uncomfortable position, and it produces the worst outcome: the disadvantage still happens, nobody is accountable, and the evidence that could have driven mitigation has been neutered. Option B keeps the finding intact and makes it actionable, which is the only combination that actually helps the affected users. Quantifying who is affected converts an abstract concern into something a delivery plan can address, and arriving with mitigation means the finding is not simply a problem dropped on people who cannot reverse the commitment. Option A is least effective — softening findings to accommodate a political commitment is exactly the failure your professional standards exist to prevent, and it disadvantages the users least able to absorb it. Option C is accurate and unhelpful. Option D is worth doing in parallel, since your head of profession should know, but it is not a substitute for doing the work.

**Question 10 (harder) — Competing demands from senior stakeholders**

Two directors want your team's research capacity in the same quarter. One has a service in live operation failing users now; the other has a high-profile new programme with ministerial visibility. Both have escalated to your head of profession. What is the most effective response?

- A) Split capacity between them.
- B) Set out, for each, what research would deliver, what decision it would inform, and what the consequence of deferral is — then recommend a sequence with reasoning, and take that recommendation to both directors and your head of profession together rather than negotiating separately.
- C) Prioritise the ministerial programme.
- D) Ask your head of profession to decide.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** The situation is a prioritisation problem being fought as a political one, and the way out is to make the trade-off explicit and visible to everyone at once. Option B does the analytical work — value of information, decision informed, cost of deferral — and then handles the politics correctly by putting the recommendation to both directors together, which prevents the parallel-negotiation dynamic where each is told something slightly different and trust collapses. Making a recommendation rather than presenting neutral options is also important; you are the expert on what research can deliver, and abdicating that is a waste of the role. Option A is least effective: splitting constrained capacity between two substantial needs typically produces two studies too thin to inform either decision, which is the characteristic failure of compromise. Option C ranks by visibility. Option D discards your own judgement, though you should certainly involve your head of profession in the conversation.

**Question 11 (harder) — A flaw in research you signed off**

Three months after you assured a study, you realise its screener excluded anyone who had contacted the support desk — so the sample systematically omitted users who had already struggled. The findings shaped a live service change. What should you do?

- A) Note it as a lesson for future assurance.
- B) Tell the team and the decision-makers immediately, scope precisely which conclusions are compromised and which stand, propose targeted follow-up research with the excluded group, and review why the assurance process did not catch it.
- C) Commission a follow-up quietly and correct the record if findings differ.
- D) Raise it with your head of profession and let them decide how to handle it.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Four things need doing and option B does all of them. Telling people now matters because a live service change is running on the findings. Scoping precisely matters because some conclusions — about how users who did not need help navigate the service — may remain sound, and overstating the damage is as unhelpful as understating it. Targeted follow-up addresses the gap. And reviewing the assurance process is the part specific to your level: you signed this off, so the interesting question is not only what the study missed but why your check did not catch it, and what to add to the check. Option A defers everything into abstraction. Option C conceals a known flaw while a service change proceeds. Option D is worth doing alongside but not instead; owning the error yourself is both faster and more credible than routing it through someone else.

**Question 12 (harder) — Building assurance where there is none**

You move to a department with no research assurance process. Reports vary widely in quality, some claims are unsupported, and researchers are working in isolation across six teams. Your head of profession asks you to fix it. What is the strongest approach?

- A) Write an assurance standard and require all reports to comply.
- B) Start by making the current variation visible — review a sample of recent reports with the researchers themselves, identify the recurring issues, and co-design a lightweight standard and peer review routine with them; then pilot it with willing teams before extending it.
- C) Introduce mandatory peer review immediately.
- D) Provide training on research quality.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** An imposed standard in a department with no assurance culture produces compliance theatre: reports formatted to satisfy a checklist while the underlying claims stay unsupported. Option B is slower and works, for reasons worth naming. Reviewing reports with the researchers surfaces the problems in a way they can see rather than being told about. Co-design means the standard reflects real constraints and has authors who will defend it. Piloting with willing teams produces evidence and advocates before you extend it to the reluctant ones. Option A is least effective because it is the fastest route to a document nobody uses. Option C introduces a mechanism without the shared understanding of quality that makes peer review anything other than a delay. Option D is a useful component and insufficient alone, since training without a routine that requires the behaviour tends not to change practice. Setting and assuring standards is a named part of your role, and how you introduce them determines whether they are real.

### Preparation tips

- **Answer as a senior practitioner.** Independent judgement, responsibility for others' practice, and capability building alongside problem solving.
- **Check whether the option develops anyone.** At your level, solving it yourself is frequently the second-best answer.
- **Protect the evidence and the people protecting it.** Options that leave a junior colleague alone under pressure are weak.
- **Calibrate escalation to severity.** Peer feedback goes to the person first; a physical-safety risk goes up immediately and in parallel.
- **Redirect rather than refuse.** "Not that, but here is the question I can answer" is the highest-scoring move in stakeholder scenarios.
- **Make trade-offs visible rather than negotiating privately.** Parallel negotiation with competing stakeholders destroys trust.
- **Bring mitigation with bad news.** A finding plus a route forward is actionable; a finding alone invites denial.
- **Discuss real cases with peers at your level.** Your organisation's actual norms are what this section measures.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Solving without developing.** The characteristic senior failure — outputs improve, capability does not.
- **Softening findings to accommodate a commitment.** It disadvantages exactly the users least able to absorb it.
- **Mandating practice before demonstrating value.** Compliance theatre is the reliable result.
- **Correcting a peer's work by going straight to stakeholders.** Speak to the person first, with a next step if they decline.
- **Deferring urgent safety concerns to scheduled forums.** Severity sets the channel.
- **Splitting constrained capacity to avoid a decision.** Two thin studies inform nothing.
- **Ranking by ministerial visibility.** Value of information, not profile.
- **Abdicating your recommendation.** You are the expert on what research can deliver; presenting neutral options wastes that.

## Conclusion

You have reached the end, and it is worth registering what that involved: forty-eight questions across four assessment types, with the reasoning worked through on each. That is a serious piece of professional preparation, and rather more than most senior candidates undertake.

Look back at the ground. In the cognitive section you scoped a methodological flaw precisely enough to say which claims survived it, spotted compounding bias where three factors pushed the same way, prioritised by value of information rather than importance, diagnosed a survey whose sampling frame made it structurally incapable of answering the question it was being used for, and worked out how to build research practice in a team that did not want it. In the numeric section you weighted rates rather than averaging them, reconstructed an implied unit value from a business case, distinguished a metric that was correctly calculated from one that was correctly interpreted, and declined to endorse a comparison that could not detect the difference it was designed to find. In the verbal section you held "entirely" in mind across five lines, separated confidence from accuracy, caught "recommends considering" pretending to be "recommends", and noticed where you were responding to a passage's tone rather than its content. And in the situational judgement section you supported a junior researcher under pressure without taking over, intervened on a safety risk in another team's fieldwork, kept a finding intact against a public ministerial commitment, and worked out how to introduce assurance without producing compliance theatre.

There is a thread running through all of it. Nearly every item, in its own idiom, was asking whether you know the limits of your evidence and will say so — and, distinctively at your level, whether you can help other people do the same. That is not an artefact of how the guide was written. It is what the role is for. Teams can generate opinions without you and read dashboards without you. What you supply is a claim that can be trusted at the strength stated, and a practice around you that produces more of them.

A word about the particular difficulty of your level. The errors that matter most to you now are not the ones you would notice. They are the ones you are confident about, made fluently, in areas where your experience is deep enough that checking feels unnecessary. Verbal reasoning is the clearest illustration — expertise actively costs marks there — but the pattern generalises. The senior researchers whose judgement holds up over a career are, almost without exception, the ones who kept a habit of deliberate scepticism about their own reasoning long after it stopped feeling necessary.

If you want to build on this, a few things repay effort more than rereading. Audit a study you signed off and ask who could not have appeared in its sample. Take a business case from your department and trace three of its figures back to source; the results are often instructive and occasionally alarming. Ask a peer to critique your last report as harshly as they would a stranger's, and return the favour properly. Sit with an analyst and ask how the metrics you rely on are defined — not calculated, defined. And when you next give a researcher feedback that has not landed twice already, try working through one output line by line instead of saying it a third time.

Finally, for the day itself. An assessment samples your reasoning on one morning under artificial conditions. It is not a verdict on your judgement or your career. Read the stem twice, set your expertise aside in the verbal section, find the denominator, trust Cannot Say, and remember that at this level the right answer is usually the one that is honest about uncertainty rather than the one that sounds most authoritative.

Good luck. The profession is stronger for practitioners who prepare like this — and so are the people who use these services.
