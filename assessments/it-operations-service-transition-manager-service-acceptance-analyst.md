## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the exact mental processes your role demands, using practical workplace scenarios rather than abstract puzzles. Instead of asking you to rotate shapes or complete number squares, it presents you with the everyday materials of your job — change requests, acceptance criteria checklists, configuration records, incident tickets, and evidence packs — and asks you to reason quickly and accurately about them.

The typical format is an online, timed test lasting 15 to 30 minutes, with somewhere between 15 and 30 questions. The questions cover pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective: your answers are compared against a norm group of people at a similar level. Many modern platforms are adaptive, which means the questions get a little harder when you answer correctly and a little easier when you do not — so do not panic if the test feels like it is getting more difficult. That usually means you are doing well. You will normally be offered a few short, ungraded practice questions before the real test starts, so you can get comfortable with the screens without any pressure.

Employers use cognitive assessments for service acceptance roles because the core of your job is careful checking. When you validate a change against its acceptance criteria, you are doing error checking. When you decide which of four waiting tasks to do first, you are prioritising. When you work out what a change freeze rule means for a specific request, you are using logical deduction. A well-designed cognitive test simulates exactly those demands in miniature, which is why practising with role-specific material is so effective.

One reassuring truth: cognitive test performance improves with practice. The techniques in this section — read the rule before the scenario, check each option against the facts, deduce only what must be true — are learnable habits, not fixed talents.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The assessment dimensions map directly to the named skills in your role summary:

- **Pattern recognition** maps to your **Asset and configuration management** skill: tracking, logging and correcting information to protect assets means noticing when a record breaks the expected pattern — a naming convention, a version sequence, a status code that does not fit.
- **Logical deduction** maps to your **Change management** skill: implementing change requests and applying change control procedures under supervision means following conditional rules correctly — "if the change is standard, then...; if it is emergency, then..." — and drawing only the conclusions the rules allow.
- **Error checking** maps directly to your core duty of validating changes against acceptance criteria and gathering evidence: your value to the team is spotting the small mismatch between what was promised and what was delivered.
- **Prioritisation** maps to your **Incident management** skill and your **Ownership and topic** skill: identifying and registering incidents, allocating them to the appropriate channel, and knowing when to pass problems on to your team all require you to rank what matters most, quickly.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to your **Problem management** and **Service focus** skills: investigating problems in systems, processes and services, and taking inputs and establishing coherent frameworks that work, both depend on breaking a messy situation into steps you can reason about.
- **Working with structured information** also supports your **Availability and capacity management** and **Continuity management** skills, because explaining those processes to others starts with understanding their logic yourself.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern recognition in change request IDs**

Your team's change requests follow a naming pattern: the letters "CHG", a hyphen, the two-digit year, a hyphen, then a four-digit sequence number. For example: CHG-25-0142. While tidying the change log, you see these five entries:

1. CHG-25-0871
2. CHG-25-0872
3. CHG-0873-25
4. CHG-25-0874
5. CHG-25-0875

Which entry breaks the naming pattern?

- A) Entry 1
- B) Entry 2
- C) Entry 3
- D) Entry 5

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The pattern is CHG, then the year, then the sequence number. Entry 3 swaps the order: the four-digit number comes before the year. Everything else follows the rule. The reliable technique here is to say the pattern to yourself in words first ("letters, year, number") and then check each entry against your words, one element at a time. This is exactly the kind of consistency check you do when you track, log and correct information in configuration records — a small naming error looks trivial, but it can stop a search from finding the record later, which matters when someone is investigating an incident under time pressure.

**Question 2 (easy) — Logical deduction from an acceptance rule**

Your acceptance criteria state: "A change may be marked as accepted only if the test evidence pack is complete AND the rollback plan has been approved." A colleague tells you: "Change CHG-25-0901 has a complete test evidence pack." Based only on this information, which conclusion is correct?

- A) CHG-25-0901 may be marked as accepted.
- B) CHG-25-0901 may not be marked as accepted.
- C) You cannot yet tell — you still need to check whether the rollback plan has been approved.
- D) The rollback plan has probably been approved, because the evidence pack is complete.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The rule needs two things to be true: a complete evidence pack AND an approved rollback plan. You have confirmed only one of them. So you cannot conclude the change may be accepted (option A), and you also cannot conclude it may not be (option B) — the rollback plan might well be approved; you just do not know yet. Option D guesses, and assessments never reward guessing about facts you can check. The safe conclusion is C. This mirrors your daily work precisely: when you validate a change against acceptance criteria, you check every criterion, and you never let one green tick persuade you the others must be green too.

**Question 3 (easy) — Error checking a configuration record**

An asset record should show: asset tag, location, status (In use / In storage / Retired), and last verified date. You are checking four laptop records against a physical audit done this morning:

| Asset tag | Location | Status | Last verified |
|---|---|---|---|
| LT-4471 | Leeds office | In use | Today |
| LT-4472 | Leeds office | Retired | Today |
| LT-4473 | Storage room B | In storage | Today |
| LT-4474 | Leeds office | In use | Today |

The audit found LT-4472 on a desk in the Leeds office, switched on and being used by a caseworker. Which correction should you log?

- A) Change LT-4472's location to Storage room B
- B) Change LT-4472's status from Retired to In use
- C) Change LT-4472's last verified date to yesterday
- D) No correction is needed

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The audit shows the laptop is being used, but the record says Retired. A retired asset should not be in active use, so the status field is wrong and should be corrected to In use (and in real life you might also flag why a supposedly retired device is still in service — that could be a security question worth raising). Option A would make the record match nothing: the laptop is genuinely in the Leeds office. Option C would falsify the verification date, which is the opposite of protecting information. This question rehearses your **Asset and configuration management** skill exactly as your role summary describes it: track, log and correct information to protect assets and components.

**Question 4 (easy) — Allocating an incident to the right channel**

Your service desk routes incidents by category: hardware faults go to the End User Computing queue, application errors go to the Applications queue, and anything affecting many users at once is flagged as a major incident candidate and escalated immediately. A caller reports that the benefits calculation screen shows an error message for her, and — as far as she knows — for everyone in her 40-person office. What should you do first?

- A) Log it in the End User Computing queue, because she called about her computer
- B) Log it in the Applications queue and move on to the next call
- C) Register the incident with the details gathered, and flag it as a major incident candidate for immediate escalation because many users are affected
- D) Ask her to restart her computer and call back if the problem continues

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Work through the routing rules in order. Is it a hardware fault? No — the application shows an error. Is it an application error? Yes — but the special rule about "many users at once" takes priority, and 40 people in one office qualifies. So the correct first action is to register the incident properly (gathering the required information, as your **Incident management** skill describes) and escalate it as a potential major incident. Option B follows the ordinary rule but misses the priority rule. Options A and D delay a response to what could be a significant outage. The technique to remember: when rules overlap, check whether one of them is explicitly marked as taking priority — in incident management, "many users affected" almost always is.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Deduction from a change freeze rule**

Your department's change policy says: "During the change freeze, only emergency changes may be implemented. An emergency change requires approval from the on-call service owner." It is currently the change freeze period. Change CHG-25-0917 was implemented yesterday. Which one of the following must be true?

- A) CHG-25-0917 was an emergency change approved by the on-call service owner.
- B) CHG-25-0917 broke the change policy.
- C) Either CHG-25-0917 was an approved emergency change, or the change policy was broken.
- D) The on-call service owner was available yesterday.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The policy allows exactly one lawful route during a freeze: an emergency change with the right approval. The change was implemented, so either it went through that lawful route, or it did not — in which case the policy was broken. You cannot tell which from the facts given, so neither A nor B must be true on its own; each is only possible. D adds information the facts do not support. C is the only conclusion guaranteed in every case. This "either the rule was followed or it was broken — check before accusing" discipline matters in your role: if you spotted CHG-25-0917 in the log, your next step would be to look for the emergency approval record, not to assume wrongdoing. That protects both the audit trail and your working relationships.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Prioritising your morning**

It is 9:00 on Tuesday. Four items are waiting for you:

1. A change is scheduled for go/no-go review at 11:00 today, and your validation evidence summary is the missing input.
2. A colleague has asked you to double-check a configuration record "sometime this week".
3. An incident you registered yesterday has been sitting unallocated in the wrong queue — users are waiting.
4. Your team lead has asked for your availability for a training course next month.

Which order is most sensible?

- A) 3, 1, 2, 4
- B) 1, 2, 3, 4
- C) 4, 1, 3, 2
- D) 1, 3, 4, 2

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Compare urgency and impact together. Item 3 involves users actively waiting while an incident sits in the wrong queue — fixing an allocation takes minutes and unblocks other people immediately, so do it first. Item 1 is next: the 11:00 deadline is hard, and your evidence summary blocks a go/no-go decision, so it needs the rest of your morning. Item 2 says "this week" and item 4 concerns next month; the colleague's check (2) has a nearer deadline than the training reply (4), but both comfortably follow the urgent pair — and A places them in that order. Option B treats your own deadline as more urgent than users already being affected. Options C and D promote low-urgency items too early. The pattern to remember: quick actions that unblock other people usually come first, then your own hard deadlines, then everything else by date.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Matching evidence to acceptance criteria**

A change has four acceptance criteria: (i) all regression tests passed; (ii) release notes published; (iii) service desk briefed; (iv) rollback plan tested. The project team sends you an evidence pack containing: a regression test report showing 212 of 212 tests passed; a link to the published release notes; an email confirming the rollback plan was written and peer-reviewed. Which criteria are NOT yet evidenced?

- A) (i) and (ii)
- B) (iii) only
- C) (iv) only
- D) (iii) and (iv)

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Match each piece of evidence to a criterion, then look for gaps. The test report evidences (i). The release notes link evidences (ii). Nothing in the pack mentions briefing the service desk, so (iii) is unevidenced. The rollback email is the trap: the plan was written and peer-reviewed, but criterion (iv) requires it to be tested — reviewing a document is not the same as testing the rollback. So (iii) and (iv) are both outstanding. This is the heart of your role: validating changes against acceptance criteria means checking that the evidence proves the specific thing the criterion asks for, not something nearby that sounds similar. "Close enough" evidence is one of the most common gaps you will catch as a service acceptance analyst.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Sequence deduction in a release plan**

A release runbook has five steps with these rules: (i) the database backup must finish before the code deployment starts; (ii) the code deployment must finish before smoke testing starts; (iii) the service desk notification must be sent before the code deployment starts; (iv) the acceptance sign-off happens last. If the database backup finishes at 19:30, the code deployment takes 60 minutes, and smoke testing takes 30 minutes, what is the earliest time acceptance sign-off can begin, assuming the service desk notification was sent in the afternoon?

- A) 20:30
- B) 21:00
- C) 21:30
- D) 19:30

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Work forwards step by step. The backup finishes at 19:30 and the notification is already done, so the deployment can start at 19:30. It takes 60 minutes: 19:30 + 1:00 = 20:30. Smoke testing can then start at 20:30 and takes 30 minutes: 20:30 + 0:30 = 21:00. Sign-off happens last, so the earliest it can begin is 21:00. Option A forgets the smoke testing step; option C adds an extra half hour that no rule requires; option D ignores every dependency. Writing the timeline down — even as a scribbled line of times — beats holding it in your head. You will use this exact skill when you help validate that a change was implemented in the approved sequence, which is part of applying change control procedures under supervision.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Spotting the inconsistency in test evidence**

An evidence pack states: "All 48 test cases were executed on 14 May. 45 passed, 2 failed and were fixed, and the remaining case was descoped with approval. The fixed cases were retested and passed on 13 May." What is wrong with this evidence?

- A) 45 + 2 + 1 does not add up to 48
- B) The retest date (13 May) is before the original test date (14 May), which is impossible as described
- C) Descoping a test case is never allowed
- D) Nothing is wrong

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Check the arithmetic first: 45 passed + 2 failed + 1 descoped = 48, so option A is wrong — the numbers reconcile. Check the rules: the descoped case had approval, so option C overstates — descoping with approval is a normal, if carefully controlled, practice. Now check the timeline: the two failures were found on 14 May, but the evidence claims they were retested and passed on 13 May — the day before they failed. That sequence is impossible as written. It is probably a typo, but your job is to notice it and ask, because an audit would. The technique: read evidence packs across three dimensions — do the numbers add up, do the rules allow it, and does the timeline make sense? Any one of the three can hide the inconsistency.

**Question 10 (hard) — Multi-rule deduction about change categories**

Your change procedure defines three categories: Standard (pre-approved, low risk, follows a documented template), Normal (needs Change Advisory Board approval), and Emergency (needs on-call service owner approval, used only when delay would cause harm). Rules: (i) any change touching the payments system is never Standard; (ii) any change during business hours affecting more than 100 users must be Normal or Emergency; (iii) Emergency is only valid if delay would cause harm. A team proposes a lunchtime (business hours) update to the payments system affecting 250 users; delaying it a week would cause no harm. Which category must it be?

- A) Standard
- B) Normal
- C) Emergency
- D) It cannot proceed under any category

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Apply each rule as a filter. Rule (i): it touches payments, so Standard is eliminated. Rule (ii): business hours and 250 users means it must be Normal or Emergency — consistent with what remains. Rule (iii): Emergency requires that delay would cause harm, and the scenario says delay causes no harm, so Emergency is eliminated. Only Normal survives, and nothing forbids Normal, so option D is wrong — the change can proceed; it simply needs Change Advisory Board approval. The elimination technique — cross out what the rules forbid and see what remains — is one of the most reliable tools in any cognitive test, and it is also exactly how you will reason when a project team asks you "can we just do this as a standard change?" and you need to explain, kindly but clearly, why the answer is no.

**Question 11 (hard) — Applied problem solving across a service framework**

Your team receives repeated incidents every Monday morning: users cannot log in to the case management system between 08:00 and 08:20. You gather these facts: (a) a weekly maintenance job runs Sundays at 23:00 and normally finishes by midnight; (b) the maintenance log shows the job has recently been finishing at 08:15 on Mondays; (c) the system rejects logins while the job runs; (d) data volumes have grown 40% in six months. Which explanation best fits all the facts, and what is the most useful next step for you at your level?

- A) Users are typing their passwords wrong on Mondays; send a password reminder email.
- B) The growing data volume has made the maintenance job overrun into Monday mornings, blocking logins; report this pattern to your team with the evidence so the job can be rescheduled or resized.
- C) The login system is broken; raise an emergency change to replace it.
- D) The 08:00–08:20 window is a coincidence; keep monitoring.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Test each explanation against all four facts. Option A explains none of them — password errors would not cluster in a 20-minute window that matches a maintenance overrun. Option C ignores facts (a), (b) and (d), and jumps to an extreme remedy. Option D calls it coincidence when facts (b) and (c) directly connect the job's new finish time to the login failures. Option B links everything: growing data (d) makes the job run longer (b), and the job blocks logins while it runs (c), producing Monday incidents. Notice the second half of B: at your level, the right move is to investigate, gather the evidence, and contribute your findings to the team — your **Problem management** skill says you investigate problems and contribute to remedies, and your **Ownership and topic** skill says you pass problems on to your team. Handing over a well-evidenced pattern is excellent work, not incomplete work.

**Question 12 (hard) — Building a coherent framework from messy inputs**

Three project teams each send you their "definition of done" for changes entering your area. Team X requires: tests passed, release notes, service desk briefing. Team Y requires: tests passed, rollback tested, security check. Team Z requires: release notes, rollback tested, tests passed. You are asked to draft a single acceptance checklist that (i) includes every item at least one team requires, (ii) lists shared items only once, and (iii) orders items so that anything needed before go-live sign-off (tests, security, rollback) comes before communication items (release notes, briefing). How many items will your checklist contain, and which item comes last under the ordering rule if communication items keep the order "release notes, then briefing"?

- A) 5 items; service desk briefing last
- B) 6 items; release notes last
- C) 5 items; rollback tested last
- D) 9 items; service desk briefing last

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** First, merge the lists and remove duplicates. The distinct items are: tests passed, release notes, service desk briefing, rollback tested, security check — five items (option D forgot to de-duplicate: 3 + 3 + 3 = 9 raw entries, but only 5 distinct). Next, apply the ordering rule: the assurance items (tests passed, security check, rollback tested) come first in some order, then the communication items — release notes, then service desk briefing. So the final item is the service desk briefing, giving option A. This is a miniature of your **Service focus** skill — "take inputs and establish coherent frameworks that work". Real acceptance checklists are almost always merged from several teams' habits, and the analyst who can de-duplicate, order, and produce one clean list is the analyst everyone wants on their transition.

### Preparation tips

- **Practise with your own artefacts.** Ten minutes spent checking a real change record against its acceptance criteria is cognitive training. Your daily work is the best rehearsal there is, so do it consciously: name the technique you are using (pattern check, rule check, timeline check) as you go.
- **Say patterns and rules in words before applying them.** "Letters, year, number." "Both conditions must be true." Verbalising the rule stops your eyes skimming past the exception.
- **Write things down during the test.** Timelines, elimination grids, quick sums. Most platforms allow rough notes, and your working memory will thank you.
- **Do the untimed practice questions first, then add time pressure gradually.** Accuracy first, speed second. Speed grows naturally from familiar technique.
- **Sleep and settle.** Cognitive scores genuinely drop when you are tired or flustered. Test on a day you can be rested, in a quiet spot, with water to hand.
- **Use the pre-test practice screens.** They are ungraded. Use them to learn the buttons, the timer position, and how to flag a question, so none of that costs attention later.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Concluding more than the facts allow.** If a rule needs two conditions and you have confirmed one, the answer is "cannot tell yet", not "probably fine". Assessments — and audits — reward restraint.
- **Accepting "nearby" evidence.** A peer-reviewed rollback plan is not a tested rollback plan. Match the evidence to the exact words of the criterion.
- **Letting one green tick colour the rest.** A pack with strong test results can still be missing its service desk briefing. Check every criterion independently.
- **Prioritising by loudness instead of impact.** The item most recently mentioned to you is not automatically the most urgent. Rank by users affected and hardness of deadline.
- **Rushing the easy questions.** Early questions in adaptive tests set your trajectory. A careless slip on an easy pattern question costs more than a hard question you genuinely could not do.
- **Freezing when the test gets harder.** In adaptive formats, harder questions usually mean you are performing well. Take a breath and keep applying your techniques.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures how confidently and accurately you work with numbers in job-realistic situations. It is not a maths exam. You will not need algebra, geometry, or anything you have not used since school beyond the basics: percentages, ratios, averages, rates of change, and reading tables and simple charts. What makes it "job-specific" is the material: instead of abstract sums, you will see change success rates, incident volumes, service level agreement (SLA) percentages, test pass rates, and capacity figures — the numbers a service acceptance analyst genuinely meets.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 20 to 30 minutes with 15 to 25 questions. Each question presents a small table, chart, or short numeric statement, followed by multiple-choice options. Calculators are usually allowed (check the instructions — they almost always are for workplace tests), and rough paper is your friend. Scoring compares your accuracy and speed against a norm group. Some platforms adapt difficulty as you go.

Employers use numeric reasoning for service acceptance roles because your evidence work is quietly numerical. "All regression tests passed" is a claim about 212 out of 212. "The change failure rate improved" is a claim about two percentages. "This service met its availability target" is a claim about minutes of downtime against minutes in a month. When you validate changes and gather evidence, you are often the person who checks that the numbers in the pack actually support the sentence written above them. A candidate who can do that quickly and calmly is genuinely valuable, and this test measures exactly that.

A reassurance before you start: the arithmetic itself is friendly. Almost every question in this section comes down to one of three moves — find the part of a whole (percentage), compare two quantities (ratio or difference), or divide a total by a count (average or rate). If you can do those three moves carefully, you can do this test. The practice below builds each move with your real workplace numbers.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Percentages and pass rates** map to your core duty of validating changes against acceptance criteria: test evidence packs are full of "X of Y passed" figures, and you must convert, compare, and sanity-check them.
- **Reading tables** maps to your **Asset and configuration management** skill: configuration and asset data arrives as tables, and correcting information starts with reading it accurately.
- **Rates and volumes** map to your **Incident management** skill: identifying and registering incidents means understanding queue volumes, arrival rates, and how they change week by week.
- **Averages and trends** map to your **Problem management** skill: investigating problems in systems and services often begins with a trend — incidents per week creeping up, a job taking longer each month.
- **Capacity and availability figures** map to your **Availability and capacity management** and **Continuity management** skills: explaining these processes convincingly includes being comfortable with the percentages they are measured in.
- **Turning data into decisions** maps to your **User focus** skill: using quantitative and qualitative data about users to turn user focus into outcomes starts with reading the quantitative data correctly.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Test pass percentage**

An evidence pack shows that 190 of 200 regression tests passed. What is the pass rate as a percentage?

- A) 90%
- B) 95%
- C) 98%
- D) 19%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Percentage = part ÷ whole × 100. Here: 190 ÷ 200 = 0.95, and 0.95 × 100 = 95%. A quick mental route: 200 tests means each test is worth half a percentage point (100% ÷ 200 = 0.5%), and 10 tests failed, so 10 × 0.5% = 5% failed, leaving 95% passed. Both routes give the same answer, and having two routes is a great habit — if they agree, you can be confident. You will use this constantly when checking whether an evidence pack's headline ("95% pass rate") matches its raw numbers.

**Question 2 (easy) — Change volumes across a month**

Your area processed these change requests over four weeks: week 1: 12, week 2: 18, week 3: 15, week 4: 15. What was the average (mean) number of changes per week?

- A) 14
- B) 15
- C) 16
- D) 60

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Add the values, then divide by how many there are. Total = 12 + 18 + 15 + 15 = 60. Divide by 4 weeks: 60 ÷ 4 = 15. Option D is the total, not the average — a classic mis-read, so always check whether the question asks for the total or the average. A sense-check habit: the average must sit between the smallest value (12) and the largest (18), so 15 is plausible and 60 is impossible. Sense-checking against the range takes two seconds and catches many slips.

**Question 3 (easy) — Reading an incident queue table**

| Queue | Monday | Tuesday | Wednesday |
|---|---|---|---|
| Applications | 24 | 31 | 27 |
| End User Computing | 18 | 16 | 21 |
| Network | 9 | 12 | 8 |

How many incidents arrived in the Applications queue across the three days?

- A) 72
- B) 82
- C) 55
- D) 27

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Read along the Applications row only: 24 + 31 + 27. Add in easy steps: 24 + 31 = 55, then 55 + 27 = 82. Option A (72) comes from misreading 31 as 21 — a genuine hazard when a table is dense, so use your finger or cursor to track the row. Option C stops adding after two days. When you register and allocate incidents, reading the right row and column of a queue report is a daily act; table discipline (row first, then columns, one at a time) is the whole skill.

**Question 4 (easy) — Percentage of changes needing rework**

Last quarter, 8 of the 64 changes you validated needed rework before acceptance. What percentage needed rework?

- A) 8%
- B) 10%
- C) 12.5%
- D) 15%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** 8 ÷ 64 = 0.125, and 0.125 × 100 = 12.5%. A neat mental route: 8 is one eighth of 64 (because 8 × 8 = 64), and one eighth as a percentage is 12.5% (since 100 ÷ 8 = 12.5). Learning a few common fractions as percentages — 1/2 = 50%, 1/4 = 25%, 1/8 = 12.5%, 1/5 = 20%, 1/10 = 10% — will save you real time in a timed test, because test writers love these tidy fractions. In your role, rework rate is a number worth watching: if it climbs, the acceptance criteria may be unclear to project teams, and you can contribute that insight to your team.

**Question 5 (moderate) — SLA percentage from raw counts**

Your service desk's SLA requires 90% of incidents to be resolved within target time. This week, 234 of 260 incidents were resolved within target. Did the desk meet the SLA, and what was the actual percentage?

- A) Yes — 90%
- B) Yes — 92%
- C) No — 88%
- D) No — 89%

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Step by step: 234 ÷ 260. Simplify first if you like: divide both by 26 → 234 ÷ 26 = 9 and 260 ÷ 26 = 10, so the fraction is 9/10 = 90%. The desk resolved exactly 90% within target, and since the SLA requires 90%, hitting it exactly counts as meeting it (unless a contract explicitly says "more than 90%", which this one does not). The learning point is twofold: first, simplifying a fraction before dividing often turns a scary sum into an easy one; second, read threshold language carefully — "at least 90%", "above 90%" and "90% or better" are not always interchangeable, and as an acceptance analyst you will meet all three in service acceptance criteria.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Ratio of standard to normal changes**

In your change log, last month there were 45 standard changes and 27 normal changes. What is the ratio of standard to normal changes in its simplest form?

- A) 45:27
- B) 9:5
- C) 5:3
- D) 3:5

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Both numbers divide by 9: 45 ÷ 9 = 5 and 27 ÷ 9 = 3, so the ratio is 5:3. Option A is correct but not in simplest form, which the question asked for. Option D reverses the order — a very common slip, so always match the ratio's order to the question's word order ("standard to normal" means standard first). Ratios matter in your work because they describe the shape of your workload: a healthy change process usually has many more pre-approved standard changes than board-approved normal ones, and a shifting ratio is a signal worth mentioning to your service transition manager.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Availability percentage**

A service was unavailable for 90 minutes during a 30-day month. Approximately what availability percentage did it achieve? (Use 30 days × 24 hours × 60 minutes = 43,200 minutes in the month.)

- A) 99.99%
- B) 99.79%
- C) 99.20%
- D) 97.90%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Downtime fraction = 90 ÷ 43,200. Simplify: 90 ÷ 43,200 = 9 ÷ 4,320 = 1 ÷ 480 ≈ 0.00208, which is about 0.208%. Availability = 100% − 0.208% ≈ 99.79%. The efficient route is to compute the small number (downtime percentage) and subtract, rather than dividing 43,110 by 43,200. Availability percentages are the native language of your **Availability and capacity management** skill: when you explain these processes, it helps enormously to know that "99.8% monthly availability" means roughly an hour and a half of downtime — turning an abstract percentage into minutes users actually feel.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Percentage change in incident volume**

The Monday-morning login incidents you have been tracking numbered 25 in April and 35 in May. What was the percentage increase from April to May?

- A) 10%
- B) 29%
- C) 40%
- D) 71%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Percentage change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100. Here: (35 − 25) ÷ 25 = 10 ÷ 25 = 0.4 = 40%. The classic error is dividing by the new value: 10 ÷ 35 ≈ 29% (option B). The rule to memorise: always divide by the value you started from. Option A confuses the raw difference (10 incidents) with a percentage. Trend figures like this are how problem investigations begin — a 40% month-on-month rise in a recurring incident is exactly the kind of evidenced pattern your **Problem management** skill asks you to investigate and contribute to your team.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Weighted evidence scoring**

Your acceptance scorecard weights evidence areas: testing counts for 50%, documentation 30%, and communications 20%. A change scores 90 (out of 100) on testing, 70 on documentation, and 60 on communications. What is its weighted overall score?

- A) 73.3
- B) 78
- C) 80
- D) 84

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Multiply each score by its weight, then add. Testing: 90 × 0.50 = 45. Documentation: 70 × 0.30 = 21. Communications: 60 × 0.20 = 12. Total: 45 + 21 + 12 = 78. Option A is the unweighted average (90 + 70 + 60 = 220; 220 ÷ 3 ≈ 73.3), which ignores the weights. Sense-check: the answer must sit between the lowest (60) and highest (90) scores, and because testing is weighted heaviest and testing scored best, the weighted result (78) should sit above the plain average (73.3) — it does. Weighted scoring appears in real acceptance frameworks precisely because not all criteria matter equally, and knowing how the arithmetic works lets you explain a score to a disappointed project team clearly and fairly.

**Question 10 (hard) — Capacity headroom projection**

A file store is at 720 GB used out of 1,000 GB capacity. Usage has been growing by 40 GB per month, and the team's standard says a capacity increase must be requested when usage reaches 90% of capacity. In how many months will the store reach the 90% trigger point?

- A) 2.5 months
- B) 4.5 months
- C) 7 months
- D) 9 months

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** First find the trigger level: 90% of 1,000 GB = 900 GB. Current usage is 720 GB, so the remaining headroom before the trigger is 900 − 720 = 180 GB. At 40 GB per month, time to trigger = 180 ÷ 40 = 4.5 months. Option C (7 months) is the time to reach full capacity (280 ÷ 40), which misreads the question — the trigger is at 90%, not 100%. This two-step structure (find the threshold, then divide the gap by the rate) is the standard shape of capacity questions, and it is exactly the reasoning behind your **Availability and capacity management** skill: processes exist so that someone requests more capacity months before users feel the squeeze, not the week the disk fills.

**Question 11 (hard) — Reconciling an asset audit**

An asset register lists 480 laptops. A physical audit finds: 462 laptops verified in expected locations, 6 found in unexpected locations, and 3 devices found on site that were not on the register at all. How many registered laptops were not found at all, and what percentage of the register does that represent?

- A) 12 laptops; 2.5%
- B) 15 laptops; 3.125%
- C) 9 laptops; 1.875%
- D) 12 laptops; 2.6%

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Careful with the unregistered devices — they are not part of the register of 480, so set them aside. Registered laptops accounted for = 462 (expected locations) + 6 (unexpected locations) = 468. Missing = 480 − 468 = 12. As a percentage: 12 ÷ 480 = 0.025 = 2.5%. Option B wrongly adds the 3 unregistered devices to the missing count; option C wrongly subtracts them. The lesson generalises: when reconciling data sets, first decide which population each number belongs to. In configuration management terms, the 12 missing devices are one problem (assets to trace), and the 3 unregistered devices are a different problem (records to create) — mixing them up muddles both investigations. This is your **Asset and configuration management** skill expressed as arithmetic.

**Question 12 (hard) — Interpreting a trend table with a target**

| Month | Changes implemented | Failed changes |
|---|---|---|
| March | 40 | 4 |
| April | 50 | 4 |
| May | 60 | 6 |

Your area's target is a change failure rate of no more than 10%. Which statement is accurate?

- A) The failure rate worsened every month and the target was missed in May.
- B) The failure rate improved from March to April, worsened in May, and the target was met in all three months.
- C) The failure rate was constant because failures stayed at 4 in March and April.
- D) The failure rate improved every month.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Compute each month's rate before comparing anything. March: 4 ÷ 40 = 0.10 = 10%. April: 4 ÷ 50 = 0.08 = 8%. May: 6 ÷ 60 = 0.10 = 10%. So the rate improved from March to April (10% down to 8%), then worsened in May (back to 10%). Against a target of "no more than 10%", every month is at or below 10%, so the target was met in all three months — exactly option B. Option C makes the classic count-versus-rate error: failures stayed at 4 in March and April, but the volume grew from 40 to 50, so the rate fell — 4 out of 40 is a different rate from 4 out of 50. Options A and D describe trends the arithmetic contradicts. The deeper lesson for your evidence work: when a pack says "failures held steady", ask whether that means steady as a count or steady as a rate. They are different claims, and only the rate tells you whether the change process is genuinely healthy as volumes grow.

**Question 13 (hard) — Combining volumes and SLA maths**

Two queues feed your team. Queue P received 150 incidents and resolved 132 within target. Queue Q received 90 incidents and resolved 84 within target. What was the combined within-target percentage across both queues?

- A) 88%
- B) 90%
- C) 91%
- D) 93%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Combine the raw numbers first, then take the percentage — never average the two percentages. Total resolved within target = 132 + 84 = 216. Total incidents = 150 + 90 = 240. Combined rate = 216 ÷ 240 = 0.9 = 90%. If you had averaged the queue percentages instead (P: 132/150 = 88%; Q: 84/90 ≈ 93.3%; average ≈ 90.7%) you would get a slightly different and wrong figure, because the queues are different sizes. With these friendly numbers the error is small; with lopsided queues it can be huge. The rule — combine counts, then compute — is essential whenever you aggregate evidence across teams or systems, which is precisely what "co-ordinating across IT operations" asks of you.

### Preparation tips

- **Rebuild your three core moves.** Part-of-whole (percentages), compare (ratios and differences), and divide-by-count (averages and rates) cover almost every question. Practise each until it feels automatic.
- **Learn the tidy fractions.** 1/8 = 12.5%, 1/5 = 20%, 1/4 = 25%, 1/3 ≈ 33%, 1/2 = 50%. Test writers use them constantly, and spotting one turns a division into a memory lookup.
- **Always sense-check against the range.** An average must sit between the smallest and largest values. A combined percentage must sit between the two queue percentages. If your answer falls outside, you slipped.
- **Divide by the starting value for percentage change.** Write "(new − old) ÷ old" on your rough paper before the test starts, so it is there when nerves arrive.
- **Practise with real reports.** Next time a service report crosses your desk, recompute one figure yourself — an SLA percentage, a failure rate. Real numbers are the best practice material, and checking them is literally your job.
- **Use the calculator for arithmetic, not for thinking.** Decide the method first (what divided by what?), then compute. Typing before thinking is the main source of calculator errors.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Averaging percentages from different-sized groups.** Combine the raw counts first. Two queues at 88% and 93% do not average to "about 90.5%" unless they are exactly the same size.
- **Dividing by the wrong base.** Percentage change divides by the old value. Missing assets divide by the register count, not the audit count. Ask "percentage of what?" every single time.
- **Confusing counts with rates.** Four failures in March and four in April is a constant count but a falling rate if volume grew. Evidence claims often blur this — do not let them.
- **Misreading tables under time pressure.** Track the row with your cursor. The most common numeric error in tests is reading the number one cell over.
- **Answering the total when the question asks for the average** (or the 100% point when the question asks about a 90% trigger). Re-read the final sentence of the question before you answer it.
- **Skipping the sense-check to save ten seconds.** The sense-check is the cheapest insurance in the whole test. An impossible answer spotted is a mark saved.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately you read, understand, and draw conclusions from written material. For your role, that material is not literary passages — it is change policies, acceptance criteria, service management framework extracts, incident procedures, emails from project teams, and continuity plans. The test presents a short passage and asks questions about it, most commonly in the true / false / cannot say format: given only the passage, is the statement definitely true, definitely false, or is there not enough information to say?

The typical format is an online, timed test of 15 to 25 minutes with 20 to 30 questions across several passages. The golden rule of the format is this: answer from the passage alone. Your own knowledge of ITIL, your department, or how things usually work must be set aside. If the passage does not say it, and it does not follow necessarily from what the passage says, the answer is "cannot say" — even if you happen to know from experience that it is true in real life. This feels strange at first and becomes natural with a little practice.

Employers use verbal reasoning for service acceptance roles because your work runs on precise reading. Acceptance criteria are legal-ish little texts: "the rollback plan has been tested" means something different from "the rollback plan has been reviewed", and you are the person who must notice. Change policies contain conditions, exceptions, and priorities, and misreading one can let a risky change through or block a safe one. Emails from project teams often claim slightly more than their evidence shows, and your careful reading is the safety net. A verbal reasoning score is, in effect, a measure of how safe the organisation is when a document crosses your desk.

There is also a writing dimension: some assessments include questions about clear writing — choosing the clearest sentence, or the best summary. That matters for you too, because gathering evidence and creating materials means writing things others must understand first time.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Precise comprehension** maps to your core duty of managing acceptance criteria: the difference between "may", "must", "should", and "unless" is the difference between a pass and a fail at a service acceptance gate.
- **True / false / cannot say discipline** maps to your **Change management** skill: applying change control procedures under supervision means acting on what the procedure actually authorises, not what you assume it probably intends.
- **Inference control** maps to your **Incident management** and **Problem management** skills: gathering the required information for an incident record means separating what the caller reported (fact) from what it might mean (hypothesis) — good records keep the two apart.
- **Reading process documents** maps to your **Continuity management** and **Availability and capacity management** skills: you are expected to explain these processes to others, and you can only explain accurately what you have read accurately.
- **Clear-writing judgement** maps to your duty of creating materials and to your **Community collaboration** skill: understanding the work of others, and being understood, is what team collaboration and feedback run on.
- **Reading user evidence** maps to your **User focus** skill: collating user needs evidence means digesting research notes and survey summaries without adding conclusions the data does not support.

### Practice questions

For questions 1 to 5, read this extract from a departmental change policy:

> "All changes to production services must be recorded in the change management system before implementation. Standard changes are pre-approved and may be implemented by any trained team member. Normal changes require approval from the Change Advisory Board (CAB), which meets every Wednesday. Emergency changes may be implemented before approval is recorded, but must be reviewed retrospectively by the CAB at its next meeting. Changes implemented without any record will be treated as unauthorised and investigated. The service transition team is responsible for auditing change records monthly."

**Question 1 (easy) — True / false / cannot say**

Statement: "Normal changes require approval from the Change Advisory Board."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The passage states this directly: "Normal changes require approval from the Change Advisory Board (CAB)". When the statement closely mirrors the passage's own words, the answer is true — but do still read carefully, because test writers sometimes flip a single word (such as changing "normal" to "standard"). Here there is no flip; it is a straightforward comprehension check, and easy questions like this are where you bank quick, confident marks. In your work, this is the equivalent of correctly quoting the policy when a project team asks what approval they need.

**Question 2 (easy) — True / false / cannot say**

Statement: "Standard changes require approval from the CAB before implementation."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage says standard changes "are pre-approved and may be implemented by any trained team member". Pre-approved means the approval already exists in the template — no CAB approval is needed per change. The statement contradicts the passage, so it is false, not "cannot say". The distinction matters: "false" means the passage makes the statement wrong; "cannot say" means the passage is silent. Here the passage speaks clearly, and it disagrees with the statement. This mirrors real conversations where a colleague assumes every change goes to the CAB — you can gently correct them, citing the policy, which is exactly what applying change control procedures well looks like.

**Question 3 (moderate) — True / false / cannot say**

Statement: "The CAB meets more than once a week when there are many emergency changes to review."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage says the CAB "meets every Wednesday" and that emergency changes are "reviewed retrospectively by the CAB at its next meeting". It says nothing about extra meetings in busy periods. The statement might be true in some real organisation — but this passage neither confirms nor denies it, so the answer is "cannot say". Notice the temptation: "reviewed at its next meeting" might make you picture extra meetings, but picturing is not evidence. This is the single most important habit the format teaches: the difference between what a document says and what you imagine around it. Incident records, audit findings, and acceptance decisions all depend on exactly this discipline.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Inference within limits**

Based on the passage, a change that was implemented yesterday with no record in the change management system will be:

- A) Approved retrospectively at the next CAB meeting
- B) Treated as unauthorised and investigated
- C) Ignored if it caused no incidents
- D) Recorded by the service transition team during the monthly audit

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage is explicit: "Changes implemented without any record will be treated as unauthorised and investigated." Option A confuses this with the emergency route — but the emergency route still involves a record; it is approval that comes later, not recording. That is a subtle and realistic distinction: emergency changes skip prior approval, never the record. Option C contradicts the passage's unconditional wording ("will be treated"), and option D misdescribes the audit role — the team audits records; nothing says it creates missing ones. Precision about "what exactly does the exception permit?" is the heart of reading policies well, and it is a skill your supervising manager will value every time a project team tries a creative interpretation.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Best summary**

Which sentence best summarises the passage as a whole?

- A) The CAB approves all changes to production services every Wednesday.
- B) Every change must be recorded, with an approval route that depends on its category, and unrecorded changes are investigated.
- C) Emergency changes are forbidden unless the CAB has met.
- D) The service transition team approves standard changes monthly.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A good summary covers the passage's main structure without contradicting any detail. The passage's skeleton is: (1) everything is recorded; (2) the approval route differs by category (pre-approved, CAB, retrospective); (3) unrecorded changes are investigated. Option B captures all three. Option A is wrong twice — standard changes do not go to the CAB, and "every Wednesday" describes meetings, not all approvals. Option C reverses the emergency rule. Option D muddles auditing with approving. Summary questions reward structural reading: before judging the options, sketch the passage's skeleton in your head, then match. You do the same thing when you brief your team on a new procedure in three sentences — creating materials that are faithful and short.

For questions 6 to 9, read this email from a project manager:

> "Hi — ahead of Friday's acceptance review for the caseworker portal release, here's where we are. Functional testing finished Monday: 96 of 100 test cases passed. The four failures are all in the reporting module, which — as agreed with the service owner in April — is out of scope for this release and switched off in production. Performance testing is complete and the results are within the agreed thresholds. The support team training session is booked for Thursday. We're still chasing the supplier for the final security certificate, which they've promised by Wednesday. Assuming that lands, we believe everything will be green for Friday."

**Question 6 (easy) — True / false / cannot say**

Statement: "Functional testing finished on Monday."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Stated directly: "Functional testing finished Monday". Bank the mark and move on — but let this easy question model the habit of locating the exact sentence that supports your answer. In the timed test, physically finding the supporting sentence (rather than answering from memory of your first read) is what keeps your accuracy high as the passages get denser. When you validate evidence packs, the same habit applies: every acceptance judgement should be traceable to a specific piece of evidence, not to an overall impression.

**Question 7 (moderate) — True / false / cannot say**

Statement: "The four failed test cases will be fixed before the release."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The email says the failures are in a module that is out of scope and switched off in production. It never says the failures will be fixed before release — and it never says they will not be. Perhaps they will be fixed in a later release; perhaps never; the email is silent. So the answer is "cannot say". Note the pull of reasonableness: switching the module off makes fixing before release seem unnecessary, and you might infer "so they won't fix them" — but that is your inference, not the email's claim, and "cannot say" covers both unstated possibilities. In your acceptance role, this is precisely the kind of point you would turn into a clarifying question for Friday's review rather than an assumption: "What is the plan for the four reporting-module failures?"

**Question 8 (moderate) — True / false / cannot say**

Statement: "The security certificate has been received."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The email says the team is "still chasing the supplier for the final security certificate, which they've promised by Wednesday". At the time of writing, the certificate has not been received — "still chasing" makes that definite, so the statement is false as of the email. Be careful not to drift into "cannot say" because the certificate might arrive later: the statement is about the situation described, and the passage speaks to it directly. This kind of reading matters for your service readiness colleagues and for you: a promised certificate is a risk, not evidence. At Friday's review, "promised by Wednesday" either became a real certificate or it did not, and acceptance rests on which.

**Question 9 (hard) — Evaluating the claim against the evidence**

The email concludes: "we believe everything will be green for Friday." Judged strictly against the email's own content, which item most weakens that claim?

- A) The four failed test cases
- B) The performance results
- C) The outstanding security certificate
- D) The Thursday training session

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Assess each item's status. The four failures (A) sit in an out-of-scope, switched-off module with an agreed decision behind them — noted, but explained. Performance (B) is complete and within thresholds — supportive, not weakening. The training session (D) is booked for Thursday — not yet done, but scheduled and under the team's control. The certificate (C) is different in kind: it is outstanding, it depends on an external supplier, and the email itself flags it with "assuming that lands". A promise from a third party is the weakest link in the chain and the item most likely to turn Friday amber. This is exactly the judgement you bring to acceptance reviews: rank the open items by how much uncertainty they carry and who controls them. External dependencies near a deadline deserve the brightest highlight in your review notes.

For questions 10 to 12, read this extract from a service management framework study guide:

> "Service transition ensures that new, modified or retired services meet the expectations of the business as documented in the service design stage. Within service transition, change management aims to ensure that standardised methods are used for the efficient and prompt handling of all changes, in order to minimise the impact of change-related incidents upon service quality. Service asset and configuration management maintains information about configuration items required to deliver services, including their relationships. Effective service transition reduces unpredicted outcomes, but it cannot eliminate risk entirely."

**Question 10 (moderate) — True / false / cannot say**

Statement: "Change management aims to eliminate all change-related incidents."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage says change management aims "to minimise the impact of change-related incidents", and separately that effective service transition "cannot eliminate risk entirely". Minimise is not eliminate; the passage's own closing sentence rules out elimination. So the statement is false. This one-word distinction — minimise versus eliminate — is a favourite of test writers and, more importantly, of auditors and of anyone writing acceptance criteria. If a criterion says "risk eliminated", it is probably badly written and impossible to evidence; if it says "risk minimised and residual risk accepted by the service owner", you can actually validate it. Careful reading and good criteria-writing are the same skill.

**Question 11 (moderate) — Comprehension of relationships**

According to the passage, what does service asset and configuration management maintain?

- A) The service design documentation
- B) Information about configuration items and their relationships
- C) The change management system
- D) The business's expectations of services

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage states it "maintains information about configuration items required to deliver services, including their relationships". Options A and D belong to the service design stage as described in the first sentence; option C is a tool no sentence assigns to this process. The technique is simple ownership-mapping: as you read, note which process "owns" which noun. Passages about frameworks (and real framework documents, which you will study for your Level 3 **Service management framework knowledge** qualification) reward exactly this kind of structured reading — each process has a purpose sentence, and exam questions probe whether you attached the right purpose to the right process.

**Question 12 (hard) — Clear-writing judgement**

You are drafting a sentence for an acceptance review summary. Which version communicates most clearly to a mixed audience of technical and non-technical stakeholders?

- A) "Utilisation of the rollback capability was validated via execution of the reversion procedure in the pre-production environment."
- B) "We tested the rollback plan by running it in the pre-production environment, and it worked."
- C) "Rollback validation activities have been progressed in line with the agreed assurance approach."
- D) "The reversion mechanism's operability was confirmed subsequent to procedural execution."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B says who did what, where, and with what result, in plain words: tested, ran it, it worked. Option A buries the same facts in noun-heavy jargon ("utilisation", "via execution of"). Option C is the most dangerous kind of sentence in government writing: it sounds like progress while committing to nothing — "progressed" could mean anything from "finished" to "started an email thread". Option D is A wearing a different hat. Clear writing is not dumbing down; it is the fastest route to shared understanding, and GOV.UK style guidance favours it for exactly that reason. When you create materials for acceptance reviews, sentences like B let a non-technical service owner make a well-informed decision — which is the entire point of the material.

### Preparation tips

- **Practise the "cannot say" discipline daily.** Take any workplace email and write down one thing it states, one thing it contradicts, and one thing it simply does not address. Three minutes, enormous payoff.
- **Find the supporting sentence.** For every answer, locate the exact words in the passage that justify it. If you cannot point to them, your answer is probably an assumption.
- **Watch the small words.** May, must, should, unless, all, some, before, by. Circle them mentally as you read — they carry most of a policy's meaning.
- **Read the question before re-reading the passage.** On your second pass, you are searching for something specific, which is much faster than re-reading generally.
- **Read good plain English.** GOV.UK content is written to a clear-writing standard; reading it tunes your ear for what clarity sounds like, which helps with summary and clear-writing questions.
- **Do not bring your job into the test.** Your real-world knowledge of how change management works is an asset at work and a liability in a "cannot say" question. Answer from the passage alone.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Answering from experience instead of the passage.** The test measures reading, not knowledge. If the passage is silent, the answer is "cannot say" no matter what ITIL says.
- **Treating "false" and "cannot say" as the same.** False means the passage contradicts the statement. Cannot say means the passage is silent. Deciding which takes one extra beat of thought — spend it.
- **Being seduced by reasonable-sounding inferences.** "They switched the module off, so they won't fix the tests" feels sensible, but feeling sensible is not textual support.
- **Missing a single flipped word.** "Standard" for "normal", "reviewed" for "tested", "minimise" for "eliminate". Slow down exactly at the point where the statement echoes the passage.
- **Spending too long on one dense passage.** Verbal tests are usually generous with questions per passage — if one statement is stubborn, flag it, answer the rest, and return.
- **Choosing the longest or most formal option in clear-writing questions.** Formality is not clarity. The best sentence is usually the one that names an actor, an action, and a result.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement test (SJT) presents realistic workplace scenarios and asks you to judge possible responses. Unlike the other three assessments, it has no arithmetic and no passages to parse — it measures your professional judgement: what you would do, or what you recognise as most and least effective, when a situation is messy, human, and time-pressured.

Typical formats vary. The most common asks you to pick the most effective response from four options; some also ask for the least effective; others ask you to rate every response on a scale or rank them in order. Timing is usually generous — 20 to 30 minutes for 15 to 20 scenarios — because the test measures judgement rather than speed. Scoring compares your choices against a key agreed by experienced professionals and occupational psychologists, usually aligned to a behaviour framework: for GDAD roles, think of behaviours like user focus, collaboration, ownership, honesty about problems, appropriate escalation, and inclusion.

Employers use SJTs for service acceptance roles because your role sits at a junction of pressures. Project teams want their change accepted on schedule. Operations colleagues want stability. Users want working services. Your supervisor wants honest, timely information. An SJT explores how you balance those pressures: do you raise concerns early or sit on them? Do you check the criteria or take someone's word? Do you pass a problem on with good information, or either hoard it or dump it? At your level, the assessment is not looking for heroics — it rewards calm, honest, procedure-aware behaviour, asking for help at the right moments, and keeping users at the centre.

A reassurance about "right answers": SJT scenarios rarely have one perfect option. They have a most effective option, defensible middles, and usually one clearly poor option. Your aim is to recognise the shape of good professional behaviour: gather facts, be honest early, follow the procedure, involve the right people, and keep the user's experience in view. The explanations below name those behaviours explicitly so you can see the pattern, not just the answer.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Escalation and asking for help** maps to your **Ownership and topic** skill — show awareness of problem resolution processes and pass problems on to your team — and to your role's design: you validate under supervision, and knowing when to involve your service transition manager is a core competence, not a fallback.
- **Integrity under delivery pressure** maps to your central duty of managing acceptance criteria: the scenarios probe whether you hold the line kindly when evidence is missing and a deadline looms.
- **Teamworking scenarios** map to your **Community collaboration** skill: understanding the work of others, valuing team dynamics, and giving and receiving feedback well.
- **Incident-handling scenarios** map to your **Incident management** skill: gathering the required information calmly and allocating to the appropriate channel, even when a caller is upset.
- **User-centred choices** map to your **User focus** skill: identifying and engaging users, collating user needs evidence, and letting real user data — not assumptions — shape what you recommend.
- **Process-improvement scenarios** map to your **Problem management** and **Service focus** skills: contributing to remedies and preventative measures, and helping build coherent frameworks that work for everyone who uses them.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — A missing piece of evidence, a pressing deadline**

A project manager asks you to sign off acceptance for a change today. Checking the pack, you find the service desk briefing — one of the agreed criteria — has not happened. The project manager says: "It's a tiny thing, we'll do it next week, can you just tick it?" What is the most effective response?

- A) Tick it — the briefing genuinely is a small item, and relationships matter.
- B) Refuse and report the project manager to their line manager for pressuring you.
- C) Explain that you cannot record the criterion as met until it is, offer to help get the briefing arranged quickly, and let your service transition manager know the pack is one item short of complete.
- D) Say nothing today and quietly leave the pack unsigned until they chase you.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C combines honesty, helpfulness, and appropriate escalation — the three behaviours this scenario is testing. You hold the line on the criterion (integrity), you actively help solve the gap rather than just blocking (collaboration and service focus), and you keep your manager informed because acceptance timing affects others (ownership at your level means passing information up, not sitting on it). Option A falsifies a record — a small tick today, but it teaches every project team that your criteria are negotiable, and it breaks the audit trail your whole framework depends on. Option B escalates a routine disagreement into a conflict before trying a constructive route. Option D is avoidance: the deadline pressure does not go away, and silence damages trust more than a clear "not yet, and here's how we get there".

**Question 2 (easy) — An angry caller with a real incident**

You take a call from a frustrated user whose case management screen has been failing all morning; she says she has already called once and "nothing happened". She is talking quickly and angrily. What is the most effective first response?

- A) Tell her that anger will not speed things up and ask her to call back when calmer.
- B) Listen, acknowledge the frustration, then calmly gather the required details — including her earlier reference number — so you can find the first ticket and allocate the incident correctly.
- C) Promise her the problem will be fixed within the hour to calm her down.
- D) Transfer her immediately to your team lead.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B does the job your **Incident management** skill describes: gather the required information and allocate to the appropriate channel — while treating the user with respect. Acknowledging frustration is not weakness; it is the fastest route to a usable conversation, and asking for the earlier reference number turns her complaint ("nothing happened") into something you can actually investigate. Option A punishes a user for being let down. Option C makes a promise you cannot evidence — and broken promises are how frustrated users become formal complaints. Option D passes a routine, if uncomfortable, call upward without adding any value; escalation is for things beyond your role, and taking an incident report is squarely within it. The professional behaviours rewarded here are user focus and calm ownership.

**Question 3 (easy) — You made a mistake**

Reviewing your own work from yesterday, you realise you allocated an incident to the wrong queue, and it has sat unworked for a day. Nobody else has noticed. What is the most effective response?

- A) Reallocate it quietly — no harm in staying silent since you fixed it.
- B) Reallocate it immediately, add a note explaining the delay, apologise to the affected queue, and mention it to your supervisor so any impact on the user can be checked.
- C) Leave it — reallocating now will draw attention to the mistake.
- D) Reallocate it and blame the ticketing system's confusing interface in the notes.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The tested behaviour is honest ownership of error. Option B fixes the problem fast (the user's wait is what matters most), documents honestly so the next person understands the timeline, and tells your supervisor — not as self-punishment, but because a day's delay might need a word with the user, and your supervisor can judge that. Option A fixes the ticket but hides the delay, which corrupts the record and forfeits the chance to check impact. Option C makes the user pay for your comfort. Option D compounds a mistake with a false explanation. Every professional makes allocation errors; assessments (and good managers) look for people whose first instinct on finding one is user impact, honest record, quick word upward. That instinct, practised early in your career, becomes the foundation of being trusted with bigger things.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Least effective: a colleague's evidence looks wrong**

A friendly senior colleague hands you an evidence pack and says: "All done, just needs your check." You notice the performance test results are from the previous release, not this one. Which response is LEAST effective?

- A) Point out the mismatch privately and ask whether the current release's results exist.
- B) Check quietly whether the current results are in the test system before raising it.
- C) Accept the pack — the colleague is senior and presumably knows what they are doing.
- D) Raise it at the acceptance review meeting in front of everyone without warning the colleague first.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** This is a least-effective question, so you are hunting for the worst behaviour. Option C is it: deferring to seniority over evidence defeats the entire purpose of your role. Acceptance criteria protect services precisely because someone checks regardless of who compiled the pack; "presumably knows" is not evidence, and seniority does not change what the criterion requires. Options A and B are both good (A is direct and kind; B adds helpful homework first). Option D is poor — ambushing a colleague publicly damages trust and your **Community collaboration** relationships — but it does at least surface the problem, so it is not as damaging to the service as C, which lets potentially wrong evidence through unchallenged. Ranking flawed options against each other is a core SJT skill: ask "which option most harms users and trust?"

**Question 5 (moderate) — Conflicting instructions**

Your service transition manager asked you to spend today finishing validation materials for Thursday's review. Mid-morning, a project manager from another team asks you to "urgently" test their change paperwork instead, saying "your manager won't mind". What is the most effective response?

- A) Do what the project manager asks — they said it was urgent.
- B) Refuse outright and continue your assigned work without explanation.
- C) Tell the project manager you have a conflicting priority, and quickly check with your service transition manager which task should come first.
- D) Try to do both at once, splitting your attention between them all day.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** At your level, the correct move with genuinely conflicting instructions is transparency and a quick check — not guessing. Option C respects both parties: the project manager hears an honest "I have a conflict" rather than a silent no, and your manager — who owns your priorities — makes the call with full information. It takes five minutes and produces a defensible answer. Option A lets assertiveness outrank your manager's actual instruction, on the strength of "your manager won't mind", which is a claim, not a confirmation. Option B protects your task but damages a relationship needlessly; a one-line explanation costs nothing. Option D usually delivers both tasks late and badly. The behaviours rewarded: honesty about constraints, appropriate escalation of priority conflicts, and protecting agreed commitments. This is also your **Ownership and topic** skill in action — passing a decision to the right owner is good ownership.

**Question 6 (moderate) — The user evidence contradicts the plan**

Your team is preparing acceptance materials for a portal change. You have been collating user needs evidence, and the survey data shows 60% of users primarily access the service on mobile phones — but the acceptance criteria only cover desktop browser testing. The go-live is in two weeks. What is the most effective response?

- A) Say nothing — the criteria were agreed months ago and reopening them causes delay.
- B) Raise the gap now with your service transition manager, showing the survey data, and suggest the review considers whether mobile testing evidence is needed before go-live.
- C) Personally test the service on your own phone this weekend and add your findings to the pack.
- D) Email the project team that go-live must be cancelled.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This scenario tests **User focus** — using quantitative data about users to turn user focus into outcomes — plus early, proportionate escalation. Option B does exactly that: you bring evidence (60% mobile usage), you bring it early (two weeks is time to act), you bring it to the right person, and you frame it as a question for the review rather than a unilateral verdict. Option A privileges the plan over the users the plan exists for; agreed criteria are not sacred when evidence shows they miss the majority use case. Option C is well-meant but wrong in method: informal weekend testing on one device is not acceptance evidence, and doing it alone bypasses the framework that makes evidence trustworthy. Option D wildly overreaches your authority and skips every step between "gap found" and "cancel". Notice the general shape: evidence, early, upward, proportionate. That shape scores well in SJTs because it is genuinely how good analysts behave.

**Question 7 (moderate) — A teammate is struggling**

A new teammate has been given their first evidence pack to compile. You notice they have been stuck for two days, visibly stressed, and this morning they snapped at someone in stand-up. Your own workload is full but not critical. What is the most effective response?

- A) Stay out of it — their work is their responsibility, and you are not their manager.
- B) Tell your manager the teammate is underperforming.
- C) Offer help directly: ask how it's going, share the checklist and example pack you used for your first one, and suggest they raise the workload pressure at stand-up or with the team lead if it continues.
- D) Take the evidence pack off them and do it yourself tonight.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Your **Community collaboration** skill — understanding the work of others and the importance of team dynamics, collaboration and feedback — is what this scenario probes. Option C offers practical, respectful help (a checklist and an example are exactly what a stuck beginner needs), keeps their ownership intact, and points them to the proper channel if the problem is bigger than one pack. Option A confuses "not my job to manage" with "not my job to care"; teams that work like that lose their new members. Option B reports a struggling colleague as a performance problem before anyone has offered support — premature and trust-destroying. Option D solves today at the cost of tomorrow: they learn nothing, you burn an evening, and the team norm becomes rescue instead of support. SJT keys consistently reward "help directly, preserve ownership, signpost the right channel" for peer-support scenarios.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Least effective: something looks like a security risk**

While validating a change, you notice the deployment notes include a shared admin password written in plain text, and the document is stored on a drive the whole department can read. Which response is LEAST effective?

- A) Flag it immediately to your service transition manager and the document owner so the password can be changed and the document secured.
- B) Follow your organisation's security incident reporting process, since credentials have potentially been exposed.
- C) Delete the document yourself without telling anyone, to remove the exposure quickly.
- D) Note it in your validation findings so the acceptance review addresses it.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Options A, B and D are all constructive, and in real life you might do all three: alert the owner (A), report through the proper security channel because exposure may already have happened (B), and record it in findings (D). Option C is the least effective because acting alone and silently destroys the evidence trail (who accessed the document while it was exposed?), fails to trigger the essential step — changing the password, which deletion does not achieve — and leaves nobody aware a security event occurred. It feels decisive but accomplishes the least. The tested behaviours are protecting information (your **Asset and configuration management** skill exists "to protect assets and components") and knowing that security events have proper channels. A useful rule: with security concerns, speed matters, silence never helps, and unilateral cleanup is usually the worst kind of speed.

**Question 9 (hard) — Pressure from above**

A senior manager outside your team stops by your desk: "The director has promised this change goes live Friday. I hear you're the one holding up the paperwork." In fact, the pack is missing rollback test evidence, and you flagged this to the project team two days ago; they have not responded. What is the most effective response?

- A) Accept the pack — a director's promise outranks a missing document.
- B) Calmly explain the specific gap and its risk, mention it was flagged two days ago, offer to re-check the moment evidence arrives, and immediately brief your service transition manager on the conversation.
- C) Tell the senior manager to talk to your manager and walk away.
- D) Work late tonight running the rollback test yourself so the pack is complete.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This is the classic acceptance-analyst pressure scenario, and the key tests three behaviours at once: composure, specificity, and escalation. Option B is calm and factual (the gap is named, not vague), fair (you show the delay is with the project team, without blame), constructive (you offer fast turnaround), and safe (your manager hears about senior pressure from you, promptly — which is exactly what supervision is for). Option A converts hierarchy into evidence, which it is not; if rollback fails on Friday night, the director's promise will not restore the service. Option C is directionally right — your manager should be involved — but done curtly, it wastes the chance to inform and reassure a senior stakeholder, and "walk away" reads as evasion. Option D has a subtle flaw: running the test yourself may be outside your competence and authority, and it blurs the line between validating evidence and manufacturing it — the project team owns producing evidence; you own checking it. Under pressure, the strongest professionals narrate facts, offer help, and widen the circle. That pattern is worth memorising.

**Question 10 (hard) — A recurring problem nobody owns**

Over a month, you notice the same pattern in three separate change validations: each project team was surprised by the service desk briefing criterion and scrambled at the last minute. Each time, acceptance was delayed by days. It is not your job to redesign the process, but you see the pattern most clearly. What is the most effective response?

- A) Keep flagging each instance as it happens — the process is not yours to change.
- B) Write a short note describing the pattern, its cost in delays, and a suggestion (add the briefing criterion to the project kick-off checklist), and share it with your service transition manager for the next process review.
- C) Unilaterally email all project managers announcing a new mandatory kick-off checklist.
- D) Remove the service desk briefing criterion from future packs since teams keep missing it.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Your **Problem management** skill says you investigate problems and "contribute to the implementation of remedies and preventative measures", and your **Service focus** skill is about coherent frameworks that work. Option B is precisely that contribution, correctly sized for your level: you have evidence (three instances, quantified delay), a plausible preventative fix, and the right route (upward, into the process review, where the people with authority can act). Option A is not wrong, but it treats symptoms forever; noticing a pattern and staying silent about it wastes your best insight. Option C exceeds your authority and would create confusion — process changes need an owner and communication, not a surprise email. Option D is the worst kind of fix: deleting a control because it is inconvenient, which "solves" the delay by transferring risk to the service desk and users. The behaviour pattern rewarded here appears throughout your career: see the pattern, evidence it, propose, route it to the owner. People who do this get invited into the process conversations early — it is how analysts grow into managers.

**Question 11 (hard) — Inclusion in the acceptance process**

You are organising an acceptance review meeting. The service desk representative — whose team the briefing criterion protects — works part-time and cannot attend the usual Friday afternoon slot. A colleague suggests: "Just go ahead without them; they can read the minutes." What is the most effective response?

- A) Go ahead Friday — minutes are a reasonable substitute.
- B) Move the meeting to a time the service desk representative can attend, or if that is impossible, gather their input beforehand and present it at the review with their agreement.
- C) Mark the service desk criterion as met since their representative is unavailable to dispute it.
- D) Postpone the review indefinitely until everyone can attend every time.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This scenario tests inclusion as a working practice, not a slogan. The service desk is the team most affected by a poorly briefed change; excluding their voice because of a scheduling default (Friday afternoons exclude many part-time colleagues) makes the review weaker as well as unfair. Option B treats inclusion as a solvable logistics problem: change the slot, or capture their input properly with their agreement — both keep the affected voice in the decision. Option A mistakes being informed (minutes) for being heard (input before the decision). Option C is startlingly bad: absence is not agreement, and recording it as such falsifies the review. Option D swings to the opposite extreme, letting perfect attendance block all progress. Note the general principle, which SJT keys reward consistently: when a process quietly excludes someone it affects, adjust the process, not the person — and if you cannot adjust it this time, carry their voice in with their consent. This is also **Community collaboration**: team dynamics include the teams next door.

**Question 12 (hard) — Honest reporting when the news is unwelcome**

Your validation of a high-profile change finds that it meets seven of nine criteria; the two gaps are real but modest. Your service transition manager is on leave today, and the review is this afternoon. The project sponsor phones: "Can you give me a preview? Tell me it's all green." What is the most effective response?

- A) Say "it's all green" — seven of nine is close enough, and the sponsor wants good news.
- B) Refuse to say anything at all until your manager returns.
- C) Give an accurate preview: seven criteria met, two specific gaps with their practical implications, and what would close them — so the sponsor walks into the review informed rather than surprised.
- D) Tell the sponsor the change has failed validation.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Accuracy with helpful framing is the winning behaviour. Option C gives the sponsor exactly what a preview is for — no surprises this afternoon — and models the analyst's core value: the numbers as they are, plus what they mean and what would fix them. "Seven of nine, here are the two, here's the path to green" is honest and constructive at the same time. Option A is a small lie with a long tail: when the gaps surface at the review (and your job is to surface them), your credibility — the entire currency of an acceptance analyst — is spent. Option B mistakes caution for safety; sharing your own completed findings is within your role, and stonewalling a sponsor creates suspicion where none is needed. If you were unsure whether previews were appropriate, the professional move would be a quick check with whoever is covering for your manager — not silence. Option D overstates: two modest gaps is a different message from "failed", and exaggerating bad news is as misleading as hiding it. The pattern to keep: report the number, the meaning, and the path forward — in that order, to anyone entitled to hear it.

### Preparation tips

- **Learn your behaviour framework.** Before any SJT, re-read the behaviours your organisation values — for GDAD roles: user focus, collaboration, ownership, honesty, appropriate escalation, inclusion. The scoring key is built from them.
- **Look for the "evidence, early, upward, proportionate" shape.** Most strong answers gather facts, raise issues promptly, involve the right person, and match the response size to the problem size.
- **Practise saying "not yet" kindly.** Much of your role is holding a line without hostility. Rehearse the sentence structure: acknowledge the pressure, state the specific gap, offer the path to yes.
- **Answer as your best professional self, consistently.** Do not try to guess a "clever" answer profile. SJTs include consistency checks, and honest, considered answers pattern better than gamed ones.
- **After each practice question, name the behaviour.** Do not just note the letter — say "this rewarded early escalation" or "this punished silent workarounds". Naming behaviours is what transfers to unseen scenarios.
- **Reflect on real situations.** Think of a time you felt pressure to skip a check, or saw a teammate struggling. What did you do? What would the best version of you do? SJT preparation doubles as genuine professional development.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Choosing deference over evidence.** Seniority, friendliness, and confidence are not evidence. The most common trap for early-career analysts is the option where you accept something because of who said it.
- **Confusing escalation with weakness.** At your level, involving your manager at the right moment is scored as strength. The weak options are silence and solo heroics.
- **Fixing things silently.** Quiet workarounds — deleting the risky document, reallocating the ticket without a note — feel efficient and score terribly, because they destroy the shared record teams depend on.
- **Over-escalating trivia.** Passing every routine difficulty upward scores poorly too. Ask: is this within my role? If yes, handle it and mention it; if no, escalate it with good information.
- **Punishing users or colleagues for being difficult.** The angry caller, the struggling teammate, the pushy project manager — strong answers stay respectful and solve the problem, never the person.
- **Answering as a rule-quoting robot.** The best options apply procedures with warmth and judgement. "Refuse and walk away" rarely wins; "explain, help, and inform the right person" usually does.

## Conclusion

Well done — you have worked through a complete set of practice materials built around your actual job. That matters more than it might seem. Generic test practice teaches you to answer generic questions; practising with change requests, acceptance criteria, incident queues, SLA tables, policy extracts, and the human pressures of an acceptance role teaches you to think the way your work already asks you to think, only more deliberately.

Take a moment to notice what you have covered. In the cognitive section, you practised the checking disciplines at the heart of service acceptance: spotting the record that breaks the pattern, deducing only what a rule guarantees, matching evidence to the exact words of a criterion, and prioritising by impact rather than noise. In the numeric section, you rebuilt the three moves — percentages, comparisons, and averages — that let you verify whether an evidence pack's numbers support its claims, from test pass rates to availability figures. In the verbal section, you sharpened the most valuable reading habit in your profession: the discipline of separating what a document says from what you assume around it, and the courage to answer "cannot say" when the evidence is silent. And in the situational judgement section, you rehearsed the professional shape of your role — honest early flags, kind firmness under pressure, asking for help at the right moments, and keeping users and excluded voices in the room.

If you are preparing for a real assessment, here is a simple plan. Spread your practice across at least two weeks, in short sessions. Revisit the questions you got wrong and re-explain the correct answer to yourself in your own words — teaching yourself is the strongest form of learning. In your final few days, add light time pressure so the pace feels familiar. And on the day itself: rest, water, a quiet room, and the reminder that the test is sampling skills you use every working day. You are not walking in cold; you are walking in practised.

Beyond any single test, the skills here are your professional development path. The analyst who checks evidence precisely, reads policies accurately, handles numbers confidently, and escalates honestly is exactly the analyst who grows into service readiness work, and in time into service transition management. Every technique in this guide — the elimination grid, the sense-check, the supporting sentence, the "evidence, early, upward, proportionate" pattern — is a habit you can start using at your desk tomorrow morning.

Be patient with yourself, keep practising in small honest steps, and take pride in what your role protects: services that real people rely on, changed with minimum disruption because someone checked carefully. That someone is you. Good luck — you are better prepared than you think.
