# Associate Interaction Designer - Psychometric Assessment Resources

## Introduction

Welcome, and congratulations on beginning your career as an associate interaction designer in the UK Government Digital and Data profession. You might be here because an assessment is coming up, because you are thinking about your next step, or simply because you are curious how psychometric tests connect to the work you are learning to do. Whatever brought you, this guide was written with someone at the very start of an interaction design career firmly in mind.

Let us take the fear out of these assessments first, because they make almost everyone nervous. Psychometric assessments are not intelligence tests. They are not designed to catch you out, and they are not a verdict on your talent. They are structured, standardised exercises that measure the everyday thinking a particular job actually uses. For you that means checking a screen against a pattern and spotting where it diverges, noticing that a form asks two questions in one field, working out what a set of numbers about a page is really telling you, reading a design standard carefully and applying it, and making sensible decisions when something in a critique or a build conversation goes sideways. None of that is exotic. It is the ordinary, careful thinking that good interaction design rests on.

Your role level shapes this whole guide. As an associate interaction designer you are a trainee in an entry-level position, working under supervision, expected to show design aptitude, potential and an understanding of the role. Nobody assessing you at this level expects you to have designed a complex journey or created a new pattern for the Design System. What they are looking for is exactly what your role description names: aptitude, potential, and understanding. Every rule, definition and figure you need appears inside the question — you never have to have memorised anything.

There is one thing worth saying about interaction design specifically before we start. Your discipline operates at two scales at once. You work on the overall flow — what order the questions come in, when to split a page, what happens when someone goes back — and simultaneously on individual elements: the label on a field, the wording of an error, whether something should be a radio group or a checkbox, whether a component behaves correctly for someone using a keyboard alone. Moving fluently between those two scales, and noticing when a decision at one level breaks something at the other, is a large part of the craft. A surprising amount of what these assessments measure is exactly that habit.

The document is organised into four main sections:

1. A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment — pattern spotting, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation and problem solving, using patterns, components, screens and design standards.
2. A numeric reasoning assessment — percentages, proportions, averages and simple tables, using the numbers interaction designers actually handle, such as error rates, page drop-off, device splits and completion figures.
3. A verbal reasoning assessment — reading passages like design standards, accessibility guidance and stakeholder emails, then answering true/false/cannot-say and comprehension questions.
4. A situational judgement assessment — realistic workplace dilemmas pitched at a trainee level, with guidance on the most and least effective responses and, importantly, on why.

Each section has the same shape: what the assessment measures, how it maps to the named skills in your role, twelve practice questions with full explanations, preparation tips, and common pitfalls.

Here is how to get the most from it. Work slowly — there is no timer here. Decide your answer and write it down before reading the explanation, because an answer you have committed to teaches you far more than one you merely considered. Read the explanations even when you were right. And treat the questions you get wrong as the most useful part of the exercise; they are showing you a technique you have not met yet.

A last thought. Being new is genuinely useful in interaction design. You have not yet learned to overlook the small awkwardnesses that everyone else has stopped seeing — the error message that does not say what to do, the field that rejects a space in a postcode, the button that moves. Hold on to that as you build the rigour this guide is about.

Grab a drink, find a quiet half hour, and let's begin.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the thinking your role genuinely demands, using practical workplace materials rather than abstract shapes and sequences. Instead of rotating cubes, you will work with what an associate interaction designer actually handles — a page against a pattern, a set of form fields, an error message, a component's behaviour, a short extract from a design standard, a list of research observations.

The typical format is an online test lasting fifteen to thirty minutes. Questions cluster into five families: pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective, comparing your answers against a norm group at a similar level. Two features of modern platforms are worth knowing in advance. Many tests are adaptive, so questions get harder after correct answers — if the test suddenly feels difficult, that usually means you are doing well. And most employers receive an acuity report showing speed against accuracy rather than a single score, which means careful work is rewarded and rushing is not.

For an associate interaction designer this section matters because so much of your early value comes from noticing. You notice that a page uses a pattern in a way the pattern was not designed for. You notice that a date field will reject the format most people type. You notice that an error message says what went wrong but not what to do about it. You notice that tabbing through a page reaches the submit button before the last question. You notice that two screens in the same service handle the same choice in two different ways. Each of those is a small act of comparison or deduction, and each protects the quality of the thing users eventually meet.

One reassurance before the questions. At your level, employers are looking for aptitude and potential, not encyclopaedic knowledge. You will never be asked to recall a real WCAG criterion or a real Design System component from memory. Every rule you need is stated in the question. Your job is to apply stated rules carefully — which is exactly what you do at work when a senior designer briefs you and you carry it out.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The cognitive dimensions map onto the skills named in your role summary:

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **Designing strategically**, where you explain the value of using patterns and components in design. Recognising when something follows an established pattern — and, more importantly, when it quietly departs from one — is the first thing that skill requires.
- **Error checking** maps to **Designing for everyone**, where you explain the importance of making services inclusive and accessible and describe different access needs and ways to meet them. Spotting that an interaction excludes somebody is error checking with a human consequence.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Evidence-based design**, where you frame your ideas as design hypotheses to be tested. A hypothesis is a claim about what follows from what, and framing one well means knowing exactly what your evidence supports.
- **Prioritisation** maps to **Iterative design**, where you describe the basics of iterative methodologies such as agile and explain how iteration supports user-centred design.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Iterative design** again, where you describe ways to prototype ideas at different fidelities and when to use them, and to **Designing together**, where you explain techniques for bringing together perspectives from multiple people.
- **Explaining your reasoning** maps to **Design communication**, where you explain the importance of articulating design decisions and describe methods for doing so.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Error checking against a pattern**

Your team's rule for a "question page" pattern says each page must: ask one thing, have a label that is a question, include a back link, and have a single primary action. You review four pages:

| Page | Questions asked | Label style | Back link | Primary actions |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1 | Question | Yes | 1 |
| 2 | 2 | Question | Yes | 1 |
| 3 | 1 | Statement | Yes | 1 |
| 4 | 1 | Question | No | 2 |

How many pages break at least one rule?

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Check each page against all four rules. Page 1 satisfies everything. Page 2 asks two things, so it breaks one rule. Page 3 uses a statement rather than a question as its label, so it breaks one. Page 4 has no back link and two primary actions, so it breaks two. Three pages break at least one rule, giving C. Note the wording carefully: the question asks how many *pages* break at least one rule, not how many *rules* are broken — there are four rule breaches across three pages, and confusing the two would lead you to D. This distinction matters at work too, because "three pages need fixing" and "four things are wrong" prompt different conversations, and the second is more useful when you are handing the list to somebody.

**Question 2 (easy) — Logical deduction from a build rule**

Your team's rule states: "A component may be used in a service only if it is in the Design System, unless it has been reviewed and approved by the lead interaction designer." A developer says: "We shipped a component last week that is not in the Design System." Which one must be true?

- A) The team broke the rule.
- B) The component was reviewed and approved by the lead interaction designer.
- C) Either the component was reviewed and approved, or the rule was broken.
- D) The component is badly designed.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The rule is a conditional with one permitted exception, and the facts do not tell you whether the exception applies. Option A assumes it does not; option B assumes it does; you have evidence for neither. Option D introduces a claim about quality that nothing supports — a component outside the Design System may be perfectly well made, and sometimes has to exist because no pattern covers the need. Only C is guaranteed in every version of events. Assessments reward this discipline of concluding only what the information guarantees, and so does the workplace: before suggesting a colleague skipped a step, the accurate and collegial move is to ask which route was used.

**Question 3 (easy) — Pattern recognition across observations**

You watch six usability sessions on the same form. Participants hesitated for more than ten seconds at:

- P1: the date of birth field
- P2: the date of birth field; the national insurance number field
- P3: the address field
- P4: the date of birth field
- P5: the date of birth field; the address field
- P6: the national insurance number field

Which field shows the clearest pattern of difficulty?

- A) Date of birth
- B) National insurance number
- C) Address
- D) No clear pattern

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Count the participants affected by each field rather than counting mentions. Date of birth affected P1, P2, P4 and P5 — four of six. National insurance number affected P2 and P6 — two. Address affected P3 and P5 — two. Four of six is clearly the strongest signal, so the answer is A. The counting discipline generalises: what matters is how many different people hit a problem, not how many times it appears in your notes. Note too that spotting the pattern is not the same as explaining it. Date of birth fields commonly cause hesitation because of format ambiguity, because they are split into three inputs, or because the user is unsure whose date of birth is being asked for — and each of those implies a different fix.

**Question 4 (easy) — Prioritisation in a design week**

It is Monday morning. Four things need attention:

1. A live page shows an error message that tells users their application has failed when it has actually been saved — people may be re-submitting today.
2. Your senior designer asked for wireframes for a new screen by Wednesday.
3. The team's design critique is at 11:00 and you agreed to bring your prototype.
4. A newsletter about the cross-government design community has arrived.

What should you do first?

- A) Start the wireframes while the morning is quiet.
- B) Raise or fix the misleading error message on the live page.
- C) Get the prototype ready for 11:00.
- D) Read the newsletter.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Prioritisation questions ask you to weigh impact and urgency together. Item 1 is both: an error message that tells people their application failed when it succeeded will cause duplicate submissions, wasted user effort and avoidable contact centre calls, today. That harm outranks everything else. As an associate you may not fix it yourself — the right first action may be to alert your senior designer or the content designer immediately — but either way it comes first. Item 3 is time-bound but takes minutes and is not due until 11:00. Item 2 has until Wednesday. Item 4 is the least urgent. The pattern to remember: harm to users outranks internal commitments, and internal commitments outrank nice-to-haves. Note also that this is a content problem inside an interaction — knowing whose problem it is matters less than making sure somebody owns it this morning.

**Question 5 (easy) — Error checking against instructions**

A senior designer asks you to make exactly three changes to a prototype: (i) split the "name and date of birth" page into two pages, (ii) change the "Continue" button on page 3 to "Save and continue", and (iii) add a back link to page 5. You get the updated prototype back from a colleague and find: the name and date of birth page has been split, page 3 now says "Save and continue", page 5 has a back link, and the whole prototype's font size has been increased. How many of the changes made were requested?

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Count carefully. Four changes were made, but the question asks how many were *requested*. Changes (i), (ii) and (iii) were all requested and all done — that is three. The font size change was not on the list, so it is an extra. The answer is 3. This mirrors an important habit: when you work after being given direction, you deliver exactly what was agreed and flag anything else rather than slipping it in. Unrequested changes are how inconsistencies creep into shared prototypes — and a font size change in particular may look like an improvement while quietly breaking the prototype's correspondence to the real service, which is the thing that makes testing valid.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Deduction about a component's behaviour**

A component's specification says: "Radio buttons must be used when the user may choose exactly one option. Checkboxes must be used when the user may choose any number of options, including none. Where the user must choose at least one option but may choose several, use checkboxes with a validation rule." A designer has used radio buttons for a question where the user may select several allergies from a list. Which must be true?

- A) Checkboxes should have been used.
- B) The design breaks the specification.
- C) Both A and B.
- D) The design is acceptable if the list is short.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The specification maps each situation to exactly one control. A user selecting several allergies may choose more than one option, so radio buttons — which permit exactly one — are the wrong control, and the specification's checkbox rule applies. So the design breaks the specification, and checkboxes should have been used; both A and B are true, making C correct. Option D introduces a condition about list length that the specification does not contain, which is the trap: control choice here is determined by how many options a user may select, not by how many exist. Note the practical consequence, which is worse than a style breach — radio buttons would make it impossible for someone with two allergies to answer truthfully, so the service would be collecting wrong data and the user would have no way to fix it.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Pattern recognition across devices**

A page is used on three device types. The percentage of users who complete it successfully is:

| Device | Users | Completed |
|---|---|---|
| Desktop | 4,000 | 3,600 |
| Tablet | 500 | 440 |
| Mobile | 5,500 | 3,300 |

Which device most clearly needs design attention, and why?

- A) Desktop, because it has the most users.
- B) Mobile, because its completion rate of 60% is far below the others and it also has the largest number of users failing.
- C) Tablet, because it has the fewest users.
- D) None; the overall rate is acceptable.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Convert to rates before comparing. Desktop completes 3,600 of 4,000, which is 90%. Tablet completes 440 of 500, which is 88%. Mobile completes 3,300 of 5,500, which is 60%. Mobile is dramatically worse, so the answer is B. Notice that mobile is unusual here in scoring worst on both measures at once — it has the lowest rate *and* loses the most users in absolute terms, 2,200 against desktop's 400. That combination makes it unambiguous. It is worth noticing because the two measures often disagree, and when they do you need both: a low rate on a small channel is an inclusion signal, while a moderate rate on a large one is a volume problem, and they call for different arguments.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Error checking an error message**

Your team's standard says an error message must: say what went wrong, say what to do about it, be in plain language, and appear both at the top of the page and next to the field. You review this message, shown only next to the field: "Error: invalid input in field DOB_1." How many parts of the standard does it break?

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Check each requirement. Does it say what went wrong? "Invalid input" is vague but arguably states that something is wrong with the input, so this requirement is met at a minimum level. Does it say what to do? No — the user is told nothing about how to fix it, so that fails. Is it plain language? "Invalid input in field DOB_1" is technical and exposes an internal field name, so that fails. Does it appear at the top of the page as well as by the field? No, only by the field, so that fails. Three requirements broken, giving C. A better message would read: "Enter your date of birth using numbers, for example 27 3 1985" — which tells the user precisely what to do, in words they use, and works whether they read it at the top of the page or next to the field. Note also why the top-of-page summary matters: someone using a screen reader may not encounter an inline message at all unless focus is moved deliberately.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Applied problem solving in a critique**

You are in a design critique. Two senior colleagues are debating the visual weight of a button, and the discussion has run for fifteen minutes. You noticed while preparing that the page asks users for information the service already holds. You are the most junior person present. What is the most sensible thing to do?

- A) Say nothing; the seniors will get to it.
- B) Wait for a pause and raise the observation as a question — "can I check something? I think we already hold the reference number we're asking for on this page" — bringing the room to a more consequential problem without criticising the discussion.
- C) Interrupt to say the button debate is a waste of time.
- D) Mention it to your senior designer afterwards.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** You have spotted something more consequential than the topic under discussion, and the useful contribution is the observation itself rather than a judgement about how the critique is being spent. Option B is phrased in the strongest available way for someone at your level: it waits for a natural pause, states a specific fact, and asks a question rather than mounting a challenge, which makes it easy for the room to engage without anyone needing to back down. Option A wastes the observation and lets a critique conclude on a page with a real defect in it. Option C is likely to be received badly and puts you in conflict with senior colleagues over a matter of taste. Option D is a reasonable backup and misses the moment when the page is actually being reviewed. Your role names explaining techniques for bringing together perspectives from multiple people, and knowing how to insert a fact into a room is the everyday version of that.

**Question 10 (moderate) — Deduction about what evidence supports**

In research with eight participants, six struggled to understand a "reference number" field because they were unsure which of two numbers on their letter to enter. Which conclusion is best supported by this evidence alone?

- A) 75% of users cannot find their reference number.
- B) The field should be removed.
- C) Six of eight participants were unsure which number to enter, which is a strong signal that the field's label and hint need to identify the number unambiguously — ideally by referring to where it appears on the letter.
- D) The letter is badly designed.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Six of eight is a strong qualitative signal, and option C states it at that strength while pointing at the mechanism the evidence actually identified: participants were not unable to find a number, they were unsure *which* number. That distinction matters, because it implies a labelling and hint fix rather than a different field or an autofill. Option A converts eight participants into a population rate, which is the commonest overreach in design reporting. Option B jumps to removing a field the service presumably needs. Option D may well be true and goes beyond what this evidence establishes — and it is worth noticing that the letter and the screen are two halves of one journey, so raising it is legitimate even though the evidence does not yet prove it. Framing ideas as testable hypotheses is a named skill in your role, and C is what a well-framed one looks like.

**Question 11 (harder) — Prioritisation with competing pressures**

Your senior designer is away. At 10:00 the following are true:

1. A developer needs your decision on how a validation error should behave before they can finish a story today.
2. The design critique is at 14:00 and your prototype is not ready.
3. A content designer needs confirmation of field labels by 12:00 to publish new guidance.
4. A colleague asked you to review their prototype this week.

What is the most sensible order?

- A) 2, 3, 1, 4 — your own commitments first.
- B) 1, 3, 2, 4 — unblock the developer, then the content designer, then your prototype, then the review.
- C) 3, 1, 2, 4 — deadlines in time order.
- D) 1, 2, 3, 4 — as listed.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Rank by consequence and deadline together. Item 1 blocks another person's work today and the decision itself is probably a five-minute conversation; leaving a developer to guess at validation behaviour is how inconsistent error handling gets built into a service. Item 3 has a hard 12:00 deadline and also blocks somebody. Item 2 is your own commitment with a 14:00 deadline, genuinely important, and within your control to absorb the earlier interruptions. Item 4 has a week. Option C is close and defensible, but it places a 12:00 task ahead of an item that is blocking someone right now and takes minutes. Option A ranks by ownership rather than consequence, which is the classic trap: your own deadlines feel most pressing precisely because they are yours.

**Question 12 (harder) — Applied problem solving about accessibility**

A designer proposes replacing a set of radio buttons with a custom dropdown that filters as you type, arguing it looks cleaner and is faster for people who know what they want. The list has six options and the service is used by a wide public audience. What is the strongest analysis?

- A) The dropdown is better because it is faster for people who know the answer.
- B) A custom filtering dropdown adds a control that must work with keyboard alone, with screen readers, with voice input and with high zoom — and a six-option list gains almost nothing from filtering, so the change swaps a simple, well-supported control for a complex one, adding accessibility risk and development cost for a marginal gain.
- C) The dropdown is fine if it is tested with screen reader users.
- D) Radio buttons should always be used.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The strongest analysis weighs both sides and names the mechanism. Filtering earns its complexity when a list is long enough that scanning it is genuinely burdensome — hundreds of countries, say. With six options, everything is visible at once and filtering saves nobody meaningful effort, while a custom component must be built and maintained to work with keyboard navigation, assistive technology, voice control and zoom, any of which can break in ways that are hard to detect and easy to ship. Option B captures the trade-off and lands on the right side of it. Option A weighs one user group's marginal convenience against another group's ability to use the service at all. Option C treats testing as a substitute for the design decision, and testing a complex custom control is expensive and does not remove the maintenance burden. Option D states a rule without reasoning, and would be wrong for a list of two hundred options. Your role asks you to explain different access needs and ways to meet them, and this question is that skill applied to a real trade-off.

### Preparation tips

- **Practise on real screens.** Take a page from your service and check it against the pattern it claims to follow. This is exactly the comparison muscle the assessment measures.
- **Slow down to speed up.** Trainees lose more marks to rushing than to not knowing. Read each rule twice before applying it.
- **Write the rules down.** For deduction questions, jot each condition as a short line. Getting rules out of your head frees your attention for reasoning.
- **Decide what you are counting.** Pages, rules, participants, mentions — these are different numbers, and the question always specifies one.
- **Check every option.** These items usually include one nearly-right distractor built from a specific error.
- **Move between scales deliberately.** Ask of any change: what does this do to the flow, and what does it do to the element?
- **Do a timed drill.** Ten minutes, four questions. You are practising composure, not speed.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Stopping at the first problem you find.** "How many" questions need a full count.
- **Counting mentions instead of participants.** One talkative person can invent a pattern.
- **Comparing raw counts across groups of different sizes.** Convert to rates first, every time.
- **Confusing "could be true" with "must be true".** Deduction rewards only conclusions guaranteed in every case.
- **Ranking by ownership rather than consequence.** Your own deadlines feel most urgent because they are yours.
- **Treating testing as a substitute for a design decision.** Testing tells you whether something works; it does not justify choosing the harder thing.
- **Adding complexity for a marginal gain.** Every custom control is a maintenance and accessibility liability.
- **Panicking when questions get harder.** On adaptive tests, rising difficulty usually means you are doing well.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A numeric reasoning assessment measures how well you work with the numbers your job produces. This is the section people worry about most, so let us be clear straight away: it is not a maths exam. There are no equations to solve and no formulae to memorise. Most platforms provide a calculator, and where they do not the arithmetic is deliberately simple. What is measured is whether you can read a table correctly, pick the right numbers out of a cluttered display, choose the right operation, and interpret the result sensibly.

The format is usually a timed online test of fifteen to thirty minutes, each item showing a small table, chart or paragraph of figures followed by a multiple-choice question. Time per item is tight, often around a minute, so the real skill is finding the relevant numbers quickly and ignoring the rest. Test designers routinely include figures you do not need, precisely to see whether you can tell the difference.

Some designers arrive here convinced they are "not a numbers person". That belief is worth challenging, because interaction design is full of numbers even though it is not a quantitative discipline. You look at how many people trigger a validation error on a field. You compare completion on mobile against desktop. You read how many users reach a page and how many leave it. You check whether a change made any difference. You work out how many people a decision affects. None of that requires advanced mathematics, and all of it requires numerical care, because getting it wrong sends a team to redesign the wrong screen for a fortnight.

At associate level the content is deliberately modest: percentages, fractions, proportions, simple averages, ratios, and reading values from tables. The techniques are equally modest. Work out what the question is actually asking before you look at the data. Identify the two or three numbers you need. Do the arithmetic one clear step at a time. Then sanity-check — if you have calculated that 3,300 out of 5,500 is 40%, a two-second check catches it.

A last note about the options. Wrong answers are almost never random. They are the results of specific, predictable mistakes: dividing by the wrong total, confusing a percentage with a percentage point, using the new figure instead of the old one. Finding your answer among the options is not proof you are right — and once you know the traps, you can often see them coming.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The numerical skills tested map onto your named skills:

- **Reading tables accurately** maps to **Evidence-based design**, where you identify common sources and types of data that inform user-centred design. Error rates, device splits and page analytics all arrive as tables.
- **Percentages and proportions** map to **Designing for everyone**, because comparing completion between devices or user groups is how exclusion becomes visible in numbers rather than only in stories.
- **Measuring change** maps to **Iterative design**, where you explain how iteration supports user-centred design. An iteration that has not been measured is a change, not an improvement.
- **Understanding scale** maps to **Designing strategically**, where aligning to your team's wider goals means knowing how many people a decision affects.
- **Interpreting figures cautiously** maps to **Design communication**, where you describe methods for explaining design decisions. A decision justified by a misread number is hard to defend twice.
- **Spotting an implausible figure** maps to **Evidence-based design**, where framing hypotheses well starts with trusting the right data.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Error rate on a field**

Of 2,000 users who reached a page, 500 triggered a validation error on the date field. What percentage triggered the error?

- A) 20%
- B) 25%
- C) 30%
- D) 40%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A percentage is the part divided by the whole, times 100: 500 ÷ 2,000 = 0.25, which is 25%, so B. Estimate first as insurance — 500 of 2,000 is a quarter, since 1,000 would be half. Option A would come from 500 ÷ 2,500, and option C from 600 ÷ 2,000, both the kind of slip a mis-keyed calculator produces under time pressure. Worth noting how you would report it: a quarter of everyone who reaches the page failing a single field is a substantial signal, and it usually points at a format or labelling problem rather than at users.

**Question 2 (easy) — Drop-off between pages**

A page is reached by 3,000 users and 2,400 continue past it. How many drop off, and what percentage of those reaching the page is that?

- A) 600 users, 20%
- B) 600 users, 25%
- C) 2,400 users, 80%
- D) 600 users, 24%

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The drop-off is 3,000 − 2,400 = 600. As a proportion of those who reached the page, that is 600 ÷ 3,000 = 0.2, or 20%, giving A. Option B is 600 ÷ 2,400, which uses the number who continued rather than the number who arrived — a denominator error and the most common mistake in page analytics. The rule worth fixing in memory: a drop-off rate is always measured against the number who reached that page, not the number who got past it. Reporting 25% instead of 20% describes the page as a quarter worse than it is, and if you are comparing two pages computed differently the comparison is simply meaningless.

**Question 3 (easy) — Reading a device table**

| Device | Users | Completed |
|---|---|---|
| Desktop | 5,000 | 4,250 |
| Mobile | 8,000 | 4,800 |
| Tablet | 1,000 | 850 |

Which device has a completion rate of exactly 60%?

- A) Desktop
- B) Mobile
- C) Tablet
- D) None

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Convert the target into what it would mean for each device, or work out each rate. Desktop is 4,250 ÷ 5,000 = 85%. Mobile is 4,800 ÷ 8,000 = 60%. Tablet is 850 ÷ 1,000 = 85%. So B. A faster route with round totals is to ask what 60% of each would be: 60% of 8,000 is 4,800, which matches immediately. Looking for the number rather than computing every rate saves real time under a clock. Note the design signal too: desktop and tablet perform identically at 85% while mobile sits 25 points below, which points at something specific to small screens rather than at the content.

**Question 4 (easy) — Average time on a task**

Five participants took 40, 55, 45, 60 and 50 seconds to complete a task. What was the mean?

- A) 48 seconds
- B) 50 seconds
- C) 52 seconds
- D) 55 seconds

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Add: 40 + 55 = 95, plus 45 = 140, plus 60 = 200, plus 50 = 250. Divide by 5: 250 ÷ 5 = 50 seconds, so B. A useful shortcut for numbers clustered around a round figure is to work from a guess: try 50, and the differences are −10, +5, −5, +10, 0, which sum to zero, confirming the mean is exactly 50. That uses much smaller numbers than long addition, which matters under time pressure when a single addition slip changes your answer.

**Question 5 (easy) — Scale of a decision**

A service receives 600,000 form submissions a year. A field you are considering removing is currently completed incorrectly by 3% of users, each of whom then has to correct it. How many corrections a year would removing the problem avoid?

- A) 1,800
- B) 18,000
- C) 20,000
- D) 180,000

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 3% of 600,000 is 0.03 × 600,000 = 18,000, giving B. Option A is 3% of 60,000 and option D is 30%. The reason this calculation belongs in a design guide is what it does to a conversation: "3% of users" sounds like a rounding error, and "18,000 people a year having to correct this field" does not. Converting a percentage into people takes five seconds and is one of the most effective moves available when arguing for a small fix that nobody thinks is worth the effort. It also gives you the next question — how long each correction takes, and what that is worth in total.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Comparing groups fairly**

Of 400 users on desktop, 340 completed a form. Of 250 users on mobile, 100 completed. Which statement is correct?

- A) Both groups completed at similar rates.
- B) The rate was 85% on desktop and 40% on mobile.
- C) Desktop completed 240 more forms, so the difference is modest.
- D) The groups cannot be compared because they are different sizes.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Convert each to a rate so the different group sizes stop distorting the comparison. Desktop: 340 ÷ 400 = 0.85, which is 85%. Mobile: 100 ÷ 250 = 0.4, which is 40%. Option B states both correctly. Option A is wrong once calculated. Option C compares raw counts across unequal groups, which is the error the question is built to catch. Option D gets it backwards — converting to rates is precisely how you compare unequal groups fairly. A 45 percentage point gap is enormous and would justify immediate investigation of what on small screens users cannot get past: a form control that is hard to operate with a thumb, a layout that pushes the error summary off-screen, or an input type that triggers the wrong keyboard.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Percentage change**

After a change to a field's hint text, weekly validation errors on it fell from 900 to 675. What is the percentage decrease?

- A) 22%
- B) 25%
- C) 33%
- D) 75%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Percentage change divides the difference by the original value. The difference is 900 − 675 = 225, and 225 ÷ 900 = 0.25, which is a 25% decrease, giving B. Option C is 225 ÷ 675, dividing by the new figure — the most common error in this question type, and one that overstates every improvement in the "decrease" direction just as it understates them in the other. Option D is the new figure as a percentage of the old, which tells you errors are now three quarters of what they were, a true statement answering a different question. The rule: for percentage change, the denominator is always where you started from.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Working back to a total**

A report says 15% of users abandon a service at the address page, and that this is 4,500 people a month. How many reach the address page each month?

- A) 5,175
- B) 6,750
- C) 30,000
- D) 67,500

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** If 15% equals 4,500, the total is 4,500 ÷ 0.15 = 30,000, giving C. Stepwise if preferred: 15% is 4,500, so 5% is 1,500, and 100% is 30,000. Verify by reversing — 15% of 30,000 is 4,500, which matches. Option A adds 15%, option B is 4,500 × 1.5, option D is 4,500 × 15. This calculation matters because colleagues quote percentages far more readily than base numbers, and 15% of 30,000 is a very different problem from 15% of 300. Being able to establish the base in ten seconds lets you judge whether a figure deserves the weight it is being given.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Ratio in device usage**

A service's traffic splits between mobile and desktop in a 3:2 ratio. It receives 45,000 visits a month. How many are on desktop?

- A) 15,000
- B) 18,000
- C) 22,500
- D) 27,000

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A 3:2 ratio divides the total into 3 + 2 = 5 shares. With 45,000 visits, each share is 45,000 ÷ 5 = 9,000. Mobile takes three shares, which is 27,000, and desktop takes two, which is 18,000. The question asks about desktop, so B. Option D answers the other half of the split, which is the trap for anyone who calculates correctly and then reads the final sentence carelessly. Write down what each share represents before calculating. The design relevance is direct: a service with 60% mobile traffic should not be designed desktop-first, and knowing the actual split rather than assuming it is how that argument gets settled.

**Question 10 (moderate) — Choosing the right denominator**

A journey shows: 12,000 users start, 9,600 pass the eligibility page, 7,200 reach the declaration, 6,480 submit. What percentage of users who reached the declaration went on to submit?

- A) 54%
- B) 68%
- C) 75%
- D) 90%

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Identify the group the question names before touching any figures. It asks about users who reached the declaration, so the whole is 7,200 and the part is 6,480: 6,480 ÷ 7,200 = 0.9, which is 90%, giving D. Option A uses 12,000, answering a whole-journey question nobody asked. This is the most common trap in funnel data and it has a real consequence: telling a team that 90% of people who reach the declaration submit is a completely different message from telling them 54% of all starters finish, and the two point at different screens. Underline the group the stem names, then calculate. Note also where the real losses are here: 2,400 lost at eligibility and 2,400 between eligibility and the declaration, against 720 at submission.

**Question 11 (harder) — Percentage points versus percentages**

Before a change, 40% of users completed a page without an error. Afterwards, 52% did. Which statement is accurate?

- A) Error-free completion rose by 12%.
- B) Error-free completion rose by 12 percentage points, which is a 30% increase.
- C) Error-free completion rose by 30 percentage points.
- D) Error-free completion rose by 23%.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The arithmetic gap between two percentages is measured in percentage points: 52 − 40 = 12 percentage points. The relative increase is a separate calculation: 12 ÷ 40 = 0.3, which is a 30% increase. Option B labels both correctly. Option A uses "12%" loosely, which is ambiguous and usually read as a relative change. Option C misapplies the percentage-point label. Option D divides by the new value. Why this matters beyond the test: "error-free completion rose 30%" and "rose 12 percentage points" describe the identical change, and the first sounds two and a half times more impressive. Being precise about which you mean is part of explaining a design decision honestly, which your role names explicitly.

**Question 12 (harder) — When an average hides something**

Seven users took the following times, in seconds, to find a link: 4, 5, 6, 6, 8, 9, and 91. A colleague reports "the average was 18 seconds". What is the best comment?

- A) The calculation is wrong; the average is 6 seconds.
- B) The calculation is right, but the mean is misleading: six of seven users found the link within nine seconds and one extreme case is pulling the figure up. Report the median of 6 seconds with the range, and describe the 91-second case separately.
- C) The figure is fine as reported.
- D) The 91-second case should be excluded as an anomaly.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Check the arithmetic first: the values sum to 129, and 129 ÷ 7 = 18.4, so about 18 is correct and option A is wrong. But no user took anywhere near 18 seconds — six finished within nine seconds, one took 91. The median, the fourth value in the ordered list, is 6 seconds, which describes the group far better. Option B says this and proposes the right fix. Option C lets a misleading summary stand. Option D is the most damaging answer: excluding an inconvenient data point is a serious failing, and that 91-second session is almost certainly the most interesting thing in the dataset — something about the page defeated that person completely, and finding out what is the entire point of looking. Report the distribution, and treat the outlier as a lead.

### Preparation tips

- **Drill four operations only.** Percentages, percentage change, averages and ratios cover nearly everything at this level.
- **Estimate before you calculate.** Three seconds of "about a quarter" catches most serious errors.
- **Find the denominator first.** Underline the group the question names.
- **Measure drop-off against the number who arrived.** Not the number who continued.
- **Convert percentages into people.** "3% of users" and "18,000 people a year" are the same fact and only one changes a decision.
- **Learn the percentage point distinction properly.** Write yourself one sentence and re-read it before any assessment.
- **Practise on your own service's numbers.** Calculate the error rate on a real field, and the drop-off at each page.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Dividing by the wrong total.** Funnel questions are built around this single error.
- **Confusing percentages with percentage points.** Both are correct labels for different things.
- **Comparing raw counts across groups of different sizes.** Convert to rates first.
- **Trusting an answer because it appears in the options.** Distractors are built from candidates' mistakes.
- **Rounding too early.** Carry a decimal and round at the end.
- **Reporting a mean without checking the spread.** Glance at the range first.
- **Leaving a percentage as a percentage.** People do not feel percentages; they feel people.
- **Assuming you are bad at this.** These are learnable techniques, not a talent.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately you read. That sounds too simple to test, but it is one of the strongest predictors of performance in roles that run on documents — and interaction design is such a role. You read design standards, accessibility criteria, component specifications, content guidance, policy summaries and the words users say. Getting any of those slightly wrong changes what you build.

The classic format presents a short passage — typically three to six sentences of dense workplace prose — followed by statements you must classify as True, False or Cannot Say. True means the statement follows necessarily. False means the passage contradicts it. Cannot Say means the passage neither confirms nor contradicts it, so answering would need information you have not been given. Alongside these you will meet comprehension questions, main-point questions, and questions asking which of four summaries is most accurate.

The golden rule is that you answer only from the passage. Everything you know about accessibility regulations, everything the Design System says, everything your team does — set it aside. If the passage contradicts what you believe, the passage still wins. This feels unnatural and it is the biggest single source of lost marks. Candidates read a statement, recognise it as true of the world, and mark it True even though the passage never said it. That is a Cannot Say.

Cannot Say deserves particular attention because people avoid it. It feels like an admission of defeat, so candidates reach for True or False to seem decisive. In reality Cannot Say is very often correct, and choosing it confidently is a mark of good reading. Whenever you find yourself supplying a link the passage does not make, you have found a Cannot Say.

Watch the small words, because they carry the meaning. "All" is not "most". "May" is not "must". "Should" is not "will". "Where practicable" and "unless" flag conditions that govern everything around them. Test writers build wrong answers almost entirely from swaps of exactly these words, so a statement reading almost identically to the passage may still be False.

For an interaction designer there is a specific payoff. Accessibility criteria and component specifications are written with unusual precision, and a great deal of poor practice comes from remembering the gist rather than the wording. Reading them exactly is how you avoid both over-compliance, which wastes effort, and under-compliance, which excludes people.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The verbal skills tested map onto your named skills:

- **Reading precisely** maps to **Designing for everyone**, where you explain the importance of making services accessible and describe different access needs and ways to meet them. Accessibility requirements are written documents.
- **Distinguishing statement from inference** maps to **Evidence-based design**, where you frame ideas as hypotheses to be tested.
- **Identifying the main point** maps to **Design communication**, where you describe methods for explaining design decisions.
- **Understanding qualified language** maps to **Designing strategically**, where you explain the value of patterns and components — and where knowing whether a pattern is mandatory or recommended tells you how much room you have.
- **Reading for what is absent** maps to **Designing for everyone**, where noticing that a specification never mentions keyboard operation has a direct consequence.
- **Following written direction exactly** maps to **Designing together**, where constructive feedback only works if it is understood as given.

### Practice questions

*Passage A — for Questions 1 to 4*

"All new public-facing services must use components from the Design System where a suitable component exists. Where no suitable component exists, teams may create a new one, provided it is documented and shared with the cross-government community. New components must be tested with assistive technology before use in a live service. Teams are encouraged, but not required, to contribute new components back to the Design System itself."

**Question 1 (easy)** — Statement: "New components must be tested with assistive technology before live use." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The third sentence states this directly, so it follows necessarily and is True. This is the most straightforward item type, and the right move is to answer fast and confidently to bank time for harder items. Resist hunting for a trick that is not there — candidates lose marks by over-thinking easy items just as often as by rushing hard ones.

**Question 2 (easy)** — Statement: "Teams must contribute new components back to the Design System." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The passage says teams are *encouraged, but not required*, to contribute components back. The statement's "must" contradicts that explicitly, so it is False. Note that the passage does two things at once here — it establishes a hard requirement to document and share, and a soft encouragement to contribute — and those are different obligations to different destinations. A reader who conflates "shared with the community" and "contributed to the Design System" will get this wrong, and would also get it wrong in practice by promising a contribution nobody asked for.

**Question 3 (moderate)** — Statement: "A team that creates a new component without documenting it has breached the requirements." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The passage permits creating a new component *provided it is documented and shared*. Those are conditions on the permission, and failing one means the permission does not apply, so creating an undocumented component breaches the requirement. The statement follows and is True. Note the contrast with Question 2: there the passage explicitly removed an obligation, giving False; here it attached conditions to a permission, and failing a condition is a breach. Distinguishing an explicit exemption from a conditional permission is one of the more useful habits this section builds.

**Question 4 (moderate)** — Statement: "A service may use a component that is not in the Design System." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The passage requires Design System components only *where a suitable component exists*, and expressly permits creating a new one where none does, subject to conditions. So a service may indeed use a non-Design-System component in the circumstances described, and the statement — which claims only that this is possible — is supported. True. Candidates sometimes mark this False by reading the first sentence in isolation as a blanket requirement, which is exactly the error the passage's structure is testing. Read to the end of a provision before concluding what it requires.

*Passage B — for Questions 5 to 8*

"The review of the application form found that users on mobile devices completed more slowly than users on desktop and were more likely to abandon at the upload step. The review notes that mobile users differed from desktop users in several respects, including age and whether they were completing the form at home. The review did not assess the quality of completed applications. It recommends that the team consider whether a redesigned upload step would reduce mobile abandonment."

**Question 5 (easy)** — Statement: "Mobile users were more likely to abandon at the upload step than desktop users." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The first sentence states this directly, and the statement restates it without addition. True. Note the passage's careful comparative framing — "more likely to abandon" rather than "usually abandon" — and the statement respects it. A statement claiming "mobile users usually abandon at the upload step" would be a much stronger claim than the passage supports and would be a Cannot Say.

**Question 6 (moderate)** — Statement: "The mobile interface causes users to abandon at the upload step." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The passage reports an association and then explicitly flags that mobile and desktop users differed in age and in whether they were completing the form at home. Either could explain the difference — someone completing a form away from home is less likely to have their documents with them, which would produce abandonment at an upload step regardless of interface quality. The passage neither asserts nor rules out an interface cause, so Cannot Say. The design consequence is direct: if the cause is the interface you redesign it, and if the cause is that mobile users do not have their documents to hand you design something else entirely, such as saving and returning or explaining what is needed before the journey starts.

**Question 7 (moderate)** — Statement: "The redesigned upload step will reduce mobile abandonment." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** Two things stand between the passage and the statement. The review recommends only that the team *consider whether* a redesign would reduce abandonment, which is a recommendation to evaluate rather than a prediction. And no redesign has happened, so nothing establishes its effect. Cannot Say. "Consider whether" is a phrase worth learning to spot: it appears constantly in real review documents and is very frequently reported afterwards as a firm recommendation to act, which is how teams end up delivering things nobody decided to do.

**Question 8 (harder)** — Statement: "Applications submitted on mobile are of lower quality." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The passage says explicitly that the review did not assess the quality of completed applications. Declared silence establishes nothing in either direction, so Cannot Say. Candidates sometimes reason that slower completion and more abandonment imply worse applications, which is the inference this item is built to catch — and it could run the other way, since users who persevere through a slower journey may produce more considered answers. A review that did not look cannot be evidence about what it did not look at, and treating its silence as reassurance is a common organisational error as well as a test error.

*Passage C — for Questions 9 to 12*

"Interactive components must be operable using a keyboard alone. Where a component uses a non-standard interaction, the team must provide a documented keyboard alternative. Focus order must follow the visual reading order of the page. Where a component displays an error, the error must be programmatically associated with the field it relates to; a visual association alone is not sufficient."

**Question 9 (easy)** — Statement: "Interactive components must work with a keyboard alone." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The first sentence states this directly. True. Answer quickly and move on. This is worth internalising beyond the test, because keyboard operability is the requirement most often broken by custom components — a control built from generic elements with click handlers will typically work perfectly with a mouse and not at all with a keyboard, and the failure is invisible unless somebody deliberately checks.

**Question 10 (moderate)** — Statement: "A visual association between an error and its field is sufficient." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The final clause states that a visual association alone is not sufficient; the error must be programmatically associated with its field. The statement contradicts this directly, so False. The reason the passage bothers to say it is worth knowing: an error message placed next to a field is obvious to a sighted user and may be entirely invisible to someone using a screen reader, who encounters fields and messages in whatever order the underlying markup dictates. The word "programmatically" is doing all the work in that sentence, and skimming past it produces exactly the failure the requirement exists to prevent.

**Question 11 (harder)** — Statement: "A component with a non-standard interaction breaches the requirements." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The passage does not prohibit non-standard interactions. It requires that where one is used, the team provides a documented keyboard alternative. So a non-standard interaction with a documented keyboard alternative complies, and the statement — which asserts a breach from the non-standard interaction alone — is contradicted. False. This is a good example of a standard setting a condition rather than a prohibition, and reading it as a prohibition produces two bad outcomes: teams either avoid legitimate design solutions unnecessarily, or conclude the rule is unrealistic and ignore it entirely.

**Question 12 (harder) — Main point**

Which best captures the main point of Passage C?

- A) Components must never use non-standard interactions.
- B) Components must be operable and comprehensible without a mouse or sight — through keyboard operation, a focus order matching the visual order, documented alternatives where interactions are unusual, and errors associated in code rather than only visually.
- C) Focus order must follow visual reading order.
- D) Errors must be programmatically associated with their fields.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The main point must cover the whole passage without adding anything. Option A overstates a conditional requirement into a prohibition the passage does not make. Options C and D are each true of one sentence and silent on the rest, making them details rather than main points. Option B captures all four requirements and the logic joining them: each one exists so that the component works for someone who is not using a mouse or not seeing the screen. Main-point questions reward the option broad enough to cover everything and disciplined enough to invent nothing, and three of these four are true details promoted too far.

### Preparation tips

- **Answer only from the passage.** Before marking anything True, point at the words that make it true.
- **Trust Cannot Say.** If you are supplying a link, that is your signal.
- **Circle the qualifiers.** All, some, most, may, must, should, encouraged, unless, provided that, where practicable.
- **Distinguish an exemption from a conditional permission.** "Not required" gives False; "provided that" makes failure a breach.
- **Read to the end of a provision.** A blanket-sounding first sentence is frequently qualified two lines later.
- **Treat declared silence as silence.** A review that did not examine something has told you nothing about it.
- **Practise on real standards.** Take a Design System page or an accessibility criterion and write three statements — one True, one False, one Cannot Say.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Answering from world knowledge.** The most frequent cause of lost marks by a distance.
- **Avoiding Cannot Say.** It converts good reading into wrong answers.
- **Reading "encouraged" as "required".** Standards put soft aspirations next to hard rules constantly.
- **Reading a condition as a prohibition.** "Where X, do Y" permits X; it does not ban it.
- **Skimming a load-bearing adverb.** "Programmatically" changes the whole requirement.
- **Treating association as causation.** A passage naming confounds is warning you off.
- **Reading "consider whether X" as "do X".** It appears constantly and is constantly misreported.
- **Confusing a true detail with the main point.** Main-point items punish accurate but partial options.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment presents realistic workplace scenarios and asks what you would do. There is no passage to decode and no arithmetic. Instead you are shown a dilemma of the kind your role produces, given four or five possible responses, and asked to identify the most effective and often the least effective one. Some versions ask you to rank all the options; others ask you to rate each on a scale from counterproductive to highly effective.

This section measures judgement rather than knowledge, and judgement in a specific sense: whether your instincts align with what the profession considers good practice at your level. That last phrase is the whole calibration. The same scenario has different best answers for a trainee and for a head of profession. An associate interaction designer who overrules a developer is overstepping; one who never raises anything is under-performing. Answer as the person your role description says you are — a trainee working under supervision, showing aptitude and potential, expected to notice things and to say so.

Four principles carry most scenarios here.

Users come first, and in interaction design that specifically means the users who cannot get past something. When an option protects somebody who cannot operate a control, read an error or complete a step, it will almost always outrank an option protecting a schedule, a budget or somebody's preference. An interaction that excludes one group is not a slightly worse interaction; for those people it is no interaction at all.

Act within your remit and be transparent at its edges. At associate level the best answers usually involve doing what you can competently do and promptly telling the right person about what you cannot. Neither silent struggling nor reflexive escalation of everything scores well. The pattern is: attempt, then tell.

Address problems directly and early, with the person involved. Options involving an honest private conversation beat options involving public criticism, silence, or complaining to someone else.

Separate the element from the flow. This is the distinctively interaction-design signature of a strong answer: when one option fixes the control in front of you and another asks whether the control should be there at all, or what happens to someone who gets it wrong, the second is usually stronger.

One trap to name. Some options are attractive because they are pleasant — agreeing, smoothing over, being maximally helpful. Others are attractive because they are decisive — taking charge, sorting it out yourself. Neither pleasantness nor decisiveness is the criterion. The criterion is what produces the best outcome for users, for the design and for the team, given your experience.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The judgement dimensions map onto your named skills:

- **Protecting people who cannot get past something** maps to **Designing for everyone**, where you explain the importance of inclusive and accessible services, describe different access needs, and describe how privilege and power influence design.
- **Explaining a design position** maps to **Design communication**, where you explain the importance of articulating design decisions and describe methods for doing so.
- **Working with others' perspectives** maps to **Designing together**, where you explain techniques for bringing together multiple perspectives and the importance of constructive feedback.
- **Using evidence honestly** maps to **Evidence-based design**, where you explain how research and analytics inform design and frame ideas as hypotheses.
- **Working in cycles** maps to **Iterative design**, where you describe agile methodologies and explain how iteration supports user-centred design.
- **Understanding where design sits** maps to **Leading design**, where you explain the role of user-centred design in an organisation and identify the leaders designers need to work with, and to **Designing strategically**.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — You do not know how to do the task**

Your senior designer asks you to "prototype the address lookup journey, including the manual entry fallback, by Thursday". You have built simple prototypes but never a branching one. What is the most effective response?

- A) Say yes, then work it out alone from tutorials.
- B) Say yes, and add that you have not built a branching prototype before and would find half an hour of guidance really useful before you start.
- C) Say you cannot do it because you have not been trained.
- D) Say yes, and quietly ask another associate to build it with you rather than telling your senior.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** Your role description says explicitly that you work under supervision and need to develop your skills, so asking for guidance is doing your job correctly. Option B combines willingness with honesty and makes a specific, small, easy-to-grant request — half an hour is far cheaper for your senior than reviewing a misconceived prototype on Wednesday night. Option A risks producing something unusable and hides the gap so it never gets filled. Option C is least effective because it declines a development opportunity and hands the work back without offering anything. Option D avoids awkwardness but leaves your senior with a false picture of your skills. The general principle for this whole section: at your level, "yes, and here is what I need to do it well" is almost always the strongest available answer.

**Question 2 (easy) — Something you notice in an existing design**

Reviewing a live page, you notice the error summary at the top of the page does not link to the fields it describes, so a keyboard user has to tab through the whole page to reach the problem. You are the most junior person in the team. What should you do?

- A) Say nothing; the page has been live for months and someone would have noticed.
- B) Raise it with your senior designer, describing exactly what you found and what a keyboard user has to do as a result.
- C) Raise a bug ticket yourself without telling anyone.
- D) Note it for the next retrospective.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** You have found a real accessibility defect, and describing the consequence — a keyboard user tabbing through a whole page to reach an error — is what makes it actionable rather than abstract. Option B raises it through the right channel with the right detail. Option A is least effective: "someone would have noticed" is how defects survive for years, and being new is precisely why you can see it. Option C is not wrong in every team, and raising a ticket without telling your senior means nobody with design authority can judge its priority or spot that the same defect probably exists on twenty other pages. Option D delays a two-minute conversation by weeks for a live problem. Note the instinct worth building: when you find one instance of a pattern-level defect, ask whether the pattern is wrong everywhere.

**Question 3 (easy) — Conflicting instructions**

Your senior designer asked you to finish the flow diagram this morning. The delivery manager then asks you to spend the morning updating the prototype for tomorrow's show and tell, calling it urgent. What is the most effective response?

- A) Do what the delivery manager asked, because they are more senior.
- B) Do what your senior designer asked and tell the delivery manager you are busy.
- C) Tell the delivery manager about the existing task, offer a specific workable option, and ask the two of them to confirm the priority.
- D) Try to do both by working through lunch.

**Correct answer: C. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Two legitimate instructions have collided and you cannot serve both. Resolving it is not yours to impose, but surfacing it promptly and helpfully is. What lifts option C above bare escalation is the offer of a specific option — "I could update the prototype this morning and finish the flow diagram after lunch, if that works" — which turns you from a bottleneck into part of the solution. Option A ranks by seniority; the delivery manager probably does not know about the flow diagram. Option B is abrupt and leaves a real need unmet. Option D is least effective, because working through lunch hides the conflict, sets an unsustainable expectation, and means nobody learns the team is over-committing you.

**Question 4 (easy) — A developer proposes a shortcut**

A developer says implementing the agreed error handling will take two extra days, and proposes instead showing a single generic message at the top of the page saying "There is a problem with your answers". What is the most effective response?

- A) Agree; two days is a lot and the message still tells users something.
- B) Explain what the generic message costs users — they have to find the problem themselves, and someone using a screen reader may not be able to at all — and ask whether there is a middle option, such as specific messages for the two fields that fail most often, that fits the time available.
- C) Insist on the full agreed behaviour.
- D) Escalate to your senior designer without talking to the developer.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** The developer has a real constraint and has proposed a solution that solves their problem by transferring the cost to users. Option B does three useful things: it makes the user cost concrete rather than appealing to a standard, it treats the time constraint as legitimate, and it proposes a middle path, which is what usually gets agreed in practice. Naming the screen reader consequence specifically matters, because "there is a problem with your answers" and no indication of where is close to unusable without sight. Option A is least effective, accepting a change that will generate confusion and repeat submissions to save two days once. Option C may be right in the end and skips the collaborative step; you also may not have the standing to insist. Option D bypasses a colleague who has not had a chance to discuss it, though telling your senior afterwards is sensible.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Evidence that contradicts your design**

You designed a page and, in testing, four of six participants clicked the secondary link instead of the primary button. You believe the design is right and the participants were unusual. What is the most effective response?

- A) Note the participants were unrepresentative and keep the design.
- B) Treat the result as a signal about the design rather than about the participants, look at what drew people to the link — wording, position, visual weight — and propose a change to test in the next round.
- C) Remove the secondary link.
- D) Ask for more participants before deciding.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Four of six people doing the same unexpected thing is a signal, not a coincidence, and the first move is to ask what in the design produced it rather than what was wrong with them. Option B does that and keeps it testable, which is exactly what your role means by framing ideas as hypotheses. Option A is least effective: dismissing consistent behaviour as unrepresentative is the single most reliable way for a designer to stop learning, and it is what "designers are not users" is meant to guard against. Option C acts on a diagnosis you have not made — the link may be essential, and the problem may be that the button is visually weak rather than that the link exists. Option D is not unreasonable and delays a cheap improvement; six participants converging is enough to justify trying something different in the next round.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Pressure to reuse a pattern badly**

A stakeholder asks you to use the standard "check your answers" pattern for a page that is not a summary of answers, but a list of documents the user must go and obtain. They say reusing the pattern will be quicker and look consistent. What should you do?

- A) Use the pattern; consistency matters and it is quicker.
- B) Explain that the pattern sets an expectation — that everything shown has already been done and can be changed — which is wrong here and will confuse users, and propose a different pattern that fits "things you still need to do", while keeping the same visual style so the service still looks consistent.
- C) Refuse and design something from scratch.
- D) Use the pattern but change its heading.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** The stakeholder's underlying goals — speed and consistency — are legitimate, and the proposal misunderstands what a pattern is. A pattern is not a layout; it is a set of expectations users have learned, and "check your answers" teaches people that what they are seeing is complete and editable. Applying it to a list of outstanding tasks tells users the opposite of the truth. Option B holds the substance and addresses both of the stakeholder's goals directly, which is what makes it persuasive rather than obstructive. Option D is least effective: changing the heading while keeping a pattern whose meaning is wrong is the worst of both, because the visual language still says "done" while the words say "to do", and users trust the layout. Option A ships a misleading page. Option C discards the reuse that would genuinely help.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Feedback that stings**

In a critique, a senior designer says your flow is "confusing and doesn't show what happens when someone answers no". You think it does. What is the most effective response?

- A) Point out where the "no" branch appears.
- B) Ask them to show you where they got lost, since if an experienced designer cannot follow the "no" branch then the flow is not communicating it — then fix that.
- C) Accept the criticism and redo the flow.
- D) Ask for the feedback in writing to consider later.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Both things can be true: the branch is on the diagram, and the diagram does not communicate it. Option B recognises that and converts feedback into information, which is far more useful than establishing who was right, and it makes senior colleagues willing to review your work again. Option A is least effective because winning the argument leaves the flow exactly as confusing as it was and teaches the room that giving you feedback is effortful. Option C accepts the verdict without extracting the detail, so you may redo it and reproduce the same problem. Option D is not unreasonable and defers a two-minute conversation. Your role names the importance of constructive feedback in the design process, and receiving it well is at least half of that skill.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Something that is not your job**

You notice that the letter the service sends after submission uses completely different wording from the confirmation screen you designed, and users are likely to think they are about two different things. Letters are handled by another team. What is the most effective response?

- A) Ignore it; letters are not your responsibility.
- B) Raise it with your senior designer, describing the mismatch and why it matters, and ask whether there is a route to the team that owns the letter.
- C) Contact the other team and ask them to change the letter.
- D) Change your confirmation screen to match the letter.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A user does not experience your screen and their letter as two products; they experience one service saying two different things, which produces exactly the uncertainty that generates phone calls. Option B evidences the problem and seeks a legitimate route through someone with the standing to use it — which is the right shape at your level, because you probably do not know what conversations already exist between the two teams. Option A is least effective, because "not my responsibility" describes an org chart rather than a user's experience. Option C acts beyond your remit and risks cutting across relationships you cannot see. Option D changes the half you control to match the half that may itself be wrong, without anyone establishing which wording is right.

**Question 9 (harder) — An accessibility problem found late**

Ten days before launch you find that a custom component cannot be operated with a keyboard. Fixing it will delay launch. What is the most effective response?

- A) Launch and add it to the backlog.
- B) Raise it immediately with your senior designer and delivery manager, describing precisely what cannot be done and by whom, and ask for a decision — while checking whether a standard component could replace the custom one quickly.
- C) Try to fix the component yourself in the ten days.
- D) Launch and note the gap in an accessibility statement.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A component that cannot be operated by keyboard means some people cannot use the service at all, and weighing a delay against an exclusion is a decision for people with the authority to make it — though raising it is entirely yours. Option B does everything within your remit: it surfaces the problem while there is time, describes the impact precisely, and adds the practical step that most often resolves this situation, which is checking whether a standard component can replace the custom one. That check is frequently the fastest fix available and nobody thinks of it under pressure. Option A is least effective, because "add it to the backlog" is where accessibility problems go to remain unfixed. Option D treats an accessibility statement as a way to launch with a known blocker, which it is not. Option C risks ten days and leaves nobody with authority aware.

**Question 10 (harder) — Being asked to design around a constraint you doubt**

A developer says a field cannot accept spaces in a postcode "because of the database". Research shows many users type postcodes with a space and then see an error. What is the most effective response?

- A) Add a hint telling users not to use a space.
- B) Ask what the constraint actually is and whether the space could be stripped when the value is saved rather than rejected when it is typed — since the requirement is presumably about what is stored, not about what the user types.
- C) Insist the field accepts spaces.
- D) Escalate to your senior designer without asking the developer.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** "The database won't allow it" is sometimes exactly true, sometimes a summary of a storage requirement that could be satisfied differently, and sometimes an inherited assumption nobody has revisited. Option B asks the productive question and, crucially, proposes the specific mechanism that resolves most versions of this problem: normalising input on save rather than validating it on entry. That distinction between what a user types and what a system stores is one of the most useful things an interaction designer can carry into conversations with developers. Option A is least effective: it makes the user responsible for accommodating an internal constraint, and hint text telling people not to do the natural thing is reliably ignored, so the errors continue and now the page is longer too. Option C asserts without understanding. Option D bypasses a colleague who has not had a chance to explain.

**Question 11 (harder) — A mistake of your own**

You realise a prototype you shared a week ago, which the team has been building from, shows a page order that does not match what was agreed. What should you do?

- A) Correct it quietly and say nothing.
- B) Tell the team straight away, explain exactly what was wrong, and flag which work in the last week may have been built against the wrong order.
- C) Correct it and mention it at the next stand-up.
- D) Wait to see whether anyone noticed.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** A shared artefact has been wrong for a week and people have built against it, so the correction alone is not enough — the important part is the last clause of option B, flagging work that may need revisiting. That is what turns an admission into something useful, and it is the step people most often skip because it is the most uncomfortable. Option D is least effective: waiting means the error persists and, if it emerges later, the question becomes why you did not say so. Option A corrects the artefact and leaves the downstream consequences unexamined. Option C delays disclosure to a scheduled meeting when the information is needed now. Everyone makes mistakes like this; what distinguishes people is what they do in the next ten minutes.

**Question 12 (harder) — An assumption in the room**

In a session, someone says "we don't need to worry about screen reader users on this page — hardly anyone uses one". You know the service has around 800,000 users a year. What is the most effective response?

- A) Let it go; the team has decided the direction.
- B) Bring the arithmetic into the room — even a very small percentage of 800,000 users is thousands of people a year — and ask what the team thinks should happen to them, so the decision is made knowingly rather than by assumption.
- C) Argue that all services must be accessible to everyone.
- D) Raise it privately with your senior designer afterwards.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** The comment rests on an intuition about proportion, and intuitions about proportion collapse instantly against a base number. Even one per cent of 800,000 is 8,000 people a year, and the actual figure is likely higher once you include people using screen magnification, voice control and keyboard-only navigation for temporary or situational reasons. Option B supplies the arithmetic and then asks a question, which is both the most effective form of challenge available to a junior person and genuinely the right thing to do, since the team may have an answer. Option A is least effective, because a decision made on an assumption that is numerically wrong will exclude real people and you had the number. Option C states a true principle in a way that invites a debate about principles, which rarely changes a room. Option D is a reasonable backup and misses the moment.

### Preparation tips

- **Answer as a trainee, not as a hero or a bystander.** Competent action plus prompt, honest communication upwards.
- **Put the excluded user first.** When an option protects somebody who cannot operate a control, start by assuming it is strongest.
- **Separate the element from the flow.** If one option fixes the control and another asks whether it should be there, the second is usually stronger.
- **Bring the number, not the principle.** Converting a percentage into people changes rooms; principles invite debate.
- **Ask what a constraint actually is.** "The database won't allow it" is a question, not an answer.
- **Offer a middle option.** When a colleague proposes a shortcut, the strongest response usually finds a cheaper way to protect the user.
- **Read the least-effective question separately.** The worst option is usually the one that conceals something or transfers cost to users.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Ranking by seniority.** Who is asking matters far less than the consequences.
- **Silence out of newness.** "Someone would have noticed" is how defects survive for years.
- **Dismissing consistent behaviour as unrepresentative participants.** Four of six doing the same thing is a signal about your design.
- **Accepting a shortcut that transfers cost to users.** Two saved days against months of confusion is a bad trade.
- **Keeping a pattern whose meaning is wrong.** Users trust the layout more than the words.
- **Making users accommodate an internal constraint.** Normalise on save rather than rejecting on entry.
- **Treating an accessibility statement as a way to launch with a blocker.** It is for gaps you are genuinely closing.
- **Delaying disclosure of your own error.** Speed is what turns a mistake into evidence of integrity.

## Conclusion

You have reached the end, and that is worth a moment. You have just worked through forty-eight practice questions across four assessment types and read the reasoning behind every one. That is a substantial piece of preparation and considerably more than most people do.

Look back at what you covered. In the cognitive section you checked pages against a pattern, worked through rules with exceptions, counted participants rather than mentions, prioritised a morning where a misleading live error outranked your own deadlines, and weighed a custom filtering dropdown against six radio buttons and found it wanting. In the numeric section you handled error rates, drop-off denominators, ratios, percentage change and funnel data — and, most usefully for a designer, you learned to convert a percentage into people, which is how a 3% problem becomes 18,000 corrections a year and finally gets fixed. In the verbal section you practised reading exactly what is written: telling an explicit exemption from a conditional permission, refusing to read "encouraged" as "required", and noticing that "programmatically" was carrying the entire weight of a requirement. And in the situational judgement section you thought through raising an accessibility defect as the most junior person in the room, finding a middle path with a developer under time pressure, asking what a database constraint actually was, and bringing arithmetic to an assumption nobody had examined.

Notice how little of that was really about tests. Every one is a working skill. A designer who counts properly, reads the standard as written, moves between the element and the flow, brings the number rather than the principle, and says so within ten minutes of finding their own mistake — that is simply a good designer. Preparing for these assessments and getting better at the job turn out to be very nearly the same activity, which is the most encouraging thing about the whole exercise.

A word about where you are. You are an associate interaction designer: a trainee, working under supervision, expected to show aptitude, potential and an understanding of the role. Please read that as the invitation it is. Nobody expects the judgement of someone with a decade behind them. What they are looking for is care, curiosity, honesty about what you do not yet know, and steady improvement — all choices available to you today. And remember the advantage you currently have: you have not yet learned to overlook the small awkwardnesses everyone else has stopped seeing.

If you want to keep going from here, a few things will help more than rereading. Unplug your mouse for an hour and try to complete your own service using only the keyboard; you will find things no review would have surfaced. Turn on a screen reader and listen to one of your pages, badly and slowly, until you understand what an error summary is actually for. Read the Design System's guidance for a component you use often, all the way through, including the "when not to use this" section. Sit with a developer and ask how a validation rule is actually implemented. And practise stating findings out loud at the right strength — "six of eight participants", not "most users" — until the accurate version is the one that comes naturally.

Finally, for the day itself. An assessment is a snapshot taken on one particular morning. It is not a verdict on your ability, your potential or your future in this profession. The designers whose work you admire have all had assessments that went badly. What actually determines how far you go is the quality of your attention to the people who use these services and your willingness to keep learning — and someone who has read this far clearly has plenty of both.

Good luck. Go carefully, ask well, and keep unplugging the mouse.
