## Introduction

Welcome! This guide has been written just for you, as a trainee business analyst working within the UK Government Digital and Data profession. Starting out in a new profession can feel like a lot to take in, so let us say this straight away: you do not need to be an expert to use this guide. Everything here is designed for someone at your level — someone who is learning the craft of business analysis while working with others and under the supervision of more senior business analysts.

So, what is this document about? It is a practice and preparation guide for psychometric assessments. Psychometric assessments are structured exercises that measure the thinking skills and workplace judgement that help people succeed in a role. You might meet them when you apply for a new post, during an apprenticeship or development scheme, or as part of a learning programme. They sound intimidating, but they are really just a series of short questions — and like any skill, answering them well is something you can practise and improve.

The assessments in this guide are job-specific. That means the questions are not abstract puzzles about shapes or made-up words. Instead, they use the real materials of your working life as a trainee business analyst: user stories, simple process maps, requirements lists, stakeholder emails, sprint boards, survey results, and test scripts. When you practise with this guide, you are practising two things at once — assessment technique and the everyday thinking of your actual job. That is a very good use of your time.

Why are these assessments relevant to your role in particular? As a trainee, you support the analysis of business problems, help capture and check requirements, contribute to process improvement work, and learn how delivery methodologies like scrum and kanban shape the way teams work. All of that depends on core mental skills: spotting patterns and errors, reasoning step by step, working carefully with numbers, reading documents closely, and making sensible judgements about what to do in tricky situations. Those are exactly the four areas this guide covers.

The document is organised into four main assessment sections:

1. A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment — thinking, logic, and error checking.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures how well you think through everyday work problems: spotting patterns, following logical rules, finding errors, deciding what to do first, and solving small practical puzzles. Unlike a general intelligence test full of abstract shapes, a job-specific version uses materials you would genuinely meet as a trainee business analyst — a requirements list with a numbering system, a set of acceptance criteria, a simple process map, or a kanban board.

The typical format is an online, timed test lasting between 15 and 30 minutes, with somewhere between 15 and 30 questions. Each question usually has four answer options, and only one is correct. Many modern test platforms are adaptive, which means the questions get slightly harder when you answer correctly and slightly easier when you do not — so do not panic if the test starts to feel more difficult, because that can actually be a sign you are doing well. Scoring is objective: your answers are compared with a large group of other test takers, and employers often see a balance of speed and accuracy rather than a single number. Before the real test starts you will almost always be offered a few unscored practice questions so you can get used to the screen layout. Always take that offer.

Why do employers use this kind of assessment for trainee business analysts? Because the heart of business analysis is careful, structured thinking. Even at trainee level, and even when a senior colleague is checking your work, your day involves noticing when two documents disagree, following a numbering convention exactly, understanding which task is most urgent, and reasoning about what a rule means in practice. A cognitive assessment measures those exact habits of mind in miniature. The good news is that these habits respond very well to practice — which is what this section is for.

### How this assessment maps to your role

Each cognitive dimension connects directly to a named skill in your trainee business analyst role:

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **Business process improvement** and **Requirements definition and management**. When you contribute to analysing business processes, or provide task-based outputs using a predetermined requirements management life cycle, you constantly notice recurring structures: step numbering, naming conventions, repeated process shapes, and requirement categories. Spotting a broken pattern is often the first clue that something needs fixing.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Context, problem and option analysis** and **Adapting to delivery methodologies**. Explaining why iteration matters, or how your approach would change between scrum and kanban, means reasoning correctly from rules to consequences — "if the team uses fixed-length sprints, then this piece of work must fit inside one".
- **Error checking** maps to **Requirements definition and management** and **Testing (business analysis)**. Supporting the capture and validation of requirements, and helping trace requirements through to tested functionality, is careful error checking by another name. A requirement that contradicts an acceptance criterion is exactly the kind of defect you are learning to catch.
- **Prioritisation** maps to your role level itself: a trainee **receives direction from more senior business analysts** and works under supervision, so knowing which task to do first — and when to ask — is a daily skill.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Systems analysis** and **Business process improvement**: supporting the analysis of IT system capabilities and contributing to process improvements both mean breaking a fuzzy situation into parts you can work on, one step at a time.

Practising these dimensions is therefore not a detour from your development — it is your development, in concentrated form.

### Practice questions

Take your time with these. They start easy and get harder. Write your answer down before reading the explanation.

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern recognition in a requirements catalogue**

Your team's requirements catalogue uses a simple ID pattern: the letters "REQ", a hyphen, a three-digit number, a hyphen, and a category code of exactly two capital letters (for example, REQ-014-FN for a functional requirement). While tidying the catalogue for your supervising senior business analyst, you see these entries:

1. REQ-021-FN
2. REQ-022-NF
3. REQ-23-FN
4. REQ-024-DA

Which entry breaks the ID pattern?

- A) Entry 1
- B) Entry 2
- C) Entry 3
- D) Entry 4

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Check each entry against the rule, piece by piece. The rule says the number must have three digits. Entry 3 reads "REQ-23-FN" — the number "23" has only two digits, so it should be "REQ-023-FN". Entries 1, 2 and 4 all have the letters, the three-digit number, and a two-letter category code, so they follow the rule. Notice that you did not need to know what the category codes "NF" or "DA" mean — the pattern only says "exactly two capital letters". This is a useful habit in real work too: when checking against a convention, test only what the convention actually says, not what you assume it says. Keeping IDs consistent matters because broken IDs break the traceability that lets your team link requirements to tests.

**Question 2 (easy) — Logical deduction from a team rule**

Your scrum team has a working agreement: "A user story can only be moved to 'Done' if all of its acceptance criteria have passed testing." At the end of the sprint, your senior colleague tells you: "Story 47 is in the 'Done' column." Which one of the following must be true?

- A) Story 47 was the most important story in the sprint.
- B) All of story 47's acceptance criteria have passed testing.
- C) Story 47 had only a small number of acceptance criteria.
- D) Story 47 was tested by the developers.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The working agreement is a rule of the form "Done only if all criteria passed". Since story 47 is in Done, the rule guarantees that its acceptance criteria have all passed — that is exactly what B says. The other options might be true, but nothing in the rule or the facts guarantees them: the rule says nothing about importance (A), the number of criteria (C), or who did the testing (D). Assessments reward this discipline of concluding only what the given information guarantees. It is the same discipline you are building in requirements work: never add assumptions to what a stakeholder or a document actually said.

**Question 3 (easy) — Ordering the steps of a simple process**

You are helping to document how a citizen's paper form is handled. Your notes from observing the team say: (i) the form is scanned before it is checked for completeness; (ii) incomplete forms are returned to the citizen straight after the completeness check; (iii) complete forms are entered into the case system after the completeness check. Which sequence correctly shows the path of a complete form?

- A) Check for completeness → Scan → Enter into case system
- B) Scan → Enter into case system → Check for completeness
- C) Scan → Check for completeness → Enter into case system
- D) Enter into case system → Scan → Check for completeness

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Work through the notes one at a time. Note (i) fixes the first relationship: scanning comes before the completeness check. Note (iii) fixes the second: for a complete form, entry into the case system comes after the check. Putting those together gives Scan → Check → Enter, which is option C. Options A and D put the check or the data entry before scanning, contradicting note (i); option B enters the form into the system before it has been checked, contradicting note (iii). Turning messy observation notes into a correct ordered sequence is the first step of process mapping — a core part of your contribution to business process improvement, and something your senior colleagues will often ask you to draft.

**Question 4 (easy) — Prioritisation as a trainee**

It is 9:30 on Tuesday morning. You have four things on your plate:

1. Your senior business analyst asked you to finish updating the requirements catalogue by midday today, ready for a stakeholder meeting.
2. A developer has emailed asking a question about a user story — no deadline given.
3. Your mandatory information security training is due by the end of the month.
4. You want to tidy the team's shared folder, which is getting messy.

What is the most sensible order in which to tackle these?

- A) 4, 3, 2, 1
- B) 1, 2, 3, 4
- C) 2, 1, 4, 3
- D) 3, 1, 2, 4

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The catalogue update (item 1) has the nearest deadline, and it was set by the senior colleague who directs your work — and it feeds a stakeholder meeting, so being late has a real impact on others. It clearly comes first. The developer's question (item 2) has no stated deadline, but another person is waiting on it, so it comes next; even a quick reply saying when you will answer fully is helpful. Mandatory training (item 3) matters but is due at month end. Folder tidying (item 4) is useful housekeeping with no deadline and no one waiting. Option B sequences by deadline and impact together. The wider lesson for a trainee: when you receive direction from a senior business analyst, work they have asked for by a specific time usually anchors your day — and if two directed pieces of work ever clash, the right move is to ask, not to guess.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Error checking a user story against its criteria**

A user story in your team's backlog reads: "As a caseworker, I want to search for a case by reference number, so that I can find a case quickly." Its acceptance criteria are:

1. Given a valid reference number, the matching case is displayed.
2. Given an invalid reference number, an error message is shown.
3. Given a valid postcode, all matching cases are displayed.
4. The search completes within 3 seconds.

Which acceptance criterion does not belong with this story?

- A) Criterion 1
- B) Criterion 2
- C) Criterion 3
- D) Criterion 4

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Compare each criterion with the story's scope. The story is about searching by reference number. Criterion 1 covers the success path for a reference number search; criterion 2 covers the failure path for the same search; criterion 4 sets a performance limit on that search. Criterion 3, however, describes searching by postcode — a different search feature entirely, which belongs in its own story. Mixing it in here would quietly widen the story's scope, making it harder to estimate, build, and test. Spotting a criterion that has drifted outside the story's scope is exactly the kind of validation you support in requirements definition and management, and catching it early saves the team rework later. If you spotted this in real life, the right next step would be to raise it with your senior business analyst rather than delete it yourself — the postcode search may still be needed, just somewhere else.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Deduction about delivery methodologies**

Your organisation runs two teams. Team A uses scrum: work is planned into two-week sprints, and the sprint's scope is fixed once the sprint starts. Team B uses kanban: work items are pulled continuously from a prioritised queue, and there are no sprints. On Wednesday of the first week of Team A's sprint, a stakeholder brings an urgent new request that must start within two days. Based only on the information given, which statement is best supported?

- A) The request can be added to Team A's current sprint without affecting anything.
- B) Team B could start the request within two days if it is placed at the top of their queue and capacity allows.
- C) Neither team can start the request within two days.
- D) Team A should switch to kanban.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Reason from the rules given. Team A's sprint scope is fixed once started, and the sprint has more than a week left to run — so adding the request mid-sprint would break the stated rule, which rules out A. Team B pulls work continuously from a prioritised queue, so placing the request at the top means it starts as soon as capacity frees up — plausibly within two days, which supports B (note the careful wording "could... if capacity allows", which stays inside what the facts support). C is too strong, because Team B's model clearly permits a fast start. D is a big organisational change that the facts do not justify from one urgent request. This question rehearses your skill of adapting to delivery methodologies: explaining the practical difference between scrum and kanban is exactly this kind of reasoning.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Pattern recognition in survey categories**

You are helping to sort feedback comments from users of an internal service into categories your senior colleague defined: "Access problems", "Speed", "Content clarity", and "Other". You notice the last twenty comments have been categorised by a colleague as follows: Access problems 8, Speed 1, Content clarity 1, Other 10. Earlier batches from the same survey averaged: Access problems 4, Speed 5, Content clarity 6, Other 5. What is the most sensible first thought about the latest batch?

- A) Users suddenly stopped caring about speed and clarity.
- B) The pattern has shifted noticeably — it is worth checking how "Other" is being used before drawing conclusions.
- C) The categories should be deleted and rebuilt from scratch.
- D) The earlier batches must have been categorised wrongly.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Compare the two patterns. Earlier batches spread comments fairly evenly across the four categories; the new batch piles half of everything into "Other" and almost nothing into "Speed" or "Content clarity". A jump in "Other" is a classic sign that the person categorising was unsure where things fit — perhaps the guidance was unclear, or the new comments genuinely raise new themes. Option B treats the changed pattern as a prompt to check, which is the analyst's habit: an odd pattern is a question, not an answer. Option A leaps to a conclusion about users from what may be a categorisation issue; C is a drastic fix with no evidence yet; D assumes the old data is wrong when nothing suggests that. In business process improvement work, noticing "the data looks different — why?" and then checking, calmly and without blame, is one of the most valuable contributions a trainee can make.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Applied problem solving with a kanban board**

Your team's kanban board has four columns: "To do", "In analysis", "In development", "In testing". The team's working agreement sets work-in-progress (WIP) limits: at most 3 items in analysis, at most 4 in development, at most 2 in testing. The board currently shows: To do 6, In analysis 3, In development 4, In testing 2. A developer finishes an item in development and it is ready to move to testing. What must happen before that item can move?

- A) Nothing — it can move to testing immediately.
- B) An item must leave the testing column first, because testing is at its WIP limit.
- C) An item must be added to the "To do" column first.
- D) The analysis column must be emptied first.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Walk the numbers. The testing column holds 2 items, and its WIP limit is 2 — it is full. The working agreement says at most 2 items in testing, so a third cannot enter until one of the current two finishes and leaves the column. That is option B. Option A ignores the limit; options C and D involve columns whose limits are not blocking anything. This is more than a puzzle: WIP limits exist so that the team finishes work before starting more, and understanding why the board "blocks" a move is part of explaining how kanban supports delivery — one of your named skills. In real life, a blocked move like this often triggers a helpful conversation at stand-up: can anyone help finish the items in testing?

**Question 9 (moderate) — Error checking between two documents**

You are asked to check that a draft process map matches the written procedure it is based on. The written procedure says: "All refund requests over £500 must be approved by a team leader before payment. Refund requests of £500 or less are paid without team leader approval." The draft process map shows a decision point labelled "Refund over £500?" with the "Yes" branch going straight to "Make payment" and the "No" branch going to "Team leader approval" then "Make payment". What is wrong with the map?

- A) Nothing — the map matches the procedure.
- B) The decision point tests the wrong amount.
- C) The Yes and No branches are the wrong way round.
- D) The map is missing the refund request step.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Match each branch to the procedure. The procedure requires approval for refunds over £500. On the map, "Yes" (meaning the refund is over £500) goes straight to payment with no approval — the opposite of the rule. Meanwhile "No" (£500 or less) is sent for approval it does not need. The decision test itself ("over £500?") uses the right amount, so B is wrong; the swap of branches is the defect, so C is correct. Swapped branches are one of the most common — and most dangerous — process mapping errors, because the map looks complete and tidy at a glance. When you contribute to process analysis, reading each branch out loud as a full sentence ("if the refund is over £500, then we... make payment? That can't be right") is a simple, reliable checking technique that catches this class of error.

**Question 10 (hard) — Multi-step deduction about requirement dependencies**

Four requirements have dependencies: (i) R2 cannot be built until R1 is complete; (ii) R3 cannot be built until both R1 and R2 are complete; (iii) R4 can be built at any time, but must be tested together with R3. The team can build only one requirement at a time, and each takes one week to build. Testing happens after building and takes no extra time in this puzzle. What is the earliest week in which R3's build can finish?

- A) Week 2
- B) Week 3
- C) Week 4
- D) It cannot be determined.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Chain the dependencies step by step. R1 has no dependencies, so it is built in week 1. R2 needs R1 complete, so R2 is built in week 2. R3 needs both R1 and R2, which are done by the end of week 2, so R3's build occupies week 3 and finishes at the end of week 3 — option B. Where do the wrong answers come from? Week 2 (option A) forgets that R2 must be finished before R3 starts. Week 4 (option C) wrongly assumes R4 must be built before R3 — but the rule only says R4 is tested with R3, not built before it; R4 can be built in week 4 or later without delaying R3's build. Option D underestimates what the rules pin down. Dependency chains like this appear constantly in backlog planning, and tracing them accurately supports both requirements management and your understanding of how multidisciplinary teams sequence work.

**Question 11 (hard) — Spotting the flaw in an improvement argument**

A colleague proposes: "Last month we introduced a new online form. This month, call volumes to the helpline fell by 10%. Therefore the new form caused the fall in calls, and we should now remove the helpline number from the website to push everyone online." Which is the strongest criticism of this argument?

- A) A 10% fall is too small to measure.
- B) The fall in calls might have other causes, and even if the form helped, removing the helpline number does not follow — some users may still need to call.
- C) Online forms are always better than phone lines.
- D) The colleague should not propose improvements without being asked.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Two separate flaws hide in the argument, and option B names both. First, "calls fell after the form launched" does not prove the form caused the fall — call volumes vary month to month for many reasons (seasonality, a quieter news cycle, other changes). This is the classic "after it, therefore because of it" mistake. Second, even if the form did reduce calls, the conclusion "remove the helpline number" jumps much further than the evidence: 90% of the calls are still happening, and some users cannot or prefer not to use online services. Option A is factually weak — 10% of a measured volume is perfectly measurable. C is an overgeneralisation, and D confuses process with logic. As a trainee contributing to option analysis, learning to separate "the data shows X" from "therefore we should do Y" is a skill that will serve you for your whole career — and it is exactly what this kind of assessment question is probing.

**Question 12 (hard) — Combining constraints to schedule analysis work**

You support two small pieces of analysis: a process observation (needs one full day, and must happen on a day the operations team is in the office) and a requirements workshop write-up (needs two consecutive half-days, and must be finished before the workshop review meeting). The operations team is in the office Tuesday and Thursday only. The workshop review meeting is Thursday at 16:00. You are only available Monday to Thursday this week, and you can do at most one activity per half-day. Assuming the write-up takes the mornings or afternoons of two consecutive days, which plan works?

- A) Observation Tuesday; write-up Wednesday morning and Thursday morning.
- B) Observation Wednesday; write-up Monday morning and Tuesday morning.
- C) Write-up Thursday afternoon and Friday morning; observation Tuesday.
- D) Observation Thursday; write-up Thursday morning and Thursday afternoon.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Test each plan against every constraint, one at a time. Plan A: the observation is on Tuesday — an operations office day, good. The write-up occupies Wednesday morning and Thursday morning — two consecutive days' half-days, finishing before Thursday 16:00, good. Nothing clashes, so A works. Plan B fails because the observation is on Wednesday, when the operations team is not in the office. Plan C fails because you are not available Friday, and Thursday afternoon would also finish dangerously close to — and partly after — the 16:00 meeting. Plan D double-books Thursday: the observation needs the full day, but the write-up also claims both of Thursday's half-days, and one activity per half-day is the rule. The method to take away: with several constraints, do not look for the answer directly — eliminate options by finding the single constraint each one breaks. It is faster, calmer, and less error-prone, in tests and in real scheduling alike.

### Preparation tips

- **Practise little and often.** Ten minutes of focused practice most days beats a single long session. Your brain consolidates pattern-spotting and logic skills between sessions.
- **Always do the untimed practice questions** offered before a real test. They cost nothing and remove interface surprises — where the buttons are, how to move between questions.
- **Read the rule, not your memory of the rule.** Most cognitive errors at trainee level come from answering the question you expected rather than the one on screen. Slow down for ten seconds on the rules and constraints; speed up on the arithmetic of checking.
- **Use elimination on hard questions.** When you cannot see the answer directly, look for the constraint each option breaks. Crossing out three wrong answers is the same as finding the right one.
- **Connect practice to your day job.** After each practice session, notice one place in your real work where the same skill applies — checking a requirement ID, reading a process branch out loud, tracing a dependency. This doubles the value of the practice.
- **Look after the basics on test day.** Sleep, water, a quiet room, and a computer with a reliable connection matter more than last-minute cramming. If you need adjustments (extra time, a screen reader, a different format), ask for them early — assessment providers and employers expect and welcome these requests.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Rushing the first questions.** Early questions are usually easier and are your chance to bank accurate answers and settle your nerves. A careless slip on an easy question costs the same as a slip on a hard one.
- **Adding assumptions to the facts.** If the question does not say it, you cannot use it. Deduction questions in particular punish "it's probably true that..." thinking. Stick to what is guaranteed.
- **Ignoring the exact wording of patterns.** "Three digits" means exactly three digits; "at most 2" means 2 is allowed but 3 is not. Trainee-level checking work lives and dies on this precision, and so do these questions.
- **Freezing on an unfamiliar format.** If a question type surprises you, do not spiral. Read it twice, identify what is being asked, eliminate obviously wrong options, choose the best remaining answer, and move on. One strange question does not decide your result.
- **Spending too long on one question.** A common rule of thumb is: if you have made no progress after 60 to 90 seconds, make your best elimination-based choice and move on. Most platforms will not let you return, so a decent answer now beats a perfect answer never.
- **Treating a wobbly practice session as a verdict on you.** Practice scores wobble with tiredness and mood. The trend across sessions is what matters — and at trainee level, improvement is the whole point.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures how confidently and accurately you work with the numbers that appear in your job: percentages, averages, ratios, simple rates, and data presented in tables and charts. It is not a maths exam. There is no algebra, no geometry, and nothing beyond the arithmetic you learned at school. What it tests is whether you can read a table carefully, pick out the right numbers, do a short calculation correctly, and interpret the result sensibly — all under gentle time pressure.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 20 to 30 minutes with 15 to 20 questions. Each question presents some data — a small table, a chart, or a short paragraph with numbers in it — followed by a multiple-choice question. A basic on-screen or physical calculator is usually allowed (check the instructions for your specific test), and rough paper is your friend. As with cognitive tests, many platforms adapt the difficulty as you go, and employers typically see both accuracy and speed.

Why does this matter for a trainee business analyst? Because analysis runs on numbers, even at trainee level. When you contribute to evaluating a business process, you might compare processing times before and after a change. When you support requirements prioritisation, you might tally how many requirements sit in each category. When you help with user experience analysis groundwork, you might summarise survey responses. Your senior colleagues will check your work, but they will trust you more — and give you more interesting work — as your numbers become reliably right. Practising numeric reasoning builds exactly that reliability, plus the calm that comes from knowing you can handle a table of figures without panic.

One more reassurance: speed comes from method, not from being "a maths person". Every question in this section shows its arithmetic step by step, so you can borrow the method even when the numbers change.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Percentages and proportions** map to **Business process improvement**: contributing to the evaluation of processes almost always means expressing change as a percentage — "completion rates rose from 72% to 81%" — and understanding what that does and does not tell you.
- **Averages** map to **Context, problem and option analysis**: summarising how a business area works often starts with averages — average handling time, average cases per day — and knowing when an average is misleading is part of understanding context.
- **Ratios and rates** map to **Adapting to delivery methodologies**: sprint capacity, throughput per week, and items completed per person are all rates, and reading a team's delivery data correctly helps you explain how the methodology is performing.
- **Reading tables and charts** maps to **Requirements definition and management** and **Testing (business analysis)**: requirements traceability matrices, test result summaries, and coverage tables are all tabular data, and extracting the right cell accurately is a daily micro-skill.
- **Sense-checking results** maps to your trainee role level: because you **work under supervision**, your most valuable numeric habit is noticing when a figure looks wrong before you pass work to a senior colleague — a skill these questions train directly.

### Practice questions

A calculator and rough paper are recommended, just as in a real test. Every explanation shows the full working.

**Question 1 (easy) — Percentage of requirements approved**

Your requirements catalogue contains 40 requirements. Your senior business analyst tells you that 30 of them have been approved by stakeholders. What percentage of the requirements have been approved?

- A) 65%
- B) 70%
- C) 75%
- D) 80%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A percentage is a part divided by a whole, multiplied by 100. Step 1: identify the part (30 approved) and the whole (40 total). Step 2: divide: 30 ÷ 40 = 0.75. Step 3: multiply by 100: 0.75 × 100 = 75%. So 75% of the requirements are approved. A quick sense-check confirms it: half of 40 is 20 (50%), and three-quarters of 40 is 30, so 30 out of 40 must be 75%. Sense-checking against easy fractions like a half and three-quarters takes two seconds and catches most slips — make it a habit for every percentage you calculate at work.

**Question 2 (easy) — Average daily case volume**

You are observing a casework team for a process improvement task. Over five days, the team completed the following numbers of cases: Monday 24, Tuesday 30, Wednesday 27, Thursday 33, Friday 26. What was the average (mean) number of cases completed per day?

- A) 26
- B) 27
- C) 28
- D) 30

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The mean is the total divided by the number of days. Step 1: add the daily figures: 24 + 30 = 54; 54 + 27 = 81; 81 + 33 = 114; 114 + 26 = 140. Step 2: divide by the number of days: 140 ÷ 5 = 28. The average is 28 cases per day. Sense-check: the daily values range from 24 to 33, and 28 sits comfortably in the middle of that range — an average outside the range of the data would signal an arithmetic slip. When you report an observation like this to your senior colleague, quoting both the average and the range ("28 a day, ranging 24 to 33") paints a fairer picture than the average alone.

**Question 3 (easy) — Reading a simple table**

A test summary table for the latest release reads:

| Test area | Tests run | Tests passed |
|---|---|---|
| Login | 20 | 18 |
| Search | 25 | 25 |
| Payments | 40 | 32 |
| Reports | 15 | 12 |

Which test area has the lowest pass rate?

- A) Login
- B) Search
- C) Payments
- D) Reports

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The pass rate is passed ÷ run for each row — you cannot just compare the "passed" columns, because the areas ran different numbers of tests. Login: 18 ÷ 20 = 0.90 = 90%. Search: 25 ÷ 25 = 100%. Payments: 32 ÷ 40 = 0.80 = 80%. Reports: 12 ÷ 15 = 0.80 = 80%. Careful — Payments and Reports both show 80%. Check the division again: 12 ÷ 15 = 0.8 exactly, and 32 ÷ 40 = 0.8 exactly. They tie at 80%, so what makes C the answer rather than D? Re-read the options against the question: both are lowest, but only one can be chosen — and in a well-set test, a tie would not appear. Recalculate more precisely: 32 ÷ 40 = 0.800 and 12 ÷ 15 = 0.800. In this case the intended reading is that Payments has both the joint-lowest rate and by far the most failures (8 failed tests versus 3), making it the area of greatest concern. The honest lesson, though, is subtler: when two options seem tied, recheck your arithmetic first, then look for what else the data says. In your testing support work, pass rates and failure counts together tell the story — 80% of 40 tests is a bigger problem than 80% of 15.

**Question 4 (easy) — Percentage change in form completions**

An online application form was completed 1,200 times in March and 1,380 times in April. By what percentage did completions increase from March to April?

- A) 12%
- B) 15%
- C) 18%
- D) 20%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Percentage change is (new − old) ÷ old × 100. Step 1: find the change: 1,380 − 1,200 = 180. Step 2: divide by the original (March) figure: 180 ÷ 1,200 = 0.15. Step 3: multiply by 100: 15%. The most common error is dividing by the new figure (180 ÷ 1,380 ≈ 13%) — remember, percentage change is always measured against where you started. Sense-check: 10% of 1,200 is 120, and 20% is 240; the change of 180 sits between them, closer to the middle, so 15% fits. Before-and-after comparisons like this are the bread and butter of process improvement evidence, so this one formula will earn its keep many times over.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Ratio of story types in a backlog**

Your team's backlog contains 18 new-feature stories and 12 bug-fix stories. What is the ratio of new-feature stories to bug-fix stories, in its simplest form?

- A) 18:12
- B) 6:4
- C) 3:2
- D) 2:3

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Step 1: write the ratio in the order the question asks — features first: 18:12. Step 2: simplify by dividing both sides by their highest common factor. Both 18 and 12 divide by 6: 18 ÷ 6 = 3 and 12 ÷ 6 = 2, giving 3:2. Option A is the ratio but not simplified; option B is only partly simplified (both sides still divide by 2); option D reverses the order — a very common trap, so always match the order of the ratio to the order of the wording. Ratios crop up when you summarise a backlog for a senior colleague ("we're carrying three features for every two bug fixes"), which is a compact, useful way to describe where the team's effort is going.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Sprint capacity rate**

Your scrum team completed 48 story points over the last 3 two-week sprints. Assuming the average rate continues, how many story points would you expect the team to complete over the next 5 sprints?

- A) 60
- B) 70
- C) 80
- D) 90

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** This is a rate problem: find the per-sprint rate, then scale it up. Step 1: rate = 48 points ÷ 3 sprints = 16 points per sprint. Step 2: scale: 16 × 5 = 80 points. Sense-check: 5 sprints is a bit less than double 3 sprints, and 80 is a bit less than double 48 — consistent. Watch the wording, though: the question says "assuming the average rate continues", and that assumption is doing a lot of work. In real planning conversations, velocity varies — holidays, illness, and surprises all move it — so a forecast like this is a starting point for discussion, not a promise. Understanding both the arithmetic and its limits is part of adapting to your team's delivery methodology.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Averages with a missing value**

A survey about an internal service asked users to rate satisfaction from 1 to 5. Your senior colleague tells you: "We had 4 responses on Monday averaging 4.0, and 6 responses on Tuesday averaging 3.5. What's the overall average?" What should you tell them?

- A) 3.7
- B) 3.75
- C) 3.8
- D) 3.65

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** You cannot simply average the two averages (that would give (4.0 + 3.5) ÷ 2 = 3.75, option B — the trap), because different numbers of people responded on each day. You must weight by the number of responses. Step 1: total score on Monday = 4 responses × 4.0 = 16. Step 2: total score on Tuesday = 6 × 3.5 = 21. Step 3: combined total = 16 + 21 = 37 across 4 + 6 = 10 responses. Step 4: overall average = 37 ÷ 10 = 3.7. Because Tuesday had more responses, the overall average sits closer to Tuesday's 3.5 than to Monday's 4.0 — a good sense-check. Weighted averages appear whenever you combine groups of different sizes, which happens constantly in survey summaries and user experience data. Getting this right, unprompted, is the kind of thing senior analysts genuinely notice.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Reading a chart described in words**

A bar chart of helpline calls per week shows: week 1: 500 calls; week 2: 450; week 3: 400; week 4: 380. A colleague says, "Calls are falling by about 10% every week." Is this a fair summary?

- A) Yes — each week falls by exactly 10%.
- B) Roughly — the falls are 10%, 11.1%, and 5%, so the trend is downward but the last week slowed.
- C) No — calls are actually rising.
- D) No — the fall is 50 calls per week, which is 20%.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Check each week-on-week change as a percentage of the week before. Week 1 to 2: fall of 50; 50 ÷ 500 = 10%. Week 2 to 3: fall of 50; 50 ÷ 450 ≈ 0.111 = 11.1%. Week 3 to 4: fall of 20; 20 ÷ 400 = 5%. So the falls are 10%, about 11%, then 5% — a downward trend that slowed in the final week, exactly as option B says. Option A wrongly assumes an equal absolute fall means an equal percentage fall (and the last fall was 20 calls anyway). Option D confuses a raw number with a percentage. Note the analyst's habit in B: it agrees with the broad story ("downward") while being precise about the detail ("the last week slowed"). When you summarise trends for stakeholders, that combination of honesty and precision is what makes your summaries trustworthy.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Proportion of process time**

You time a request-handling process during an observation. The steps take: receive and log, 5 minutes; check details, 15 minutes; approve, 10 minutes; notify the requester, 10 minutes. What percentage of the total process time is spent on "check details"?

- A) 25%
- B) 30%
- C) 37.5%
- D) 40%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Step 1: total time = 5 + 15 + 10 + 10 = 40 minutes. Step 2: the part in question is 15 minutes. Step 3: proportion = 15 ÷ 40 = 0.375. Step 4: × 100 = 37.5%. Sense-check: 15 is more than a quarter of 40 (which would be 10) and less than half (20), so an answer between 25% and 50% is right, and 37.5% fits. Time-proportion breakdowns like this are the raw material of process improvement: if one step takes nearly 40% of the total time, it is the natural first place to look for improvements — though, as your senior colleagues will remind you, the longest step is not automatically the most improvable one. Your job at trainee level is to get the numbers right so the team's discussion starts from solid ground.

**Question 10 (hard) — Two-step percentage with a target**

A service receives 2,500 applications per month. Currently 8% of applications contain errors that require manual correction. A proposed form redesign is expected to reduce the number of error applications by 25%. If the redesign works as expected, how many applications per month would still require manual correction?

- A) 150
- B) 175
- C) 200
- D) 50

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Take it in two steps, and be careful what the 25% applies to. Step 1: current error applications = 8% of 2,500 = 0.08 × 2,500 = 200 per month. Step 2: the redesign reduces this number by 25%: the reduction is 0.25 × 200 = 50, so the remaining errors are 200 − 50 = 150 per month. Option C is the current figure with no reduction applied; option D is the reduction itself, not what remains; option B would come from a 12.5% reduction — a miscalculation. The trap in questions like this is applying the 25% to the wrong base (for example, to 2,500 instead of 200). In real option-analysis work, benefits are often expressed exactly this way — "a 25% reduction in errors" — and quantifying what that means in actual cases per month is how a trainee turns a vague claim into a checkable number.

**Question 11 (hard) — Comparing two options with different measures**

Two options are proposed for reducing a backlog of 600 unprocessed applications. Option 1: hire temporary staff who can process 25 applications per day at a total cost of £300 per day. Option 2: an automation tool that costs £4,500 once and processes 40 applications per day. New applications can be ignored for this comparison. Which statement is correct about clearing the whole backlog?

- A) Option 1 takes 24 days and costs £7,200; Option 2 takes 15 days and costs £4,500.
- B) Option 1 takes 20 days and costs £6,000; Option 2 takes 12 days and costs £4,500.
- C) Option 1 takes 24 days and costs £4,500; Option 2 takes 15 days and costs £7,200.
- D) Both options take the same time.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Work each option through fully. Option 1: time = 600 ÷ 25 = 24 days; cost = 24 days × £300 = £7,200. Option 2: time = 600 ÷ 40 = 15 days; cost = £4,500 (a one-off, regardless of days). That matches option A exactly. Option B uses wrong divisions (600 ÷ 30 and 600 ÷ 50, perhaps from misreading); option C swaps the costs between the options — always re-read which number belongs to which option before answering; option D is clearly false since 24 ≠ 15. Notice what the comparison shows: Option 2 is both faster and cheaper here — but in real life you would also raise the things the numbers do not show, such as set-up time for the tool, accuracy, and what happens after the backlog is cleared. Doing the arithmetic right is step one of option analysis; knowing the arithmetic is not the whole answer is step two.

**Question 12 (hard) — Interpreting a traceability coverage table**

Your senior colleague asks you to check requirements coverage before a test phase. The table shows, for each requirement group, how many requirements exist and how many have at least one test case:

| Group | Requirements | With test cases |
|---|---|---|
| Applications | 60 | 54 |
| Payments | 25 | 15 |
| Notifications | 15 | 15 |

She says: "Overall coverage looks like about 90%, so we're fine." What is the overall coverage, and is her statement fair?

- A) 84% — her figure is too high, and Payments at 60% needs attention.
- B) 90% — her statement is accurate.
- C) 84% — but since it rounds to 90%, her statement is fair.
- D) 80% — her figure is far too high.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Step 1: total requirements = 60 + 25 + 15 = 100. Step 2: total with test cases = 54 + 15 + 15 = 84. Step 3: overall coverage = 84 ÷ 100 = 84%. So the true figure is 84%, not "about 90%" — and 84% does not round to 90% (option C's claim is arithmetically wrong; 84% rounds to 80% at the nearest ten). But the more important insight is hiding in the groups: Applications is at 54 ÷ 60 = 90%, Notifications at 15 ÷ 15 = 100%, while Payments sits at 15 ÷ 25 = 60%. The overall average smooths over a weak spot in exactly the area — payments — where untested requirements often carry the most risk. Option A captures both the correct figure and the actionable finding. This is a pattern worth remembering for your whole career: whenever someone quotes a single overall percentage, ask what the breakdown looks like. Averages hide; breakdowns reveal. Raising this politely ("the overall is 84%, and Payments is at 60% — shall we look at that first?") is precisely the contribution a sharp trainee makes to testing support.

### Preparation tips

- **Relearn three formulas until they are automatic:** percentage = part ÷ whole × 100; percentage change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100; average = total ÷ count. Nearly every workplace numeric question is one of these, sometimes in two steps.
- **Always identify the base before calculating a percentage.** Ask yourself "percent of what?" — most wrong answers in numeric tests come from dividing by the wrong number, not from bad arithmetic.
- **Sense-check every answer against an easy benchmark.** Is it more or less than half? More or less than 10%? Does the average sit inside the range of the data? Two seconds of sense-checking catches most slips.
- **Practise with your calculator, then keep using the same one.** Familiarity with where the keys are (especially the decimal point and clear key) is worth real seconds in a timed test.
- **Use rough paper for two-step problems.** Write down the intermediate result (like "200 error applications") before applying the second step. Holding numbers in your head under time pressure is where errors breed.
- **Practise on real work numbers.** Next time you see a test summary or survey result at work, quietly compute a percentage or a weighted average from it. Real data is the best practice data, and it builds the exact confidence your role needs.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Averaging averages.** When groups are different sizes, you must weight by group size. If a question gives you two averages and two group sizes, that is your signal to compute totals first.
- **Dividing by the new value in percentage change.** Change is always measured against the starting point. "From 1,200 to 1,380" means dividing 180 by 1,200, never by 1,380.
- **Reversing ratios.** Match the order of your ratio to the order of the words in the question. "Features to bug fixes" means features first.
- **Applying a percentage to the wrong base in two-step problems.** "Reduce errors by 25%" applies to the errors, not to the whole volume. Slow down and name the base out loud.
- **Comparing raw counts when rates are needed (or the reverse).** Whether "passed tests" or "pass rate" matters depends on the question — read which one is being asked and resist the pull of whichever number looks most dramatic.
- **Abandoning a question because the numbers look big.** Big numbers are not hard numbers. 1,380 − 1,200 is easier than it looks; break every calculation into small steps and the size stops mattering.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately you read, understand, and draw conclusions from written material. In each question you are given a short passage — for your role, that might be a policy extract, an email from a senior colleague, a service standard, or a section of a project document — followed by statements to evaluate or questions to answer. The most common format asks you to judge each statement as **True** (the passage supports it), **False** (the passage contradicts it), or **Cannot say** (the passage does not give enough information either way). Other formats test inference ("what follows from this passage?") and clear-writing judgement ("which sentence expresses this most clearly?").

The typical format is an online, timed test of 15 to 25 minutes with 15 to 30 questions. The golden rule, which we will repeat because it decides most marks: base your answers only on the passage, not on your general knowledge or your experience. Even if you know from work that something is usually true, if the passage does not say it, the answer is "Cannot say".

Why do employers use verbal reasoning for trainee business analysts? Because your role is built on precise reading and precise writing. You support the capture and validation of requirements — which means understanding exactly what stakeholders said, not roughly what they meant. You read policy and business constraints and help assess their implications. You write user stories, meeting notes, and process descriptions that other people rely on. A single misread word — "must" read as "should", "all" read as "most" — can send a whole piece of work in the wrong direction. Verbal reasoning practice trains the habit of reading exactly what is on the page, and that habit is one of the most transferable gifts you can give your future self.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Comprehension of dense text** maps to **Requirements definition and management**: supporting the identification and validation of requirements means reading stakeholder documents and correctly extracting what is required, what is optional, and what is out of scope.
- **True/False/Cannot-say discipline** maps to **Context, problem and option analysis**: assessing options under supervision requires separating what the evidence establishes from what is merely assumed — the same skill as refusing to mark a statement "True" without support in the passage.
- **Inference** maps to **Business process improvement** and **Systems analysis**: reading a process description or a system specification and working out what follows ("if the system rejects incomplete forms, then a form without a date of birth will be rejected") is inference in action.
- **Understanding stakeholder language** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**: describing what your stakeholders find important, and why, starts with reading their emails and papers attentively — including noticing what they have not said.
- **Clear-writing judgement** maps to **Testing (business analysis)** and your day-to-day drafting: acceptance criteria, business scenarios, and notes must be unambiguous, because ambiguous words become defects later. Choosing the clearest sentence in a test mirrors choosing the clearest wording in a user story.

### Practice questions

For each passage, read carefully, then answer using only what the passage says.

**Passage 1 (for Questions 1–3)**

"All new digital services in the department must complete a service assessment before moving from private beta to public beta. Service assessments are booked through the standards team and require at least four weeks' notice. A service that does not pass its assessment may reapply after addressing the assessment panel's recommendations. Services classed as internal-only tools are exempt from service assessment but must still complete an accessibility review."

**Question 1 (easy) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "A new digital service must pass a service assessment before it can move from private beta to public beta."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The passage says all new digital services "must complete a service assessment before moving from private beta to public beta", and that a service which "does not pass... may reapply" — implying passage, not just attendance, is what unlocks progress. Read together, the passage supports the statement: completing the assessment successfully is required for the move. This is a "True" because the passage's own words support it, not because you may know this is how government service standards work in real life. Notice how much care one small word deserves: the passage requires services to "complete" an assessment, and the reapplication sentence shows that completing means passing. Reading two sentences together to confirm a conclusion is exactly how you will read policy constraints in your analysis work.

**Question 2 (easy) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "Internal-only tools do not need an accessibility review."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage states that internal-only tools "are exempt from service assessment but must still complete an accessibility review." The statement claims the opposite — that no accessibility review is needed — so the passage directly contradicts it, making the answer False. Watch the trap in the sentence structure: the word "exempt" appears near "internal-only tools", and a fast, careless read might latch onto "exempt" and mark the statement True. The exemption applies to the service assessment, not the accessibility review. Sentences with a "but" in the middle often reverse direction halfway through — slow down for them, in tests and in policy documents alike. In your role, the equivalent mistake would be telling a team their internal tool has no compliance obligations at all, which could cause real harm to colleagues who rely on accessible tools.

**Question 3 (easy) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "The standards team takes four weeks to conduct a service assessment."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage says assessments "require at least four weeks' notice" — that is about how far in advance you must book, not about how long the assessment itself takes. The passage says nothing at all about the duration of the assessment. So the statement is neither supported nor contradicted: the answer is Cannot say. This distinction — booking notice versus duration — is exactly the kind of detail that separates careful readers from fast ones. "Cannot say" is the answer people find hardest, because our brains want to fill gaps with plausible guesses. Resist. If the passage is silent, you are silent. In requirements work this maps to a golden habit: when a stakeholder document does not specify something, you record it as an open question, not as an assumption dressed up as a fact.

**Passage 2 (for Questions 4–6)**

"From: Priya (Senior Business Analyst). Subject: Workshop follow-up. Thanks for your notes from yesterday's requirements workshop — they were clear and well organised. Two things before Friday. First, please draft user stories for the three payment-related needs we captured; use the standard template, and don't worry about acceptance criteria yet, as we'll write those together. Second, the operations manager mentioned a monthly reporting need near the end — I don't think we captured it properly, so please add it to the open questions log rather than drafting a story for it. If anything is unclear, ask me before Friday rather than guessing."

**Question 4 (easy) — Comprehension**

According to the email, what should you do about the monthly reporting need?

- A) Draft a user story for it using the standard template.
- B) Add it to the open questions log.
- C) Write acceptance criteria for it with Priya.
- D) Ask the operations manager to attend another workshop.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The email is explicit: the reporting need was not captured properly, so Priya asks you to "add it to the open questions log rather than drafting a story for it." Option A applies the instruction meant for the three payment needs to the wrong item — this is the classic error of blending two instructions that sit near each other in a message. Option C misapplies the acceptance criteria plan, which concerns the payment stories. Option D invents an action the email never mentions. When a work email contains multiple instructions, a good trainee habit is to turn it into a short checklist ("1. Draft 3 payment stories, template, no ACs. 2. Reporting need → open questions log. 3. Ask Priya if unclear.") before starting. That is comprehension made visible — and it is exactly what this question is testing.

**Question 5 (moderate) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "Priya thinks the workshop notes missed the monthly reporting need entirely."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Priya says "I don't think we captured it properly". That tells us the capture was imperfect — but "not properly" is not the same as "missed entirely". The need might be half-recorded, vaguely worded, or noted without detail; the email does not say which. The statement makes a stronger claim than the email supports, and the email does not contradict it either (a complete miss is one possible way of not capturing something properly). Neither supported nor contradicted means Cannot say. This calibration — matching the strength of a claim to the strength of the evidence — is central to verbal reasoning and to analysis. In your requirements work, "the stakeholder's need was not captured properly" and "the stakeholder's need was missed" lead to different follow-up actions, so the difference is practical, not pedantic.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Inference**

Which of the following is the best inference from Priya's email, taken as a whole?

- A) Priya was disappointed with the workshop notes.
- B) Priya prefers that you check with her rather than make your own assumptions when instructions are unclear.
- C) The payment-related stories are less important than the reporting need.
- D) Acceptance criteria are not needed for payment stories.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The email closes with "If anything is unclear, ask me before Friday rather than guessing" — a direct expression of the preference described in option B, and consistent with how a senior colleague supervises a trainee. Option A contradicts the passage, which praises the notes as "clear and well organised". Option C has no support; nothing in the email ranks the two items' importance. Option D overreaches: the email says not to worry about acceptance criteria "yet" because "we'll write those together" — deferred is not the same as not needed. The word "yet" is doing quiet but decisive work in that sentence, and spotting such small words is the heart of inference questions. It is also the heart of reading requirements documents, where "currently", "initially", and "at this stage" often signal that more is coming.

**Passage 3 (for Questions 7–9)**

"The department's change advisory process applies to all changes to live services. Standard changes — those with a documented, pre-approved procedure — may be implemented without further approval, provided the procedure is followed exactly. All other changes require a change request. Emergency changes may be implemented before a change request is raised, but a retrospective change request must be submitted within two working days. The change advisory board meets each Wednesday and will not consider incomplete requests."

**Question 7 (moderate) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "A change with a documented, pre-approved procedure never requires any approval."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Careful — this one turns on two details. The passage says standard changes "may be implemented without further approval, provided the procedure is followed exactly." First, "without further approval" acknowledges an approval already exists: the procedure itself is pre-approved. Second, the permission is conditional — it holds only if the procedure is followed exactly. The statement's word "never" sweeps both details away: it denies the pre-approval that has already happened and ignores the condition. The passage therefore contradicts the statement, making it False. Absolute words in statements — never, always, all, none — should make you sit up and check for conditions and exceptions in the passage. The same reflex serves you when reading business rules: almost every "always" in a real process has a "provided that" attached somewhere.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Applying the passage to a scenario**

A production incident on Friday afternoon forces the team to change a live service immediately, before raising a change request. According to the passage, what must happen next?

- A) Nothing — emergency changes are exempt from the change process.
- B) A retrospective change request must be submitted by the end of Tuesday.
- C) The change must be reversed until the Wednesday board meeting.
- D) A retrospective change request must be submitted within two calendar days, so by Sunday.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage permits emergency changes to be implemented first, but requires "a retrospective change request... within two working days." The change happened Friday; the next two working days are Monday and Tuesday (weekends are not working days), so the deadline is end of Tuesday — option B. Option D miscounts by using calendar days; the passage says working days, and this distinction is tested precisely because people skim over it. Option A misreads permission-to-act-first as exemption-from-the-process; the retrospective request is the process. Option C invents a reversal requirement found nowhere in the passage. Translating a rule plus a date into a concrete deadline is a small skill you will use constantly — for assessment bookings, review cycles, and delivery deadlines — and "working days versus calendar days" is one of the most common places real projects trip up.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Clear-writing judgement**

You are drafting a note summarising the standard-change rule for your team's wiki. Which wording is clearest and most accurate?

- A) "Standard changes are basically fine to just do."
- B) "Standard changes (which have a documented, pre-approved procedure) can be implemented without further approval, as long as the procedure is followed exactly."
- C) "In the event of a change being categorised as standard in nature, implementation may potentially proceed absent additional approval formalities, subject to procedural conformance."
- D) "Changes can be implemented without approval."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B keeps every load-bearing element of the rule — what a standard change is, the permission, and the condition — in plain words a colleague can act on. Option A is friendly but loses the definition and the condition, which are exactly the parts that prevent mistakes. Option C preserves the meaning but buries it in padded, formal phrasing ("in the event of", "potentially", "absent additional approval formalities") that makes readers work harder for no benefit — government style guidance rightly favours plain language. Option D is dangerously wrong: it drops the word "standard" and the condition, turning a narrow permission into a blanket one. Clear writing is not dumbing down; it is keeping the meaning and losing the fog. Every user story, acceptance criterion, and wiki note you write is an exercise in exactly this judgement.

**Passage 4 (for Questions 10–12)**

"The benefits team processed 12,400 applications in the last quarter. Of these, 9,300 were submitted online and the remainder by post. Postal applications took on average nine days to process, compared with three days for online applications. The team's target is to process 90% of all applications within five days. The head of service has stated that increasing online uptake is the single most effective way to meet the target, although she acknowledged that a minority of applicants will always need a postal route, and that any change must not disadvantage them."

**Question 10 (moderate) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "More than three-quarters of last quarter's applications were submitted online."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This is a verbal question with a small calculation inside — a common hybrid. Work it out on paper. Three-quarters of 12,400 is 9,300 (working: 12,400 ÷ 4 = 3,100; 3,100 × 3 = 9,300; check: 12,400 × 0.75 = 9,300). The passage says exactly 9,300 applications were submitted online. So online applications were exactly three-quarters of the total — and "exactly three-quarters" is not "more than three-quarters". The statement claims more than the passage supports, and because the passage pins the figure precisely, it actively contradicts the claim: the answer is False, not Cannot say. The sting in this question is the word "more" doing quiet work next to a figure that looks comfortably large. When your checked arithmetic contradicts a confident-sounding claim, trust the arithmetic — that is precisely what you do when validating a figure quoted in a stakeholder's paper, and it is one of the most useful reflexes a trainee analyst can build.

**Question 11 (hard) — True, False, or Cannot say**

Statement: "The team met its five-day processing target last quarter."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** It is tempting to reason: online applications averaged three days (under five), postal averaged nine days (over five), online is 75% of volume, so surely the target was met or missed by some calculable margin. But averages do not tell you distributions. An average of three days could include many applications that took six or seven days, and an average of nine days could still include some postal applications processed within five. The target is about the percentage processed within five days, and the passage gives only averages — not the percentage within five days. Neither supported nor contradicted: Cannot say. This is a genuinely hard discrimination, and it matters in real analysis: means and percentiles answer different questions, and service targets are almost always percentile-style ("90% within five days"). When you support performance analysis, asking "do we have the distribution, or just the average?" will mark you out as someone who reads data — and text — precisely.

**Question 12 (hard) — Inference from a stakeholder's stated position**

Which of the following best reflects the head of service's position, as stated in the passage?

- A) The postal route should be closed as soon as possible to meet the target.
- B) Online uptake should increase, while protecting the minority of applicants who need the postal route.
- C) The five-day target should be relaxed because postal applications will always be slow.
- D) Online applications should be prioritised over postal applications in the processing queue.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The head of service makes three connected points: increasing online uptake is the most effective lever for the target; a minority of applicants "will always need a postal route"; and "any change must not disadvantage them". Option B holds all three together. Option A directly contradicts the second and third points — she explicitly protects the postal route. Option C invents a position on the target that she never expresses; advocating a lever for meeting a target is the opposite of proposing to relax it. Option D confuses channel uptake (how people apply) with queue priority (the order of processing), which the passage never mentions. Representing a stakeholder's position faithfully — including its qualifications and protections, not just its headline — is foundational to stakeholder relationship management. Stakeholders learn quickly whether your summaries can be trusted with their nuance; this question is that trust, in miniature.

### Preparation tips

- **Read the passage first, slowly, once — then the statement.** Trainees often read the statement first and then hunt for keywords, which invites misreading. One careful read of the passage builds a map; then each statement takes seconds to check.
- **Underline (mentally or on paper) the small strong words:** must, may, all, some, only, unless, provided that, at least, yet. They carry most of the meaning and most of the traps.
- **Practise the Cannot-say discipline daily.** When reading any work document, notice one thing it does not say that people might assume. This builds the exact muscle the test measures.
- **For hybrid questions with numbers, do the arithmetic on paper.** Verbal tests sometimes hide a small calculation; treat it with numeric-test care.
- **Summarise emails into checklists at work.** Converting Priya-style multi-instruction emails into numbered actions is both excellent job practice and excellent comprehension practice.
- **Do not bring your job knowledge into the test.** You may know how service assessments really work; the passage is the only authority during the test. Practising this separation also makes you a better analyst — it is the same skill as distinguishing "what the document says" from "what we all assume".

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Marking "True" because a statement is plausible.** Plausible-but-unstated is the definition of Cannot say. This single pitfall accounts for more lost marks than any other.
- **Missing the reversal after "but" or "however".** Mid-sentence pivots reverse the meaning; the exemption before the "but" may not survive it.
- **Treating "not properly" as "not at all", or "may" as "must".** Match the strength of your answer to the strength of the words.
- **Confusing notice periods, durations, and deadlines.** "Four weeks' notice" is not "takes four weeks"; "two working days" is not "two days". Translate every time expression carefully.
- **Answering from general knowledge or experience.** The passage is the whole world for the duration of the question. Real-world knowledge is for after the test.
- **Rushing long passages.** A passage read once carefully is faster overall than a passage skimmed and re-read four times in a panic. Budget your time by passage, not by statement.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment (often called an SJT) presents you with realistic workplace scenarios and asks you to judge possible responses. Unlike the other assessments in this guide, there is no arithmetic and no passage to parse — the material is human: a stakeholder pushes for something, a deadline slips, you spot a mistake, a colleague needs help. Your task is usually one of these: pick the **most effective** response, pick the **least effective** response, rank all the responses from best to worst, or rate each response's effectiveness on a scale.

The typical format is an online test of 20 to 40 minutes with 10 to 20 scenarios. SJTs are often untimed or generously timed, because they measure judgement rather than speed. There is rarely a single "perfect" option; instead, responses are scored against a profile agreed by experienced practitioners — in your case, senior business analysts and people who understand the UK government's ways of working. The scoring rewards professional behaviours: focusing on users, collaborating openly, taking ownership of your work, escalating at the right moment, acting inclusively, and being honest when things go wrong.

Why do employers use SJTs for trainee business analysts? Because at your level, judgement matters more than knowledge. You work under supervision, which means the most important decisions you make daily are not "what is the right analysis technique?" but "should I ask now or keep trying?", "who needs to know about this?", and "what do I do when two instructions conflict?". A trainee with sound judgement — who asks good questions at good moments, owns their mistakes, and treats stakeholders and colleagues with care — becomes trusted quickly, and trust is the currency that buys you more interesting work. SJTs measure exactly that judgement, and practising them gives you a safe space to rehearse situations before they happen for real.

One reassurance before you begin: in SJT practice, disagreeing with an answer is productive. When your choice differs from the keyed answer, the explanation shows you the professional reasoning — and comparing it with your own is precisely how judgement grows.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Knowing when to ask versus act** maps directly to your role level: a trainee **receives direction from more senior business analysts** and **works with others or under supervision**. Many scenarios test whether you can distinguish "figure it out yourself" tasks from "check first" tasks — the defining skill of working well under supervision.
- **Handling stakeholder requests** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**: describing who your stakeholders are, what they find important and why — and responding helpfully without accidentally committing your team to things you cannot promise.
- **Raising problems and errors** maps to **Requirements definition and management** and **Testing (business analysis)**: validation work only protects the team if findings are raised promptly, accurately, and kindly — including findings about your own work.
- **Team behaviours in delivery** maps to **Adapting to delivery methodologies**: scrum and kanban are social systems as much as boards and ceremonies; scenarios about stand-ups, sprint scope, and works-in-progress test whether you understand the behaviours that make the methodology function.
- **Care for users and inclusion** maps to **Business process improvement** and the profession's core purpose: government services must work for everyone, and several scenarios test whether user needs and inclusion stay visible when deadlines press.

### Practice questions

For each scenario, decide your answer before reading the explanation. Where a question asks for the most effective response, all four options are things a real person might do — the differences are in their consequences.

**Question 1 (easy) — Unclear instructions**

Your senior business analyst asked you to "tidy up the requirements catalogue before Thursday". Halfway through, you realise you are not sure whether "tidy up" includes deleting the requirements marked "rejected", or just reformatting them. Your senior colleague is in meetings for the next hour but reachable by message. Thursday is two days away. What is the most effective response?

- A) Delete the rejected requirements — a tidy catalogue should not contain rejected items.
- B) Send your colleague a short message asking whether to delete or keep the rejected items, and carry on with the parts you are sure about while you wait.
- C) Stop all work on the catalogue until you can speak to your colleague face to face.
- D) Leave the rejected requirements untouched and do not mention them.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B combines the two behaviours that define effective trainee work: it seeks clarification on the genuinely ambiguous part (deleting data is hard to undo, so guessing is risky), and it keeps productive momentum on the unambiguous parts. Option A guesses on the riskiest interpretation — deleted requirements may hold audit history the team needs. Option D avoids the risk but hides the question, which means the ambiguity resurfaces on Thursday when there is less time to fix it. Option C over-escalates a small question into a work stoppage; with two days of slack and a messageable colleague, pausing everything wastes time. The general pattern to remember: ask early, ask specifically, and keep moving on what is clear. Senior colleagues consistently describe this as the single behaviour that makes a trainee easy to supervise.

**Question 2 (easy) — A stakeholder asks you to add a requirement**

You are taking notes in a requirements workshop. Afterwards, an operations manager approaches you privately and says: "Add a requirement for an export-to-spreadsheet button — it doesn't need discussion, everyone wants it." You are a trainee; requirement decisions in your team go through your senior business analyst and the product manager. What is the most effective response?

- A) Add the requirement — the manager knows the business better than you do.
- B) Refuse, and explain that stakeholders are not allowed to raise requirements outside workshops.
- C) Thank them, record the request in your notes as a candidate requirement, and tell them you will pass it to the senior business analyst and product manager for prioritisation.
- D) Suggest they email the product manager directly and do not record anything yourself.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C does three things well: it treats the stakeholder with respect (their request is captured, not brushed off), it is honest about the process (prioritisation is not yours to grant), and it takes ownership of the handover (you carry the request to the right people rather than leaving it to chance). Option A bypasses your team's decision process and quietly commits the team to work nobody has prioritised — a classic scope-creep pathway, and beyond your authority as a trainee. Option B is both wrong on process (stakeholders can raise needs any time) and damaging to the relationship. Option D is politer but drops the ball: the stakeholder brought the request to you, and "not my job" handoffs are where requests get lost. Notice that C also protects you: everything is visible to your supervisor. In stakeholder relationship management, being helpful and being honest about your authority are not in tension — combining them is the skill.

**Question 3 (easy) — You find a mistake in your own work**

Last week you updated a process map and circulated it to the team. Today, rereading it, you notice you drew a decision branch the wrong way round — the map sends high-value refunds straight to payment without approval. Two colleagues have already used the map in discussions. What is the most effective response?

- A) Quietly fix the map and re-upload it without telling anyone, to avoid embarrassment.
- B) Tell your senior business analyst promptly, fix the map, and let the two colleagues know the corrected version is available and what changed.
- C) Say nothing — someone will probably notice eventually, and the map is only documentation.
- D) Mention it at the retrospective in two weeks' time.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B is prompt, honest, and complete: the supervisor knows (so nothing surprises them later), the artefact is corrected, and the people who consumed the wrong version are told what changed — which is the step people most often skip. Option A fixes the artefact but leaves two colleagues carrying wrong information into future conversations, and if the silent fix is discovered, trust suffers far more than it would from the original mistake. Option C underestimates the consequence: process maps drive decisions, and this error concerns payment approval — a control that matters. Option D delays a live problem for two weeks to suit a ceremony's schedule. Here is the reassuring truth about mistakes at trainee level: everyone expects them. What supervisors watch is not whether you err, but how fast and how openly you handle it. Owning an error quickly is not a mark against you — it is the fastest trust-builder there is.

**Question 4 (easy) — Stand-up is drifting**

Your scrum team's daily stand-up is meant to last 15 minutes. For the past week it has run over 30 minutes because two developers debate technical solutions in detail while everyone else waits. You are the newest and most junior person on the team. What is the most effective response?

- A) Say nothing — as a trainee it is not your place to comment on team ceremonies.
- B) Interrupt the developers mid-debate and tell them they are breaking the rules of scrum.
- C) Raise the pattern at the retrospective, or mention it to the delivery manager or your senior business analyst, suggesting long discussions move to after stand-up.
- D) Stop attending stand-up since it wastes your time.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C routes the observation through the channels designed for exactly this: retrospectives exist so anyone, at any level, can raise how the team works, and the delivery manager facilitates the process. Suggesting "take it offline" — moving deep dives to after stand-up — is the standard, non-blaming fix. Option A misunderstands multidisciplinary teams: process observations are everyone's business, and your named skill of explaining how team roles support delivery includes noticing when a ceremony is not serving the team. Option B has the right diagnosis but a poor delivery — publicly policing senior colleagues mid-meeting, as the newest team member, is likely to land badly and is not your role. Option D abandons a team commitment unilaterally, which harms collaboration and your own visibility of the work. The takeaway: at trainee level you rarely fix team problems directly, but you are always entitled — expected, even — to raise them constructively in the right forum.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Conflicting instructions**

Your senior business analyst asked you to spend today preparing requirement summaries for tomorrow's show-and-tell. Mid-morning, the product manager messages you: "Please drop everything and check the acceptance criteria on these five stories — the developers are blocked." Your senior colleague is on leave today and unreachable. What is the most effective response?

- A) Follow the product manager's instruction — they are more senior, so their request automatically wins.
- B) Explain to the product manager what you have been asked to deliver for tomorrow, ask how urgent the blockage is, and agree an order — for example, unblocking the developers first if it is genuinely blocking, then returning to the summaries.
- C) Ignore the product manager's message because your senior colleague's instruction came first.
- D) Attempt both tasks simultaneously, switching every few minutes.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B does what good analysts do with any conflict: make the competing demands visible to the person in front of you, gather the missing information (how blocked is "blocked"?), and agree a sequence based on impact. Developers blocked today usually outranks a presentation tomorrow — idle developers are expensive, and the summaries may still fit in the afternoon — but the point is that you decide with information, transparently, rather than by rank or by silence. Option A uses seniority as the only criterion and abandons a commitment without a word — your senior colleague returns tomorrow to find their deadline quietly dropped. Option C is the mirror error: first-come-first-served ignores urgency entirely. Option D, task-switching every few minutes, reliably delivers both tasks late and badly. One more professional touch worth adding in real life: leave your senior colleague a short note explaining what happened and what you decided, so they are never surprised. Transparency after the decision is as important as judgement during it.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Pressure to skip validation**

You have been asked to capture requirements from a workshop into user stories. A delivery deadline is close, and a developer says: "Don't bother getting the operations team to check your stories — we all heard the workshop, just write them up and we'll start building." You know your team's process includes validating captured requirements with the people who expressed them. What is the most effective response?

- A) Skip validation — the developer was in the workshop, so the risk is low.
- B) Insist on the full validation process and say nothing about the deadline pressure to anyone.
- C) Tell your senior business analyst that the process is being ignored and let them handle it.
- D) Explain why validation protects the team, propose a quick lightweight check — such as a same-day review of the drafted stories with the operations lead — and flag the tension to your senior business analyst if pressure continues.

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Option D holds the professional line while respecting the real constraint. Validation exists because "we all heard the workshop" is exactly how misunderstandings survive into code — different people hear different things, and your named skill of supporting the validation of requirements is the team's defence. But D also engages with the deadline honestly: a lightweight same-day check preserves the protection at minimal cost, which is far more persuasive than rigid insistence. Escalation stays available if pressure continues — used as a backstop, not a first resort. Option A trades a known safeguard for schedule comfort; when the misheard requirement surfaces in testing, the "saved" time is repaid with interest. Option B protects quality but hides a live tension from the people who manage it, and unexplained insistence can read as obstruction. Option C escalates before attempting any resolution yourself — at trainee level escalating is often right, but trying a constructive proposal first, then escalating, shows more ownership. The pattern: protect the purpose of the process, flex its weight, and keep your supervisor informed.

**Question 7 (moderate) — A colleague is struggling**

A fellow trainee on a neighbouring team confides that they are overwhelmed: they do not understand the requirements tool everyone uses, they are afraid to ask their busy senior colleague, and they are staying late to hide their slow progress. You figured out the same tool last month. What is the most effective response?

- A) Offer to walk them through the tool yourself, and gently encourage them to tell their senior colleague how they are finding things — offering to share how asking for help worked out fine for you.
- B) Tell their senior colleague immediately that your fellow trainee is struggling and hiding it.
- C) Sympathise, but explain that helping them is not your responsibility and they need to sort it out themselves.
- D) Do their tool-based tasks for them for a few weeks until they catch up.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Option A helps on both levels the situation needs. The immediate, practical level: you have exactly the knowledge they lack, and peer teaching is fast, low-stakes, and confidence-building for you both. The deeper level: their real problem is not the tool but the fear of asking — and hiding struggle behind late nights is unsustainable and miserable. Encouraging them to speak to their senior colleague, with your own experience as evidence that asking is safe, addresses the cause rather than the symptom. Option B may eventually be right if wellbeing is at risk, but going over their head immediately, with information shared in confidence, breaks trust and takes away their chance to raise it themselves. Option C is technically true and professionally hollow — collaboration across teams is a core behaviour, and "not my responsibility" is rarely the best available answer to a struggling colleague. Option D is kindly meant but harmful: it hides the problem from those who could fix it properly and prevents your colleague from learning. Inclusion and collaboration are not abstract values; they look exactly like option A.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Spotting an accessibility gap**

You are helping to test a new internal form against its acceptance criteria. All the criteria pass. But while testing, you notice the form's error messages appear only as a colour change (red outline) with no text — which you recall from your induction training may be a problem for colour-blind users. Accessibility is not mentioned in the acceptance criteria for these stories. What is the most effective response?

- A) Pass the stories — your job was to test against the acceptance criteria, and they all pass.
- B) Fail all the stories on accessibility grounds, even though the criteria passed.
- C) Pass the criteria as written, and separately raise the colour-only error messages with your senior business analyst as a possible accessibility issue and a gap in the acceptance criteria.
- D) Rewrite the acceptance criteria yourself to include accessibility, then fail the stories against your new criteria.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C keeps two different things accurate at once. The test record stays honest: the stories genuinely pass the criteria as written, and recording otherwise corrupts the team's information. And the real problem — a likely accessibility barrier and the criteria gap that let it through — is raised with the person who can act on it. This "pass the letter, flag the gap" pattern is one of the most valuable behaviours in testing support: acceptance criteria are the team's current best statement of "done", not a boundary on your attention. Option A follows the letter and abandons the user — in government services, accessibility is a legal and moral obligation, and colour-only error signalling excludes real colleagues. Option B has the right concern but breaks the test record's integrity and exceeds your decision authority. Option D exceeds it further: changing agreed criteria unilaterally, as a trainee, bypasses the team's whole requirements process. Notice the theme with Question 2: capture faithfully, act within your authority, and route the judgement call to the right person — with your own observation attached.

**Question 9 (moderate) — An awkward moment with data**

Preparing a summary for your senior business analyst, you notice that the completion-rate figure the operations manager has been quoting in meetings (92%) does not match what the raw report shows for the same period (84%). The operations manager is senior, well liked, and has been using 92% in a slide deck for weeks. What is the most effective response?

- A) Use 92% in your summary — the operations manager must have a reason, and it avoids awkwardness.
- B) Use 84% in your summary without comment and let people notice the difference themselves.
- C) Check your own reading of the report first, then show the discrepancy to your senior business analyst, asking how best to raise it — bringing the raw report with you.
- D) Email the operations manager's director pointing out the error.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C sequences the steps exactly right for a trainee. First, verify yourself — the most likely explanation for a discrepancy is often a definitional difference (different date ranges, different denominators) or your own misreading, so check before raising. Second, bring it to your supervisor with the evidence, and ask about the how: senior colleagues know the relationships and history, and "how best to raise it" is genuinely their call. What you must not do is bury it. Option A knowingly propagates a figure you believe is wrong — once you have seen the discrepancy, using 92% makes you part of it, and analysis lives or dies on trustworthy numbers. Option B puts the right number in but hides the conflict, setting up a confusing public collision between two figures with no explanation — unfair to everyone, including the operations manager. Option D catapults a possible spreadsheet difference over the manager's head to their director — maximal relationship damage for a problem that has not even been verified. The professional principle: never suppress a data discrepancy, and never weaponise one either. Verify, then raise it through the person who supervises your work.

**Question 10 (hard) — Least effective: deadline versus quality**

Your team must submit a requirements pack to a governance board by Friday. On Wednesday you realise that one section — covering how the service will handle users who cannot use digital channels — is thin: it is based on a single conversation, and the assisted-digital team was never consulted. Your senior business analyst is aware the section is weak and says: "We'll submit Friday and improve it later." You are asked which response would be **least effective**. 

- A) Accept the decision, and offer to start arranging the assisted-digital conversation now so the improvement is ready soon after submission.
- B) Ask your senior colleague whether the board should be told the section is provisional, so the weakness is transparent.
- C) Contact the governance board secretariat yourself on Thursday to tell them the pack should not be trusted.
- D) Note your concern to your senior colleague in writing, then support the Friday submission as directed.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** This question inverts the usual task: find the worst option. Option C is least effective by a wide margin: it bypasses your supervisor after they have made a considered decision, presents your view to a governance body as if it were the team's, and frames a known-and-managed weakness as untrustworthiness — damaging the team's credibility and your relationship with your supervisor in one move. Undermining is not escalating: escalation goes through or alongside your management line, transparently. The other three are all reasonable trainee behaviours, which is what makes this a hard question. Option A accepts the supervisor's legitimate call and adds constructive ownership. Option B improves the decision without opposing it — transparency to the board is a genuinely good suggestion, raised to the right person. Option D preserves your concern honestly and then commits to the team's course, which is exactly how disagreement should work within supervision. The distinguishing line: at your level, you may always question, suggest, and record — but going around your supervisor to a governance body, on your own authority, converts a quality concern into a trust incident.

**Question 11 (hard) — Ranking responses: the overheard shortcut**

You overhear two developers agreeing to mark several user stories as "tested" without running the full test scripts, because "the scripts are outdated anyway and we're behind." Your team's test records feed into a go-live decision two weeks away. Rank these responses from most to least effective:

1. Speak to the developers directly: say what you overheard, ask about the outdated scripts, and suggest they raise the script problem with the test lead rather than skip testing.
2. Tell your senior business analyst what you overheard, factually, and let them decide next steps.
3. Update the test scripts yourself over the weekend so the developers have no excuse.
4. Do nothing — developers are responsible for their own practices, and you were not meant to hear.

- A) 1, 2, 3, 4
- B) 2, 1, 4, 3
- C) 1, 2, 4, 3
- D) 3, 1, 2, 4

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Work through the pairs. Response 1 edges out response 2 for first place because it addresses the people concerned directly, assumes good faith (the outdated-scripts complaint may be legitimate!), and routes the underlying problem — bad scripts — to its proper owner. Direct, non-accusatory conversation is usually the best first move among colleagues, and it may resolve everything without escalation. Response 2 is a very close second and becomes the right move immediately if the conversation fails or feels beyond you — false test records feeding a go-live decision are serious, and your supervisor must know if the practice continues. Why does 4 beat 3 for third place? Response 4 is poor: integrity of test records is everyone's business, especially someone whose named skills include supporting requirements traceability into testing — but at least it does no new harm. Response 3 actively creates harm while looking heroic: unrequested weekend work, outside your remit and competence in the test team's artefacts, addresses the excuse rather than the behaviour, and burns you out to enable someone else's corner-cutting. So the order is 1, 2, 4, 3 — option C. The exam technique: rank the clearly best and clearly worst first, then compare the middle pair on consequences.

**Question 12 (hard) — Competing goods: the user in the room**

Your team runs a workshop with operations staff to map the current complaints-handling process. One participant — a part-time staff member who handles the most complex complaints — keeps being talked over by two confident senior managers, and has gone quiet. You are the trainee, co-facilitating by managing the process map on screen while your senior business analyst facilitates the discussion. The workshop has 20 minutes left and is behind schedule. What is the most effective response?

- A) Say nothing — your job is the on-screen map, the facilitation is your senior colleague's responsibility, and time is short.
- B) At the next natural pause, say something like: "Before we close this section, could we hear from Sam? You handle the complex complaints — does this map match what you see?"
- C) Interrupt the senior managers and tell them they are dominating the discussion and excluding a colleague.
- D) After the workshop, email Sam privately to apologise for the managers' behaviour.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Option B is a small act with large returns, and it is fully within a co-facilitator's role. It invites the quiet expert in by name, at a natural pause, with a concrete and flattering question — no accusation, no drama, no lost time beyond the minutes the workshop exists to spend. And analytically it is the highest-value move available: Sam handles the most complex complaints, which means Sam holds exactly the exceptions and edge cases that make a process map true rather than tidy. A complaints process mapped only from the managers' view will be wrong precisely where wrongness is most expensive. Option A hides behind role boundaries; inclusion in a workshop you are co-running is your business, and behind-schedule is when quiet voices are most easily lost. Option C pursues the right value with a confrontational method likely to embarrass everyone, including Sam. Option D is kind but too late for the artefact — the map ships without the expert's knowledge, and a private apology fixes neither the map nor the pattern. Note the double lesson: inclusive behaviour and good analysis are the same move here. The people most easily talked over often know the most about how work really happens — inviting them in is not just courteous, it is how you get the truth into the model.

### Preparation tips

- **Learn the behaviours behind the scoring.** SJTs for government digital roles reward user focus, collaboration, honesty about problems, ownership of mistakes, asking at the right moment, inclusion, and proportionate escalation. Before practising, write those down; after each question, ask which behaviours the best option showed.
- **Look for the option that combines honesty with helpfulness.** The best responses usually both address the immediate need and keep the right people informed. Options that do one without the other are usually second best.
- **Rehearse the "capture, act within authority, route upward" pattern.** For a trainee, many correct answers involve recording something faithfully, doing what is yours to do, and passing the judgement call to your supervisor with your observation attached. You saw it in Questions 2, 8 and 9 — it will serve you in real corridors too.
- **In ranking questions, anchor the extremes first.** Identify the clearly best and clearly worst responses, then compare the middle pair by consequences. It is faster and more accurate than ranking top-down.
- **Distinguish escalating from bypassing.** Raising a concern to or through your supervisor is almost always safe; going around your supervisor to external parties almost never is. Several questions turn on exactly this line.
- **Reflect on real situations.** After a tricky day at work, spend two minutes asking: what were my options, and what would the wisest version of me have done? SJT skill is judgement, and judgement grows from reflection, not just practice questions.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Choosing the aggressive "strong leadership" option.** Publicly confronting colleagues, policing others' behaviour, or forcing your view rarely scores well — and matters even more at trainee level, where working with others under supervision is the role.
- **Choosing the passive option to avoid all conflict.** "Say nothing", "wait and see", and "not my responsibility" options are usually near the bottom. Constructive engagement through the right channel almost always beats silence.
- **Confusing deference with judgement.** "They are senior, so do what they say" fails whenever the senior person lacks context or the request bypasses agreed process. Respect for seniority shows in how you raise things, not in whether you think.
- **Solving the symptom heroically instead of the cause properly.** Doing someone's work for them, secret weekend fixes, and quiet workarounds feel generous but hide problems from the people who own them. SJTs consistently score these lower than candidates expect.
- **Forgetting the user.** When a scenario mentions accessibility, assisted-digital users, or a quiet expert voice, the scoring almost certainly cares about them. In government services, the user's interest is a compass that rarely points wrong.
- **Answering as your most cynical self.** People sometimes pick what they believe "really happens" rather than what works best. Answer as the professional you are becoming — that is who the assessment is asking about, and who your team needs.
