## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the mental operations your role demands, using realistic material rather than abstract puzzles. At lead level, the emphasis shifts from item-level checking toward systems reasoning: multi-constraint scheduling across a portfolio, policy application with exceptions and precedence, detection of structural inconsistencies across documents produced by different teams, and diagnosis of patterns that span suppliers, projects, and time.

The format is typically an online, timed test of 15 to 30 minutes with 15 to 30 multiple-choice questions covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective against a norm group — for senior roles, often a norm group of comparable seniority, so the comparison is with peers, not with the general population. Adaptive difficulty is common: a test that escalates in complexity is a test you are performing well on.

Employers use cognitive assessment at your level because co-ordination at scale is applied reasoning under load. When you sequence three programmes through one operations calendar, you are solving a constraint problem whose errors surface months later as collisions. When you apply release policy to a high-impact change, you are executing conditional logic where a misread exception becomes an unauthorised deployment. When you notice that three unrelated transition failures share a structural cause, you are pattern-matching across noisy data — the seed of every process improvement you will ever initiate. And because you are solely responsible for cross-portfolio co-ordination, there is often no second reader for your reasoning: the assessment's simulation of accurate-thinking-under-time-pressure is unusually faithful to your working conditions.

Approach it as you approach a portfolio review: rules first, then evidence, then conclusions sized to the evidence — with your time allocated across the whole set rather than sunk into any single stubborn item.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The assessment dimensions map directly to the named skills in your role summary:

- **Multi-constraint reasoning and prioritisation** map to your core accountability — planning and co-ordination of resources across projects, suppliers, and service teams — where sequencing errors are the expensive ones, and to your **Change management** skill of managing high-impact, complex change requests.
- **Logical deduction with exceptions and precedence** maps to your **Change management** duty to ensure release policies, procedures, and processes are applied: policy application is conditional logic, and the exceptions are where it fails.
- **Pattern recognition across noisy, multi-source data** maps to your **Problem management** skill — initiating and monitoring actions to investigate patterns and trends — and to your **Ownership and topic** skill of proactively searching for potential problems before they occur.
- **Error checking at the structural level** maps to your **Asset and configuration management** skill (maintaining secure configuration and accurate information across areas) and your **Availability and capacity management** duty to ensure standards are correctly implemented — both are verification applied to systems rather than items.
- **Applied problem solving across the life cycle** maps to your **Service focus** skill: maintaining focus on the entire life cycle of the service means reasoning about how a decision at transition echoes through operation, improvement, and retirement.
- **Capacity and remediation logic** maps to your duty to identify capacity issues, implement required changes, and initiate remedial action — diagnosis plus intervention, which is problem solving with authority attached.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern recognition across a release calendar**

Your release calendar shows the last eight major releases and their outcomes: releases deployed in the first week of the month — four releases, zero rollbacks; releases deployed in the last week of the month — four releases, three rollbacks. All eight followed the same release process. A colleague concludes the process is unreliable. What is the more precise observation?

- A) The process fails 37.5% of the time, confirming unreliability.
- B) Failures cluster in end-of-month deployments; something that varies with month-end — freeze pressure, batch processing, staff leave patterns — deserves investigation before the process itself is blamed.
- C) The first-week releases were smaller, which explains everything.
- D) Eight releases is too few to observe anything.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The aggregate failure rate (3 of 8) is real but unilluminating; the structure inside it is stark — all three rollbacks sit in one calendar position. When outcomes cluster on a variable, the variable is the lead, and month-end is rich in candidate mechanisms: change freezes compressing schedules, month-end batch loads colliding with deployments, thinner staffing. Option A averages away the signal. Option C proposes a plausible confounder as though it were established — release size is worth checking, but the stem gives no size data, so C states evidence it does not have. Option D overcorrects: eight data points cannot prove a cause, but a 0-versus-3 split across a clean partition is exactly enough to justify investigation, which is all B claims. The lead-level habit: read failure data for structure before rate, and size your conclusion to "worth investigating", not "proven" — that is your **Problem management** skill operating at the correct evidential temperature.

**Question 2 (easy) — Release policy deduction**

Your release policy states: "High-impact changes require a full Change Advisory Board (CAB) review. Changes to services in their protected early-life period require CAB review regardless of impact classification. Low-impact changes to services outside early-life may use the standard pre-approved route." A team proposes a low-impact configuration change to a service that entered live service twelve days ago, with a protected early-life period of thirty days. Which route applies?

- A) Standard pre-approved route — the change is low-impact.
- B) CAB review — the service is inside its protected early-life period, which overrides impact classification.
- C) Either route, at the team's discretion.
- D) No change is permitted during early-life.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Parse the policy's precedence. The early-life rule is explicitly unconditional on impact ("regardless of impact classification"), and the pre-approved route is explicitly scoped to services "outside early-life". Twelve days into a thirty-day protected period, the service is inside; the low-impact classification, which would otherwise open the pre-approved route, is overridden. Option A applies the general rule while ignoring the specific one — and specific-over-general is the default precedence in well-drafted policy. Option C invents discretion the policy nowhere grants. Option D over-reads protection as prohibition: early-life heightens scrutiny; it does not freeze the service. Ensuring release policies are applied — your explicit duty — mostly means catching exactly this error: teams applying the rule that suits them rather than the rule that governs them, usually innocently, at pace.

**Question 3 (easy) — Error checking a portfolio status summary**

Your programme office produces a portfolio summary: "Twelve transitions in flight: seven green, three amber, one red. Green transitions require no lead review this cycle." What is wrong with the summary before you even examine any individual transition?

- A) Twelve transitions is too many to run concurrently.
- B) Seven plus three plus one is eleven — one transition is unaccounted for, and the unaccounted one could be anywhere from unstarted to failing.
- C) Green transitions should still be reviewed.
- D) Red should be listed before green.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Reconcile the parts against the stated whole: 7 + 3 + 1 = 11, against "twelve in flight". One transition has fallen out of the classification entirely — and unclassified is more concerning than red, because red is at least being watched. The most dangerous items in any portfolio are the ones that vanish from summaries: a transition nobody is rating is a transition nobody is escalating. Option C is a defensible governance opinion but not an arithmetic defect; options A and D are style judgements. The lead-level reflex this trains: before engaging with a report's content, verify its accounting — every item in exactly one category, categories summing to the total. Thirty seconds, and it catches the class of error that status processes are structurally blind to, because status processes report what is on their list, never what has slipped off it.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Prioritisation across the portfolio**

Monday, 8:30. Four items compete for your first hours:

1. A supplier on your largest transition has emailed overnight invoking a contract clause to delay their deliverable by three weeks; your commercial team asks for your impact analysis by Wednesday.
2. The go/no-go review for a mid-sized transition is at 14:00 today; your delegate prepared the recommendation, and you have not yet read it.
3. An operations manager reports that two of your transition managers gave their teams contradictory instructions about the shared release calendar, and the teams have stopped booking slots pending clarity.
4. Your quarterly portfolio report to the digital board is due Friday.

Which sequencing is most defensible?

- A) 3, 2, 1, 4
- B) 2, 3, 1, 4
- C) 1, 2, 3, 4
- D) 3, 2, 4, 1

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Item 3 first: a contradiction between your own managers has operationally frozen a shared resource — teams have stopped booking slots — and it is likely resolvable in minutes with a single clarifying message; nothing else on the list is both this quick and this actively blocking other people. Item 2 next: a 14:00 governance gate at which your delegate's recommendation carries your authority — reading it this morning is due diligence on a same-day hard deadline, and delegation without review at a decision gate is abdication, not empowerment. Item 1 has a Wednesday deadline for substantial analysis: it owns the afternoon and tomorrow. Item 4 is Friday. Option B reverses the first two, leaving the calendar frozen for hours to read a paper the morning still has room for; option C mistakes the biggest item for the most urgent; option D relegates the contract analysis behind a Friday report. The principle: unblock-others-fast first, then same-day gates, then near deadlines by size, then far deadlines — impact-weighted urgency, with speed-to-resolve as the tiebreaker.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Deduction across supplier commitments**

Three suppliers feed one cutover. Supplier X commits to deliver their component "within ten working days of contract variation signature". Supplier Y commits to start integration "within five working days of receiving X's component". Supplier Z commits to complete security verification "within five working days of integration completing", and integration takes Y exactly ten working days. The variation was signed on day 0. Assuming every supplier hits the latest date its commitment permits, on which working day does security verification complete?

- A) Day 25
- B) Day 30
- C) Day 20
- D) Day 35

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Chain the worst-case commitments. X delivers by day 10. Y starts integration by day 15 (10 + 5) and, taking ten days, completes by day 25. Z completes verification by day 30 (25 + 5). Option A forgets Y's five-day start window; option C compounds that omission; option D double-counts somewhere in the chain. Two lead-level observations travel with the arithmetic. First, "within N days of [event]" clauses chain multiplicatively across suppliers — three modest-sounding commitments quietly produce a thirty-day worst case, which is why cross-supplier co-ordination cannot be read one contract at a time. Second, planning against anything better than the worst case is planning on goodwill: suppliers are entitled to their full windows, and a cutover date that requires them not to use those windows is a hope, not a plan. Your sole responsibility for co-ordination across suppliers makes this chain — and its habit of being computed by nobody in particular — specifically yours.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Capacity logic with a remedial decision**

A shared integration platform serves four of your in-flight transitions. Its sustained utilisation is 71%; the platform team's standard requires remedial action when sustained utilisation exceeds 75%. The four transitions will each add load at go-live: transition 1 adds 3 points (going live in 4 weeks), transition 2 adds 4 points (6 weeks), transition 3 adds 2 points (8 weeks), transition 4 adds 5 points (12 weeks). Additional capacity takes ten weeks to provision. When does utilisation first breach the standard, and is the ten-week provisioning lead time compatible with waiting for the breach?

- A) Breach at week 6 (71+3+4 = 78); no — provisioning must start now, since waiting for the breach means capacity arrives at week 16, ten weeks late.
- B) Breach at week 12 when all transitions are live; yes — provision at week 2.
- C) Breach at week 4 (74); provisioning is already too late.
- D) No breach — each individual addition is small enough to keep utilisation under 75%.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Accumulate the load against the calendar. Week 4: 71 + 3 = 74 — under the standard (option C misreads 74 as a breach of a 75 threshold). Week 6: 74 + 4 = 78 — first breach. Weeks 8 and 12 worsen it (80, 85). Option D commits the incremental fallacy: each addition is individually modest, but utilisation is cumulative, which is precisely why shared platforms fail — every project's load is reasonable and the sum is not. Now the planning half: capacity takes ten weeks; the breach is at week 6; starting provisioning at the breach delivers capacity at week 16, ten weeks after it was needed. Compatibility requires starting now (capacity at week 10 — still four weeks late against the week-6 breach, which argues for also examining whether transition 2's go-live can follow the capacity rather than precede it). This is your **Availability and capacity management** duty in full: identify the issue by projection, initiate remedial action while the lead time still fits, and co-ordinate go-live sequencing with infrastructure reality — the arithmetic is simple; the discipline of running it across everyone's transitions at once is the job.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Structural inconsistency across documents**

Three documents describe the same transition. The project plan says: "Cutover weekend: 14–15 November; operations acceptance sign-off due 7 November." The operations readiness checklist says: "Sign-off will be granted only after the final disaster-recovery test, scheduled 11 November." The supplier's statement of work says: "Disaster-recovery test environment available from 10 November." Which is the structural problem?

- A) The supplier's environment comes too late for the DR test.
- B) The DR test (11 November) postdates the sign-off deadline (7 November) that depends on it — the plan's milestone is impossible as scheduled, regardless of how well each team performs.
- C) Cutover should not be on a weekend.
- D) There is no problem — all dates precede the cutover.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Build the dependency chain across the documents: sign-off requires the DR test (checklist); the DR test is on 11 November and cannot be earlier than the environment's 10 November availability (statement of work — so the test date itself is internally fine, eliminating A); but the plan demands sign-off by 7 November, four days before its own prerequisite can occur. No team is failing; the documents jointly specify an impossibility. Option D checks each date against cutover and misses that the inconsistency is between intermediate milestones. This error class — each document locally coherent, the set globally infeasible — is invisible to every individual document owner and is exactly why your role exists: the project reads its plan, operations reads its checklist, the supplier reads its statement of work, and only the person solely responsible for co-ordination reads all three against each other. The practical fix (move sign-off after 11 November, or negotiate an earlier DR window) is trivial once seen and expensive when discovered on 7 November.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Sequencing three transitions through one freeze**

An organisation-wide change freeze runs weeks 9–10 (inclusive). Three transitions need go-live slots, at most one go-live per week: transition A needs two consecutive non-freeze weeks for go-live plus stabilisation, and its earliest readiness is week 6. Transition B needs one week, is ready from week 8, and must go live before week 12 for contractual reasons. Transition C needs one week, is ready from week 5, but reuses A's engineers, so it can go live no earlier than two clear weeks after A's go-live week (A live in week 6 means C in week 9 at the earliest). Freeze weeks cannot host any go-live or stabilisation week. What is the earliest feasible schedule?

- A) A: weeks 6–7; B: week 8; C: week 9
- B) A: weeks 6–7; B: week 8; C: week 11
- C) A: weeks 7–8; B: week 11; C: week 12
- D) A: weeks 6–7; B: week 11; C: week 8

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Place the largest footprint first. A needs two consecutive non-freeze weeks from week 6 onward: weeks 6–7 work; 8–9 and 9–10 would put stabilisation or go-live inside the freeze, and starting at week 11 would be needlessly late. With A live in week 6, C's earliest permitted week is week 9 — but week 9 is frozen, and so is week 10, pushing C to week 11. B is ready from week 8 and must precede week 12; week 8 is free and beats week 11, and the one-per-week rule creates no conflict since C now sits in week 11... which forces a check: B and C cannot both take week 11, and they do not need to — B takes week 8, C takes week 11. That is option B. Option A ignores the freeze (C in week 9); option D violates C's engineer constraint (week 8 is not two clear weeks after week 6); option C delays A a week for no reason and then pays for it twice — B slides to week 11 and C to week 12, and week 12 breaches nothing but delivers everything later than necessary. The method: place the most constrained item first, propagate its consequences through the resource-sharing constraints, then slot the flexible items into what remains — and always test the winning schedule against every constraint once assembled, because pairwise-feasible placements can still collide in the aggregate. That final whole-schedule check is precisely what portfolio co-ordination adds over project planning.

**Question 9 (hard) — Diagnosing a cross-supplier pattern**

Over six months, transitions involving supplier P show these features: deliverables arrive on the contractual due date 94% of the time; post-delivery defect rates run three times higher than your other suppliers; P's staff turnover on your account has been 40% in the period; and P's project managers consistently decline your offer of early informal design reviews, citing "efficiency". Which hypothesis best integrates all four observations, and what intervention does it suggest?

- A) P is contractually compliant and simply less skilled; replace P at the next procurement.
- B) P is optimising to the letter of its contract — hitting dates by compressing quality, with churned staff and no early feedback loops making defects structurally likely; the intervention is to renegotiate the incentive structure (quality gates and defect liability alongside date compliance) and make early reviews contractually expected rather than informally offered.
- C) P's turnover is the sole cause; require named-individual continuity clauses.
- D) Your acceptance testing is too strict for P's deliverables.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Integrate, don't cherry-pick. The 94% date compliance alongside triple defect rates is the signature of letter-optimisation: when only dates are contractually sharp, dates absorb all the pressure and quality becomes the release valve. High turnover contributes (knowledge walks out) and declined early reviews complete the mechanism (defects that informal review would catch at design instead surface post-delivery, where they are contractual arguments rather than conversations). Option B explains all four observations through one mechanism — incentives — and intervenes on the mechanism rather than its symptoms. Option A treats a structural behaviour as a capability deficit and reaches for the most expensive, slowest remedy first. Option C elevates one contributing factor to sole cause; continuity clauses without incentive change retain the same pressures with less flexibility. Option D has no support in the stem — other suppliers pass the same testing. The lead-level principles: suppliers behave as their contracts incentivise, not as their brochures promise; patterns across compliance, quality, staffing, and engagement style should be read as one system; and your proactive-problem-search duty (**Ownership and topic**) extends to commercial structures, because the cheapest defect fix on a multi-supplier portfolio is usually a renegotiated incentive.

**Question 10 (hard) — Life-cycle reasoning about a transition decision**

A programme proposes transitioning a new case system in two phases: phase 1 migrates active cases only (280,000 records, clean data, three months); phase 2 migrates historical cases (1.9 million records, poor data quality, timeline unknown) "after phase 1 stabilises". The legacy system costs £40,000 per month to run and cannot be decommissioned until phase 2 completes. The programme's benefits case claims legacy decommissioning savings from month 4. Which is the most important structural observation?

- A) Phase 1's three months is optimistic for 280,000 records.
- B) The benefits case books decommissioning savings from month 4, but decommissioning depends on phase 2, which has no committed timeline — the savings are tied to the phase the plan leaves unbounded, so the benefits case is structurally unsupported as written, and the legacy run-cost continues indefinitely at £40,000/month.
- C) Historical data should never be migrated.
- D) Two-phase migrations are inherently riskier than single-phase.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Follow the money through the dependency chain. Savings require decommissioning; decommissioning requires phase 2 complete; phase 2 has "timeline unknown". The benefits case therefore claims, from month 4, savings whose enabling event is unscheduled — and every month of phase-2 drift costs £40,000 against those booked savings. This is not an argument against phasing (phased migration of clean-first data is often the right engineering call, so C and D overreach into generic assertions) — it is an argument that the plan and the benefits case describe different projects. Option A is a fair challenge but a judgement call the stem gives no evidence to settle; B is a structural defect visible from the documents alone. The observation is quintessentially yours: your **Service focus** skill spans the entire life cycle including decommissioning, and the pattern — visible, well-managed phase 1; unbounded, savings-bearing phase 2 — is among the most common in government transitions, precisely because phase 1 is where the demonstrable progress lives and phase 2 is where the benefits do. Insisting that phase 2 acquire a committed, resourced timeline before the benefits case is accepted is the transition leadership move that keeps benefits honest.

**Question 11 (hard) — Reasoning about your own decision under uncertainty**

Your flagship cutover is Saturday. Wednesday evening, the supplier reports their final performance fix reduced response times in test — but their test environment has just been found to run on newer hardware than production, overstating all performance results by an unknown factor. A production-representative re-test needs four days you do not have. Postponement costs a six-week delay (the next available window) and significant credibility; proceeding risks a degraded service for 40,000 daily users. Monitoring can detect degradation within an hour of go-live, and rollback is tested and takes two hours. Which reasoning is soundest?

- A) Proceed — the fix probably works, and six weeks is too costly.
- B) Postpone — never go live with unquantified performance risk.
- C) Reframe the decision around the tested rollback: the effective worst case of proceeding is roughly three hours of degraded service before recovery completes, against a certain six-week delay for postponement; verify the rollback path and monitoring thresholds are genuinely ready, define the rollback trigger criteria in advance, brief the service owner on this as the actual risk trade — and let the go/no-go be made on that basis.
- D) Proceed, but only if the supplier accepts contractual liability for any degradation.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Options A and B share a defect: they weigh "unquantified risk" against "certain cost" as though the risk's consequence were unbounded — but the scenario's own facts bound it. Tested rollback plus one-hour detection converts "the fix may not work in production" from an open-ended catastrophe into an approximately three-hour worst case (detection + decision + two-hour rollback), which is a comparable, priceable alternative to a six-week delay. That reframing — from "how likely is failure?" (unknowable by Saturday) to "how expensive is failure, given our recovery position?" (computable now) — is the soundest available reasoning, and it is why transition disciplines invest in rollback: reversibility is what buys decision freedom under uncertainty. Note C's conditions, which do the real work: verify the rollback and monitoring are genuinely ready (an untested safety net changes the whole calculation), pre-commit the trigger criteria (deciding thresholds during an incident is how three hours becomes nine), and brief the decision owner on the true trade. Option D manages the department's money, not its users' Saturday — liability compensates; it does not restore service. The principle to keep: when you cannot shrink uncertainty in the time available, shrink the cost of being wrong — and make the decision explicitly about that cost.

**Question 12 (hard) — Portfolio triage under a sudden constraint**

A central directive lands: for the next eight weeks, the operations team can support at most one go-live per fortnight (four slots total) due to a major infrastructure programme. You have six transitions expecting go-live in the window: (i) a statutory service with a legally mandated start date in week 3; (ii) a replacement for a system whose supplier support expires in week 6; (iii) a high-profile ministerial commitment with "expectation" of week 4; (iv) an internal efficiency service saving £25,000/month, ready now; (v) a service whose delay pushes a dependent local-authority programme into its own freeze, costing that programme three months; (vi) a well-progressed service with flexible timing. Which allocation principle produces the most defensible schedule?

- A) First-ready, first-scheduled: reward the teams that hit their dates.
- B) Seniority of sponsor: the ministerial commitment takes slot one.
- C) Constraint-type triage: hard external constraints first in deadline order — statutory (week 3), supplier-support expiry (week 6), then the dependency that cascades three months of cost onto another organisation (week 5 area); the ministerial expectation is negotiated against evidence, and the efficiency service and flexible service queue behind, with the displaced go-lives given committed post-window slots.
- D) Equal shares: give each transition two-thirds of a slot by splitting weekends.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Four slots, six claimants: the allocation principle matters more than any single allocation, because it is the principle you will defend to five disappointed sponsors. Option C triages by constraint type: legal mandates and support expiries are hard walls with dates (miss them and the organisation is non-compliant or unsupported — not merely embarrassed); the cross-organisational cascade is next because its cost is large, external, and borne by a partner who did nothing wrong; a ministerial "expectation" is a strong preference from a powerful stakeholder — real, but negotiable with evidence in a way statute is not, and senior stakeholders are better served by an honest constraint conversation in week 1 than a missed statutory date in week 3. The efficiency saving (£25,000/month is £50,000 per fortnight of delay — quantify it in the paper) and the flexible service queue with committed follow-on slots, which converts "displaced" from grievance into schedule. Option A rewards readiness while ignoring consequence — a queue discipline, not a triage. Option B converts hierarchy into priority, which fails precisely when the statutory deadline and the ministerial preference collide. Option D is arithmetic satire — but its instinct (spread the pain visibly) survives in C's committed follow-on slots. The reusable lead principle: under sudden scarcity, publish the triage principle before the schedule, order by hardness of constraint then size and externality of consequence, and convert every displacement into a commitment. Defensibility is a property of principles, not of outcomes.

### Preparation tips

- **Practise on your portfolio's real documents.** Take three artefacts describing one transition — plan, checklist, supplier statement — and build the cross-document dependency chain deliberately. The structural inconsistencies you find are both practice and product.
- **Verbalise policy precedence before applying it.** Specific over general; unconditional over conditional; "regardless of" as an override marker. Most policy misapplication is precedence error, in tests and in release management.
- **Compute worst-case chains, not expected-case ones.** "Within N days of X" clauses across suppliers multiply; practise chaining them until the reflex is worst-case-first.
- **Read failure data for structure before rate.** Partition by time, place, team, supplier before averaging. The cluster is the clue; the average is the camouflage.
- **Rehearse bounded-worst-case reasoning.** For any risky decision, ask: what bounds the downside, and is the bound real (tested rollback) or aspirational? Decision quality under uncertainty is mostly downside engineering.
- **Time-box ruthlessly.** Senior norm groups punish sunk-cost behaviour on stubborn items. Flag, move, return — portfolio discipline applied to a question set.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Averaging away the signal.** A 37.5% failure rate and a 0-versus-3 cluster are the same data; only one of them tells you where to look.
- **Applying the convenient rule rather than the governing one.** Impact classification does not outrank an unconditional early-life clause. Precedence first, then application.
- **Trusting reports whose parts don't sum.** Verify the accounting before the content — the transition missing from the classification is the one that hurts you.
- **Planning on suppliers' goodwill.** Commitments chain at their limits; schedules built on better-than-contractual performance are hopes wearing Gantt charts.
- **The incremental fallacy on shared resources.** Every project's load is individually reasonable; the platform fails anyway. Accumulate before you approve.
- **Treating unquantified risk as unbounded risk.** Recovery position bounds the downside; compute the bound before choosing between proceed and postpone — and verify the bound is tested, not aspirational.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures the accuracy and confidence with which you handle quantitative information in realistic working contexts. The mathematical toolkit is deliberately modest — percentages, ratios, averages, rates, compound change, and disciplined table-and-chart reading — because the difficulty in professional numeracy is rarely the operations; it is choosing the right operation, the right base, and the right level of scepticism for the numbers in front of you.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 20 to 30 minutes with 15 to 25 multiple-choice questions built around small data sets. Calculators are almost universally permitted; senior-role variants are normed against senior comparison groups and lean toward multi-step questions where the challenge is structuring the calculation rather than performing it.

At lead level, the case for numeric assessment is straightforward: you preside over numbers produced by other people. Suppliers present pass rates; programmes present benefits projections; platform teams present capacity forecasts; your own managers present portfolio dashboards. Every one of those numbers arrives with an agenda attached — usually benign, occasionally not, always shaped by what its producer wanted to show. Your value is the ability to recompute the headline from the raw counts, spot the averaged-away cluster, notice the denominator that changed between two slides, and project the trend the deck stopped one quarter short of. And because you initiate remedial action on capacity, negotiate with suppliers, and defend portfolio decisions to boards, your own numbers must withstand the same scrutiny you apply to everyone else's.

The questions below therefore emphasise the numeric situations your role actually produces: multi-supplier aggregation, capacity and cost projection, benefits arithmetic, weighted portfolio measures, and the forensic reading of trend tables. Nearly all reduce to three moves — part-of-whole, comparison, division-by-count — executed in the right order with the right bases. Master the structure and the arithmetic follows.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Multi-source aggregation** maps to your sole responsibility for co-ordination across projects, suppliers, and service teams: portfolio truth is assembled from unequal-sized parts, and correct aggregation (counts before percentages) is what makes your dashboards honest.
- **Trend analysis and projection** maps to your **Problem management** skill — investigating patterns and trends quantitatively — and your **Ownership and topic** duty to search proactively for problems before they occur: a projected breach is a found problem.
- **Cost and benefits arithmetic** maps to your **Service focus** skill across the entire life cycle: run-costs, decommissioning savings, and delay costs are the currency in which life-cycle decisions are argued, and to your duty to communicate service benefits credibly.
- **Change and release metrics** map to your **Change management** skill: managing high-impact change includes reading failure rates, and ensuring release policies are applied includes measuring whether they are working.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Recomputing a portfolio headline**

Your dashboard claims "91% of portfolio milestones met this quarter". The underlying data: programme A met 34 of 36 milestones; programme B met 22 of 26; programme C met 17 of 18. Is the headline accurate?

- A) Yes — the rate is 91.25%
- B) Yes, almost exactly — the rate is 91.3%
- C) No — the rate is 87.5%
- D) No — the rate is 94%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Combine counts, then divide: met = 34 + 22 + 17 = 73; total = 36 + 26 + 18 = 80; 73 ÷ 80 = 0.9125 = 91.25%, which rounds to 91.3% — the headline's "91%" is fair. Confirming a correct headline matters as much as catching a false one: your scepticism should be calibrated, not performative, and a dashboard that survives spot-checks earns trust that makes your challenges land harder when they come. Note the method discipline: the per-programme rates (94.4%, 84.6%, 94.4%) averaged naively give 91.1% — coincidentally close here because the groups are similar sizes, but the counts-first method is the one that stays correct when they are not.

**Question 2 (easy) — Cost of delay**

A transition's delay keeps a legacy system running at £38,000 per month and defers efficiency savings of £22,000 per month. What is the total cost of a five-month delay?

- A) £190,000
- B) £110,000
- C) £300,000
- D) £60,000

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The monthly cost of delay is both components: £38,000 continuing run-cost + £22,000 deferred savings = £60,000 per month. Over five months: £60,000 × 5 = £300,000. Option A prices only the legacy run-cost; option B only the lost savings; option D is one month, not five. The double-counting worry some readers feel here is misplaced: the run-cost is cash out of the door and the deferred saving is cash that fails to arrive, and both are real relative to the on-time counterfactual. Cost-of-delay figures are the strongest single number in portfolio negotiation — "each month this slips costs £60,000" reframes a scheduling discussion as a value discussion, which is exactly where a lead should hold it.

**Question 3 (easy) — Weighted portfolio risk score**

Your portfolio risk model weights transitions by user base: transition X (500,000 users) has risk score 4; transition Y (250,000 users) has risk score 8; transition Z (250,000 users) has risk score 2. Using user-base weights, what is the portfolio's weighted average risk score?

- A) 4.67
- B) 4.5
- C) 5.0
- D) 3.5

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Weights: X = 500k of 1,000k total = 0.5; Y = 0.25; Z = 0.25. Weighted average = (4 × 0.5) + (8 × 0.25) + (2 × 0.25) = 2 + 2 + 0.5 = 4.5. Option A is the unweighted mean ((4 + 8 + 2) ÷ 3 ≈ 4.67), which treats a half-million-user transition as equal to a quarter-million-user one. Sense-check: the answer must lie between the extremes (2 and 8), pulled toward the heaviest-weighted score (X's 4) — 4.5 fits. The design point matters more than the arithmetic: weighting by user base encodes the judgement that risk to more people counts for more, which is your **User focus** skill built into the measurement itself. Portfolio metrics are policy in numeric form; a lead who cannot audit the weighting cannot audit the policy.

**Question 4 (easy) — Release failure rate against a policy threshold**

Your release policy triggers a process review if the rolling change failure rate exceeds 8%. The last 150 changes include 13 failures. Is the review triggered?

- A) No — the rate is 7.8%
- B) Yes — the rate is about 8.7%
- C) No — the rate is exactly 8%
- D) Yes — the rate is 13%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 13 ÷ 150 = 0.0867 ≈ 8.7%, which exceeds 8% — the review is triggered. A quick route: 8% of 150 = 12, so the threshold sits at 12 failures; 13 breaches it by one. Option D mistakes the count for the rate; options A and C require arithmetic errors. The threshold-as-count conversion (8% of 150 = 12) is worth cultivating: it turns an abstract percentage policy into a concrete tripwire you can watch approaching — at 11 failures you are one incident from a mandated review, which is management information the percentage form hides. Ensuring release policies are applied, your explicit duty, includes running the arithmetic that tells you when a policy clause has actually fired — thresholds nobody computes are thresholds that do not exist.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Compound growth against a capacity ceiling**

A platform processes 2.0 million transactions monthly, growing 8% per month compound. Its tested ceiling is 2.9 million transactions monthly. After how many complete months does demand first exceed the ceiling?

- A) 4 months
- B) 5 months
- C) 6 months
- D) 9 months

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Compound forward: month 1: 2.0 × 1.08 = 2.16m; month 2: 2.333m; month 3: 2.519m; month 4: 2.721m; month 5: 2.939m — first breach of 2.9m. Option A stops a month early (2.721m is still under); option D likely comes from simple growth arithmetic done wrong (simple 8% adds 0.16m/month, breaching during month 6 — option C is the simple-growth answer). The compound-versus-simple gap is small at month two and decisive by month five, which is precisely why demand forecasts understate late-period load when they assume linearity. For your remedial-action duty, the number that matters is not "month 5" but "month 5 minus provisioning lead time": if capacity takes three months to add, the decision point is month 2 — computing backward from the breach to the last responsible decision moment is the whole discipline of proactive capacity management.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Aggregating supplier quality across contracts**

Supplier M delivered 12 releases with a 25% defect-escape rate on a small contract, and 4 releases with a 5% rate on your flagship. Supplier N delivered 8 releases at 15% across all work. Counting each release equally, which supplier has the better (lower) overall defect-escape rate, and by how much?

- A) N, by 5 percentage points
- B) M, by 5 percentage points
- C) They are equal at 15%
- D) N, by 2 percentage points

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Weight M's rates by releases: (12 × 25% + 4 × 5%) ÷ 16 = (300 + 20) ÷ 16 = 320 ÷ 16 = 20%. N's rate is 15% across its 8 releases. So N is better by 5 percentage points — option A. The trap is option C: averaging M's two contract-level rates (25% and 5%) gives 15%, apparently equal to N — but that treats a 12-release contract and a 4-release contract as equal evidence. M's small contract dominates its true average because it carries three-quarters of M's releases; the flagship's excellent 5% is diluted to a 20% overall. The lesson layered in: if you had weighted by contract (25% and 5% averaged = 15%), M would appear equal to N — and that is exactly how M's account manager will present it. Which weighting is "right" depends on the question: per-release weighting answers "how likely is M's next release to escape defects?", which is the transition-planning question. Choose the weighting to fit the decision, state it explicitly, and expect the party with the worse number to prefer the other one.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Availability across a chain of dependent services**

A citizen journey traverses three services in sequence; the journey succeeds only if all three are up. Each service independently achieves 99.0% availability. What is the availability of the end-to-end journey, and what does it mean at 100,000 journeys per month?

- A) 99.0% — about 1,000 failed journeys
- B) 97.0% — about 3,000 failed journeys
- C) 98.0% — about 2,000 failed journeys
- D) 99.7% — about 300 failed journeys

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Chained availability multiplies: 0.99 × 0.99 × 0.99 = 0.9703 ≈ 97.0%. Failure rate ≈ 3%, so roughly 3,000 of 100,000 monthly journeys fail. (The precise figure: 1 − 0.9703 = 2.97%, ≈ 2,970 journeys.) Option A commits the commonest availability error — assuming the chain inherits the components' figure; option C adds the two extra services' shortfalls but misses that all three contribute; option D bears no relation to the arithmetic. The strategic content: three services each meeting a respectable 99.0% SLA jointly deliver an end-to-end experience that would breach a 98% journey-level target — component SLAs can all be green while the user's journey is red. This is your **Service focus** skill in its numeric form: seeing groups of services as the user traverses them, and arguing for journey-level measures in acceptance criteria, because per-service metrics structurally overstate what users experience.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Benefits realisation shortfall**

A transition's benefits case promised £480,000 of annual savings: £300,000 from decommissioning, £120,000 from reduced manual processing, £60,000 from lower incident volumes. One year on: decommissioning delivered in full; manual-processing savings achieved 65%; incident-related savings achieved 40%. What percentage of the promised total was realised?

- A) 68%
- B) 84%
- C) 79%
- D) 88%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Compute each stream: decommissioning £300,000 (100%); manual processing £120,000 × 0.65 = £78,000; incidents £60,000 × 0.40 = £24,000. Total realised: 300 + 78 + 24 = £402,000. Against the promise: 402 ÷ 480 = 0.8375 ≈ 84%. Option A averages the three percentages (100 + 65 + 40) ÷ 3 ≈ 68%, ignoring that the fully delivered stream is also the largest — the unweighted average understates performance here by sixteen points, and in a benefits review that gap is the difference between "broadly delivered" and "significantly short". The composition tells the managerial story the total hides: the hard-edged saving (decommissioning — a system either runs or it does not) delivered fully, while both behaviour-dependent savings underperformed, which is the near-universal pattern. Scrutinise behaviour-dependent benefits hardest at the business-case stage; they are the ones that arrive at 40–65%.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Reading a multi-series trend table**

| Month | Changes implemented | Emergency changes | Failed changes |
|---|---|---|---|
| April | 80 | 6 | 5 |
| May | 88 | 9 | 6 |
| June | 96 | 14 | 7 |
| July | 104 | 22 | 8 |

Which statement best captures the most decision-relevant trend?

- A) Failure rates are stable at 6–8%, so the change process is healthy.
- B) Total change volume is growing 10% monthly, requiring more CAB capacity.
- C) Emergency changes are growing far faster than volume — from 7.5% to 21% of all changes in four months — signalling that work is increasingly bypassing normal planning; the failure rate's stability (6.3%, 6.8%, 7.3%, 7.7% — gently rising) is reassurance the emergency trend will eventually erode.
- D) All three series are rising, which is expected in a growing organisation.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Normalise each series against volume before judging. Failures: 5/80 = 6.3%, 6/88 = 6.8%, 7/96 = 7.3%, 8/104 = 7.7% — gently rising, not yet alarming. Emergencies: 6/80 = 7.5%, 9/88 = 10.2%, 14/96 = 14.6%, 22/104 = 21.2% — near-tripling as a share in four months. That is the decision-relevant signal: emergency routes exist for genuine urgency, and a fifth of all changes travelling through them means planning is failing upstream — dates being missed and recovered via the expedited route, scrutiny being systematically reduced. Option A reads only the series that looks fine; option B is true and secondary; option D commits the growth-excuses-everything fallacy — proportional growth would hold the emergency share at 7.5%, and 21% is not proportional. The layered point: today's stable failure rate is partly borrowed against tomorrow's, because emergency changes fail more often once volume normalises. Your duty to ensure release processes are applied is served exactly by this decomposition — the aggregate looked healthy; the shares did not.

**Question 10 (hard) — The denominator shift**

A supplier's two consecutive monthly reports state: March: "94% of incidents resolved within SLA (of 380 incidents)". April: "96% of incidents resolved within SLA (of 310 incidents, excluding the 45 incidents reclassified as service requests under the new categorisation agreed with your service desk)". The report does not say how the 45 reclassified items would have performed against SLA under March rules. What can honestly be concluded about April's performance on a March-comparable basis?

- A) 96% — the reclassification was agreed, so April's figure stands.
- B) Between 83.9% (if all 45 would have breached) and 96.6% (if none would have), so the reported improvement from 94% is unverifiable without knowing the reclassified items' outcomes — the comparison basis changed mid-series.
- C) 90% exactly.
- D) 85.5% exactly.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** April on its own terms: 96% of 310 = 297.6 ≈ 298 resolved within SLA. On a March-comparable basis the denominator is 310 + 45 = 355, and the numerator depends on how the 45 would have fared: if all would have breached, 298 ÷ 355 ≈ 83.9%; if all would have met SLA, 343 ÷ 355 ≈ 96.6%. The truthful statement is the range — and therefore the honest conclusion is that April's "improvement" over March's 94% cannot be verified from the report: the 94%-to-96% comparison spans a definitional change, and the reclassified population's outcomes are precisely the data the new report no longer shows. Options C and D manufacture false precision; option A accepts that an agreed reclassification (which may be entirely legitimate) also legitimises a broken time series, which it does not — legitimacy of the change and comparability of the metric are separate questions. The lead-level reflexes: when a denominator changes mid-series, demand the metric restated on both bases for the transition period; and note which direction the redefinition moved the number — redefinitions that flatter their reporter deserve arithmetic, not suspicion alone.

**Question 11 (hard) — Provisioning economics under uncertainty**

Storage for a growing service can be expanded now for £90,000, or expanded in an emergency later for £150,000 plus an expected £60,000 of incident costs if growth continues and capacity is breached. Your analysts estimate a 70% probability that growth continues at the current rate (breach in roughly five months) and 30% that a planned archiving feature (due in three months) flattens growth and no breach occurs. Comparing expected costs, what is the case for expanding now versus waiting?

- A) Expand now: £90,000 certain beats the alternative's expected cost of £147,000 (0.7 × £210,000).
- B) Wait: there is a 30% chance of spending nothing, and £90,000 is certain if you act now.
- C) The options are equal in expectation.
- D) Expand now, but only because emergencies are always worse.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Price both branches. Expand now: £90,000, certain. Wait: with probability 0.7, growth continues and you pay £150,000 + £60,000 = £210,000; with probability 0.3, the archiving feature lands and you pay £0. Expected cost of waiting: 0.7 × 210,000 + 0.3 × 0 = £147,000 — against £90,000 for acting now, a £57,000 expected saving for early action. Option B correctly describes the branch probabilities and then reasons from hope: a 30% chance of zero does not outweigh a 70% chance of £210,000, and stating both numbers is what stops "there's a chance we won't need it" surviving in a decision meeting. Option D reaches the right act by superstition — always-expand is wrong whenever the probabilities reverse (at 30% continuation, waiting expects £63,000 and wins). Two refinements a board will test you on: the timeline (breach in five months, archiving proof at three) offers a real option — wait three months for information if provisioning still fits in the remaining two, which is why decision timing, not just decision direction, is the lead's craft; and expected-value reasoning assumes the organisation can absorb the bad branch — £210,000 plus a capacity incident on a citizen service may be intolerable at any probability, which is a judgement, not an equation. Your remedial-action duty is exercised exactly here: initiating action early enough that the cheap branch is still available.

**Question 12 (hard) — The portfolio dashboard that disagrees with itself**

Your monthly dashboard reports: "Portfolio SLA attainment: 92.4%. Component services: service 1: 96% (12,000 measured interactions); service 2: 89% (30,000); service 3: 95% (8,000)." Verify the portfolio figure — is it consistent with its components?

- A) Yes — 92.4% is the correct weighted result.
- B) No — the weighted figure is about 91.6%; the reported 92.4% overstates attainment by 0.8 points, and the discrepancy should be queried before the dashboard circulates.
- C) No — the correct figure is the simple average, 93.3%.
- D) It cannot be checked without the raw data.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Weight by interactions: service 1: 0.96 × 12,000 = 11,520; service 2: 0.89 × 30,000 = 26,700; service 3: 0.95 × 8,000 = 7,600. Total within SLA: 11,520 + 26,700 + 7,600 = 45,820, of 50,000 interactions: 45,820 ÷ 50,000 = 0.9164 ≈ 91.6%. The dashboard says 92.4% — a 0.8-point overstatement, and note which direction it errs: toward flattery, most plausibly by under-weighting service 2, the largest and worst-performing component. Option C endorses the simple average (96 + 89 + 95) ÷ 3 = 93.3%, which is wrong in principle at these unequal volumes — service 2 carries 60% of the interactions and must dominate the true figure. Option D is defeatist: the components as published are sufficient to check the aggregate's consistency, which is the entire point of publishing them. The habit: any aggregate whose components are visible can be verified in under a minute, aggregates err toward flattery more often than chance allows, and a lead who circulates an unverified flattering aggregate to a board has staked their credibility on someone else's spreadsheet.

### Preparation tips

- **Verify one aggregate per week.** Any dashboard figure whose components are visible: recompute it. Calibrated trust in your own reporting chain is a leadership asset, and the practice is exactly what senior numeric tests sample.
- **Normalise before comparing.** Counts into rates, series into shares of volume. The emergency-change signal lives in the share, never the count.
- **Chain availabilities and commitments multiplicatively.** Three 99% services are a 97% journey; three "within N days" clauses are their sum at worst case. Practise both until they are reflexes.
- **Compute backward from breaches to decision points.** Breach month minus lead time equals the last responsible moment. Capacity questions — in tests and in budget meetings — are really deadline questions.
- **Price both branches of wait-versus-act.** Probability times consequence, each branch, side by side. Then apply judgement about whether the bad branch is absorbable at all.
- **State your weighting and your denominator aloud.** Per-release or per-contract; including or excluding reclassified items. Most senior-level numeric disputes are definitional disputes wearing arithmetic.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Averaging percentages across unequal groups.** The unweighted mean flattered the benefits case by sixteen points and would flatter supplier M by five. Counts first, always.
- **Reading stable aggregates as healthy systems.** The failure rate held at 6–8% while the emergency share tripled. Decompose before declaring health.
- **Accepting mid-series redefinitions at face value.** A changed denominator breaks comparability regardless of how legitimate the change is. Demand both bases for the transition period.
- **Assuming linearity in compounding phenomena.** Simple-growth arithmetic understates the curve exactly when the ceiling approaches — and overstates your time to act.
- **Treating component SLAs as user experience.** Journey availability is the product, not the minimum, of the chain. Green components, red journeys.
- **Deciding on the good branch.** "30% chance we won't need it" is half a calculation. Price both branches, then decide — and record the calculation, because the 30% will be quoted at you if the archiving feature lands.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment measures precision in reading, interpretation, and inference from written material. At lead level, the material mirrors what crosses your desk: contract schedules and their carefully engineered caveats, policy documents with interacting clauses, board papers whose framing does quiet work on their readers, supplier correspondence written with one eye on future disputes, and the assurance reports on which your own recommendations to senior decision-makers rest.

The core format remains true / false / cannot say — judging statements as stated-or-entailed, contradicted, or unaddressed by a passage — supplemented by best-summary, logical-completion, and clear-writing questions. A typical test runs 15 to 25 minutes over 20 to 30 questions, normed for senior roles against senior comparison groups. The differentiating skill at this level is not vocabulary but discipline under plausibility: senior readers carry deep contextual knowledge, and the test rewards those who can hold that knowledge apart from what a given document actually establishes.

Why employers test this at your level: because you operate at the altitude where words are commitments. A supplier's "we anticipate", a policy's "should" versus "must", a board paper's "on track" defined nowhere — each is a load-bearing ambiguity, and the lead who reads past it inherits the dispute it was built to create. Equally, you are one of your organisation's principal producers of consequential text: transition strategies, portfolio recommendations, escalation letters to supplier executives, and the acceptance frameworks other people are held to. The verbal assessment samples both directions — whether you extract exactly what documents commit to, and whether you recognise writing that commits exactly what it should.

There is also a leadership layer: your reading errors propagate. When an analyst misreads a report, their manager catches it; when you misread a contract clause in a board paper, the misreading acquires authority. The premium on precise reading rises with seniority precisely because the correction mechanisms above you thin out.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Precise comprehension of commitments** maps to your sole responsibility for co-ordination across suppliers and projects: cross-supplier plans are built from contractual language, and the plan is only as sound as your reading of each clause.
- **True / false / cannot say discipline** maps to your **Ownership and topic** skill — proactively searching for potential problems means cataloguing precisely what is established, what is contradicted, and what remains unknown in every assurance artefact, because the unknowns are where the problems live.
- **Reading policy with interacting clauses** maps to your **Change management** duty to ensure release policies and procedures are applied: application begins with correct parsing, especially of precedence and exception structure.
- **Inference control under expertise** maps to your **Problem management** skill: pattern investigation generates hypotheses, and senior credibility depends on labelling them as hypotheses until documents or data confirm them.
- **Clear-writing judgement** maps to your **Service focus** and **User focus** skills: communicating service benefits to end users, and representing users internally, are exercises in saying exactly what is meant to audiences with no obligation to decode you — and to your **Community collaboration** skill of adapting feedback so it is effective and lasting.
- **Reading framing critically** maps to your board-facing duties: recognising what a paper's structure emphasises, omits, or predecides is the reading skill that keeps governance honest.

### Practice questions

For questions 1 to 5, read this extract from a supplier contract schedule:

> "The Supplier shall deliver the Transition Deliverables by the milestone dates in Annex 4, provided that the Authority has fulfilled its dependencies in Annex 5 by their respective due dates. Where an Authority dependency is delayed, affected milestone dates shall be extended day-for-day, and the Supplier shall notify the Authority of the affected milestones within five working days of the dependency's original due date. Failure to notify within this period shall constitute waiver of the extension for the affected milestones. Liquidated damages apply to Supplier delay only where the milestone date, as extended under this clause, is missed."

**Question 1 (easy) — True / false / cannot say**

Statement: "If the Authority delays a dependency, affected milestone dates are extended day-for-day."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Stated directly: "Where an Authority dependency is delayed, affected milestone dates shall be extended day-for-day." The statement tracks the clause without addition or omission — true. Even at the easy tier, note what precise contract reading involves: the extension is automatic in form ("shall be extended") but, as the next sentence reveals, conditional in practice on the supplier's timely notification. Statements that quote one sentence of a multi-sentence mechanism are frequently true in isolation and misleading in use — the test asks about the sentence; your job asks about the mechanism. Both readings matter, in that order.

**Question 2 (moderate) — True / false / cannot say**

Statement: "A supplier who notifies the Authority of affected milestones seven working days after a dependency's original due date retains the day-for-day extension for those milestones."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The clause requires notification "within five working days of the dependency's original due date", and failure to notify within that period "shall constitute waiver of the extension". Seven working days exceeds five; the extension is waived; the statement asserting retention is contradicted — false. Notice the clause's design: it converts the Authority's delay into the Supplier's procedural burden, and a supplier who misses the notification window loses protection for a delay that was not their fault. Clauses like this are why your co-ordination role must track not only who owes what by when, but who must say what by when — notification obligations are deadlines too, and in disputes they are frequently the decisive ones.

**Question 3 (moderate) — True / false / cannot say**

Statement: "Liquidated damages apply automatically whenever the Supplier misses an Annex 4 milestone date."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The final sentence scopes liquidated damages to misses of "the milestone date, as extended under this clause" — the original Annex 4 date is not the test where an extension has arisen. A supplier can miss the Annex 4 date yet be inside the extended date, attracting no damages; "automatically whenever" is therefore contradicted. The phrase "as extended under this clause" is four words doing heavy commercial work, and reading past it produces exactly the wrong escalation: a lead who threatens damages against a validly extended milestone spends credibility and hands the supplier a grievance. Contract phrases that modify a noun ("the milestone date, as extended") deserve the same attention as whole clauses — modifiers are where the meaning moves.

**Question 4 (moderate) — True / false / cannot say**

Statement: "Annex 5 lists dependencies owed by the Supplier to the Authority."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage describes Annex 5 dependencies as ones "the Authority has fulfilled" — they are owed by the Authority, not to it by the Supplier. The statement reverses the direction of obligation, contradicting the passage — false, not cannot say. Direction-of-obligation errors are among the most consequential misreadings in multi-party transition work: dependency registers, RACI charts, and contract annexes all encode who owes whom, and a reversed reading assigns risk to the wrong balance sheet. When a statement names two parties and a relationship, verify the direction explicitly — the vocabulary of dependency ("fulfilled by", "owed to", "provided that") makes reversals easy to write and easy to miss.

**Question 5 (hard) — Integrating the clause**

The Authority delivers a dependency six working days late. The Supplier notifies affected milestones four working days after the dependency's original due date, and subsequently delivers eight days after the original milestone date. Applying the clause, which is correct?

- A) The Supplier is liable for liquidated damages for eight days of delay.
- B) The Supplier is liable for liquidated damages for two days of delay — the milestone was validly extended six days, and delivery exceeded the extended date by two.
- C) The Supplier has no liability — the Authority caused the original delay.
- D) The extension was waived because notification followed the dependency's due date.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Assemble the mechanism step by step. The dependency was six days late, entitling day-for-day extension: six days. Notification came four working days after the original due date — within the five-day window, so the extension is preserved (option D misreads the window as expiring at the due date itself; the clause allows five working days after it). The milestone therefore moves six days; delivery at eight days late exceeds the extended date by two; damages apply to those two days — option B. Option A ignores the valid extension; option C over-extends the Authority's culpability, which excuses six days, not eight. This is what hard verbal questions at senior level look like: no single sentence answers them — the answer emerges only from operating the clause's full machinery in sequence. It is also, precisely, the pre-escalation analysis your role owes before any damages conversation with a supplier executive: compute the position under the clause as written, or negotiate from fiction.

- B) False

Reading precisely means crediting the paper with exactly what it said, and noticing exactly what it did not.

**Question 7 (moderate) — True / false / cannot say**

Statement: "The independent assurance review found no significant issues."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The paper says the review "has reported" and that "its recommendations are being incorporated" — nothing about what the review found, how severe its findings were, or how many recommendations it made. A review with minor suggestions and a review with red-rated findings would both fit the sentence as drafted; that is arguably why it is drafted this way. The finding's absence is not evidence of absence: cannot say. The governance instinct this trains is the one your board-facing role requires — "recommendations are being incorporated" is the classic formula for acknowledging a review without disclosing it, and the correct response is neither suspicion nor acceptance but the question the drafting has made necessary: what did the review actually find, and may the board see it before approving the tranche?

**Question 8 (moderate) — True / false / cannot say**

Statement: "The board has already released the funding tranche that the paper discusses."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The paper "asks" the board to "approve the release of the next funding tranche" — a request for a future decision. If the tranche were already released, there would be nothing to approve; the request's existence contradicts the statement — false. Worth noticing en route: the paper couples "note progress" and "approve funding" into a single motion, a framing that invites approval as though it followed automatically from noting. A board (or a lead reviewing the paper before submission) can decouple them — noting progress while withholding approval pending the assurance findings from question 7. Reading governance papers means reading their requested decisions as drafted choices, and remembering that the coupling of items is itself a choice someone made.

**Question 9 (hard) — What the paper establishes**

Judged strictly on the passage, which statement most accurately characterises the support the paper provides for releasing the funding tranche?

- A) Strong — on-track status, stabilised supplier, normal risk, and a completed assurance review all point to release.
- B) The paper asserts on-track status and offers one verifiable fact (October receipts) plus three unverifiable characterisations (stabilised performance, "consistent" risk profile, recommendations "being incorporated") — and it withholds the assurance findings that bear most directly on the funding decision; the support as documented is thin, whatever the underlying reality.
- C) None — the paper is misleading and the programme is in trouble.
- D) The support is irrelevant since funding decisions are financial, not delivery, matters.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Audit the paper's evidential texture claim by claim. "On track" — asserted, criteria undisclosed. "Stabilised following interventions" — a characterisation whose baseline and measure are not given. "Consistent with comparable programmes" — comparison set unnamed, so unverifiable as offered. "Recommendations being incorporated" — activity claimed, content withheld. Against these, one concrete verifiable: October deliverables received on schedule. Option B counts precisely this — one fact, three characterisations, one material omission — and draws the calibrated conclusion: the documented support is thin, which is a statement about the paper, not the programme. Option C converts thin documentation into an accusation of trouble the passage cannot support (programmes with healthy realities produce thin papers too — that is why the distinction matters). Option A accepts framing as evidence. Option D is a category error: tranche approval is precisely a delivery-confidence decision. The lead-level skill being sampled: evaluating what a document establishes, as distinct from what it asserts and from what is actually true — three layers your governance reading must hold apart, because your signature on a covering recommendation merges them for everyone downstream.

For questions 10 to 12, read this extract from a government service standard guidance page:

> "Service teams must understand the difference between user needs and user wants. A need is something a user must be able to do to achieve their goal — for example, prove their identity to access a service. A want is a preference about how that happens. Teams should prioritise meeting needs for all users, including those with access needs or low digital confidence, over optimising preferences for confident users. Research with a representative range of users, conducted throughout the service's life cycle rather than only at the start, is the primary mechanism for distinguishing needs from wants. Metrics alone cannot make this distinction, because usage data shows what users do within the options offered, not what they were trying to achieve."

**Question 10 (moderate) — True / false / cannot say**

Statement: "According to the passage, usage metrics can establish what users were trying to achieve."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage states the opposite explicitly: "usage data shows what users do within the options offered, not what they were trying to achieve." The statement is contradicted — false. The underlying argument deserves a lead's attention because it is load-bearing for your **User focus** skill: metrics are bounded by the option space the service offers, so a service can score excellent task-completion on tasks that miss the user's actual goal — users complete what exists, not what they need. This is the analytical basis for the passage's claim that research, not metrics alone, distinguishes needs from wants — and it is the argument you will deploy when a dashboard-rich programme claims research is redundant. Championing user research, which your role summary names explicitly, begins with being able to reproduce this reasoning under challenge.

**Question 11 (moderate) — Logical completion**

According to the passage, a team that optimises the experience of digitally confident users while some users with access needs cannot complete the service has:

- A) Correctly prioritised the majority.
- B) Inverted the passage's stated priority — preferences of confident users have been served ahead of the needs of all users, which the passage explicitly subordinates them to.
- C) Followed the guidance, provided metrics show high satisfaction.
- D) Made a decision the passage does not address.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage's priority rule is explicit: "prioritise meeting needs for all users, including those with access needs or low digital confidence, over optimising preferences for confident users." Users who cannot complete the service have unmet needs (they cannot achieve their goal — the passage's own definition); optimising confident users' experience is preference-optimisation; serving the latter while the former persists is precisely the inversion option B names. Option A imports a majoritarian principle the passage rejects — the rule is needs for all before preferences for some, not preferences of many before needs of few. Option C compounds the error with the metrics fallacy question 10 dismantled. Option D is false: this is the passage's central case. The completion question format rewards applying a stated rule to a concrete configuration — which is also the form your internal user advocacy takes: not "research says so" but "the standard's own priority rule, applied to this design, yields this conclusion."

**Question 12 (hard) — Clear-writing judgement at leadership level**

You must write to a supplier's account director after their transition team missed a second consecutive readiness milestone, with the relationship still needed for two more years. Which opening paragraph is most effective?

- A) "It has come to our attention that certain milestones may not have been achieved in accordance with previously agreed timescales, which is potentially a matter of some concern at this juncture."
- B) "Your team's repeated failures demonstrate a fundamental lack of competence and commitment that we are no longer prepared to tolerate."
- C) "The readiness milestones of 14 June and 12 July were both missed, the second by nine working days. I want to resolve the underlying cause with you before the September milestone, which protects the cutover date we both need. Please come to Thursday's review able to discuss what changed on your side since May, when delivery was consistently on time, and what you need from us to restore that position."
- D) "Further to previous correspondence and pursuant to the terms of the agreement between our organisations, we hereby formally register dissatisfaction with recent delivery performance and reserve all contractual rights and remedies available to us."

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C does five things the situation demands: it states the facts with dates and magnitudes (unarguable, and therefore un-argued-about); it declares intent (resolve, not punish) anchored to a shared stake (the cutover both parties need); it references the positive baseline (on time until May), which converts the message from character judgement to change investigation — something specific changed, and change investigations are answerable; it assigns a concrete next step with a date; and it opens the door to bilateral causes ("what you need from us"), which costs nothing and frequently surfaces the real blocker. Option A is fog — "may not have been achieved", "potentially", "at this juncture" — writing designed to be deniable, which reads as weakness and provides no facts to act on. Option B converts a delivery problem into a relationship injury two years before you can afford one; adjectives about character invite rebuttal, while dates invite explanation. Option D performs firmness legalistically without stating a single fact or asking for anything — "reserving rights" as an opening move signals the dispute you claim to be avoiding. This is your **Community collaboration** skill — adapting feedback so it is effective and lasting — exercised in writing at commercial altitude: firm on facts, purposeful about the future, and impossible to dismiss as either vague or vindictive.

### Preparation tips

- **Operate clauses, don't just read them.** For any contract or policy extract, run a concrete scenario through its full machinery (as in question 5). Mechanisms reveal what sentences conceal, and hard senior questions are mechanisms.
- **Audit evidential texture.** In any board paper or supplier report, sort each claim: verifiable fact, characterisation, or omission. The sort itself is the senior reading skill — and the source of your best review questions.
- **Track verbs for their event type.** Received is not accepted; reported is not disclosed; incorporated is not resolved. Each verb names a specific event; check it is the event that matters.
- **Verify direction of obligation.** Who owes, who is owed, who must notify whom by when. Reversals are silent and expensive.
- **Read requested decisions as drafted choices.** Coupled motions, undefined "on track", pre-narrowed options — governance papers frame; your reading should notice the frame.
- **Draft difficult messages with facts first.** Dates and magnitudes before adjectives, shared stakes before demands. Practise rewriting one vague paragraph a week into question-12-C form.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Crediting mechanisms from single sentences.** The extension clause gives with one sentence and conditions with the next. Read to the end of the machinery before concluding anything.
- **Hearing more than the verb said.** "Received on schedule" tells you nothing about acceptance. Expertise makes these auto-completions faster and therefore more dangerous.
- **Treating absence of findings as absence of issues.** "Recommendations are being incorporated" discloses nothing. Silence is a fact about the document, never about the world.
- **Confusing thin documentation with bad reality — or accepting framing as evidence.** What a paper establishes, what it asserts, and what is true are three layers; senior reading holds them apart.
- **Reversing obligations in multi-party language.** Annex 5 belonged to the Authority. Check direction every time two parties share a sentence.
- **Writing to be deniable or writing to wound.** Fog invites drift; adjectives invite war. Facts with dates, intent with stakes, and a next step — that is what effective looks like in consequential correspondence.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement test (SJT) presents realistic workplace dilemmas and asks you to identify the most effective — and often least effective — courses of action. At senior levels it is the most direct assessment of the four, because it samples the substance of leadership: how you use authority, how you handle conflict between legitimate interests, when and how you escalate, how you develop and protect your people, and whether the interests of users and the integrity of your processes survive sustained organisational pressure.

Formats include most-effective selection, most-and-least pairs, full ranking, and independent rating of each response. Timing is generous — typically 20 to 30 minutes for 15 to 20 scenarios — and scoring keys are developed with experienced senior practitioners and occupational psychologists against behaviour frameworks. For GDAD leadership roles, expect the key to reward evidence-based decision-making, transparent escalation, genuine empowerment of teams, proportionate intervention, commercial judgement with suppliers, and visible championing of user outcomes and inclusion.

For a lead service transition manager, the scenarios have a distinctive shape. You hold sole responsibility for co-ordination across projects, suppliers, and service teams — so the dilemmas are rarely "what should be done?" and usually "what should be done by whom, at what level, in what order, and at what cost to which relationship?" You manage managers, so people scenarios concern development, delegation, and repair rather than task supervision. You face suppliers as a commercial counterpart, so firmness and relationship preservation must coexist. And you operate in governance structures where your recommendations acquire institutional force — so honesty under pressure is tested not as private virtue but as public act.

A calibration note: senior SJT keys penalise two patterns with special consistency — the heroic override (solving problems personally that your systems and people should solve, which scores as failure to lead) and the procedural hide (following process into outcomes you know are wrong, which scores as failure to own). The consistently rewarded pattern sits between: design the system, empower the people, intervene at the right level, and own the outcome regardless.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Portfolio and escalation scenarios** map to your sole responsibility for cross-project, cross-supplier co-ordination and your **Ownership and topic** skill: accountability for issues, proactive problem-searching, and excellent user outcomes as the test of everything.
- **People and team scenarios** map to your **Community collaboration** skill: active networking, feedback adapted to be effective and lasting, diagnosing and rectifying team-dynamic problems, and using Agile health checks to surface issues and stimulate the right responses.
- **Supplier scenarios** map to your **Change management** skill (managing high-impact, complex change) exercised commercially: holding release policy against contractual pressure and incentive games.
- **Capacity and risk scenarios** map to your **Availability and capacity management** duties: initiating remedial action on your own authority, at the right moment, with defensible arithmetic.
- **Life-cycle and benefits scenarios** map to your **Service focus** skill: keeping the entire life cycle in view, communicating service benefits credibly, and championing service focus within your team and beyond.
- **User advocacy scenarios** map to your **User focus** skill: collaborating with user researchers, representing users internally, distinguishing needs from desires, and championing research when it is inconvenient.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Your manager's transition is in trouble**

One of the service transition managers reporting to you is running a mid-sized transition that is visibly slipping: two readiness milestones missed, and the operations team is complaining to you directly. The manager is capable but newly promoted, and has not raised any concerns with you. What is the most effective response?

- A) Take over the transition yourself — it is slipping, and you are accountable.
- B) Wait for the manager to ask for help — intervening uninvited undermines their development.
- C) Meet the manager promptly: share what you are seeing and hearing, ask for their honest assessment, agree what support or intervention the situation needs, and establish why the slippage was not surfacing upward — fixing that channel matters as much as the milestones.
- D) Ask the operations team to raise their complaints with the manager instead of you.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The scenario contains two problems, and the durable one is not the milestones — it is that your reporting channel failed: you learned about the slippage from complaints, not from your manager. Option C addresses both at the right level: the conversation is prompt (two milestones is a pattern, not noise), evidence-led ("what I'm seeing and hearing"), and developmental rather than punitive — a newly promoted manager who is not escalating is usually protecting an image of competence, and the fix is making escalation feel like competence, which is your **Community collaboration** environment-setting duty. Option A is the heroic override: it recovers one transition at the cost of teaching your manager that trouble means removal — guaranteeing the next problem is hidden longer. Option B confuses empowerment with absence; development happens in supported difficulty, not unsupervised drift. Option D fixes your inbox, not the situation — the complaints are data, and redirecting them discards it. Rewarded behaviours: intervene at the level of the system (the escalation channel), support the person, and stay accountable for the outcome throughout.

**Question 2 (easy) — The health check tells you something**

Your quarterly Agile health check across transition teams shows one team's scores collapsing on "safe to raise problems" and "sustainable pace" while its delivery metrics remain green. The team's manager attributes it to "a couple of negative personalities" and recommends no action. What is the most effective response?

- A) Accept the manager's reading — they know their team best.
- B) Treat the divergence as the signal it is: green delivery with collapsing safety and pace scores is the signature of a team burning reserves to hide problems; look deeper yourself — skip-level conversations, retrospective observation — and coach the manager on why "negative personalities" is usually the diagnosis that prevents diagnosis.
- C) Remove the "negative personalities" from the team.
- D) Wait one more quarter to see if the scores recover.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Your role summary names this exact instrument — identifying issues through Agile health checks and helping to stimulate the right responses — and the scenario tests whether you can read one. Green delivery with red safety-and-pace is not a contradiction; it is a sequence: teams deliver on borrowed effort and suppressed concerns for several quarters before the delivery line follows the safety line down, and by then the recovery is expensive. Option B responds proportionately: verify with your own eyes at skip level (not to bypass the manager permanently, but because the manager is currently part of the measurement problem), and develop the manager's diagnostic frame — "negative personalities" localises a systemic signal in individuals, which is the standard way managers accidentally shoot messengers. Option A delegates the reading to the person whose reading is the anomaly. Option C acts on the manager's undiagnosed theory with maximum harm — removing the people most likely to be telling the truth. Option D spends a quarter confirming what the instrument already told you. Rewarded behaviours: instruments taken seriously, proportionate verification, manager development over manager override.

**Question 3 (easy) — The benefits claim you cannot support**

A programme board paper, drafted by a programme manager and circulated for your input before submission, claims that a transition you co-ordinate will deliver "£2 million of annual savings from month one". You know the savings depend on decommissioning a legacy system that cannot happen until at least month nine, and the realistic year-one figure is nearer £700,000. The programme manager says the £2 million "keeps the board supportive" and the detail "can be managed later". What is the most effective response?

- A) Let it pass — benefits cases are always optimistic, and it is the programme manager's paper.
- B) Correct the figure in your input: state the month-nine dependency and the £700,000 year-one estimate, tell the programme manager you cannot support the paper as drafted, and offer to help present the honest trajectory compellingly — a credible £700,000 rising to £2 million beats a £2 million that unravels in the month-four review.
- C) Report the programme manager to the board chair for attempted misrepresentation.
- D) Decline to provide input, so your name is not associated with the paper.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Your **Service focus** skill includes communicating service benefits — and communicating them credibly is the entire value of the skill, because benefits claims are a currency that inflates: the first unravelled £2 million devalues every future claim from the whole portfolio. Option B refuses the false figure and does the harder, more useful thing: it makes the honest number persuasive, with the trajectory and dependency visible — which is also better board management, since boards punish surprises far more severely than they punish honest phasing. The offer to help matters; correction without help is just criticism. Option A launders the misstatement through your silence — your input was requested precisely so the paper could claim co-ordination-level review. Option C escalates before attempting resolution and converts a correctable drafting decision into a conduct allegation — disproportionate as a first move, though the escalation path remains open if the figure reappears. Option D protects your name and abandons the board to the number you know is wrong: at your level, withholding input you uniquely hold is not neutrality, it is a decision with a beneficiary. Rewarded behaviours: honesty made useful, escalation held in reserve, credibility treated as portfolio infrastructure.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Least effective: the supplier games the release policy**

You discover that a major supplier has been splitting large, high-impact changes into several "low-impact" change requests to qualify for the pre-approved route and avoid CAB scrutiny — technically within the letter of the policy's impact definitions, clearly against its intent. Nothing has failed yet. Which response is LEAST effective?

- A) Close the loophole: amend the policy so related changes within a defined window are assessed at their aggregate impact, and communicate the change to all suppliers with its rationale.
- B) Raise it directly with the supplier's delivery director: name the pattern, state that you regard aggregate impact as the true measure, and require their proposals to reflect it ahead of the formal policy change.
- C) Since nothing has failed and the letter of the policy was followed, log it and take no further action.
- D) Review recent split changes retrospectively at their aggregate impact to establish whether unassessed risk has already entered production.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Options A, B, and D are the three components of the complete response — fix the rule (A), fix the behaviour ahead of the rule (B), and fix the exposure already created (D); in practice you would sequence all three, with D arguably first since unassessed risk may already be live. Option C is least effective, and its reasoning is the trap: "nothing has failed yet" is survivorship logic — the absence of failure after unassessed high-impact change is luck wearing a compliance certificate, and "the letter was followed" concedes that your control was defeated while declining to repair it. A supplier who learns that gaming the policy draws only a log entry has learned the policy's real content. Your **Change management** duty — ensuring release policies are applied — means ensuring they are applied in substance; policies whose letter can be arbitraged against their intent are policies in name. Rewarded behaviours: defend the intent of controls, repair rule, behaviour, and exposure together, and never let quiet luck stand in for assurance.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Two of your managers are in conflict**

Two service transition managers reporting to you are in escalating conflict over a shared release calendar: each has now emailed you separately blaming the other, their teams have begun mirroring the hostility, and a joint planning session collapsed last week. Both are strong performers. What is the most effective response?

- A) Decide the calendar dispute yourself and instruct both to comply.
- B) Address the two layers separately and in order: first meet the two managers together to resolve the substantive calendar conflict against portfolio priorities — modelling the joint problem-solving that failed last week; then, with the substance settled, deal with the working relationship explicitly, naming the team-mirroring as the cost that makes repair non-optional.
- C) Tell them both to sort it out between themselves — they are managers.
- D) Separate their portfolios so the shared calendar dependency disappears.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Conflicts between managers arrive in two layers — a substantive dispute and a damaged working relationship — and the order of operations matters: resolving substance first gives the relationship repair a foundation (people reconcile more easily over a solved problem than an open one), while relationship talk with the dispute still live reads as asking them to feel better about losing. Option B also works the right mechanism on each layer: the calendar is resolved against portfolio priorities (your legitimate authority, exercised with both present — no separate audiences for separate versions), and the relationship is addressed as a professional obligation with a named business cost: their teams are already copying the hostility, which is how manager conflict metastasises into organisational conflict. Your **Community collaboration** skill — identifying problems in team dynamics and rectifying them — is being tested at one remove: the dynamics are your managers'. Option A settles the calendar and leaves the conflict armed for its next occasion. Option C abdicates: they demonstrably cannot, and the collapsed session is the evidence. Option D redesigns the portfolio around a relationship failure — organisational surgery to avoid a management conversation, and a precedent that conflict is rewarded with separation. Rewarded behaviours: substance before sentiment, authority exercised transparently, relationship repair required rather than requested.

**Question 6 (moderate) — The user researcher brings unwelcome findings late**

Three weeks before a flagship go-live, the user researcher you embedded in the programme reports that assisted-digital users cannot complete the new application journey without help, and that the promised telephone-assistance route was quietly descoped four months ago to save money — a descoping decision that never reached you. The programme director's reaction is that the research is "too late to act on". What is the most effective response?

- A) Agree — three weeks is too late; note it for the post-implementation review.
- B) Back the researcher publicly and act on the substance: establish what the assistance route would cost to restore (fully or minimally), put the descoping decision and its user impact in front of the go/no-go with options — restore before launch, launch with an interim measure and a dated commitment, or accept the exclusion explicitly — and separately fix the governance gap that let a user-impacting descope bypass your co-ordination.
- C) Refuse the go-live outright until the assistance route is fully restored.
- D) Instruct the researcher to soften the finding, since the descoping cannot be undone in three weeks.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** "Too late to act on" is a framing, not a fact — three weeks is late for comfort but early enough for options, and option B generates them: restoration cost (possibly small — telephone assistance may be a staffing arrangement, not a build), interim measures, or explicit, owned acceptance of the exclusion. The essential move is placing the decision where it belongs: at the go/no-go, with the user impact quantified, rather than letting a four-month-old cost saving decide by default. Backing the researcher publicly is not a courtesy — your **User focus** skill says you champion user research and represent users internally, and researchers who are punished for late unwelcome findings become researchers who stop finding; the lateness, note, is the descoping's fault, not the research's. The governance repair matters equally: a user-impacting descope bypassed the person solely responsible for co-ordination, and that channel failure will recur on the next programme if unfixed. Option A accepts exclusion of the users least able to object. Option C pre-empts the service owner's decision with your own absolutism — restoration may be the outcome, but as a chosen option, not an ultimatum. Option D is the answer the key punishes hardest: instructing a researcher to soften findings corrupts the evidence channel your entire user-focus duty depends on. Rewarded behaviours: reframe lateness into options, decisions to their owners with impacts visible, researchers protected, systems repaired.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Capacity remediation meets budget refusal**

Your capacity analysis shows a shared platform breaching its performance threshold in roughly four months under committed go-live load, with remediation needing a ten-week procurement. You initiate remedial action, but the infrastructure budget holder refuses: "Next financial year. Nothing has actually failed." Two of the go-lives driving the load belong to other directorates. What is the most effective response?

- A) Accept the refusal — it is their budget, and the new financial year is only five months away.
- B) Escalate immediately to the budget holder's director with an accusation of negligence.
- C) Convert the analysis into a decision the organisation cannot mislay: quantify the breach's service impact and date, price the remediation against the cost of a capacity incident, put the paper jointly to the budget holder and the affected directorates — whose go-lives are the load — and if refusal stands, require it as a recorded risk acceptance at the right level, while re-examining whether go-live sequencing can trade time.
- D) Quietly deprioritise the two other directorates' go-lives to keep the platform under threshold.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** "Nothing has actually failed" is the budget-holder's version of survivorship logic, and the five-month wait exceeds your four-month breach horizon — the refusal, as it stands, schedules an incident. Option C is your **Availability and capacity management** duty (identify the issue, initiate remedial action) executed at organisational altitude: the analysis becomes a costed, dated decision paper; the affected directorates are enlisted because their go-lives are both the cause of the load and the casualties of the breach — turning a bilateral budget dispute into a multilateral priority decision, which is what it actually is; and the crucial mechanism is the recorded risk acceptance: if the organisation chooses the risk, it does so knowingly, at the level that owns the consequence, in writing. That is not defensive paperwork — it is how organisations make real decisions instead of default ones, and it very often changes the answer, because risks are cheap to accept verbally and expensive to sign. The sequencing lever (can a go-live move?) shows co-ordination authority working the demand side too. Option A accepts a timeline the arithmetic has already defeated. Option B escalates with accusation instead of analysis — spending the relationship before spending the evidence. Option D uses your co-ordination authority covertly to solve a funding dispute, harming two directorates without their knowledge or appeal — the kind of quiet unilateralism that ends leads' careers when discovered. Rewarded behaviours: arithmetic into decisions, stakeholders enlisted by interest, risk acceptance formalised, authority used openly.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Least effective: your predecessor's process, your team's habit**

Six weeks into the role, you find your inherited team running a transition checklist containing several steps nobody can explain ("we've always done it"), while two incidents in the last quarter trace to gaps the checklist does not cover. The team is proud of its discipline and wary of you as a newcomer. Which response is LEAST effective?

- A) Run a structured review with the team: trace each checklist step to the risk it controls, retire the ones that control nothing, and add coverage for the two incident-shaped gaps — letting the team lead the tracing.
- B) Announce that the checklist is legacy bureaucracy and replace it with a new one you draft yourself.
- C) Use the two incidents as the entry point: work back from each to the missing control, and let that evidence open the broader review.
- D) Leave the checklist untouched for another quarter while you build trust.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Options A and C are complementary strengths — A is the systematic version (every step must name its risk: steps that protect nothing cost attention that steps protecting something need), C is the evidence-led version that meets a wary team where their own pain is; a skilled lead opens with C and lands A. Option D is weak but survivable — though "building trust" by tolerating known gaps mistakes conflict-avoidance for relationship-building, and a third incident during your patience is on you. Option B is least effective on every axis at once: it discards whatever undocumented wisdom the checklist encodes (some "unexplainable" steps are scar tissue from real incidents — the explanation retired with its author), it replaces a team-owned artefact with an imposed one, and it does so through a newcomer's announcement to a proud, wary team — converting process improvement into an identity attack and guaranteeing quiet non-compliance. The change-leadership principle the key rewards: reform inherited practice through the people who run it, anchored to evidence, preserving what traces to real risk — respect for the team and rigour about the process are the same move, not competing ones.

**Question 9 (hard) — The go-live your peer needs, the risk your evidence shows**

A fellow lead — a peer who has supported you in the past, under visible pressure from their director — asks you to accept a two-week compression of your co-ordinated testing window so their programme's go-live can hit a politically committed date. Your evidence from the last three compressed windows in the portfolio: two produced major early-life incidents. The compression is technically possible, and refusing will strain a relationship you rely on. What is the most effective response?

- A) Accept — the relationship and the political commitment together outweigh a risk that is only historical.
- B) Refuse flatly, citing the two incidents, and copy both directors.
- C) Treat it as a risk decision, not a favour: share the compression evidence with your peer openly, identify what could make this compression different from the failed two (scope reduction, added support cover, staged go-live), and if a residual gap remains, take the choice jointly to the service owner as a documented risk decision with both options priced — supporting whatever is decided with full effort.
- D) Accept, but privately document your objection in case the go-live fails.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The request arrives dressed as a relationship transaction — a favour between peers — and option C's first move is the reframe: window compression is a risk decision about a service, and risk decisions are made on evidence by their owners, not traded between colleagues. The evidence is shared with the peer openly (they are under pressure, not adversarial, and may not know the portfolio's compression history), the genuine engineering question is asked — what would make this time different? — because sometimes there is an answer: reduced scope, strengthened early-life cover, a staged approach; history informs rather than forecloses. And the residual gap, if any, goes to the service owner as a documented choice with both branches priced — political cost of delay against incident probability and cost — followed by full-effort support of whatever is decided, which is what makes the process collegial rather than obstructive. Option A prices the users' risk at the value of your working relationship — a currency conversion your role exists to refuse. Option B may reach the right answer through the wrong mechanism: flat refusal with directors copied converts a solvable joint problem into a positional dispute and spends the relationship without even examining the mitigations. Option D is the pattern senior keys punish most reliably: consent in public, dissent in a drawer — the objection that protects you precisely because it never protected the service. Rewarded behaviours: favours reframed as decisions, evidence shared not brandished, mitigation genuinely explored, decisions documented at their owner, and support after decision regardless of your recommendation.

**Question 10 (hard) — What the portfolio pattern says about you**

Preparing your annual review of the transition function, you find a pattern you did not expect: transitions where you personally chaired the readiness reviews show faster gate decisions but materially worse early-life incident rates than those chaired by your managers. Digging further: in your reviews, open issues were more often "noted and accepted" to keep pace; in theirs, more often converted into conditions with owners and dates. Nobody else has noticed. What is the most effective response?

- A) Note it privately and chair fewer reviews, letting the pattern quietly improve.
- B) Treat yourself as the finding: adopt your managers' conditions-with-owners discipline explicitly, tell your managers what the data showed and what you are changing — including that their practice outperformed yours — and add decision-quality measures (conditions set, conditions honoured, early-life outcomes) to the function's standard review metrics so chair-dependent variation becomes visible for everyone, permanently.
- C) The sample is small and confounded — your reviews may have covered harder transitions; commission more analysis before changing anything.
- D) Standardise review chairing on yourself to eliminate variation, applying more care than before.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The data has handed you the rarest and cheapest kind of finding — a leadership behaviour with measurable consequences, discovered before anyone else frames it for you — and option B extracts its full value. The behavioural fix is specific, not aspirational: "noted and accepted" versus "condition with owner and date" is a concrete mechanism (pace purchased with unmanaged risk), and adopting the better discipline is immediately actionable. Telling your managers is the leadership multiplier: publicly crediting their practice over your own converts an awkward finding into cultural capital — it licenses every one of them to examine their own patterns honestly, which is your **Community collaboration** environment-setting duty performed by demonstration, the only way it is ever really performed. Instrumenting decision quality makes the improvement structural rather than personal: chair-dependent variation becomes a measured property of the function, not a private resolution. Option C's methodological caution is half-legitimate — the sample is small, confounds are possible — but it functions here as sophisticated procrastination: the remedy (conditions with owners) is beneficial under every hypothesis, so waiting for causal certainty costs incidents and buys nothing; investigate the confounds and change the practice. Option A improves the numbers while hiding the lesson — the function learns nothing, and your managers never learn their practice was superior. Option D doubles down on the underperforming variable — you — while reading the data as a mandate for more of it, and "more care" is a resolution, not a mechanism. Rewarded behaviours: self-directed evidence handling, credit given downward, personal findings converted into institutional instruments, and action not deferred behind resolvable uncertainty. Your **Ownership and topic** skill — accountability for issues, proactive search for problems — includes the problems whose root cause reports to nobody but you.

**Question 11 (hard) — The freeze exception with a minister's name on it**

During the organisation's pre-Christmas change freeze — instituted after a catastrophic December release three years ago — a director asks you to authorise an exception: a "small" release enabling a feature the minister has publicly promised for the new year. Your exception process requires a documented risk assessment, service owner approval, and rollback verification; the director wants your authorisation today, saying the process "will take longer than the work itself". The release is genuinely small; the political commitment is genuinely real. What is the most effective response?

- A) Authorise it today — the release is small, the commitment is real, and leads exist to exercise judgement over process.
- B) Refuse all discussion of exceptions during freeze — that is what a freeze means.
- C) Run the exception process at emergency pace rather than waiving it: convene the risk assessment and service owner today, verify the rollback, and give the director a decision within days — while being honest that the answer follows the assessment, and putting the process's speed, not its existence, on the table.
- D) Authorise today on condition the process is completed retrospectively in January.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The director has framed the choice as process versus speed; option C refuses the frame — the exception process is the speed mechanism, existing precisely so freezes can bend for genuine cases without breaking for convenient ones, and a genuinely small release should clear a well-run assessment in days. The distinction between accelerating a process and waiving it is the whole content of this scenario: acceleration preserves every control (risk assessed, owner accountable, rollback real) while compressing elapsed time; waiver deletes the controls and keeps the signature. Option C is also honest about the one thing the director must hear: the process's answer is the answer — an assessment run to ratify a decision already made is theatre, and saying so up front, civilly, is what your authorisation is worth. Option A trades the control regime for a relationship moment and re-creates, with a minister's name attached, the exact unassessed December release the freeze memorialises. Option B is the procedural hide: freezes with no exception path are fictions that force urgent work underground — and your own process explicitly provides the path; refusing to operate it is refusing your role. Option D inverts the sequence that gives assurance meaning — retrospective risk assessment of a live release is documentation, not decision; if the January assessment finds the risk unacceptable, the release is already in production during the freeze. The lead-level principles: controls flex by accelerating, never by waiving; political reality is a fact to weigh inside the process, not a key that bypasses it; and the moment to say "the assessment's answer will be the answer" is before it runs, when it costs candour, not after, when it costs everything. Rewarded behaviours: process as enabler, honesty with power, urgency absorbed by pace rather than by controls.

**Question 12 (hard) — Succession, and what you owe the function**

You have led the transition function for four years. A restructure will move you to a broader role in six months. Reflecting honestly: the function performs well, but a striking amount of it routes through you personally — suppliers escalate to you by name, the portfolio's real dependencies live in your head and inbox, and your managers, though strong, each see only their slice. Nobody has asked you to do anything about this. What is the most effective use of your remaining six months?

- A) Maintain performance to the end — succession is your successor's and your director's problem to solve.
- B) Document everything: spend the six months producing a comprehensive handbook of the function's dependencies, relationships, and judgement calls.
- C) Systematically convert personal capital into institutional capability: move supplier escalation points from your name to role-based routes, make the portfolio dependency picture a maintained shared artefact rather than your private model, rotate your managers through cross-portfolio exposure so someone other than you has seen the whole, and give your director an honest succession risk assessment — while still running the function well.
- D) Identify your strongest manager and privately groom them as your successor, sharing your networks and knowledge with them alone.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Nobody asked — that is the point: your **Ownership and topic** skill defines itself by proactive search for potential problems, and the function's dependence on your person is the largest unlogged risk on its register, with a six-month fuse you alone can see clearly. Option C names the real work: personal capital converts to institutional capability only through structural moves — escalation routes attached to roles rather than names (suppliers will still call you; the route must outlive the habit), the dependency picture converted from private model to maintained artefact (documents decay unless a process owns them — the artefact needs a steward, not just an author), managers given cross-portfolio sight so that portfolio-level judgement stops being a sample size of one, and the residual risk stated honestly upward, because your director cannot manage a succession risk described to them as "fine". Option B mistakes documentation for transfer — the handbook captures what you know, not what you do; judgement, relationships, and the habit of seeing across slices transfer through practice and exposure, which is why C rotates people rather than only writing things down. Option A is technically true and an abdication in substance: succession may be others' decision, but the transferability of the function is the current lead's product, and four years of routing through yourself is a design choice you are best placed to reverse. Option D concentrates the risk it claims to solve — a single anointed successor, chosen privately, reproduces the single point of failure with less legitimacy and pre-empts a selection that is not yours to make; developing all your managers and letting succession be decided properly serves both the function and fairness. The deepest behaviour being sampled: whether you understand leadership's final deliverable — a function that performs after you, indifferent to your presence. Rewarded behaviours: unprompted risk ownership, structure over documents, capability spread rather than anointed, and honest upward accounting of what remains.

### Preparation tips

- **Locate every scenario on the override–hide axis.** Senior keys punish heroic takeover and procedural hiding with equal consistency; the rewarded option almost always designs, empowers, or accelerates instead. Ask of each option: whose job does this do, and whose control does this delete?
- **Rehearse the reframe.** Favours into risk decisions, lateness into options, budget refusals into recorded risk acceptances, process-versus-speed into process-at-pace. The strongest responses usually begin by refusing the question as framed.
- **Practise decision routing.** For each dilemma, name the decision's rightful owner and what they need to decide well. Options that make someone else's decision — or let it be made by default — score poorly regardless of their other virtues.
- **Prepare your feedback and conflict patterns.** Facts with dates before adjectives; substance before sentiment; credit given downward publicly. These patterns repeat across people scenarios, and having them pre-formed frees attention for the scenario's specifics.
- **Audit your own instincts against the keyed behaviours.** Where would you have taken over? Where softened a finding? Honest self-review of past choices is the highest-yield SJT preparation — and is also simply the job.
- **Answer as one consistent leader.** Keys include consistency measures, and a persona assembled question-by-question underperforms a genuine, considered professional identity.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **The heroic override.** Taking over the slipping transition, drafting the replacement checklist yourself, standardising decisions on yourself. It reads as strength and scores as failure to build capability.
- **The procedural hide.** "The letter was followed", "nothing has failed yet", "the freeze means no discussion". Controls exist for outcomes; following them into known harm is abdication with paperwork.
- **Consent in public, dissent in a drawer.** Private objections documented for later vindication are the reliably worst-scored behaviour in senior keys. Timely, addressed honesty or nothing.
- **Spending relationships instead of evidence — or evidence instead of relationships.** Flat refusals with directors copied, and accusations where analysis would serve, both waste what the situation needs. Share the evidence with the person first.
- **Shooting messengers, softening findings, anointing favourites.** Researchers, health checks, and your own uncomfortable data are instruments; protecting their integrity is protecting your ability to lead.
- **Confusing documentation with transfer, and presence with capability.** Handbooks do not transfer judgement; performance that routes through you is risk, not indispensability. The function you leave is the measure of the function you led.
