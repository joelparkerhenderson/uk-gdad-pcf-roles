# Service Owner - Psychometric Assessment Resources

## Introduction

Welcome! This guide has been written specifically for you as a service owner working within the UK Government Digital and Data profession. Whether you are preparing for an internal assessment centre, applying for a new post, benchmarking your own capability against the framework, or simply curious about how psychometric assessments connect to the leadership you exercise every day, this document will help you practise, reflect, and build confidence.

Psychometric assessments are structured, standardised exercises that measure the mental capabilities and behavioural tendencies that predict success in a role. For a service owner, that means far more than abstract puzzles. The assessments described here are job-specific: they use the kinds of artefacts you genuinely handle — service performance dashboards, business cases, benefits realisation tables, governance papers, service assessment reports, user research findings, supplier contracts and stakeholder correspondence — and the kinds of decisions you genuinely make, such as prioritising between user needs, business requirements and policy; deciding when a team should continue, change direction or stop; allocating money across competing priorities; and negotiating with senior stakeholders so that work can progress.

Why do these assessments matter for your role in particular? You are accountable for the quality, performance, benefits and outcomes of an end-to-end service. Accountability of that breadth demands sustained, high-quality reasoning under pressure: reading a performance dashboard and knowing within seconds whether the service is genuinely healthy; extracting the one material fact from a dense assessment report; testing whether a benefits claim in a business case actually survives arithmetic; and choosing a defensible course of action when a minister's office, a policy team and your user researchers are pulling in three directions. Assessments of cognitive ability, numerical reasoning, verbal reasoning, and situational judgement map directly onto those demands. Practising them sharpens exactly the muscles your accountability depends on — and, because you coach others and create the culture for teams to do their best work, it also equips you to guide colleagues through their own assessment and development journeys with empathy and technique.

This document is organised into four main assessment sections, each following the same pattern: an overview of what the assessment measures and why employers use it for service leadership roles, a mapping of the assessment dimensions to the specific named skills in your capability framework, a substantial set of practice questions progressing from easy through moderate to hard with full worked explanations, practical preparation tips, and common pitfalls to avoid.

Here is how to get the most from it. First, read each "About this assessment" section so the format holds no surprises. Second, attempt every practice question honestly — commit to an answer in writing before reading the explanation, because the act of committing is where the learning happens. Third, treat every explanation as a mini-lesson: even when you answer correctly, the worked reasoning will deepen your technique, and many explanations deliberately model the strategic framing you can reuse in governance boards and stakeholder conversations. Fourth, pay particular attention to the numeric questions: they show the arithmetic step by step, because at your level the discipline of auditable working is also the discipline that makes your benefits claims and funding cases credible. Finally, use the preparation tips and pitfalls for self-reflection — they connect assessment performance back to your professional development as an accountable leader who defines success and quality measures, and then drives continuous improvement towards them. That habit — define the measure, practise against it, improve deliberately — is precisely what this document invites you to apply to yourself.

Take your time, be kind to yourself, and enjoy the practice. Let's begin.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive ability assessment measures the exact mental processes your role demands, using practical workplace scenarios rather than abstract shapes and sequences. Instead of asking you to complete a number series in the void, it presents you with the everyday materials of service ownership — performance dashboards, benefits maps, roadmaps, governance schedules, operating model diagrams, assessment reports — and asks you to reason quickly and accurately about them.

The typical format is an online, timed test lasting 15 to 30 minutes, containing somewhere between 15 and 30 questions covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective, comparing your responses against a norm group of people at a similar level, and many modern platforms adapt question difficulty to your previous answers — so a test that feels progressively harder is usually a sign you are doing well, not badly. Employers typically receive a breakdown of speed versus accuracy rather than a single number, which means working both quickly and carefully matters. You will normally be offered short, ungraded practice questions before the real test begins, so you can settle into the interface without pressure.

For a service owner, cognitive assessment is particularly relevant because your core professional value is structured judgement across an entire service. You provide end-to-end direction, prioritisation, risk management and decision making for teams; you use evidence to decide when a team should continue, change direction or stop; and you must hold the whole system in mind — users, operations, policy, funding, suppliers — while deciding what matters most this quarter. Each of those duties is, cognitively, an exercise in spotting the pattern in noisy data, deducing what follows from a constraint, catching the inconsistency in a paper before a board does, and ranking competing demands defensibly. A well-designed cognitive assessment simulates precisely those demands in miniature — which is why employers trust it as a predictor of performance in accountable service leadership.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The assessment dimensions map directly to the named skills in your capability framework:

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **Leading performance and benefits** and **Applying user-centred insights**: ensuring metrics are used to monitor, manage and prioritise delivery means noticing the trend that matters in a dashboard of forty numbers — the channel quietly shifting, the satisfaction score decoupling from completion rates, the operational metric that always deteriorates two weeks before an incident.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Governance and assurance** and **Life cycle management**: guiding teams through governance processes such as service assessments, and moving through life cycle phases at the right time, both require reasoning validly from rules — standards, spend controls, assessment conditions — to their consequences for your service.
- **Error checking** maps to **Financial ownership** and **Leading performance and benefits**: a business case whose benefits table does not reconcile, or a performance report whose percentages cannot all be true, is your accountability to catch — before it reaches an investment board with your name on it.
- **Prioritisation** maps to your duty to **prioritise areas of development, balancing user needs, business requirements and policy**: when four demands arrive at once, ranking them by impact-weighted urgency — and being able to defend the ranking to the people ranked lower — is the daily texture of service ownership.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Operational management** and **Strategic ownership**: identifying and overcoming operational constraints, and turning a strategic intent into a sequence of achievable moves, are both exercises in decomposing a tangled situation, modelling its constraints, and finding the move that unblocks the system.

Because you also coach others — supporting colleagues in creating strategy, guiding stakeholders through the life cycle — several questions below place you in a reviewing position: checking a team's paper, spotting the flaw in a proposed measure, or deciding what feedback most improves someone else's reasoning. That is deliberate: at your level, cognitive skill is something you exercise on your own work and transmit through others'.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern recognition in a performance dashboard**

Your service dashboard reports four metrics weekly. Over the last five weeks: digital completion rate: 71%, 72%, 71%, 73%, 72%; phone channel share: 18%, 18%, 19%, 18%, 18%; user satisfaction: 4.2, 4.2, 4.1, 4.2, 4.2; average processing time: 6.1, 6.0, 6.4, 7.1, 8.2 days. Which metric most warrants investigation this week?

- A) Digital completion rate
- B) Phone channel share
- C) User satisfaction
- D) Average processing time

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Read each series for shape, not just level. Completion rate oscillates within a two-point band — stable. Phone share is flat. Satisfaction is flat. Processing time, however, has broken pattern: after two stable weeks it has risen three weeks running, and the rises are accelerating (+0.4, +0.7, +1.1). An accelerating monotonic trend is the signature of a compounding problem — a growing backlog, a staffing gap, a failing integration — and it will not correct itself. Notice also the leading-indicator logic: satisfaction is still flat because users have not yet felt the delay, but if processing time keeps climbing, satisfaction follows in a few weeks. Ensuring metrics are used to monitor, manage and prioritise delivery means exactly this: reading trajectory before it becomes user harm, and asking your operations lead the open question today rather than explaining the satisfaction dip to a board next month.

**Question 2 (easy) — Logical deduction from a spend control rule**

Your department's spend control rules state: "Any digital service spend above £100,000 requires spend approval, unless the spend is within a programme's existing approved envelope." A colleague tells you: "The payments team committed £250,000 last month without seeking spend approval." Which one of the following conclusions must be true?

- A) The payments team breached the spend control rules.
- B) The spend was within an existing approved envelope.
- C) Either the spend was within an existing approved envelope, or the rules were breached.
- D) The payments team's service owner authorised the spend.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The rule is a conditional with one permitted exception. From the facts given you cannot tell whether the envelope exception applies, so neither A nor B must be true on its own — each is merely possible. D introduces information the facts never mention. The only conclusion guaranteed in every case consistent with the facts is the either/or in C. Assessments reward this discipline of deducing only what the information guarantees — and so does governance. Ensuring appropriate and proportionate assurance is in place includes the restraint of verifying which case you are in before acting: checking the approval envelope takes ten minutes and protects both your logic and a working relationship, whereas opening with an accusation costs you both. Verify first, then act.

**Question 3 (easy) — Prioritisation of a service owner's morning**

It is 9:00 on Tuesday. Four items are waiting for you:

1. Your operations lead reports the service's document-upload function has been failing intermittently since 7:30; roughly a quarter of users are affected and the team is investigating.
2. The policy director's office asks for your comments on a draft ministerial submission by Thursday.
3. A supplier account manager wants to discuss next year's contract renewal, proposing a call this week.
4. Your delivery manager asks you to approve the revised sprint goals before stand-up at 10:00.

What should you address first?

- A) Item 2 — ministerial business takes precedence
- B) Item 1 — a live incident affecting users outranks everything schedulable
- C) Item 4 — it takes two minutes and unblocks a team immediately
- D) Item 3 — contract renewals have long-term financial consequences

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Item 1 is the only item currently causing user harm: a quarter of users failing to upload documents is live service degradation, and as the accountable leader and escalation point for major operational issues, your first move is to engage — confirm severity, ensure the incident process is running, decide whether communications to users are needed, and clear obstacles for the responding team. Items 2 and 3 have deadlines measured in days. Item 4 is quick and tempting — and C's logic ("two minutes, unblocks a team") would be right if nothing were on fire; a common senior trap is clearing satisfying small approvals while a user-facing incident runs unmanaged. Option A confuses seniority of the requester with urgency of the need; the policy director would expect you to handle the incident first. The pattern to internalise: user harm now beats stakeholder requests later, and your accountability for quality and performance is exercised most visibly in the first hour of an incident.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Deduction through a service assessment condition**

Your service passed its beta assessment with a condition: "The service may enter public beta only after (i) the assisted digital support route is live, and (ii) accessibility issues rated critical are resolved. A follow-up review of these conditions will be held eight weeks after the assessment." The assessment was on 1 March. The assisted digital route went live on 22 March. Two critical accessibility issues were resolved on 5 April; a third was found on 12 April and is still open. It is 20 April. Which statement must be true?

- A) The service may enter public beta, because the conditions were met on 5 April.
- B) The service may not yet enter public beta.
- C) The service must wait until the follow-up review on 26 April before entering public beta.
- D) The service's beta assessment result is void because a new critical issue was found.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Test each condition at today's date. Condition (i): assisted digital live on 22 March — met. Condition (ii): requires critical accessibility issues to be resolved; two were resolved on 5 April, but a third critical issue was found on 12 April and remains open, so condition (ii) is not currently met. The condition is a state, not an event — it must hold when you enter public beta, not merely have held once — so A fails: the conditions were only fully satisfied for the week before the new issue appeared, and entering beta now with an open critical issue would breach the condition. C misreads the follow-up review as a gate: the text says a review will be held, not that beta must wait for it; if the third issue were resolved tomorrow, the conditions would be met regardless of the review date. D invents a voiding mechanism the text does not contain. B is the only statement that must be true. Guiding teams through governance processes such as service assessments depends on exactly this precision: conditions, gates and reviews are three different mechanisms, and teams look to you to know which is which — both to keep the service compliant and to stop anyone imposing waits the conditions never required.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Error checking a benefits table**

A draft business case for your service's next phase includes this benefits table:

| Benefit | Annual value | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Reduced phone contact | £480,000 | 60,000 fewer calls × £8 per call |
| Faster processing | £375,000 | 25,000 hours saved × £15 per hour |
| Reduced error rework | £150,000 | 12,000 fewer errors × £12.50 per error |
| **Total** | **£1,005,000** | |

Checking the arithmetic, how many of the three benefit lines are internally consistent, and is the total correct for the lines as stated?

- A) All three lines are consistent, and the total is correct.
- B) All three lines are consistent, but the total is wrong.
- C) Two lines are consistent, one is not, and the total matches the stated line values.
- D) One line is consistent, two are not.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Verify each line from its own stated basis before touching the total. Line 1: 60,000 × £8 = £480,000 — consistent. Line 2: 25,000 × £15 = £375,000 — consistent. Line 3: 12,000 × £12.50 = £150,000 — consistent. Now the total: £480,000 + £375,000 = £855,000; £855,000 + £150,000 = £1,005,000 — matches the stated total. Every line reconciles and the sum is right: the correct answer is A. Options C and D are traps for readers who assume a question framed as "error checking" must contain an error, and who therefore "find" one. The deliberate lesson: a checker who reports errors that are not there is as dangerous as one who misses real ones — false positives burn analyst time and teach teams to discount your reviews, and your credibility when you do flag a genuine error next month rests on the times you correctly certified clean work. Note also what arithmetic checking is not: whether 60,000 fewer calls is a defensible forecast at all is a different review — evidence quality — with different methods. Identifying measurable benefits that represent good value for money means testing both layers, and always knowing which one you are testing.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Pattern recognition across the operating model**

Your service runs in four regions with comparable volumes. This quarter's figures per region — cases completed per staff member per week: North 42, Midlands 44, South 43, East 29. First-time resolution rate: North 81%, Midlands 79%, South 80%, East 91%. What is the most reasonable first interpretation?

- A) East is underperforming and needs a performance improvement plan.
- B) East may be trading throughput for quality — or handling a different case mix — and warrants investigation before judgement; the other regions cluster on both measures.
- C) North, Midlands and South are cutting corners and East is the only region doing the job properly.
- D) The figures cannot be compared because regions differ.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Read the two series together, not separately. On throughput, three regions cluster at 42-44 and East sits at 29 — an outlier low. On resolution, the same three cluster at 79-81% and East sits at 91% — an outlier high. The joint pattern is the interesting fact: East is slower and more thorough, which suggests a deliberate or structural difference — a different case mix (perhaps complex cases are routed to East), a different local process, or a genuine quality-throughput trade-off. B holds the interpretation open and investigates; A reads only the first series and reaches for performance management; C reads only the second and moralises in the other direction; D refuses the comparison the stem licenses (comparable volumes). For a service owner the follow-up matters as much as the reading: if East's routing explains it, your regional comparisons need case-mix adjustment before they are used for anything; if East has genuinely found a better process at a throughput cost, that is a strategic quality-versus-capacity decision — yours to make with evidence, and exactly what coordinating teams from operations, policy, digital and delivery to iterate a service looks like in practice.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Deduction about life cycle sequencing**

Your roadmap has these rules: (i) the legacy case system cannot be switched off until all case types are migrated to the new service; (ii) the final case type (appeals) cannot migrate until the policy team signs off revised appeal guidance; (iii) the operations team can only support dual running (both systems live) for a maximum of six months without additional funding; (iv) dual running began on 1 April. Policy sign-off is expected on 1 July, and appeals migration takes two months after sign-off. If everything runs to expectation, what follows?

- A) Legacy switch-off occurs by 1 September, within the dual-running window.
- B) Legacy switch-off occurs by 1 September, breaching the dual-running window.
- C) Legacy switch-off cannot occur before 1 September, leaving one month of funded dual running as contingency.
- D) Additional funding is already unavoidable.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Chain the dates. Policy sign-off 1 July; appeals migration takes two months, completing 1 September; only then can the legacy system switch off (rule i), so switch-off cannot occur before 1 September. The dual-running window is six months from 1 April, ending 1 October (rule iii). Switch-off at 1 September lands inside the window with one month to spare — so A is almost right but claims switch-off occurs by 1 September, which the facts support only if migration completes exactly on time and switch-off is instant; C states the logically guaranteed version: not before 1 September, with one month of funded contingency. D is false on the expected timeline — funding becomes unavoidable only if slippage exceeds a month. The service-owner reading of this arithmetic is risk appetite made visible: your entire contingency for policy delay plus migration overrun is one month, and rule (ii) puts the critical dependency outside your teams, in policy sign-off. The practical move this deduction should trigger: confirm the 1 July date with the policy director now, agree an early-warning trigger, and cost the funding extension as a contingency option before you need it — using evidence to decide when a team should continue, change direction or stop includes deciding when to buy insurance.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Error checking a governance paper's logic**

A paper to your service board argues: "User satisfaction has risen from 3.9 to 4.3 since we launched the new interface in January. This proves the redesign caused the improvement. We should therefore apply the same design patterns to the staff-facing casework system, where satisfaction will rise similarly." Which is the weakest link in the argument?

- A) The satisfaction data itself — a 0.4 rise is within measurement noise and should be ignored.
- B) The causal claim — other changes since January (a fee reduction, faster processing, seasonal case mix) could explain some or all of the rise, so "proves" overstates.
- C) The transfer claim — staff users, tasks and contexts differ from citizens', so even a genuine citizen-facing gain does not imply a similar staff-facing gain.
- D) Both B and C are weak, but C is the weakest because it compounds the causal leap with a generalisation to a different population.

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Grade each link. The data (A) is not the problem: a 0.4 shift on a large sample is typically meaningful; dismissing it as noise without knowing the sample size overcorrects. The causal claim (B) is genuinely weak — post hoc reasoning; "satisfaction rose after the redesign" supports, but cannot prove, causation while other January-onwards changes exist. The transfer claim (C) is weaker still, because it inherits B's flaw and adds one: even granting that the redesign caused the citizen-facing gain, staff performing casework are a different population doing different tasks under different constraints, and design patterns notoriously do not transfer unexamined between the two. D captures the full grading. The board-room version of this skill: strong evidence for continuing citizen-facing design investment; a fair case for a discovery or prototype on the staff system; no case yet for "apply the same patterns and expect the same rise". Advocating for a range of effective research approaches — and using user insights to make strategic decisions — means insisting the staff-system decision gets its own evidence, and coaching the paper's author to claim exactly what the data supports, which makes their next paper stronger too.

**Question 9 (hard) — Multi-constraint scheduling of governance events**

You must schedule three events next week (Monday to Friday, at most one per day): a service assessment mock (M), a quarterly performance review with your director (Q), and a supplier contract negotiation (N). Constraints: (i) M must take place on Monday or Tuesday, so the team has at least two clear working days to address findings before the real assessment the following week; (ii) your director is available only Tuesday and Thursday, and must attend Q; (iii) the commercial lead must attend both N and Q, and is unavailable all day Tuesday; (iv) N must occur later in the week than Q, because the negotiation strategy depends on the review's decisions; (v) the delivery team attending M is fully occupied with a production release on Monday. On which days do the three events take place?

- A) M Monday, Q Tuesday, N Thursday
- B) M Tuesday, Q Thursday, N Friday
- C) M Monday, Q Thursday, N Friday
- D) M Tuesday, Q Wednesday, N Thursday

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Pin the most constrained event first. Q needs the director (Tuesday or Thursday only, by (ii)) and the commercial lead (not Tuesday, by (iii)): the only day satisfying both is Thursday, so Q is Thursday — which immediately eliminates A (Q on Tuesday puts the commercial lead in a meeting she cannot attend) and D (Q on Wednesday loses the director). N must fall later in the week than Q (iv) and include the commercial lead (iii): the only day after Thursday is Friday, so N is Friday. Finally M: constraint (i) allows Monday or Tuesday, and constraint (v) removes Monday (the team is running a release), leaving Tuesday. The schedule is M Tuesday, Q Thursday, N Friday — option B. Option C survives four constraints and fails only the fifth, which is the design of near-miss options: they reward the candidate who verifies every constraint mechanically against the surviving choice rather than stopping when an option "looks right". The transferable technique — anchor the event with the tightest intersection of constraints, propagate the consequences, then verify the winner against the full list — is exactly how you build a governance calendar in real life, where the service assessment, the director's diary, the commercial lead's availability and the team's release schedule are all genuinely fixed points, and a plan that ignores any one of them collapses on the day it matters.

**Question 10 (hard) — Operational constraint analysis**

Your service processes applications through four stages: intake (capacity 500/day), verification (capacity 300/day), decision (capacity 350/day), notification (capacity 600/day). Demand is 400 applications per day. A proposal offers £200,000 to raise decision capacity from 350 to 500 per day, projected to "clear the growing backlog". What is the flaw in the proposal?

- A) There is no flaw — decision capacity below demand is the constraint.
- B) The constraint is verification at 300/day; raising decision capacity leaves throughput at 300/day and the backlog still growing by 100/day, so the £200,000 buys nothing until verification is expanded.
- C) The constraint is intake, which should be reduced to 300/day to stop backlog forming.
- D) Notification's spare capacity should be redeployed to decision instead.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Find the bottleneck: throughput of a sequential pipeline is the minimum stage capacity. Stage capacities are 500, 300, 350, 600 against demand of 400/day. The minimum is verification at 300/day, so the service completes at most 300/day while 400 arrive: the backlog grows by 100/day, accumulating between intake and verification. Decision (350) is above current throughput (300) — it never receives more than verification releases, so it already has 50/day of slack; raising it to 500 adds slack to a stage that is not the constraint, and system throughput stays 300. The proposal spends £200,000 for zero backlog effect — B. A misidentifies the constraint by comparing decision (350) to demand (400) without checking upstream. C would stop the backlog by refusing users — capping intake at the bottleneck rate just moves the queue outside the service, where it becomes invisible and worse. D gestures at reallocation but to the wrong stage; redeploying flexible capacity to verification would be the constructive version. The theory-of-constraints reading is a core operational-management instrument for a service owner: identify and overcome operational constraints means finding the true bottleneck before spending, because investment anywhere else is stranded capacity. It also sharpens your escalation-point role: when a well-meaning stakeholder offers targeted money, the most valuable thing you can say is "thank you — and here is the stage where that money actually changes user outcomes".

**Question 11 (hard) — Prioritisation across the whole service**

Budget allows two of four candidate investments this year. Your service's strategy commits to three outcomes: reduce failure demand, improve accessibility, and achieve sustainable running costs. The candidates: (1) Rebuild the address-change journey — removes the single largest source of failure demand (35% of avoidable contact), cost £300k. (2) Accessibility remediation of the account area — resolves all known WCAG failures, legally required, cost £150k. (3) A native mobile app duplicating the web journey — requested by a vocal stakeholder, adds no new capability, cost £250k, adds permanent run cost. (4) Automation of a manual back-office step — saves £180k per year in running costs, cost £200k, payback about 13 months. Budget: £500k. Which pair best serves the strategy?

- A) 1 and 3
- B) 2 and 3
- C) 1 and 2
- D) 2 and 4

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Apply filters in order: obligations, strategy fit, then value. Option 2 is legally required — accessibility failures are not discretionary for a public service — so any defensible pair includes it, eliminating A immediately. That leaves pairing 2 (£150k) with one of: 1 (£300k, total £450k), 3 (£250k, total £400k), or 4 (£200k, total £350k). Candidate 3 fails the strategy test outright: it advances none of the three outcomes, duplicates existing capability, and adds permanent run cost — moving away from the sustainability outcome; a vocal stakeholder request is a fact to manage, not a strategic justification, which eliminates B. The live choice is C (1+2, £450k) versus D (2+4, £350k). Candidate 1 attacks the largest failure-demand source — 35% of avoidable contact — directly serving outcome one and reducing operational load, with user experience gains on top. Candidate 4 has a genuinely strong financial case (13-month payback serving the sustainability outcome). The tiebreaker is strategic weight and system effect: failure demand is both a user-harm and a cost multiplier (every avoidable contact is a cost and a frustrated user), the strategy names its reduction as an outcome, and 1's £300k fits within budget alongside 2 — while 4, attractive as it is, can be funded next year from the operational headroom that 1 creates, and its payback case will still be true then. C serves two of three strategic outcomes with the legal obligation covered; the disciplined disappointment of the stakeholder behind option 3 — with reasons anchored in the published strategy — is itself part of the job: prioritising areas of development by balancing user needs, business requirements and policy, and being seen to do it by criteria rather than by volume of advocacy, is how your prioritisation stays trusted.

**Question 12 (hard) — Coaching a team's reasoning before a board**

Your delivery manager rehearses a board pitch with you: "Velocity is up 20% since March, so the team is 20% more productive, so we can commit to the expanded scope in the same timeline. Also, we've had no severity-1 incidents for six months, which shows our quality engineering investment has eliminated major incident risk, so we can reduce the on-call rota." What is the most useful coaching feedback on the reasoning?

- A) Both arguments are sound; the pitch is board-ready.
- B) The velocity argument confuses a relative, gameable internal measure with productivity, and the incident argument treats absence of rare events as proof of eliminated risk; both conclusions (scope commitment, rota reduction) need stronger evidence — though the underlying trends are worth presenting honestly as positive signals.
- C) Velocity should never be mentioned to boards; remove the first argument entirely.
- D) The incident argument is sound because six months is a long time; only the velocity argument needs work.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Take the arguments in turn. Velocity is a relative planning measure: story-point inflation, changed estimation practices, or team composition changes can move it 20% with zero change in delivered value — so "velocity up 20%" cannot carry "commit to expanded scope in the same timeline", a commitment the team will be publicly held to. The stronger evidence would be throughput of finished, valuable work and delivery predictability. The incident argument commits a subtler error: severity-1 incidents are rare events, and six months without one is consistent with genuinely reduced risk and with unchanged risk plus luck; more importantly, "eliminated major incident risk" is unfalsifiable optimism, and the proposed action — reducing the on-call rota — is precisely the move that converts a future incident from managed to catastrophic. Absence of evidence, for rare events, is weak evidence of absence. B also keeps the coaching balanced: both trends are real positives worth presenting at their true strength ("velocity trend supports cautious scope discussion; incident record supports continued, not reduced, investment"). A waves through two commitments the evidence cannot cash. C throws away a useful signal because it can be misused — overcorrection. D grades the weaker argument as the stronger one. The service-owner frame: boards remember your service's claims, and demonstrating the value of the service with effective data and insights means calibrating every claim so that none of them ever has to be walked back — and teaching your delivery manager that calibration is what makes the difference between a team that boards trust and a team boards audit.

### Preparation tips

- **Practise on your own dashboard.** Ten minutes with this week's service metrics, deliberately hunting for one trajectory and one outlier, trains the same pattern recognition the test uses — on material you must read anyway.
- **Externalise constraints.** For deduction and scheduling questions, write the rules as a short list and eliminate options mechanically. The habit frees working memory for reasoning, in tests and in governance diaries alike.
- **Check both layers of any table.** Line-level arithmetic and totals are separate checks; so are arithmetic consistency and evidential quality. Practising the separation makes you faster at both.
- **Time-box firmly.** In a 20-question, 25-minute test, give each item roughly 70 seconds; flag and return rather than sinking minutes into one puzzle. It is the same triage you apply to your inbox.
- **Verify the survivor.** After eliminating options, spend ten seconds confirming your remaining answer satisfies every stated rule — the discipline that catches the near-miss options designed to survive casual checking.
- **Coach someone with a question.** Explaining one of these items to a delivery manager or product manager deepens your own technique and doubles as a development conversation — the multiplier habit of your role.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Importing your organisation's rules into the stem.** The passage's rules are the whole rulebook. Seniors lose marks by "knowing" how assessments or spend controls really work; answer from the stated facts only.
- **Reading one metric series at a time.** The signal is often in the joint pattern — slow but thorough, fast but failing. Cross-read before concluding.
- **Assuming an error must exist.** Error-checking questions sometimes present clean artefacts. Certify clean work confidently; false positives cost credibility in tests and in real reviews.
- **Confusing conditions, gates and reviews.** A condition is a state that must hold; a gate blocks until passed; a review is a scheduled look. Governance questions — and governance itself — punish readers who blur them.
- **Optimising a non-bottleneck.** In any pipeline or capacity question, find the minimum-capacity stage before evaluating proposals. Investment away from the constraint is the most common trap, in tests and in budgets.
- **Letting satisfying small tasks jump the queue.** The two-minute approval is not the priority while users are being harmed. Impact-weighted urgency first, always.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures your ability to interpret, manipulate and draw sound conclusions from quantitative information presented the way it actually arrives in your job: service performance tables, benefits realisation trackers, budget forecasts, supplier pricing schedules, channel-shift charts and user research statistics. It is not a mathematics exam. The arithmetic itself rarely goes beyond percentages, ratios, averages, rates and simple projections — the difficulty lies in reading the data accurately, choosing the right operation, and resisting plausible-looking wrong answers under time pressure.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 20 to 30 minutes with 15 to 25 questions, each based on a table, chart or short data extract. Calculators are usually permitted, and many platforms are adaptive. Employers receive accuracy and speed measures against a norm group. For senior accountable roles the questions lean towards interpretation and decision relevance: not "compute this percentage" in isolation, but "given this cost-per-transaction table, which channel strategy does the data support".

For a service owner, numeric reasoning is not a supporting skill — it is the language of your accountability. You ensure a business case is in place that provides sustainable funding; you identify measurable benefits that represent good value for money; you allocate money and make spending decisions across competing priorities; and you demonstrate the value of the service to stakeholders through data and insights. Every one of those duties is exercised through numbers that someone else prepared and you must be able to interrogate. A misread cost per transaction, a percentage computed on the wrong base, or a benefits projection that quietly confuses gross with net can misdirect funding decisions worth millions and erode the stakeholder trust your service depends on. Employers use this assessment because it predicts, cheaply and fairly, whether a candidate can do that quantitative interrogation correctly at speed.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Percentages and variances** map to **Financial ownership**: allocating money across competing priorities and managing financial risk means constantly computing spend against budget, benefit against forecast, and unit costs against benchmarks — and always knowing which base a percentage stands on.
- **Rates, unit costs and trends** map to **Leading performance and benefits**: cost per transaction, digital take-up rate, completion rate, failure demand rate — ensuring metrics are used to monitor, manage and prioritise delivery means computing and comparing rates correctly, and projecting them honestly.
- **Ratios and proportions** map to **Operational management**: a scalable operating model is a set of ratios — staff to case volume, capacity to demand across pipeline stages — and identifying operational constraints is usually ratio arithmetic done before anyone else has bothered.
- **Reading tables and charts** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management** and **Applying user-centred insights**: you translate performance data for directors, ministers' offices and policy teams, and you weigh user research statistics; you cannot simplify a chart honestly, or judge a sample's strength, unless you first read it precisely.
- **Benefits and investment arithmetic** maps to **Financial ownership** and **Strategic ownership**: business cases, break-even analysis, benefits realisation tracking and supplier pricing comparisons are the numeric spine of the strategy you are accountable for delivering.

The questions below use the data your role genuinely handles: transaction volumes, channel costs, SLA percentages, benefits trackers, supplier pricing and research samples. Every explanation shows the arithmetic step by step, because at your level the discipline of auditable working is part of the professional standard you set for others.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Digital take-up percentage**

Last month your service handled 45,000 transactions: 33,750 completed online, the rest by phone or post. What was the digital take-up rate?

- A) 70%
- B) 72%
- C) 75%
- D) 80%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Divide digital transactions by total transactions: 33,750 ÷ 45,000 = 0.75, so 75%. A quick estimation check: three-quarters of 45,000 is 33,750 exactly, confirming the answer. Two habits to build from even a simple calculation like this. First, define the base out loud when you report it: 75% of transactions is not the same as 75% of users (one user can transact several times, and assisted digital users may transact less often), and channel-shift targets are routinely muddled by exactly this slippage. Second, ask what the remaining 25% represents before celebrating: 11,250 phone and post transactions may be your assisted digital route working as designed for users who need it — a success — or failure demand from users who tried online and gave up, which is a design defect. The number alone cannot tell you which; the number plus user insight can.

**Question 2 (easy) — Average cost per transaction**

Your service's monthly running cost is £360,000, covering 45,000 transactions. A comparable service reports a unit cost of £7.20. What is your cost per transaction, and how does it compare?

- A) £8.00 — 80p higher than the comparator
- B) £8.00 — 80p lower than the comparator
- C) £7.20 — the same as the comparator
- D) £12.50 — £5.30 higher than the comparator

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Unit cost = total cost ÷ volume: £360,000 ÷ 45,000 = £8.00 per transaction. Compare: £8.00 − £7.20 = £0.80 higher than the comparator. Estimation check: 45,000 × £8 = £360,000 — confirmed. Distractor D comes from dividing the wrong way or slipping a digit; if you ever produce a unit cost wildly unlike your sector's range, recompute before reporting. The senior-level caution sits in the word "comparable": before this 80p gap goes anywhere near a board paper, you would ask what each figure includes — does the comparator's £7.20 include staff overheads, depreciation, and its share of corporate costs, as yours does? Unit-cost league tables are only as good as their cost boundaries, and demonstrating the value of your service with data means being the person who checks the boundary before quoting the comparison, because a stakeholder will certainly quote it back to you later.

**Question 3 (easy) — SLA percentage from a volume table**

This week your service desk resolved cases as follows: Monday 210 of 230 within SLA; Tuesday 190 of 200; Wednesday 160 of 200; Thursday 180 of 190; Friday 160 of 180. What was the whole-week SLA achievement rate?

- A) 88%
- B) 90%
- C) 92%
- D) 94%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Sum both columns first — never average the daily percentages. Within SLA: 210 + 190 + 160 + 180 + 160 = 900. Total: 230 + 200 + 200 + 190 + 180 = 1,000. Rate: 900 ÷ 1,000 = 90%. The trap is computing five daily rates (91.3%, 95%, 80%, 94.7%, 88.9%) and averaging them: (91.3 + 95 + 80 + 94.7 + 88.9) ÷ 5 = 89.98% — almost identical here because daily volumes are similar, but the two methods diverge badly when volumes vary, and the averaged-percentages method is simply wrong: a percentage of a percentage loses the weighting. Notice Wednesday: 80% against 88-95% elsewhere. The weekly headline of 90% conceals a day that missed badly, and if Wednesday's miss has a cause — a staffing gap, an outage — it will recur. Report the aggregate, investigate the outlier: metrics are for managing, not just for reporting.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Channel shift savings projection**

Phone transactions cost £6.50 each; online transactions cost £0.90 each. Your service currently handles 12,000 phone transactions per month. A proposed improvement is projected to move 40% of phone transactions online. What is the projected monthly saving?

- A) £31,200
- B) £26,880
- C) £74,880
- D) £22,464

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Step 1: transactions moving = 40% of 12,000 = 4,800. Step 2: saving per moved transaction = £6.50 − £0.90 = £5.60 (the transaction still costs something online — this is the step distractor A skips: 4,800 × £6.50 = £31,200 assumes moved transactions become free). Step 3: monthly saving = 4,800 × £5.60 = £26,880. Distractor C (£74,880) applies the saving to all 12,000 transactions plus other confusions; D applies a further spurious discount. The business-case habit: savings from channel shift are always net of the receiving channel's cost, and — one step deeper, for when you present this to an investment board — the £6.50 phone cost is partly fixed (advisers, telephony contracts, accommodation): removing 4,800 calls only realises cash savings if capacity is actually reduced or redeployed. A projection is "up to £26,880 per month, realised as staffing is rebalanced", and identifying measurable benefits that represent good value for money means writing the benefit at the strength the mechanism supports, so your benefits tracker never has to quietly write it back down.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Percentage points versus percentages in a KPI report**

A draft board paper about your service states: (i) "Digital take-up rose from 68% to 75% this year — a 7% increase." (ii) "Failure demand fell from 20% of contact to 15% — a 25% reduction." Which statement uses its terminology correctly?

- A) Both
- B) Only (i)
- C) Only (ii)
- D) Neither

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Statement (i): 68% to 75% is a rise of 7 percentage points; as a percentage increase it is (75 − 68) ÷ 68 = 7 ÷ 68 ≈ 10.3%. Calling it "a 7% increase" conflates points with percent — wrong as written. Statement (ii): 20% to 15% is a fall of 5 percentage points, and as a percentage reduction it is (20 − 15) ÷ 20 = 0.25 = 25% — exactly as claimed, so (ii) is correct and the answer is C. This is among the most common genuine errors in government performance reporting, and you are the last check before your board reads it. It also cuts both ways rhetorically: "take-up rose 10.3%" sounds better than "7 points", while "failure demand fell 25%" sounds better than "5 points" — the same facts, different force. Your standard as an accountable leader should be to publish both forms ("7 percentage points, a 10% relative increase") whenever the distinction could mislead, because sharing effective data and insights with stakeholders means never letting the framing do work the facts have not earned.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Weighted service performance score**

Your quarterly service score weights four measures: user satisfaction (weight 40%), completion rate (30%), cost per transaction versus target (20%), and accessibility compliance (10%). This quarter's scores out of 100: satisfaction 80, completion 90, cost 60, accessibility 100. What is the weighted score?

- A) 80.0
- B) 82.5
- C) 81.0
- D) 79.0

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Multiply each score by its weight and sum: satisfaction 0.40 × 80 = 32; completion 0.30 × 90 = 27; cost 0.20 × 60 = 12; accessibility 0.10 × 100 = 10. Total: 32 + 27 + 12 + 10 = 81. The unweighted mean, (80 + 90 + 60 + 100) ÷ 4 = 82.5, is distractor B — the error of ignoring weights. Two senior observations. First, the composite conceals its worst component: cost at 60 is dragged upward by accessibility's 100, and a stakeholder shown only "81" learns nothing about the cost problem — so present the composite with its components, and intervene on the 60. Second, the weights themselves are a strategic statement you own: this scheme says satisfaction matters four times as much as accessibility, which may or may not reflect your obligations — and note that a composite can score 81 while accessibility fails outright, which is why legally required measures often sit outside composites as pass/fail conditions rather than weighted inputs. Defining success and quality measures for a service is not just choosing metrics; it is choosing the weights and structure that make the headline number tell the truth.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Reading a benefits realisation tracker**

Your benefits tracker shows a benefit baselined at £1,200,000 over four years, profiled evenly at £300,000 per year. Actuals: year 1, £180,000; year 2, £240,000. The benefit owner's commentary says: "Realisation is accelerating; we remain on track for the four-year total." At the current trajectory of improvement, what is a realistic four-year total, and is the commentary fair?

- A) £1,200,000 — the commentary is fair
- B) About £1,020,000 — realisation is genuinely accelerating, but reaching the full baseline requires years 3 and 4 to over-deliver against profile, which the commentary should say plainly
- C) £420,000 — the benefit has failed
- D) £840,000 — assume years 3 and 4 repeat year 2

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Establish the position: two years should have delivered 2 × £300,000 = £600,000; actuals are £180,000 + £240,000 = £420,000 — a shortfall of £180,000. The trend is genuinely improving (+£60,000 year on year). Project the improvement forward: year 3 ≈ £300,000, year 4 ≈ £360,000, giving 180 + 240 + 300 + 360 = £1,080,000 — call it around £1.0-1.1 million; option B's £1,020,000 sits in that band (a slightly gentler improvement assumption). To hit £1,200,000, years 3 and 4 must deliver £780,000 between them — 30% above their combined profile — which is possible but is a stretch target, not "on track". So the commentary is half fair: "accelerating" is true; "on track for the four-year total" asserts a recovery the trajectory does not yet evidence. D's method (repeat year 2 flat) ignores the established improvement trend and undershoots; C confuses position to date with final outcome; A accepts assertion over arithmetic. The service-owner discipline: benefits commentary should state position (£180k behind), trajectory (improving £60k/year), and the explicit condition for full recovery (years 3-4 at 130% of profile) — because a board that discovers the gap in year 4 will ask why the tracker said "on track" in year 2, and it will be your tracker.

**Question 8 (moderate) — User research sample interpretation**

Your user researcher reports: "In usability testing, 4 of 5 participants completed the new journey without assistance, versus 2 of 5 on the old journey. Separately, our survey of 1,200 users shows satisfaction with the current service at 71% ± 3% (95% confidence)." A stakeholder writes: "Research proves the new journey doubles completion (80% vs 40%), and satisfaction is definitely between 68% and 74%." Which critique of the stakeholder's summary is most accurate?

- A) The summary is fine — it restates the figures correctly.
- B) The usability sample (n=5) supports a directional finding, not stable percentages like "80% vs 40%", and "proves" and "doubles" overstate it; the survey claim is roughly right in spirit, though 95% confidence means "very probably", not "definitely".
- C) Both studies are worthless because the samples differ in size.
- D) The survey is invalid because 1,200 is too few of the service's users.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Take the two studies on their own terms. Usability testing with five participants is designed to find problems and indicate direction, and 4/5 versus 2/5 is a genuinely encouraging signal — but percentages computed on n=5 move by 20 points per participant, so "80% vs 40%" dresses a small-sample signal in big-sample clothes, and "proves… doubles" converts indication into fact. The correct claim: "strong directional evidence the new journey completes more easily; quantify with a larger test or funnel analytics before citing figures." The survey claim is nearly right: 71% ± 3% at 95% confidence means the interval 68-74% would capture the true value in 95% of repeated samples — "definitely" overstates only mildly, and B correctly grades this as the lesser error. C commits the opposite sin — dismissing useful evidence because it is imperfect; different methods have different jobs. D misunderstands sampling: 1,200 is ample for ±3% regardless of population size. As someone who coaches others in making decisions that meet user needs and uses insights strategically, your role in this exchange is calibration in both directions: protect the research from overstatement by enthusiasts and from dismissal by sceptics — both distortions eventually cost the service its evidence base.

**Question 9 (hard) — Supplier pricing comparison across volume**

Two suppliers bid to run your service's document-processing function. Supplier A: £2.10 per document, no minimum. Supplier B: £1.60 per document plus a fixed platform fee of £14,000 per month. Your volume is currently 24,000 documents per month, forecast to grow 25% next year. (a) Which supplier is cheaper at current volume, and (b) at what monthly volume do the suppliers cost the same?

- A) A is cheaper now; break-even at 28,000 documents
- B) B is cheaper now; break-even at 28,000 documents
- C) B is cheaper now; break-even at 20,000 documents
- D) A is cheaper now; break-even at 35,000 documents

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Part (a), current volume. Supplier A: 24,000 × £2.10 = £50,400. Supplier B: 24,000 × £1.60 = £38,400, plus the £14,000 platform fee = £52,400. A is cheaper today by £2,000 — the fixed fee reverses the impression the per-document rates give, which is exactly what distractors B and C reward you for missing. Part (b), break-even: the suppliers cost the same when £2.10 × V = £1.60 × V + £14,000, so £0.50 × V = £14,000, giving V = 28,000 documents. Option A pairs both correct results. Now apply the forecast: 25% growth takes volume to 24,000 × 1.25 = 30,000 documents — past break-even, where B wins (at 30,000: A costs £63,000, B costs £62,000). The full commercial reading is therefore richer than either bid line alone: A is cheaper today, B is cheaper at forecast volume, and the decision hinges on contract length, confidence in the growth forecast, and switching costs — exactly the analysis behind enhancing supplier relationships to deliver good value outcomes. The arithmetic pattern (break-even volume = fixed fee ÷ rate difference) takes thirty seconds and should be automatic before any per-unit pricing conversation; the strategic pattern — never evaluate volume-sensitive pricing at a single volume point — is what makes you the person suppliers cannot out-model in a negotiation.

**Question 10 (hard) — Backlog and recovery arithmetic**

An incident took your service's application pipeline offline for 5 working days, during which 400 applications per day continued to arrive. Normal capacity is 460 applications per day against the 400 daily arrivals. After restoration: (a) in how many working days will the backlog clear, and (b) roughly how many days of processing does the restoration-day backlog itself represent at full capacity?

- A) 20 days to clear; the backlog represents about 10 days of full-capacity processing
- B) About 33 days to clear; the backlog represents about 4.3 days of full-capacity processing
- C) 20 days to clear; the backlog represents about 20 days of full-capacity processing
- D) 5 days to clear; the backlog represents about 2.5 days of full-capacity processing

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Step 1, backlog at restoration: 5 days × 400 arrivals = 2,000 applications. Step 2, net recovery rate: while clearing backlog the service must also absorb the daily 400 new arrivals, so backlog falls by only capacity minus demand = 460 − 400 = 60 applications per day. Step 3, days to clear: 2,000 ÷ 60 = 33.3 — about 33 working days. Step 4, the backlog's size in full-capacity terms: 2,000 ÷ 460 ≈ 4.3 days of pure processing — which is why intuition says "a week or so to catch up" while the arithmetic says seven weeks: catch-up runs on the thin 60-a-day margin, not on the full 460. Distractor D treats outage length as recovery length; A and C invent friendlier margins. The operational lesson is the shape of the numbers: a 5-day outage takes 33 days to recover because headroom is only 13% of capacity — every incident day costs more than six recovery days, and any demand spike during recovery stretches it further. This recovery multiplier (arrival rate ÷ headroom) is worth computing for your own service today: ensuring a scalable operating model provides a resilient, high quality service means knowing the multiplier before an incident, not during one — it materially changes the business case for surge capacity, and it is the difference between announcing an honest recovery date to stakeholders and revising a hopeful one weekly for two months.

**Question 11 (hard) — Business case break-even with optimism bias**

A business case for replacing a legacy component states: build cost £800,000 spread evenly over years 1 and 2; net benefits £0 in years 1-2, then £320,000 per year from year 3. Treasury guidance requires you to apply 40% optimism bias to build costs and reduce benefits by 20% for appraisal. After those adjustments, what is the cumulative break-even year?

- A) End of year 5
- B) During year 6
- C) During year 7
- D) During year 8

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Adjust first, then model. Adjusted build cost: £800,000 × 1.40 = £1,120,000 (spread over years 1-2: £560,000 each). Adjusted annual benefit: £320,000 × 0.80 = £256,000 from year 3. Cumulative position: end year 1: −£560,000; end year 2: −£1,120,000; end year 3: −£1,120,000 + £256,000 = −£864,000; end year 4: −£608,000; end year 5: −£352,000; end year 6: −£96,000; during year 7: −£96,000 + £256,000 turns positive £96,000 ÷ £256,000 ≈ 0.375 of the way through — break-even during year 7. Distractor A (end of year 5) is the unadjusted case: build £800,000, benefits £320,000/year from year 3 give −800 + 320 × 2.5 = 0 midway through year 5 — the number the case's author will quote. The adjusted case pushes break-even out by roughly two years. Both numbers are true statements about different scenarios, and the service-owner skill is knowing which one belongs in which conversation: the adjusted figure is the planning basis (ensuring a business case provides sustainable funding means funding the adjusted case, not the optimistic one), while the gap between the two is your delivery challenge — every week of build discipline and benefits management claws the real outcome back from the 40%-bias assumption towards the original plan. If the investment only makes sense unadjusted, it does not yet make sense.

**Question 12 (hard) — Interpreting a demand forecast against capacity plans**

Applications to your service have grown: 18,000 (two years ago), 21,600 (last year), 25,920 (this year). Your head of operations proposes next year's capacity plan based on adding the recent average annual increase (about 3,960) to this year's volume. If growth is actually proportional (compound), how many applications should you plan for, and how large is the shortfall in the proposed plan?

- A) Plan for 29,880; no shortfall — the proposal matches
- B) Plan for 31,104; the proposal underestimates by about 1,224
- C) Plan for 31,104; the proposal underestimates by about 5,184
- D) Plan for 33,696; the proposal underestimates by about 3,816

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Identify the growth pattern first. Check ratios: 21,600 ÷ 18,000 = 1.20; 25,920 ÷ 21,600 = 1.20 — growth is 20% per year, compound, not linear. Next year's forecast: 25,920 × 1.20 = 31,104. The proposal's linear method: average absolute increase = (3,600 + 4,320) ÷ 2 = 3,960; 25,920 + 3,960 = 29,880. Shortfall: 31,104 − 29,880 = 1,224 applications — option B. (Distractor C subtracts the wrong pair; D compounds two years forward.) A 1,224-application planning gap at, say, 60 seconds of staff time per application is about 20 working days of unplanned effort — or, read through Question 10's lens, a backlog seed planted at the start of the year. The deeper point is method selection: linear extrapolation systematically underestimates compound growth, and the error compounds annually — next year the same method would fall short by even more. When you review a capacity plan, the first question is not "what is the number?" but "what growth model produced it, and does the history support that model?" — two ratio checks settle it in under a minute. Coordinating operations, digital and delivery teams around a service includes being the person who makes the forecast's assumptions explicit, because every downstream decision — recruitment, funding, supplier volumes — inherits them silently.

### Preparation tips

- **Rebuild one real number each day.** Take a figure from your service's latest performance pack — a unit cost, a take-up rate, a benefit actual — and reproduce it from raw components. Ten minutes daily builds speed on genuine material, and occasionally finds a real error.
- **Say the base before you calculate.** Percentage errors are base errors: change on the old value, rates on the correct denominator, savings net of the receiving channel. Naming the base aloud is a thirty-second insurance policy.
- **Model the structure before the arithmetic.** For multi-step questions, sketch the shape first ("backlog ÷ net rate", "fixed fee ÷ rate difference") and only then compute. Structural errors cost the mark; arithmetic slips are catchable with an estimate.
- **Check ratios before extrapolating.** Two divisions tell you whether a series is linear or compound. Make that check reflexive — in tests and in every capacity or demand forecast you sign off.
- **Use the calculator, verify by estimate.** Sanity-check each result against a rough mental figure ("about three-quarters", "roughly £27k") so a keying slip cannot survive.
- **Practise the adjusted case.** Rework one live business case with optimism bias and benefit haircuts applied. The habit of planning on adjusted numbers is both test material and Treasury-compliant professional practice.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Averaging percentages across different volumes.** Aggregate the numerators and denominators, then divide once. Averaged daily or regional percentages are wrong whenever volumes differ — which is always.
- **Counting gross savings as net.** Channel shift, automation and supplier switches all involve a receiving cost. Subtract it before the benefit enters any table with your name on it.
- **Points-versus-percent confusion.** A rise from 68% to 75% is 7 points and about 10%. Publish both when it matters; never let the flattering form travel alone.
- **Evaluating volume-sensitive pricing at one volume.** Any comparison involving a fixed fee changes winner with volume. Compute the break-even point before concluding — and before negotiating.
- **Linear extrapolation of compound growth.** Check the ratios. If they are stable, the series is compound, and the linear method will undershoot — increasingly, every year.
- **Trusting a tracker's commentary over its arithmetic.** "On track" and "accelerating" are claims; position, trajectory and the condition for recovery are calculations. Do the calculation before accepting the adjective.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately you extract meaning from written material under time pressure, and how well you distinguish between what a text states, what it implies, and what it simply does not address. The materials mirror your working reading diet: service standard guidance, assessment reports, policy instructions, ministerial correspondence, supplier letters, user research summaries and board minutes.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 15 to 25 minutes with 15 to 30 questions. The commonest question type presents a short passage followed by statements you must classify as True (definitely follows from the passage), False (definitely contradicted by the passage), or Cannot Say (the passage provides insufficient information either way). Other question types test inference strength, identifying an author's main point, and judging which of several rewrites communicates most clearly. Scoring is objective against a norm group, and the cardinal rule is that you answer from the passage alone — not from your professional knowledge, however extensive.

For a service owner, verbal reasoning is core infrastructure. You inform and influence stakeholders and build the consensus that enables work to progress — which means reading their letters, minutes and policy papers at exactly the precision they were drafted with. You guide teams through governance and assurance processes, where the difference between a condition, a recommendation and an observation in an assessment report determines what your team must actually do. You direct the stakeholder relationship strategy for your teams, negotiate with senior stakeholders to resolve issues, and share performance narratives that must be exactly as strong as the evidence beneath them. A misread sentence at your level does not stay private: it becomes a misbriefed director, a wrongly scoped commitment, or a governance breach discovered at the worst moment. Employers assess verbal reasoning because it predicts whether a candidate reads and writes with the precision that accountability demands.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Comprehension of dense passages** maps to **Governance and assurance**: assessment reports, spend control letters and standards guidance are written in careful, conditional language; guiding teams through these processes starts with extracting their meaning exactly — including the hedges and conditions.
- **True/False/Cannot Say discipline** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management** and your accountability for decisions: acting on what a policy letter actually commits to, rather than what you hope it means, is the difference between consensus that holds and consensus that evaporates at the first test.
- **Inference judgement** maps to **Applying user-centred insights** and **Leading performance and benefits**: research summaries and performance narratives invite over-reading; your job is to detect what the evidence supports at what strength — and to brief others on exactly that, no more.
- **Clear-writing judgement** maps to **Strategic ownership** and **influencing and persuading stakeholders**: strategies, business cases and board papers persuade through clarity; you set the writing standard for your service and improve others' drafts through feedback that names the technique.
- **Identifying the main point** maps to your duty to **inform and influence stakeholders, getting consensus that enables work to progress**: a twelve-page report has one message your director needs; finding it fast, without distorting it, is a daily act of verbal reasoning — and choosing which message leads is itself a strategic act.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — True/False/Cannot Say on service standard guidance**

Passage: "An assessment at the end of discovery is not required; however, services with over 100,000 transactions per year must be assessed before moving from alpha to private beta, and again before moving from private beta to public beta. Assessment reports are published openly unless publication would compromise security."

Statement: "All assessment reports are published openly."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage states reports are published openly unless publication would compromise security — an explicit exception. The statement's "all" requires publication in every case, which the stated exception contradicts, so the statement is false. The discipline: absolute words in a statement ("all", "always", "never") are contradicted by any stated exception in the passage, so hunt for the "unless" before answering. Note the boundary of the reasoning: the passage does not tell you how often the security exception is used — a statement like "some reports are withheld" would be Cannot Say, because the exception's existence does not prove it has ever been exercised. This mirrors a real habit of governance reading: the operative content of standards and policies usually lives in their exception and threshold clauses (notice the 100,000-transaction threshold quietly deciding which services the assessment duty touches at all), and teams look to you to know not just the rule but the conditions under which it bends.

**Question 2 (easy) — Reading a spend approval letter precisely**

Passage (from a spend approval letter): "Approval is granted for expenditure of up to £2.4 million on the case management replacement, conditional on: (a) reuse of the department's existing hosting arrangements; (b) a benefits review at the six-month point; and (c) return to this board before any contract extension beyond the initial two-year term. Approval does not constitute endorsement of the proposed supplier."

Statement: "The board has approved £2.4 million of spending on the case management replacement."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The letter grants approval for expenditure "of up to £2.4 million" on the named purpose — the statement paraphrases this accurately (approving spending up to a ceiling is approving that spending; the statement does not claim the money must all be spent). The conditions do not negate the approval; they qualify how it must be exercised, and none has failed in the text. Note what would make similar statements fail: "The board has endorsed the supplier" is directly contradicted by the final sentence; "The board has approved a three-year contract" is contradicted by condition (c); "The benefits review has been scheduled" would be Cannot Say. Reading approvals at their exact scope — amount, purpose, conditions, and what was pointedly not approved — is the daily craft of financial ownership: many governance failures begin with someone remembering an approval as broader than its letter, and you are the person your teams rely on to remember it exactly.

**Question 3 (easy) — Identifying the main point of an assessment report extract**

Passage: "The team demonstrated a strong understanding of its users, supported by regular research with participants across the full range of access needs. The service performed well in usability testing. However, the panel was not shown evidence that the service can operate within its projected running costs, and the operating model relies on manual steps that will not scale at public-beta volumes. The panel's view is that these operational and financial gaps, not the user experience, represent the principal risk to the service's sustainability."

What is the report's main message?

- A) The team does not understand its users.
- B) The service's usability needs improvement before public beta.
- C) The principal risk to sustainability is operational and financial, not user experience.
- D) The service should be stopped.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage uses the "praise, however, verdict" structure, and the main point is stated explicitly after the pivot: "these operational and financial gaps, not the user experience, represent the principal risk". A and B contradict the praised elements; D overreaches — the panel identifies a principal risk but nowhere recommends stopping. Two service-owner notes. First, when you relay this report upward, lead with C — the temptation is to lead with the praise (it is your team's work) and bury the risk, but stakeholders who later discover the buried sentence will re-read every future summary you write with suspicion. Second, the finding lands squarely in your accountabilities — a scalable operating model and sustainable funding are your named duties, not the team's — so the honest brief also says who owns the fix. Assessment reports are most useful when the accountable person reads them as a to-do list addressed personally.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Cannot Say discipline on a policy letter**

Passage (from a policy team letter): "Ministers have agreed in principle to the simplification of the eligibility rules from next April. Detailed regulations are being drafted and will be subject to consultation this autumn. We anticipate the consultation will conclude by December. The service should therefore begin exploratory work on implementing the simplified rules, while recognising that final policy positions may change through the consultation process."

Statement: "The simplified eligibility rules will take effect next April."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Parse the commitments precisely. "Agreed in principle" is not a final decision; the letter then says explicitly that "final policy positions may change through the consultation process". So the April date is a stated intention surrounded by stated uncertainty: the passage neither guarantees the rules will take effect in April (blocking True) nor says they will not or that the date has moved (blocking False). Cannot Say. The tempting wrong answer is True, anchored on "from next April" read as a settled fact — but "in principle" and "may change" are load-bearing qualifiers, and policy letters choose such words deliberately. The service-owner translation of this reading: plan exploratory work now (the letter licenses it), architect for rule flexibility (the letter warns you), and do not commit hard delivery dates or public communications to April until regulations are laid. Balancing user needs, business requirements and policy starts with reading policy signals at exactly their stated strength — teams that build to "agreed in principle" as if it were law rebuild twice.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Inference from board minutes**

Passage (board minutes extract): "The board received the service's quarterly performance report. Members noted the improvement in digital take-up and the reduction in failure demand. The chair asked for a deep-dive on accessibility compliance at the next meeting. The proposed expansion to a second user group was deferred pending the outcome of the spending review. The service owner was asked to provide monthly, rather than quarterly, financial reporting until further notice."

Which inference is best supported?

- A) The board is dissatisfied with the service's overall performance.
- B) The board has increased its financial scrutiny of the service.
- C) The expansion to a second user group has been cancelled.
- D) The service has failed its accessibility obligations.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Moving from quarterly to monthly financial reporting is a concrete, stated act of increased financial scrutiny — B follows directly from a recorded decision. A overreaches: the minutes record praise for two headline metrics, and heightened scrutiny coexists with satisfaction (the spending review context is a sufficient explanation). C distorts "deferred pending the outcome of the spending review" into "cancelled" — deferral with a named trigger is the opposite of a closed door. D converts a requested deep-dive into a finding; a chair can ask to examine compliance without any failure existing. The skill is calibrated inference — concluding at exactly the strength the text supports — and its workplace value is in how you brief your teams afterwards: "the board wants closer financial sight during the spending review, and accessibility is on next quarter's agenda — let's be excellent in both" is accurate and motivating, while "the board doesn't trust us" is unsupported and corrosive. Minutes are read by many people; the service owner's version of events becomes the team's reality, so calibrate it.

**Question 6 (moderate) — True/False/Cannot Say on supplier correspondence**

Passage (from a supplier letter): "We acknowledge the service credits applied for the October performance failures. We have implemented the agreed remediation plan, and November performance met all service levels. Given this recovery, we request that the credits for October be waived as a gesture of partnership. We note that continued application of credits may require us to review the resourcing model for this account."

Statement: "The supplier has threatened to withdraw from the contract if the credits are not waived."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Examine what the final sentence actually says: continued credits "may require us to review the resourcing model for this account". That is a pointed signal — commercial readers will feel its weight — but it is not a stated threat to withdraw from the contract: "review the resourcing model" could mean reducing senior staff on the account, changing personnel, or, at the limit, something more serious; the letter deliberately does not say. So the statement is not supported (no withdrawal is mentioned) and not contradicted (the vague warning is consistent with many intentions): Cannot Say. The craft here runs both ways. As a reader, you log the fact (no threat made) while acting on the subtext (the account is under commercial strain — raise it at the next service review, and check your exit and continuity provisions as routine prudence). As the accountable owner of supplier relationships, notice the letter's technique — deliberate ambiguity that signals pressure while remaining deniable — because recognising the register stops you both under-reacting and escalating a letter that never quite said what you felt it said.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Judging the clearest rewrite**

A product manager drafts this for your service's board paper: "It is considered that the currently observed deterioration in the timeliness of case processing could potentially be attributable, at least in part, to the fact that the volume of incoming cases has been trending upwards, although other contributing factors cannot at this stage be definitively ruled out." Which rewrite is clearest while preserving the meaning?

- A) "Case processing has slowed because case volumes have risen."
- B) "Case processing has slowed. Rising case volumes are the likely main cause; we are checking other possible factors."
- C) "Rising volumes may possibly be a potential factor in timeliness deterioration, among others not yet excluded."
- D) "Case processing timeliness has deteriorated and volumes have risen, but no conclusions can be drawn."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The original contains one finding (processing has slowed), one probable cause (rising volumes) and one honest caveat (other factors not yet excluded) — buried under seven hedges. B preserves all three elements in eleven working words per sentence: fact, likely cause with calibrated confidence ("likely main cause"), and an active caveat ("we are checking") that tells the board work is underway. A over-claims: it converts "could potentially be attributable, at least in part" into settled causation, changing the meaning. C reproduces the fog with fewer words — hedges stacked three deep ("may possibly be a potential") say nothing at any strength. D under-claims: the original does support a probable-cause reading, and "no conclusions can be drawn" discards genuine information, which is as distorting as inflation. The coaching point for your product manager — name the technique, so they own it next time: state the fact plainly; give the leading explanation at its honest probability; convert caveats into actions with owners. Boards fund and unblock services whose papers read like B; papers like C teach boards to stop reading, and influencing stakeholders to enable progress is, at base, a writing standard you enforce kindly and relentlessly.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Translating research findings for a policy audience**

Passage (from a user research summary): "Across 18 usability sessions with participants who have low digital confidence, 14 abandoned the identity verification step. The most common failure point was the requirement for a photocard driving licence or passport: 11 of the 14 who abandoned held neither document. Participants who reached the alternative postal route completed it successfully, but 9 participants did not notice the postal option existed."

You must brief the policy director in two sentences. Which briefing is accurate?

- A) "Users cannot complete identity verification; the service is failing and needs a policy change to remove verification."
- B) "Most low-digital-confidence users in our research abandoned at identity verification, mainly because they lack passports or driving licences; the postal alternative works, but most participants never found it. We should make the postal route prominent now, and this evidence supports the case for accepting a wider range of documents."
- C) "14 of 18 users failed the service, proving that 78% of citizens cannot verify their identity."
- D) "There are some usability issues at verification which the team is addressing; no policy involvement is needed."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Test each briefing against the passage. A inflates a segment finding ("participants with low digital confidence") into "users" generally, and prescribes removing verification — far beyond the evidence, which actually shows verification can work when the right route is findable. C commits the sampling sin: 18 qualitative sessions with a deliberately selected group cannot yield a population percentage, and "proving… 78% of citizens" is the kind of claim that discredits the research when challenged. D minimises in the other direction and — worse for a service owner — hides the policy-relevant fact: the document requirement is a policy choice, and 11 of 14 abandonments trace to it, so telling the policy director "no policy involvement needed" withholds exactly the insight they own. B preserves the segment scope, the causal chain (documents → abandonment; discoverability → postal route unused), and splits the response correctly between what the team can fix now (prominence of the postal route) and what needs policy movement (accepted documents) — with the evidence attached at its honest strength. Using user insights to make strategic decisions means exactly this translation: findings carried across the technical-policy boundary without gaining or losing strength in transit.

**Question 9 (hard) — Cannot Say under diplomatic drafting**

Passage (from another department's letter about a shared component): "We recognise the dependency your service has on the address-lookup component and the importance of its availability to your users. Funding for the component beyond March is part of our current planning round, in which a number of priorities are being weighed. We will communicate the outcome as soon as decisions are made. In the meantime, we suggest your continuity planning reflect the full range of possible outcomes."

Statement: "Funding for the address-lookup component will end in March."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The letter places post-March funding inside an undecided planning round and explicitly declines to pre-announce the outcome. Nothing states funding will end (blocking True); nothing states it will continue (blocking False): Cannot Say. But grade the signals, because this letter is doing careful work: "a number of priorities are being weighed" declines to rank your dependency highly; and "continuity planning should reflect the full range of possible outcomes" is a drafted warning — a department confident of continued funding does not advise you to plan for its absence. The professional response operates on both layers simultaneously. On the record: the letter confirms no decision, so you log the dependency risk with a decision date to chase. In action: you treat the warning as real — commission the continuity options now (alternative providers, caching, degraded service modes), cost them, and raise the dependency at director level while options remain open. Ensuring appropriate governance includes ensuring the risk register reflects what letters actually said; directing stakeholder strategy includes hearing what they carefully did not say. Confusing the two layers — logging subtext as fact, or ignoring subtext because it is not fact — is how services get surprised in March.

**Question 10 (hard) — Evaluating argument strength in a prioritisation debate**

Passage: "At the prioritisation board, the operations head argued: 'Complaint volumes rose 30% in the quarter after we launched the new portal. The portal is clearly driving complaints and we should roll it back.' The product manager responded: 'Total transactions rose 45% in the same period, so complaints per transaction actually fell. Moreover, the complaint categorisation shows the rise concentrated in fee disputes, which relate to the fee change that took effect the same month as launch. Rolling back the portal would sacrifice the take-up gain without addressing the actual driver.'"

Which is the most accurate analysis?

- A) The operations head's argument stands because absolute complaint volumes are what users experience.
- B) The product manager's response is stronger: it normalises by volume and offers a competing cause with distinguishing evidence — though "fee disputes" categorisation would still benefit from verification before the rollback question is finally closed.
- C) Both arguments are equally valid interpretations of the same data.
- D) The product manager has proven the portal caused no complaints.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Work through the logic. The operations head's argument rests on a raw count across a period when the denominator changed: 30% more complaints alongside 45% more transactions means the complaint rate fell (1.30 ÷ 1.45 ≈ 0.90 — roughly 10% fewer complaints per transaction). Rate, not count, is the right measure of whether the portal is "driving complaints". The product manager adds a second, independent strand: the rise concentrates in fee disputes, coinciding with a simultaneous fee change — a competing cause with distinguishing evidence, which is exactly how you defeat a post hoc claim. So B's ranking is right, and its caveat is what makes it the best answer rather than merely a partisan one: the categorisation data should be verified (was coding consistent? did the portal make fee information more visible, contributing indirectly?) before the question closes. A mistakes salience for validity — absolute volumes do matter operationally (staffing the complaints team), but they cannot carry the causal claim or the rollback decision. C is false balance: one argument normalises and distinguishes; the other does neither. D overcorrects — the product manager has shown the complaint rate fell and offered a better explanation, not proven portal innocence. Chairing this well — crediting the strongest version of both sides, naming the missing verification, and keeping the decision open until it lands — is what evidence-led prioritisation looks like from the chair you occupy.

**Question 11 (hard) — Reading a governance framework against a scenario**

Passage (from your department's assurance framework): "Services in public beta must report performance monthly to the service board. Where a service misses a key performance indicator for two consecutive months, the service owner must present a recovery plan to the board. Where a KPI is missed for three consecutive months, the board must consider whether the service should return to private beta. A KPI change may be proposed at any time but takes effect only from the start of the next reporting quarter."

Scenario: Your service missed its completion-rate KPI in April and May. In early June you proposed a revised (lower) completion-rate KPI, accepted by the board on 20 June. The service's June performance met the revised threshold but missed the original one. It is early July, at the board meeting. Which statement is accurate?

- A) No recovery plan is required, because the revised KPI was met in June.
- B) A recovery plan was already required after May's miss, and June counts as a third consecutive miss against the original KPI — which remained in effect through June — so the board must consider a return to private beta.
- C) The June KPI change applies retrospectively to the whole quarter, so only April and May count as misses.
- D) The board may not consider a return to private beta because a KPI change was in progress.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Apply the framework's clauses in order. April and May are two consecutive misses: the recovery-plan duty was triggered at that point, regardless of anything that happened later. The KPI change: proposed in June, accepted 20 June — but the framework says changes take effect "only from the start of the next reporting quarter", so through June the original KPI remained the measure in force, and June's performance missed it: three consecutive misses. The three-month clause then obliges the board to consider a return to private beta — "consider", note, not "decide": the framework mandates the discussion, not the outcome, and a board satisfied by trajectory and recovery plan can consider and decline. A applies the revised KPI a quarter early; C invents retrospection the text excludes; D invents a suspension mechanism nowhere stated. The uncomfortable subtext deserves naming, because assessments at your level increasingly probe it: proposing a KPI reduction in the middle of a miss sequence is procedurally legitimate and reputationally expensive — it reads as moving the goalposts, whatever its merits. The stronger sequence is recovery plan first, evidence that the original target was miscalibrated second, KPI proposal third, with the board walked through all three. Sharing your governance processes so stakeholders trust them means never letting a technically valid move look like an evasion — trust in governance, once spent, prices every future proposal you bring.

**Question 12 (hard) — Synthesising three documents into a decision brief**

Passage: Three documents about your service's proposed expansion. (1) The user research report: "Research with 24 participants from the proposed new user group indicates their needs differ substantially from current users; 60% of tested journeys required assisted support. Serving them well would require significant journey redesign." (2) The finance paper: "Expansion within the current operating model adds an estimated £900,000 annual cost against £600,000 in attributable annual benefits. A redesigned model could reduce the cost gap but carries delivery risk and an unquantified build cost." (3) The policy steer: "Ministers attach high priority to coverage of the new group within this Parliament and expect a credible plan by year end; timing within that window is for the department to propose."

Which decision brief most accurately synthesises the three documents?

- A) "Research, finance and policy all point against expansion; we should decline and record the risk."
- B) "Ministers require immediate expansion; we should proceed with the current model and manage the losses."
- C) "The documents converge on a credible path: not whether but how and when — a redesigned journey and operating model, sequenced to produce a plan by year end, meeting policy priority within this Parliament while avoiding the current model's £300,000 annual shortfall and the research-evidenced mismatch."
- D) "The documents conflict irreconcilably; the decision should be escalated to ministers to resolve."

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Read what each document actually constrains. The policy steer fixes the destination (coverage within this Parliament) and one milestone (credible plan by year end) but explicitly delegates timing "within that window". The research does not oppose expansion — it opposes expansion through the current journeys, and specifies what good would require (redesign for a group with different needs, 60% of tested journeys currently needing assisted support). The finance paper does not oppose expansion either — it prices the current model at a £300,000 annual shortfall (900 − 600) and points at the redesigned model as the route to closing the gap, flagging honest unknowns (build cost, delivery risk). Laid side by side, the three documents are consistent: expand, but not like this, and not immediately — which is C. A manufactures a consensus against by reading each caveat as a veto — and would put you at odds with a ministerial priority on evidence that does not support refusal. B manufactures urgency the policy steer explicitly does not impose ("timing… for the department to propose") and commits to the one model all three documents undermine. D escalates a decision the documents equip you to make — ministers have already said what they want; proposing the how is precisely the accountable leader's job, and escalating it back up spends credibility. This synthesis pattern is the verbal core of strategic ownership: stakeholder documents rarely conflict as much as they first appear; the service owner's value is finding the reading that honours every genuine constraint — then writing the plan that proves it, with the unquantified build cost and delivery risk from document (2) as its first two workstreams.

### Preparation tips

- **Practise the passage-only discipline daily.** Take one paragraph from a real assessment report or policy letter, write one True, one False and one Cannot Say statement about it, and check yourself. Ten minutes a day for two weeks builds the core habit the test rewards.
- **Collect qualifier vocabulary.** "In principle", "subject to", "we note", "may require", "consider", "anticipate" — governance English encodes commitment levels in these words. For a week, flag them in your inbox and ask what each one commits its author to. The test's hardest items turn on them.
- **Read the statement before re-reading the passage.** Read the passage once for structure, then hunt for each statement's specific evidence. Faster and more accurate than holding everything in memory.
- **Rehearse two-sentence briefings.** Take a research summary or performance report and brief it for a named audience in two sentences; then audit whether every fact survived at its original strength. This is simultaneously test preparation and your actual job.
- **Grade arguments, not arguers.** Practise identifying normalisation (rates versus counts), competing causes, and evidence-versus-assertion in meeting papers. The verbal test's argument items are your prioritisation board in miniature.
- **Time-box and return.** Verbal items reward a steady 45-75 seconds each; if a passage resists, flag it and come back. Comprehension collapses when forced under frustration.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Importing professional knowledge.** You know how spend controls and assessments really work; the passage may define them differently. Answer from the text alone — separating the two is precisely what is being measured.
- **Reporting subtext as fact.** Diplomatic letters warn without stating. Cannot Say questions punish readers who log the warning as a commitment — even when acting on the warning is professionally correct.
- **Reading intentions as decisions.** "Agreed in principle", "anticipate", "deferred pending" — each marks an open question. Statements that treat them as settled are the classic Cannot Say (or False) traps, in tests and in delivery planning.
- **Absolutism asymmetry.** "All/always/never" statements fail on a single stated exception; "may/some" statements are cheap to support. Match the statement's strength to the passage's before answering.
- **Choosing rewrites that trade accuracy for punch.** The clearest option is the one that preserves fact, confidence level and caveat in plain words — not the shortest or most decisive-sounding. Over-claiming rewrites are deliberately planted.
- **Missing the delegation in a steer.** Stakeholder documents often fix the destination but delegate the route. Synthesis questions — and ministerial steers — reward readers who notice exactly what was left for them to decide.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement test (SJT) presents realistic workplace dilemmas and asks you to judge the effectiveness of possible responses. Formats vary: you may be asked to pick the most effective response, to pick both the most and the least effective, to rank all options, or to rate each option independently on an effectiveness scale. There are rarely absolute right answers in the way a numeric item has one; instead, scoring reflects the judgement of experienced practitioners and the employer's professional behaviour framework — for GDAD roles, behaviours such as user focus, collaboration, ownership, transparency, proportionate escalation, and inclusion.

The typical format is 12 to 25 scenarios in 25 to 40 minutes, usually untimed per item but timed overall. Unlike the other assessments, an SJT measures judgement and values in action: what you believe good looks like when goods conflict — user needs versus political pressure, candour versus consensus, pace versus assurance, this year's budget versus the service's long-term health.

For a service owner, the SJT is arguably the most role-relevant assessment of the four, because your work is a continuous stream of exactly these dilemmas. You are accountable for outcomes you deliver through teams you must empower rather than command. You prioritise between user needs, business requirements and policy — which means telling someone senior "no, and here is why" regularly and well. You act as the escalation point for major operational issues and financial risk, guide teams through governance without smothering them, hold suppliers to account while keeping partnerships warm, and create the open, trust-based culture in which teams do their best work — a culture whose reality is set by what you do under pressure, not by what you say at away days. Employers use SJTs at this level because sound judgement in these moments is precisely what accountability requires, and because scenario responses reveal how a candidate thinks about users, power and candour before an employer entrusts them with all three.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Accountability and escalation scenarios** map to being **an accountable leader** and **the escalation point for major operational issues**: knowing what to absorb, what to fix, what to escalate, and how to act when an incident, a funding gap or a governance breach lands on you — because everything about the service eventually does.
- **Stakeholder scenarios** map to **Stakeholder relationship management** and your duty to **inform and influence stakeholders, getting consensus that enables work to progress**: delivering unwelcome evidence to senior people, negotiating between policy and delivery, and disagreeing without damaging the relationship.
- **User-versus-pressure scenarios** map to **Applying user-centred insights**: advocating for user needs when they are the least powerful voice in the room, and using insights strategically rather than decoratively.
- **Financial and supplier scenarios** map to **Financial ownership**: making spending decisions across competing priorities, managing commercial risk, and keeping benefit claims honest.
- **Governance scenarios** map to **Governance and assurance**: keeping assurance proportionate and trusted, guiding teams through assessments, and never trading the record for comfort.
- **Team and culture scenarios** map to **creating the culture and environment for the team to do their best work**, **Life cycle management** (using evidence to decide when a team should continue, change direction or stop) and **Operational management**: developing people, protecting psychological safety, and making the hard calls — including stopping things — humanely.

In each explanation below, the reasoning references these professional behaviours explicitly, because SJT preparation is less about learning answers and more about internalising the value hierarchy behind them: users and outcomes first, transparency over comfort, evidence over advocacy, fix the system before blaming the person, and escalate facts early with options attached.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — The dashboard nobody wants shown**

Preparing for your service board, you find this month's completion rate has dropped four percentage points — the first decline in a year. Your delivery manager suggests: "Let's leave it off the summary slide this month; it's probably a blip and we'll know more by next board." What is the most effective response?

- A) Agree — presenting unverified bad news wastes board time and may be reversed next month.
- B) Include the figure with what you know so far and the investigation under way, and brief the chair before the meeting so the board hears it from you with context rather than discovering it.
- C) Include the figure but present it as a data-quality issue until the cause is confirmed.
- D) Postpone the board's performance item until the cause is understood.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Your accountability for performance includes accountability for the truthfulness of the performance story. B does the three things senior transparency requires: the fact goes in (a curated dashboard, once discovered — and it will be discovered, because board members read the underlying packs — converts every future green into a question); the fact goes in with its context (a decline plus "here is what we know and what we are doing" reads as a service under management, not a service in trouble); and the chair is pre-briefed, which is not spin but respect — chairs manage discussion better when material items do not ambush them. A gambles your credibility on "probably a blip", and if it is not, next month you present a two-month decline you knew about. C is worse than omission: labelling an unexplained decline a "data-quality issue" without evidence is a false statement to a board. D holds governance hostage to your comfort. The culture point cuts deepest: your delivery manager just proposed curating the record and watched how you responded — demonstrating the value of the service with honest data starts with what you do in exactly this moment, because whatever you do becomes the team's definition of acceptable.

**Question 2 (easy) — Credit where it is due**

At a cross-government showcase, a director praises "your brilliant redesign of the eligibility checker". The redesign was conceived and led by your interaction designer, who is standing nearby. What is the most effective response?

- A) Thank the director and describe the results the redesign achieved.
- B) Redirect the credit immediately and specifically — name the designer, their role in conceiving it, and invite them into the conversation.
- C) Say "the whole team worked hard on it" and continue.
- D) Thank the director now and pass the compliment to the designer privately later.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** B costs nothing and builds three things at once. It develops the designer: visibility with directors is career fuel that you, not they, are positioned to dispense, and sponsorship — making people's work visible to power — is a concrete form of creating the environment where people do their best work. It strengthens the culture: everyone within earshot learns that good work under you is seen and named, which is worth more than any values poster. And it enhances rather than diminishes you: leaders who redirect credit specifically are read, accurately, as leaders with strong teams — while accountability (which remains yours) and credit (which is theirs) are different currencies that senior people are expected to keep straight. A quietly banks the applause. C launders an individual's achievement into an anonymous collective — better than A, but a known mechanism by which quieter specialists go chronically under-recognised, which makes it an inclusion issue as well as a fairness one. D pays real currency at a private exchange rate; the public moment was the valuable one. Small scenario, foundational behaviour: advocating for your teams is listed in your role for a reason, and it is practised in seconds, not strategies.

**Question 3 (easy) — The minister's office wants a date**

A minister's office asks your director for a public launch date for your service's next major feature. Your director asks you for "a date we can announce". The team's honest position: high confidence in delivery within a three-month window, but a specific date now would be guesswork; private beta results in six weeks will narrow it. What is the most effective response?

- A) Give the middle of the window as the announced date — a 50/50 chance is acceptable for announcements.
- B) Give the end of the window as the announced date, privately treating the margin as contingency.
- C) Explain the confidence position to your director, recommend announcing the window now or a firm date in six weeks when beta evidence lands, and offer to help draft lines that give the office something concrete without a guess.
- D) Decline to provide any date until delivery is certain.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The test is whether you convert delivery uncertainty into stakeholder-usable honesty rather than into false precision or unhelpful refusal. C gives the director the full decision material (what is known, when it firms up), a recommendation rather than a shrug (window now, or date in six weeks — both announceable), and practical help with the political need (draft lines: "public launch in the autumn, with a confirmed date after the current testing phase" serves a minister's office perfectly well). A manufactures a coin-flip commitment; announced dates are remembered, and a public miss costs the service and the minister far more than a window ever would. B is the subtler trap: padding to the window's end feels prudent but announces your worst case as the plan — inviting either a "why so slow?" challenge or, if beta goes well, a service sitting finished while the announcement ages; and if the window itself was optimistic, even the padded date misses. D mistakes rigidity for integrity: "no date until certain" is not a position a service owner accountable to ministers can hold, and consensus that enables work to progress is built by offering stakeholders forms of certainty you can honour. The pattern for all announcement dilemmas: never guess, never stonewall — reshape the ask into something honest and useful, and put a date on when better information arrives.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Policy pressure against user evidence**

A policy team, under deadline pressure from their director, asks you to add a mandatory declaration step to your service's application journey within six weeks. Your user researcher's evidence from similar steps shows significant drop-out among users with low literacy, and your accessibility specialist flags likely compliance issues with the proposed wording. The policy intent itself is legitimate and has ministerial backing. What is the most effective response?

- A) Implement as requested — policy with ministerial backing outranks service preferences.
- B) Refuse to implement until full research on the specific proposal is complete.
- C) Take the policy intent as fixed and the implementation as negotiable: bring the evidence to the policy team, co-design an alternative that achieves the declaration's legal purpose with accessible wording and lower-friction placement, test it quickly, and agree the six-week plan around the revised design — escalating jointly only if the intent-versus-evidence conflict proves real.
- D) Implement the step as specified but add an analytics dashboard to monitor drop-out so the impact is documented.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Separate what is actually fixed (the policy intent, legitimately backed) from what is assumed fixed (this wording, this placement, this friction). Nearly all policy-versus-user conflicts live in the second category, and C works exactly there: the evidence travels to the policy team as design input rather than as a veto, the declaration's legal purpose is achieved through wording the accessibility specialist can pass and placement the research supports, and rapid testing inside the six weeks keeps the deadline honest. This is balancing user needs, business requirements and policy as a synthesis discipline, not a ranking exercise. A treats implementation detail as if it were ministerial instruction — it is not; ministers backed an intent, not a form design — and knowingly ships harm to your least powerful users, with the compliance risk landing on your accountability, not policy's. B inverts A: refusing pending "full research" reads as delivery obstruction, spends your relationship capital on a maximal position, and ignores that existing evidence already supports a better design now. D is the quiet failure mode with a clear conscience: implementing known harm while measuring it documents your foreknowledge without preventing anything — monitoring is C's companion, never its substitute. Behaviours: user advocacy exercised through collaboration, evidence over assertion, and escalation reserved for genuine intent-level conflict — jointly, so it reaches the directors as a shared problem, not a turf war.

**Question 5 (moderate) — The supplier relationship and the record**

Your service's main supplier has missed its availability SLA for two consecutive months. The account director — capable, likeable, and personally invested in the relationship — asks you to hold the service credits "in abeyance" while they fix an inherited platform problem, arguing that applying credits will trigger their internal red-account process and divert their best people into reporting. Your contract requires credits to be applied automatically on SLA breach. What is the most effective response?

- A) Hold the credits in abeyance for one quarter in exchange for a written recovery commitment.
- B) Apply the credits as the contract requires, tell the account director openly that you are doing so and why, and offer genuine partnership on the recovery plan — including senior air-cover for their engineers' remediation time.
- C) Apply double scrutiny: credits, plus immediate escalation to a formal contract dispute, to establish deterrence.
- D) Apply the credits but agree privately to refund them via a future change order if recovery succeeds.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The request is to suspend a contractual mechanism as a personal favour, and however warm the relationship, the answer that survives audit, benefit hindsight and precedent is B. The credits apply because the contract says they apply automatically — a service owner has no more authority to waive them informally than the supplier has to miss the SLA; "in abeyance" creates an off-book position that fails every test later (what do you tell the next supplier who asks? what does internal audit make of unapplied automatic credits? what is your negotiating position if recovery fails after two quiet quarters?). B applies the mechanism while doing everything legitimate for the relationship: candour before the credits land (no ambush), genuine collaboration on recovery, and — the senior touch — offering air-cover, which addresses the account director's real fear (their internal process consuming the fixers) through means that are actually yours to give. Note also that their red-account process is their governance doing its job: senior supplier attention on a failing account serves you. A trades your organisation's remedies for a promise, with you personally as the hole in the record. C is disproportionate — a dispute posture during a cooperative recovery poisons the partnership the contract exists to protect. D is A wearing a disguise, plus a misuse of change orders that converts a side deal into a procurement irregularity. The principle, worth internalising verbatim: the record is the record, the relationship is the relationship — act as escalation point for commercial risk by keeping both healthy and never trading one for the other.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Major incident, imperfect information**

At 11:20 you learn your service has been intermittently failing for 40 minutes: perhaps 30% of users cannot submit applications, cause unknown, team investigating. The press office has seen social media complaints and wants a public line "within the hour". Your director is in a meeting and unaware; the incident team's best guess at resolution is "hours, not minutes". What is the most effective set of first actions?

- A) Wait for the cause to be identified before communicating anything — premature statements create risk.
- B) Confirm the incident process is running with a clear lead; get a brief, honest holding line to the press office (impact acknowledged, workaround and alternative channel signposted, update time promised); send your director a two-line heads-up; ensure users see status information in the service itself; and schedule the next internal update cycle.
- C) Take direct command of the technical response yourself, relocating to sit with the incident team until resolution.
- D) Issue a public apology accepting responsibility and promising resolution by end of day.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Major incident response at your level is an information-flow role, not a heroic-technical one, and B covers the five flows in the right order: the fixing system (confirmed running, clearly led — your job is to check the machine works, not to become a part in it); users (in-service status and the assisted-digital alternative signposted — user harm is live, so user information is urgent; note this is where user focus shows up under pressure); the press office (an honest holding line: acknowledging impact, offering the workaround, promising an update time — all sayable without knowing the cause); your director (a two-line heads-up now, because seniors must never learn of incidents from the press office or social media; the escalation duty runs upward as well as toward you); and rhythm (a promised update cadence, which is what makes "we don't know yet" tolerable for everyone). A confuses cause with impact: you cannot yet explain the failure, but its user impact is known and already public on social media — silence cedes the narrative and reads as absence. C is the classic senior anti-pattern: it decapitates your coordination role, crowds the responders, and signals distrust of the incident lead — support is presence and obstacle-clearing, not command. D over-commits twice: responsibility before cause is known (what if it is an upstream provider?) and a deadline the team has told you is a guess. Acting as the escalation point for major operational issues means being the calm centre through which accurate information moves in every direction — starting in the first hour, which is the hour stakeholders remember.

**Question 7 (moderate) — The benefits figure that will not survive scrutiny**

Assembling your annual benefits report, you find the largest benefit — £1.1 million of "staff time released" — rests on a two-year-old assumption about task frequency that operational data now shows was roughly double reality. The corrected benefit is nearer £600,000. The report feeds the department's return to the Treasury; the original figure has been quoted publicly by your director; and the service's next funding case cites the £1.1 million. What is the most effective response?

- A) Report the £1.1 million this year — the assumption was legitimate when made — and quietly correct the methodology from next year.
- B) Correct the figure now, brief your director before the report lands (with the reason, the corrected number, and revised lines to take), and re-cut the funding case on the corrected benefit — strengthening it with other evidenced value where the case needs it.
- C) Report a range of £600,000 to £1.1 million to acknowledge uncertainty without singling out the old figure.
- D) Ask the finance team to decide which figure to use, since benefits methodology is their domain.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Once you know the assumption is wrong, the £1.1 million is no longer an estimate — it is a misstatement, and every day it survives in Treasury returns and funding cases it compounds: this is a question about what accountability means when the correction is expensive. B absorbs the cost now, while it is smallest: the report goes out true; the director is briefed before, not after, the corrected figure surfaces (protecting them from defending a number you knew was wrong — directors forgive corrections and do not forgive ambushes); and the funding case is rebuilt on ground that cannot collapse under a spending team's due diligence, which is worth more than the £500,000 of phantom benefit it loses. A is knowing misstatement with a tidy rationalisation — "legitimate when made" stopped being relevant the day the operational data arrived. C uses the language of uncertainty to launder a known error: the evidence does not support a range, it supports £600,000, and a reviewer who asks "what drives the top of the range?" leaves you defending the indefensible. D outsources an accountability that is named in your role — identifying measurable benefits is yours; finance can advise on method, but the decision to correct is not delegable, and routing it away from yourself is its own answer to the integrity question. Behaviours: transparency over comfort, protecting your seniors with candour, and the deepest one — ensuring a business case provides sustainable funding means funding won on evidence, because funding won on error is a liability with interest.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Two teams, one culture problem**

Your service's two delivery teams have diverged. Team A ships steadily, runs honest retrospectives, and its delivery manager routinely reports problems early. Team B ships erratically but its delivery manager presents polished, always-green updates — and you have just learned from a departing developer that Team B's stand-ups have become status theatre for the delivery manager, with real blockers discussed privately afterwards. What is the most effective response?

- A) Commission an independent health check of both teams to gather comparable evidence before acting.
- B) Move Team A's delivery manager to run both teams, since their approach demonstrably works.
- C) Address the system you have created before the individual: examine whether your own reactions reward green reporting and punish candour; talk with Team B's delivery manager directly about what you have observed and what you actually value; make problem-surfacing visibly safe and rewarded across the service; and follow up with Team B's members to hear the unfiltered picture.
- D) Raise Team B's delivery manager's reporting behaviour as a performance concern through line management.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The presenting problem is one delivery manager's theatre; the underlying question is why your service's incentive landscape produced honest reporting in one team and performance art in the other — and the departing developer's account (real blockers discussed privately after stand-up) tells you the team has already adapted to what it believes leadership wants. C starts where a culture-accountable leader must: with your own contribution. If board preparation praises green and interrogates amber, if Team A's honest problem-reporting earns them more scrutiny than Team B's polish, then Team B's delivery manager is responding rationally to signals you control. The direct conversation is still essential — specific observations, without staging an ambush from the departing developer's report alone — and the follow-up with team members tests whether change is real. Creating an open, trust-based culture is your named accountability, and it is made or unmade precisely here. A buys evidence you largely have (the shipping record, the developer's account, your own meeting experience) at the price of months and of signalling that concerns disappear into reviews. B punishes Team A's manager with a doubled job, decapitates Team B without diagnosis, and teaches every delivery manager that polish plus failure gets rescued while candour gets loaded. D converts a probable system problem into an individual case at the first move — if the behaviour persists after the environment demonstrably changes, line management becomes right; as an opening move it prosecutes the symptom. Behaviours: ownership of the system you lead, psychological safety as infrastructure, fix causes before people — and the quiet discipline of asking "what did my reactions teach them?" before asking "what is wrong with them?"

**Question 9 (hard) — The assessment your team wants to game**

Your service's beta assessment is in three weeks. Your delivery manager proposes: "Let's schedule the assessment demo around our strongest journeys, brief everyone to avoid mentioning the manual workaround in the verification step, and if the panel asks about operational metrics, we say monitoring is 'in development'." The manual workaround is real, used for about 15% of cases, and the team has a credible plan to remove it next quarter. What is the most effective response?

- A) Accept the approach — assessments are adversarial by nature, and every team presents its best face.
- B) Overrule it and instruct the team to present everything, including a full account of every known weakness.
- C) Reframe the preparation: showing your strongest journeys is legitimate; concealment is not — so present the workaround proactively with its usage data and removal plan, answer metric questions with what actually exists, and use the remaining three weeks to rehearse honest, evidence-backed answers rather than avoidances.
- D) Postpone the assessment until the workaround is removed next quarter, avoiding the issue entirely.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Draw the line where it actually sits: curation is legitimate (choosing strong journeys to demo is presentation, and every team does it); concealment is not (briefing people to avoid a known material fact, and describing absent monitoring as "in development", are attempts to obtain an assessment outcome by misleading the panel). C keeps the first and refuses the second — and then does something better than refusal: it converts the liability into evidence of competence. A team that says "15% of verifications use a manual workaround; here is the usage data, here is the removal plan, here is the risk we carry meanwhile" demonstrates exactly the self-aware, evidence-led operation that assessments exist to find — panels are composed of practitioners who have seen every avoidance technique, and discovered concealment fails assessments that disclosed weaknesses would have passed. B overcorrects into self-sabotage: an unstructured confession of "every known weakness" is not honesty, it is the absence of preparation; assessment is a discipline of accurate presentation, not a penance. D spends a quarter to avoid a conversation the service could win now, delays user value, and — note the incentive it sets — teaches the team that gaps are scheduling problems rather than things you talk about straightforwardly. The deepest reason C matters is in your framework verbatim: sharing the team's governance processes so stakeholders trust them to deliver value sustainably. Trust in your governance is an asset you spend for years — smooth assessments, lighter-touch assurance, benefit of the doubt in hard moments — and it is bought in exactly these three-week windows, by what you tell your team about how you win.

**Question 10 (hard) — Stop, pivot or persevere**

A workstream you sponsored personally — eighteen months, £700,000 spent — was meant to automate document checking. The evidence is now consistent: accuracy has plateaued well below the viable threshold, the supplier's roadmap offers no credible fix, and two comparable services have abandoned similar approaches. The team believes six more months and £200,000 "might crack it". Meanwhile the manual checking process the automation was meant to replace is deteriorating, and the operations lead has proposed a £150,000 workflow redesign with strong evidence from a comparable service. What is the most effective response?

- A) Fund the six-month extension — the £700,000 investment is only recovered if the automation eventually works.
- B) Stop the automation workstream on the evidence, fund the workflow redesign, and handle the ending deliberately: harvest what was learned and built, communicate the decision as evidence-led to the team and stakeholders, and protect the team members' standing so that working on a stopped initiative carries no stigma.
- C) Fund both the extension and the redesign in parallel, hedging the decision.
- D) Ask the team for one more assessment report in three months before deciding.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Read the evidence structure first: plateaued accuracy, no credible supplier fix, and two independent comparators abandoning the approach — three converging lines, against "might crack it", which is hope wearing an estimate. Using evidence to decide when a team should continue, change direction or stop is in your framework verbatim, and this is the stop case; the £700,000 is sunk and belongs in no forward-looking calculation (A is the sunk-cost fallacy in its natural habitat — the £200,000 question is only ever "is this the best use of the next £200,000?", and against a cheaper, better-evidenced alternative it is not). C sounds sophisticated but funds the weakest option anyway, splits attention, and delays the manual process's relief — hedges are for genuine uncertainty, and the evidence here has converged. D purchases three months of deterioration to learn what three lines of evidence already establish; deferral has costs, and "more data" is not free when the data you have is decisive. What elevates B beyond "stop it" is the second half, which is where service owners earn their accountability: harvesting the residual value (the document-classification components, the labelled data, the integration work — stopped initiatives are rarely worthless); telling the story truthfully upward and outward ("the evidence says stop, and here is what we learned" — which, done well, increases stakeholder confidence in every continue decision you make afterwards); and the culture move that determines your service's honesty for years — the team that worked eighteen months on this must visibly land well, because if stopping reads as career damage, no team will ever again bring you the evidence that their own workstream is failing. Your personal sponsorship raises the bar and the value: leaders who can stop their own initiatives on evidence are the ones whose judgement stakeholders come to trust without checking.

**Question 11 (hard) — The funding squeeze and the invisible investment**

Mid-year, your budget is cut by 15%. After honest analysis, you can protect the cut by pausing one of two things: (a) the front-end improvement roadmap — visible features that stakeholders and users notice, or (b) the resilience programme — legacy component replacement and failover work that prevents the class of outage your service suffered twice last year. A senior stakeholder urges you to protect the visible roadmap: "Nobody thanks you for outages that don't happen." Your operations data shows the legacy components' failure rate rising quarter on quarter. What is the most effective response?

- A) Protect the visible roadmap — stakeholder confidence funds the service, and resilience can resume next year.
- B) Protect the resilience programme, and treat the stakeholder's warning as the communications requirement it really is: present the decision with the failure-rate trend and the cost and user harm of last year's outages, publish leading indicators so the invisible work has visible progress, and give stakeholders reduced but honest front-end commitments.
- C) Split the cut evenly between both programmes to avoid choosing.
- D) Escalate the choice to your director, since stakeholder relations are at stake either way.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The data decides the substance: a rising failure rate on components that have already produced two outages is a trajectory, not a possibility, and pausing the work that addresses it while demand continues is choosing a future incident — one that will consume, at a stroke, more stakeholder confidence than a year of visible features can buy (ask what last year's outages cost in exactly the currency the stakeholder cares about). So the interesting content of this scenario is what B does with the stakeholder's argument, because "nobody thanks you for outages that don't happen" is not wrong — it is a true statement about the visibility asymmetry of preventive work, and the correct response is to fix the visibility, not the priority: bring the failure-rate trend and outage costs into the open (evidence, not assertion, carries the decision); create leading indicators so resilience progress is witnessable (components migrated, failover tests passed, recovery time trending down); and give the front-end roadmap honest reduced commitments rather than silence, so the visible programme is slowed, not abandoned. That is what informing and influencing stakeholders to get consensus actually means when the evidence points at the unpopular option. A trades a known rising risk for applause and schedules the outage that spends the applause. C halves both programmes' effectiveness to avoid the discomfort of deciding — prioritising across competing priorities is your named duty, and an even split is its abdication dressed as fairness. D escalates a decision that is squarely within your accountability and arrives without a recommendation; the defensible version of involving your director is B with a briefing — decision, evidence, communications plan — which is escalation as transparency, not as transfer. Behaviours: evidence over advocacy, long-term service health over short-term comfort, and the specifically senior skill of making invisible value visible instead of letting visibility set the priority.

**Question 12 (hard) — The confidence a junior colleague risked**

A junior operations analyst asks to speak privately. Nervously, they tell you that monthly performance figures sent to your service board have been "smoothed" for at least three months: their team leader adjusts the worst daily outage figures before aggregation, calling it "removing noise", and the analyst's questions were waved off. They beg you not to reveal who raised it — the team is small, and the team leader is well liked. You are accountable for these figures, and the smoothed version has understated downtime in three board reports. What is the most effective response?

- A) Thank them, then wait and watch next month's figures closely to catch the smoothing directly before acting.
- B) Confront the team leader immediately, without naming sources, and demand the practice stop and the figures be restated.
- C) Act on your own authority over the data rather than on the tip: initiate a routine-framed reconciliation of reported figures against raw monitoring data (a check your accountability justifies at any time), which will surface the discrepancy independently; then address what it finds — corrected reporting, restatement to the board, and a considered conversation with the team leader about what "removing noise" has meant — while checking back with the analyst and watching for any sign of reprisal.
- D) Refer the matter to internal audit as a data-integrity concern and let the formal process run.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Three duties collide: the record (board reports you are accountable for have understated downtime for a quarter — that is a live misstatement, not a management style issue); the person (a junior analyst took a real career risk, in a small team, on your discretion); and fairness to the team leader (whose conduct spans a spectrum from defensible-if-undocumented data cleaning to deliberate misrepresentation — "removing noise" is sometimes a legitimate practice done transparently, and you have one account of it). C is the only option that serves all three. The reconciliation is the move that matters: checking reported figures against raw monitoring data is squarely within your accountability, needs no justification, and — crucially — surfaces the discrepancy on evidence that owes nothing to the analyst, protecting them structurally rather than by promise alone. What follows then rests on independent findings: restating the board figures (your Question 7 discipline again — a known misstatement is corrected at the point of knowledge, with the board told why), and a conversation with the team leader that can now be evidence-led and fair — starting with what the reconciliation shows and hearing their account, which distinguishes coaching from discipline honestly. The check-back with the analyst and vigilance about reprisal honour the trust that brought you the information at all. A leaves three known-wrong reports standing while you fish for a fourth — the record's integrity does not wait on your surveillance convenience. B acts on a single unverified account, and "without naming sources" is paper protection in a small team where the team leader can count who asked questions; it also opens a conversation you cannot yet win if the answer is "my adjustments are documented, here is the method". D is not wrong so much as premature and heavy: audit may become right if C's reconciliation reveals deliberate falsification, but as a first move it converts a possibly-coachable failure into a formal case, removes your ability to calibrate the response, and — the culture cost — teaches everyone that quiet concerns go straight to formal machinery, which is how services stop hearing quiet concerns. Behaviours: accountability for the record exercised through your own authority, structural (not promissory) protection of psychological safety, fairness on evidence, and proportionate escalation — the formal route held ready, not reached for first.

### Preparation tips

- **Study your organisation's behaviour framework first.** SJTs are scored against a value hierarchy. Re-read the Civil Service behaviours and your framework's service owner expectations; the "most effective" option is the one those documents describe under pressure.
- **Practise separating the fixed from the assumed-fixed.** Many scenarios (policy asks, announcement pressure, supplier requests) hinge on noticing that the intent is fixed but the implementation is negotiable. Train the reflex of asking "what exactly is actually constrained here?"
- **Rehearse the record-versus-relationship line.** Several dilemmas turn on keeping records truthful while keeping relationships warm — credits applied with candour, corrections briefed before they land. Draft the actual sentences; you will need them verbatim in real reviews.
- **Audit your own incentive shadow.** For a week, notice what your reactions reward: which reports earn praise, which earn interrogation. Culture scenarios are answered well by leaders who already know what their behaviour teaches.
- **Debrief real dilemmas.** After each week, pick one live judgement call and map it to the option you actually chose — including the times you chose the right substance with the wrong sequence. Honest self-review is the highest-yield preparation there is.
- **Answer as the accountable leader you are, consistently.** Do not guess a "test personality". Modern SJTs include consistency signals, and the judgement being measured is the one you exercise daily; calibrated authenticity outperforms performance.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Choosing decisive-looking over effective.** Taking command of the incident room, overruling the delivery manager, confronting on a tip — unilateral action reads as leadership but scores as skipped collaboration and skipped evidence. Effectiveness usually routes through systems and people.
- **Protecting people from information.** Curated dashboards, held-back declines, smoothed figures, quiet abeyances — kindly motivated concealment is the most heavily penalised pattern in senior SJTs, because it is the most damaging pattern in senior reality.
- **Treating sunk costs as reasons.** "We've invested too much to stop" appears in some form in most stop/pivot scenarios. The only question is the best use of the next pound; the framework language — use evidence to decide when to continue, change direction or stop — is the scoring key.
- **Escalating instead of deciding — or deciding instead of briefing.** Sending your own accountabilities upward scores poorly; so does exercising them silently. The pattern that scores: decide within your accountability, with evidence, and keep seniors informed before consequences reach them.
- **Splitting the difference to avoid choosing.** Even splits, parallel funding, and ranges that straddle known truths look balanced and score as abdication. Prioritisation is the job; the middle is rarely where the evidence points.
- **Solving the instance and damaging the system.** Watch the second layer: the confrontation that fixes one report but burns the channel that surfaces the next one; the rescue that teaches polish beats candour. Effective options fix the case in a way that improves what the whole service learns.
