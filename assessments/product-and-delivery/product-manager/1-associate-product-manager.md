# Associate Product Manager - Psychometric Assessment Resources

## Introduction

Welcome! This guide has been written especially for you as an associate product manager working within the UK Government Digital and Data profession. So what is a psychometric assessment? It is a structured, standardised exercise that measures the thinking skills and workplace behaviours that predict success in a role. That may sound intimidating, but it does not need to be. The assessments described in this guide are job-specific: they use the everyday materials of your working life — backlogs, user stories, kanban boards, sprint plans, user research findings, stakeholder emails, and simple product data — rather than abstract puzzles with no connection to your job. If you can reason clearly about the work you already do, you can succeed at these assessments. Practice simply helps you show that ability under timed conditions.

Why do these assessments matter for your role in particular? As an associate product manager, you are responsible for features, components, or part of a product, and you contribute to the delivery of value. Day to day, you work with product managers to advocate for user needs, you help prioritise so the greatest value is delivered first, and you manage a smaller product with support. All of that depends on four abilities that assessments measure directly: thinking logically about product information (cognitive ability), working confidently with numbers such as velocity and satisfaction scores (numeric reasoning), reading and understanding written material accurately (verbal reasoning), and choosing sensible actions in tricky workplace situations (situational judgement).

Finally, remember that assessments are just one way of showing your ability. ## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive ability assessment measures the exact mental processes your role demands, using practical workplace scenarios rather than abstract shapes or riddles. Instead of asking you to complete a sequence of triangles, it asks you to reason about the things you handle every day as an associate product manager: user stories, acceptance criteria, kanban boards, sprint plans, simple roadmaps, and notes from user research sessions.

The typical format is an online, timed test lasting 15 to 30 minutes, with somewhere between 15 and 30 questions. Questions cover pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective: your answers are compared against a norm group of people at a similar level, and many modern platforms adapt the difficulty of questions based on how you are doing. Employers usually see a balance of speed and accuracy rather than a single number, so it pays to work steadily and carefully rather than rushing. Most platforms offer a few ungraded practice questions before the real test starts, so you can get comfortable with the screens without any pressure.

Why do employers use this kind of assessment for product roles? Because product management is, at its heart, structured thinking. Even at associate level, you are asked to bring together the views of a multidisciplinary team, user needs, and business needs, and to help frame the problem clearly. You check that stories are consistent with each other, you notice when a plan contradicts itself, and you help decide what the team should do first. A well-designed cognitive assessment simulates those demands in miniature, which is why performance on it predicts performance in the job. The good news is that these are learnable skills, and every question you practise makes the real thing feel more familiar.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The assessment dimensions map directly to the named skills in your role summary:

- **Pattern recognition** maps to your **Agile and Lean practices** skill: when you visualise and make visible the work of the team — on a kanban board or a sprint plan — you need to spot recurring patterns, such as work that keeps getting stuck in the same column, or stories that always miss a required field.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Product management** and **Managing product outcomes**: making or recommending an evidence-based decision using insight and data means reasoning correctly from the evidence you have, and only from that evidence.
- **Error checking** maps to **Agile and Lean practices** and **Product management**: providing a clear, open, and transparent framework for delivery means keeping backlogs, boards, and plans internally consistent, and noticing quickly when they are not.
- **Prioritisation** maps to **Product management** and your duty to prioritise delivery of greatest value: you create and prioritise actionable goals for your teams, balancing user needs and business perspectives.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Applying user-centred insights** and **Creating value for money**: you work with others to define the problem, decide when you have enough insight to make a decision, and identify opportunities for reuse that save time and money.

Every question below uses artefacts from this list, so as you practise the assessment technique you are also rehearsing the real job.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern recognition on a kanban board**

Your team's kanban board has five columns in a fixed order: Backlog, Ready, In progress, In review, Done. Your team's working agreement says every card must move one column at a time, left to right, and never skip a column. On Friday you review the board history for four cards:

1. Card A: Backlog → Ready → In progress → In review → Done
2. Card B: Backlog → Ready → In progress → In review
3. Card C: Backlog → In progress → In review → Done
4. Card D: Backlog → Ready → In progress

Which card has broken the working agreement?

- A) Card A
- B) Card B
- C) Card C
- D) Card D

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Work through each card against the rule: one column at a time, left to right, no skipping. Card A visits every column in order — fine. Cards B and D have simply not finished yet; nothing in the rule says a card must reach Done by Friday, so an incomplete journey is not a breach. Card C jumps straight from Backlog to In progress, skipping Ready — that is the breach. This is exactly the kind of check you do when you make the team's work visible: a card that skips Ready may not have been refined or estimated, which can cause surprises mid-sprint. In the assessment, the technique is to test each option against the stated rule and nothing else — do not penalise cards for being unfinished when the rule says nothing about finishing.

**Question 2 (easy) — Logical deduction from a definition of done**

Your team's definition of done states: "A story is done only if it has passed accessibility checks and has been reviewed by another developer." A colleague tells you: "Story 42 has passed accessibility checks." Which one of the following must be true?

- A) Story 42 is done.
- B) Story 42 has been reviewed by another developer.
- C) Story 42 is not done.
- D) Story 42 might or might not be done — you cannot tell from this information alone.

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** The definition of done sets two conditions, and both must be met. You know one condition (accessibility checks) is satisfied, but you know nothing about the other (developer review). So you cannot conclude the story is done (option A), and you equally cannot conclude it is not done (option C) — the review may or may not have happened. Option B invents information that was never given. The only safe conclusion is D. Assessments reward this discipline of concluding only what the information guarantees, and so does your job: when you make or recommend an evidence-based decision, you must be honest about what the evidence does and does not show. Saying "we don't know yet — let me check" is a strength, not a weakness.

**Question 3 (easy) — Error checking user story format**

Your product manager has asked you to check that all new user stories follow the team's template: "As a [type of user], I want [goal], so that [benefit]." You review four stories:

1. "As a benefits claimant, I want to save my application part-way through, so that I don't lose my progress."
2. "As a caseworker, I want to filter cases by date, so that I can find the oldest cases quickly."
3. "Add a print button to the confirmation page."
4. "As a first-time visitor, I want a plain-English explanation of eligibility, so that I know whether to apply."

Which story does not follow the template?

- A) Story 1
- B) Story 2
- C) Story 3
- D) Story 4

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Stories 1, 2, and 4 each name a type of user, a goal, and a benefit, in the required order. Story 3 is a solution instruction with no user, no goal phrased from the user's perspective, and no benefit. It might still be a perfectly good idea — but as written, nobody can tell who needs the print button or why, which makes it impossible to prioritise against other work or to test whether it succeeded. Spotting this quickly matters in your role because a backlog full of solution statements hides the user needs you are meant to advocate for. In the assessment, template-checking questions are usually quick wins: scan each item against the pattern methodically rather than trusting your general impression.

**Question 4 (easy) — Prioritisation with a value and effort matrix**

You and your product manager use a simple two-by-two matrix to sort backlog ideas: value to users (high or low) and effort to build (high or low). Your team has capacity for one item this sprint. The four candidate items are:

- Item W: high value, low effort
- Item X: high value, high effort
- Item Y: low value, low effort
- Item Z: low value, high effort

Following the usual logic of the matrix, which item should the team pick up first?

- A) Item W
- B) Item X
- C) Item Y
- D) Item Z

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The matrix logic is straightforward: prefer high value, and for equal value prefer low effort. Item W delivers high value for low effort — the classic "quick win" — so it comes first. Item X may well be worth doing later, but it costs more for the same value. Item Y is cheap but delivers little, and Item Z is the worst of both worlds. This mirrors your duty to prioritise delivery of greatest value: at associate level you will often prepare or sense-check exactly this kind of ordering before discussing it with your product manager. In an assessment, when a question gives you an explicit framework, apply the framework as stated — do not import extra considerations the question has not mentioned.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Logical deduction about sprint scope**

Your sprint plan contains these facts: (i) the team can complete at most three stories this sprint; (ii) story P must be completed this sprint because of a legal deadline; (iii) story Q can only be done in the same sprint as story R, because they change the same page and must be tested together; (iv) story S has no constraints. The candidate stories are P, Q, R, and S. Which combination of three stories is possible this sprint?

- A) P, Q, S
- B) P, Q, R
- C) Q, R, S — dropping P to fit both halves of the pair
- D) P, S, and half of Q

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Check each option against all the constraints. Option A includes Q without R, breaking rule (iii). Option C drops P, breaking rule (ii) — the legal deadline makes P non-negotiable. Option D splits a story in half, which no rule permits, and "half of Q" would still change the shared page without R's changes being tested alongside. Option B contains P (satisfying the deadline), and Q together with R (satisfying the pairing rule), and exactly three stories (satisfying capacity). Only B survives every rule. Notice the working method: rather than searching for the "best" answer by instinct, eliminate options that break any stated rule. This is the same discipline you use when planning a sprint with your team — a plan that violates a hard constraint is not a plan, however attractive it looks.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Pattern recognition in retrospective themes**

You keep a simple log of the top issue raised at each of the last six sprint retrospectives:

- Sprint 1: "Stories were too big to finish"
- Sprint 2: "Testing environment was down"
- Sprint 3: "Stories were too big to finish"
- Sprint 4: "Unclear acceptance criteria"
- Sprint 5: "Stories were too big to finish"
- Sprint 6: "Unclear acceptance criteria"

Your product manager asks you what the log suggests the team should tackle first. What is the most reasonable reading of the pattern?

- A) The testing environment is the biggest problem, because infrastructure always matters most.
- B) Story sizing is the most persistent issue, appearing in three of six sprints, with acceptance criteria an emerging second theme.
- C) There is no pattern — retrospectives raise random issues.
- D) The team should stop holding retrospectives because the same issues keep coming up.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Count the occurrences: "too big to finish" appears three times across the period, including the most recent odd-numbered sprints; "unclear acceptance criteria" appears twice, both in the second half of the log; the environment issue appears once. The strongest signal is the recurring sizing problem, with acceptance criteria worth watching as an emerging theme — exactly what option B says. Option A promotes a one-off to top priority based on a general belief, not this data. Option C ignores obvious repetition. Option D confuses "the process surfaces recurring problems" with "the process is failing" — surfacing recurring problems is the retrospective doing its job; the gap is in acting on them. Reading frequency and recency from small logs like this is a core part of visualising the work of the team and helping it improve.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Error checking a roadmap against a plan**

Your product's simple roadmap says: "Quarter 1: private beta with invited users. Quarter 2: public beta. Quarter 3: add save-and-return feature. Quarter 4: live." The sprint plan you are reviewing for Quarter 1 includes a story: "Promote the service on the department's public homepage so anyone can use it." What is the inconsistency?

- A) There is no inconsistency; promotion is always good.
- B) The story belongs in Quarter 3, alongside save-and-return.
- C) The story invites the general public into the service during private beta, which contradicts "invited users only" and belongs no earlier than the public beta in Quarter 2.
- D) The roadmap is wrong and should be rewritten to match the sprint plan.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Line up the two artefacts. The roadmap says Quarter 1 is a private beta with invited users; the story would open the service to anyone who visits the homepage. Those two statements cannot both hold, and the natural resolution is to move the promotion story to Quarter 2 at the earliest, when the public beta begins. Option B pairs the story with an unrelated feature for no stated reason. Option D reverses the burden of proof: a single story does not overturn an agreed roadmap without a deliberate decision. Option A ignores the contradiction entirely. Checking that plans agree with each other is part of providing a clear, open, and transparent framework in which teams can deliver — and in an assessment, "find the inconsistency" questions are solved by comparing statements pairwise, not by judging each statement alone.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Prioritisation of incoming requests**

It is Tuesday morning. Four requests arrive for you within an hour:

1. A developer says a story in the current sprint is blocked because the acceptance criteria contradict each other, and the team cannot proceed.
2. A stakeholder emails asking for a new report feature "sometime soon".
3. Your product manager asks you to prepare two slides for Thursday's show-and-tell.
4. A user researcher shares interesting findings from last week's sessions, with no action requested.

Which is the most sensible order to deal with them?

- A) 2, 3, 1, 4 — stakeholders come first
- B) 1, 3, 2, 4 — unblock the team, then meet the Thursday deadline, then respond to the request, then read the findings
- C) 4, 1, 2, 3 — always read research first
- D) 3, 1, 4, 2 — the product manager outranks the team

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Rank by impact and urgency together. Item 1 is both urgent and high impact: the team is stopped right now, and clarifying acceptance criteria is squarely your job. Item 3 has a real deadline (Thursday) and comes next. Item 2 has no deadline; a short, polite acknowledgement telling the stakeholder when you will look at it properly is enough for today — that keeps the relationship warm without derailing your day. Item 4 is valuable but explicitly needs no action yet. Option A confuses stakeholder importance with stakeholder urgency; option C treats interesting information as more pressing than a stopped team; option D prioritises by seniority rather than by impact. Assessments and real product work both reward the same habit: ask "what happens if this waits an hour?" — for item 1 the answer is "a whole team waits with it".

**Question 9 (moderate) — Applied problem solving with user insight**

Analytics show that 40% of users abandon your online form at the address page. You have three further pieces of evidence: (a) user research notes say two of eight participants could not find their address in the postcode lookup; (b) the support desk logs say "address problems" are the most common call topic; (c) a stakeholder believes the page "looks old-fashioned". Your product manager asks you to recommend the next step. Which is the strongest recommendation?

- A) Redesign the page's visual style, because the stakeholder's view came from the most senior person.
- B) Investigate and improve the postcode lookup, because three independent sources point to users failing to enter an address, then re-check the abandonment rate.
- C) Remove the address page entirely.
- D) Wait for another quarter of analytics before doing anything.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The skill being tested is combining quantitative and qualitative insight. The analytics show where users leave; the research and support logs both suggest why — they cannot complete the address step, with the postcode lookup named directly. Three independent sources agreeing is called triangulation, and it is the strongest evidence available here. The stakeholder's aesthetic view is one opinion with no user evidence behind it, so option A prioritises seniority over insight. Option C solves the symptom by deleting a step that may be legally or operationally necessary — the scenario gives no evidence the address is optional. Option D delays despite already having converging evidence; part of the **Applying user-centred insights** skill is deciding when you have enough insight to act, and here you do. Note also the final clause of option B: recommending a change and then re-checking the measure is what managing product outcomes looks like in practice.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Multi-step deduction about a release**

Your team releases to the live service only on Wednesdays. A release must first pass a security review, which takes exactly two working days and starts no sooner than the working day after the code is finished. The team will finish the code for the new feature on a Thursday. Assuming no weekend working and no holidays, what is the earliest Wednesday the feature can be released?

- A) The Wednesday six days after the code is finished
- B) The Wednesday immediately before the security review
- C) The Wednesday thirteen days after the code is finished
- D) The Wednesday of the same week the code is finished

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Walk the calendar step by step. Code finishes Thursday. The review starts no sooner than the next working day, which is Friday. It takes two working days: Friday and Monday. So the review completes at the end of Monday. The next release day is the Wednesday of that same week — six days after the Thursday finish (Friday, Saturday, Sunday, Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday). Option D is impossible: that Wednesday has already passed when the code is finished. Option B describes an event before the review completes, so nothing could be released then. Option C adds a full extra week without justification. Working-day puzzles reward slow, explicit counting — write the days down rather than juggling them in your head. The same care applies when you help plan delivery around fixed release windows: one skipped day in your counting can silently cost the team a whole week.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Spotting the flaw in an argument**

At a planning meeting, a colleague argues: "Feature F was released last month, and this month our user satisfaction score went up. Therefore Feature F caused the improvement, and we should build more features like F." As the associate product manager, which is the best assessment of this argument?

- A) The argument is sound: the improvement came after the release, so the release caused it.
- B) The argument is weak: satisfaction rose after the release, but other changes or seasonal factors could explain it; the team should look for supporting evidence, such as usage of Feature F and user feedback mentioning it, before generalising.
- C) The argument is wrong: satisfaction scores never mean anything.
- D) The argument is irrelevant: only business needs matter, not satisfaction.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The flaw is the classic leap from "after" to "because of". Two events in sequence do not prove cause and effect: the same month may have seen a content change, fewer service outages, a different mix of users, or simple random variation in the score. That does not mean the claim is false — it means it is unproven, and the professional response is to seek corroborating evidence: did many users actually use Feature F? Does feedback mention it? Did satisfaction rise among users of F more than among non-users? Option A accepts the fallacy; options C and D throw away useful evidence entirely, which is just as unbalanced. This question rehearses two of your named skills at once: using quantitative and qualitative user insights together, and explaining the need for baseline data — without a baseline and a comparison, a rising score is a hint, not a conclusion.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Reconciling constraints across three artefacts**

You hold three artefacts. The roadmap says: "The payment feature ships before the reminder feature." The dependency note says: "The reminder feature must ship in the same release as, or earlier than, the account page redesign." The stakeholder commitment says: "The account page redesign ships in Release 2." Releases are numbered 1, 2, 3 in time order, and each feature ships in exactly one release. What is the latest release in which the payment feature can ship?

- A) Release 1
- B) Release 2
- C) Release 3
- D) It cannot be determined

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Chain the constraints backwards. The redesign ships in Release 2 (fixed). The reminder feature ships in the same release as or earlier than the redesign, so reminder ships in Release 1 or Release 2 — Release 2 at the latest. The payment feature ships strictly before the reminder feature, so payment must ship in an earlier release than reminder's release. If reminder took its latest slot, Release 2, payment must ship in Release 1. Could payment ship later than Release 1 in any valid arrangement? Only if reminder shipped in Release 3 or later — which the redesign constraint forbids. So the latest possible release for payment is Release 1, option A. The technique is to anchor on the fixed fact and propagate the constraints one step at a time, exactly as you would when sense-checking a roadmap against dependencies before your product manager presents it. If your instinct said B, you probably read "before" as "no later than" — precise reading of ordering words is half the battle in these questions.

### Preparation tips

- Practise with your own artefacts. Open your team's board and ask: which cards break our working agreement? Open the backlog and check five stories against the template. Real-world checking builds exactly the muscles the assessment measures, and it improves your product at the same time.
- Slow down on rules questions. When a question states rules — a definition of done, a set of dependencies, a working agreement — read each rule twice and test every option against every rule. Most wrong answers come from skipping a rule, not from failing to understand it.
- Write down working for calendar and sequencing questions. Counting days or ordering releases in your head invites silent mistakes. A scribbled list of days or releases takes ten seconds and removes the risk.
- Distinguish "must be true" from "could be true". Many deduction questions hinge on this. Before choosing, ask: is there any situation consistent with the facts where this option is false? If yes, it is not a "must".
- Do a little, often. Three short practice sessions a week beat one long weekend slog. Timed thinking is a skill of familiarity, and at associate level nobody expects instant mastery — steady improvement is the goal.
- Rest before the real thing. Cognitive assessments genuinely reflect alertness. Sleep well, avoid last-minute cramming, and use the ungraded practice questions to settle your nerves.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- Answering from general product opinion instead of the question's facts. You may know a better way to run a board than the scenario describes — but the assessment tests reasoning within the given rules. Save your improvement ideas for the retrospective.
- Assuming unfinished means wrong. In error-checking questions, an item that is incomplete but consistent with the rules is not a defect. Check what the rule actually forbids.
- Letting seniority decide priority. A recurring trap, in questions and in real life, is ranking work by who asked for it. Impact and urgency, considered together, are the defensible basis — and you can always acknowledge a senior request quickly while scheduling the work sensibly.
- Confusing correlation with causation. "The score went up after we released" is a hint to investigate, never a proof. Look for the option that seeks corroborating evidence.
- Rushing the easy questions. Early questions are where careless slips happen, and lost easy marks cost the same as lost hard ones. Bank the easy marks with care, then spend your remaining time on the harder items.
- Freezing when a question looks unfamiliar. Every question here can be solved with reading care plus step-by-step checking. If you feel stuck, restate the rules in your own words and eliminate options one at a time.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures how confidently and accurately you work with the kinds of numbers your role genuinely produces. This is not an exam in advanced mathematics. The arithmetic involved — percentages, ratios, averages, rates, and simple trends — is the everyday arithmetic of product work: sprint velocity, completion rates, user satisfaction scores, uptake percentages, cost per transaction, and small tables and charts of analytics data.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 20 to 40 minutes, with 15 to 25 questions. Each question presents a small dataset — a table, a chart, or a short description with numbers — and asks you to extract, calculate, or compare values. A basic on-screen or physical calculator is usually allowed, and rough paper is your friend. Scoring is objective, and as with cognitive tests, many platforms adapt difficulty as you go and report both speed and accuracy to the employer.

Why does this matter for an associate product manager? Because value, your central concern, is very often expressed in numbers. When you determine or estimate the benefits your work is expected to deliver, that is a calculation. When you explain the need for baseline data, you are saying that a number only means something compared with another number. When you make or recommend an evidence-based decision, the evidence frequently arrives as analytics: completion rates, drop-off percentages, satisfaction scores, and cost figures. And when you help prioritise for greatest value, you often compare rough numeric estimates of value and effort. Employers use numeric reasoning assessments for product roles because a product person who misreads a percentage or confuses a rate with a count can steer a team confidently in the wrong direction. The reassuring news: every technique you need is covered in the questions below, with the arithmetic shown step by step.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Percentages and rates** map to **Managing product outcomes**: completion rates, drop-off rates, and satisfaction percentages are the outcome measures you identify and explain for your product or feature, and comparing them against baseline data is how you know whether things are improving.
- **Averages and simple trends** map to **Agile and Lean practices** and **Product management**: sprint velocity is an average over recent sprints, and reading its trend helps the team plan realistic increments.
- **Ratios and proportions** map to **Applying user-centred insights**: research findings often arrive as proportions — "five of eight participants struggled" — and analytics as ratios of users taking different paths.
- **Cost and benefit arithmetic** maps to **Creating value for money**: clearly describing the value of your work, estimating benefits, and identifying savings from reuse all require simple, careful money calculations.
- **Reading tables and charts** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management**: communicating clearly and regularly with stakeholders often means presenting a table of numbers honestly — which starts with reading it correctly yourself.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Reading a completion rate**

Last week, 1,200 users started your online application form and 900 completed it. What was the completion rate?

- A) 90%
- B) 75%
- C) 66%
- D) 80%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Completion rate = completers ÷ starters × 100. Step by step: 900 ÷ 1,200 = 0.75. Then 0.75 × 100 = 75%. So the completion rate is 75%, option B. A quick sanity check: 900 is three-quarters of 1,200, and three-quarters is 75%. Getting comfortable with "part ÷ whole × 100" pays off constantly, because completion and drop-off rates are among the most common outcome measures for a form-based government service. Note also the complement: if 75% completed, then 25% dropped out — assessments sometimes ask for the drop-off rate instead, hoping you will report the completion figure by reflex. Always re-read which rate the question wants.

**Question 2 (easy) — Average sprint velocity**

Your team completed 18, 22, 20, and 24 story points in its last four sprints. What is the team's average velocity over those sprints?

- A) 20 points
- B) 21 points
- C) 22 points
- D) 24 points

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Average = total ÷ number of sprints. Step 1: add the values: 18 + 22 = 40; 40 + 20 = 60; 60 + 24 = 84. Step 2: divide by 4: 84 ÷ 4 = 21. The average velocity is 21 points, option B. In practice, teams use a recent average like this to plan the next sprint — so if your product manager asks whether the team can take on 27 points next sprint, this average suggests that would be optimistic. One caution the question also illustrates: an average smooths over variation (here from 18 to 24), so quote the range as well as the average when you help with planning conversations.

**Question 3 (easy) — Percentage increase in users**

Your service had 4,000 active users in March and 5,000 in April. What was the percentage increase?

- A) 20%
- B) 25%
- C) 10%
- D) 125%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Percentage increase = (new − old) ÷ old × 100. Step 1: find the change: 5,000 − 4,000 = 1,000. Step 2: divide by the original (March) figure: 1,000 ÷ 4,000 = 0.25. Step 3: multiply by 100: 25%. Option A (20%) is the classic error of dividing the change by the new figure (1,000 ÷ 5,000) instead of the old one; option D reports the new value as a percentage of the old rather than the increase. Always anchor percentage change on the starting value. This calculation is one you will make often when reporting product outcomes — "uptake grew 25% month on month" — and stakeholders will trust your numbers more when they are consistently correct.

**Question 4 (easy) — Reading a simple table**

Your monthly analytics summary shows sessions by device:

| Device | Sessions |
|---|---|
| Mobile | 6,300 |
| Desktop | 2,700 |
| Tablet | 1,000 |

What proportion of sessions came from mobile devices?

- A) 63%
- B) 6,300%
- C) 53%
- D) 70%

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** First find the total: 6,300 + 2,700 + 1,000 = 10,000 sessions. Then take the mobile share: 6,300 ÷ 10,000 = 0.63 = 63%, option A. The round total of 10,000 makes this quick, but the method is the same for messier totals: always compute the whole before the share, because a common trap is dividing by the wrong denominator (for example, mobile ÷ desktop). Why it matters for you: a service used mostly on mobile should be designed and tested mobile-first, and when you advocate for user needs, "63% of sessions are on mobile" is exactly the kind of clear, evidence-based statement that moves a prioritisation conversation.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Drop-off between form pages**

Your five-page form had these page-view counts last month: Page 1: 2,000; Page 2: 1,800; Page 3: 1,350; Page 4: 1,300; Page 5: 1,250. Between which two consecutive pages is the largest percentage drop-off, measured as a percentage of the earlier page's views?

- A) Page 1 to Page 2
- B) Page 2 to Page 3
- C) Page 3 to Page 4
- D) Page 4 to Page 5

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Calculate each step's drop as a percentage of the earlier page. Page 1→2: (2,000 − 1,800) ÷ 2,000 = 200 ÷ 2,000 = 10%. Page 2→3: (1,800 − 1,350) ÷ 1,800 = 450 ÷ 1,800 = 25%. Page 3→4: (1,350 − 1,300) ÷ 1,350 = 50 ÷ 1,350 ≈ 3.7%. Page 4→5: (1,300 − 1,250) ÷ 1,300 = 50 ÷ 1,300 ≈ 3.8%. The largest drop is Page 2 to Page 3 at 25%, option B. This is precisely how a product team finds the problem page in a journey: the absolute numbers matter less than the step-by-step rates. In your role, this calculation would justify pointing user research at Page 2's content — a neat example of quantitative insight telling you where to look and qualitative insight telling you why.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Satisfaction score change against baseline**

Your baseline user satisfaction score, measured before a redesign, was 72%. The target was to reach 80% within two quarters. After one quarter the score is 76%. Which statement best describes progress?

- A) The target has been met.
- B) The score has improved by 4 percentage points, which is half of the 8-point improvement needed, with one of two quarters elapsed.
- C) The score has improved by 4%, so the redesign has failed.
- D) Without a baseline it is impossible to say anything.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Work with percentage points when comparing scores. The needed improvement was 80 − 72 = 8 percentage points. The achieved improvement so far is 76 − 72 = 4 percentage points. So the team is halfway to the target with half the time elapsed — on track, as option B states. Option A jumps the gun; option C wrongly declares failure while progress is on schedule (and blurs "4 percentage points" with "4%", which strictly would mean 4% of 72, a different quantity); option D is wrong because the baseline is stated — 72%. This question rehearses two skills by name: explaining the need for baseline data, and identifying and measuring product outcomes. A score only tells a story relative to its baseline and target, and precise language — points versus percent — keeps the story honest.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Cost saving from reuse**

Your team needs an address-lookup component. Building one from scratch is estimated at 40 developer-days. Reusing a component from another government team would take 10 developer-days of integration work. A developer-day costs £450. How much money would reuse save?

- A) £4,500
- B) £18,000
- C) £13,500
- D) £22,500

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Step 1: find the difference in effort: 40 − 10 = 30 developer-days saved. Step 2: multiply by the daily cost: 30 × £450. Break it down: 30 × £400 = £12,000, and 30 × £50 = £1,500; together £13,500, option C. Option B is the cost of the from-scratch build minus nothing (40 × £450 = £18,000), a trap for those who forget reuse still costs something; option A is the cost of the integration work itself, not the saving. This is the **Creating value for money** skill in its purest form: identifying opportunities for reuse and putting a credible number on the saving. A sentence like "reusing the cross-government component saves an estimated £13,500" is far more persuasive to stakeholders than "reuse is cheaper".

**Question 8 (moderate) — Ratio of research findings**

In a round of usability testing, 6 of 8 participants completed the main task, and 2 did not. In the previous round, 9 of 12 participants completed it. Comparing completion proportions, which statement is correct?

- A) This round was better: 6 of 8 is 75%, versus 9 of 12 at 66%.
- B) The rounds had the same completion proportion: both are 75%.
- C) The previous round was better: 9 completions beat 6 completions.
- D) The comparison is impossible because the group sizes differ.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Convert both to proportions before comparing. This round: 6 ÷ 8 = 0.75 = 75%. Previous round: 9 ÷ 12 = 0.75 = 75%. They are equal, option B. Option C compares raw counts across different group sizes — the most common ratio error in the wild. Option D is too pessimistic: differing group sizes are exactly why we convert to proportions; the comparison remains valid, though with samples this small you should treat any difference cautiously anyway. In your day-to-day, research findings usually arrive as small-sample fractions, and part of applying user-centred insights well is neither over-reading them ("completion is stuck at 75%, we have failed") nor under-reading them ("only 2 people struggled, ignore it") — with 8 participants, 2 struggling is a genuine signal worth investigating.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Cost per transaction trend**

The table shows your service's quarterly figures:

| Quarter | Transactions | Total cost |
|---|---|---|
| Q1 | 20,000 | £50,000 |
| Q2 | 25,000 | £55,000 |

What happened to the cost per transaction from Q1 to Q2?

- A) It rose from £2.50 to £2.75.
- B) It fell from £2.50 to £2.20.
- C) It stayed at £2.50.
- D) It fell from £2.75 to £2.50.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Cost per transaction = total cost ÷ transactions. Q1: £50,000 ÷ 20,000 = £2.50. Q2: £55,000 ÷ 25,000 = £2.20. So unit cost fell from £2.50 to £2.20, option B — even though total cost rose by £5,000. This is a valuable pattern to recognise: totals and unit rates can move in opposite directions, and a stakeholder glancing at rising total cost might conclude the service is getting less efficient when the opposite is true. Being able to say "total cost rose because volume rose 25%, but each transaction now costs 30 pence less" is exactly the kind of clear value story your role calls for. Assessment tip: when a table gives you two columns, expect the question to be about the ratio between them.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Projecting uptake against a target**

Your digital service's target is that 60% of all applications are made online by the end of Quarter 4. The quarterly figures so far:

| Quarter | Online applications | Paper applications |
|---|---|---|
| Q1 | 8,000 | 12,000 |
| Q2 | 10,000 | 10,000 |
| Q3 | 12,000 | 8,000 |

If the trend in online share continues at the same rate of change per quarter, what online share would you project for Q4, and would it meet the target?

- A) 55%; the target would be missed.
- B) 60%; the target would be exactly met.
- C) 70%; the target would be exceeded.
- D) 65%; the target would be exceeded.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** First compute each quarter's online share. Q1: total = 8,000 + 12,000 = 20,000; online share = 8,000 ÷ 20,000 = 40%. Q2: total = 20,000; share = 10,000 ÷ 20,000 = 50%. Q3: total = 20,000; share = 12,000 ÷ 20,000 = 60%. The share has risen by 10 percentage points each quarter: 40% → 50% → 60%. Continuing that trend, Q4 projects to 60% + 10 = 70%, option C — comfortably above the 60% target, which Q3 has in fact already reached. Two lessons travel with this question. First, always convert to shares before spotting the trend; the raw online counts (8,000, 10,000, 12,000) rise by 2,000 per quarter, but the share is what the target is written in. Second, treat straight-line projections as an assumption, not a fact: uptake curves often flatten as the remaining paper users include people who face real barriers to digital channels — which is a user-needs conversation, not just an arithmetic one.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Weighted average satisfaction**

Your product has two user groups. Last month, 800 citizens rated satisfaction at 85%, and 200 caseworkers rated it at 60%. What was the overall satisfaction across all 1,000 respondents?

- A) 72.5%
- B) 80%
- C) 85%
- D) 75%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** You cannot simply average 85% and 60% to get 72.5% (option A), because the groups are different sizes — you must weight by group size. Step 1: satisfied citizens: 800 × 0.85 = 680. Step 2: satisfied caseworkers: 200 × 0.60 = 120. Step 3: total satisfied: 680 + 120 = 800. Step 4: overall rate: 800 ÷ 1,000 = 80%, option B. The unweighted average understates the true figure here because the happier group is four times larger. This matters beyond arithmetic: the headline "80% satisfied" hides that caseworkers — internal users your service also serves — are far less happy. When you report outcomes to stakeholders, showing the split (85% citizens, 60% caseworkers) is more honest and more actionable than the single blended figure, and it points your next round of user research straight at the caseworker experience.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Estimating benefit of a feature**

The support desk receives 1,500 calls per month about your service, and call logs show 40% of them are password-reset requests. Each call costs £6 to handle. A proposed self-service password-reset feature is expected to remove 70% of password-reset calls. What is the expected monthly saving?

- A) £3,600
- B) £2,520
- C) £6,300
- D) £900

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Chain the percentages carefully, one step at a time. Step 1: password-reset calls per month: 1,500 × 0.40 = 600. Step 2: calls removed by the feature: 600 × 0.70 = 420. Step 3: monthly saving: 420 × £6 = £2,520, option B. Option A (£3,600) is what you get if you assume all 600 password calls disappear (600 × £6) — forgetting that the feature only removes 70% of them; option C applies 70% to all 1,500 calls. The discipline is to apply each percentage to the correct base, writing the intermediate figure down at every step. This is textbook **Creating value for money** work at associate level: estimating the benefits your work is expected to deliver, with assumptions (40%, 70%) stated openly so stakeholders can challenge them. A good habit is to note what would change the estimate — if the feature is hard to find, the 70% assumption will not be achieved, which is a design and research question as much as a numeric one.

### Preparation tips

- Rehearse the four core recipes until they are automatic: part ÷ whole × 100 for a rate; (new − old) ÷ old × 100 for a change; total ÷ count for an average; and weight-then-combine for mixed groups. Nearly every question above is one of these recipes, sometimes chained.
- Write down intermediate results. Multi-step questions (like the call-saving estimate) go wrong when steps are held in your head. One line per step costs seconds and saves marks.
- Check the denominator every time. "Percentage of what?" is the single most valuable question in numeric reasoning. Drop-off as a share of which page? Increase relative to which month?
- Use your own product's data to practise. Pull up last month's analytics and compute the completion rate, the biggest page drop-off, and the month-on-month change. It is free practice and genuinely useful to your team.
- Learn the difference between percentage points and percent, and use the terms precisely — it will serve you in stakeholder conversations as much as in tests.
- Keep an eye on the clock but do not panic. If a question is taking too long, make your best elimination-based choice, note it, and move on. Accuracy on the questions you do attempt matters more than attempting everything badly.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- Dividing by the new value instead of the original when calculating percentage change. Anchor on the starting value, always.
- Comparing raw counts across groups of different sizes. Six completions from eight participants and nine from twelve are the same proportion; convert before comparing.
- Averaging percentages from unequal groups. Weight by group size, or you will misreport the overall figure.
- Forgetting that a saving is a difference. Reuse still has a cost; a feature only removes some calls. Subtract the remaining cost from the avoided cost before announcing a benefit.
- Reading the wrong figure under time pressure — completion when the question asks for drop-off, totals when it asks for unit cost. Re-read the final sentence of every question before answering.
- Treating projections as certainties. In the assessment, follow the stated assumption ("if the trend continues"); in real work, always say out loud that it is an assumption.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately you read, understand, and draw conclusions from written material of the kind your role deals with daily. For an associate product manager, that means service standards, policy extracts, stakeholder emails, user research summaries, meeting notes, and product documentation — not dense literary passages.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 15 to 30 minutes. You are shown a short passage and asked questions about it. The most common style is "true / false / cannot say": you decide whether a statement is definitely true based on the passage, definitely false based on the passage, or impossible to determine from the passage alone. Other question styles include picking the best summary of a passage, identifying what can properly be inferred, and judging which of several sentences communicates a point most clearly. Scoring is objective, and the golden rule throughout is: answer only from the passage, not from your outside knowledge — even when you happen to know a lot about the topic.

Why do employers use verbal reasoning assessments for product roles? Because so much of product work is reading and writing under time pressure. You read policy and standards documents to ensure teams comply with appropriate standards. You read research findings and decide what they do and do not show. You read stakeholder emails and need to grasp exactly what is being asked, promised, or assumed. And you write — user stories, show-and-tell slides, status updates — where a fuzzy sentence can send a team in the wrong direction. Careful reading is also a quiet act of user advocacy: many of the documents you interpret exist to protect users, and misreading them has real consequences. The skills tested here are learnable, and the practice below will make the "cannot say" discipline — the hardest part for most people — feel natural.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Comprehension of standards and policy** maps to your duty to **ensure teams comply with appropriate standards**: you must read a standard and know precisely what it requires, what it permits, and what it is silent about.
- **True/false/cannot-say discipline** maps to **Applying user-centred insights** and **Product management**: deciding when you have enough insight to make a decision means being honest about what the evidence in front of you actually states, and making or recommending evidence-based decisions means not stretching that evidence.
- **Inference** maps to **Managing product outcomes** and **Strategic ownership**: connecting a written strategy to your day-to-day decisions requires drawing sound conclusions from documents, and explaining how your decisions align to the strategy of your organisation.
- **Clear-writing judgement** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management** and **Agile and Lean practices**: tailoring communication to stakeholders' needs, and providing a clear, open, and transparent framework for the team, both begin with plain, unambiguous sentences.
- **Reading research summaries accurately** maps to **Applying user-centred insights**: qualitative findings are words before they are decisions, and the words must be read for exactly what they claim.

### Practice questions

For questions 1 to 6, read each passage and decide whether the statement is True (definitely follows from the passage), False (definitely contradicted by the passage), or Cannot say (the passage does not give enough information). A fourth option is included where relevant.

**Question 1 (easy) — Reading a service standard extract**

Passage: "Teams building government services must carry out user research with people who have access needs. Research should take place throughout the service's development, not only at the start. Teams should also test their service with users of assistive technology."

Statement: "Teams must carry out user research with people who have access needs."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, but only during private beta

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The statement repeats the passage's first sentence almost word for word: "must carry out user research with people who have access needs." When a statement matches the passage directly, the answer is True — resist the urge to overthink. Option D adds a restriction ("only during private beta") that the passage not only fails to state but actively contradicts, since research should happen "throughout the service's development". Easy questions like this are about careful matching: confirm the statement against the exact words, check no qualifier has been quietly added or dropped, and move on with the mark banked. In your role, this is the same care you apply when checking whether your team's plans meet the Service Standard's actual wording rather than a half-remembered version of it.

**Question 2 (easy) — What the passage does not say**

Passage: "The department's content style guide requires services to use plain English. Sentences should average no more than 25 words. Technical terms may be used where the audience is specialist, provided they are explained on first use."

Statement: "The style guide bans all technical terms."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True for citizen-facing services only

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage explicitly permits technical terms in defined circumstances: for specialist audiences, explained on first use. A ban on all technical terms directly contradicts that permission, so the statement is False. This question illustrates a pattern worth memorising: False is for statements the passage contradicts, not merely statements it fails to support. If the passage had said nothing about technical terms at all, the answer would have been Cannot say. Option D invents a distinction between service types that appears nowhere in the passage. Reading rules for what they actually permit — not for a stricter version that feels safer — matters when you ensure teams comply with standards: over-reading a rule wastes the team's effort just as surely as under-reading it creates risk.

**Question 3 (easy) — Cannot say discipline**

Passage: "In last month's usability testing, five of the six participants completed the renewal journey without assistance. Two participants commented that the confirmation email arrived later than they expected."

Statement: "Most users of the service can complete the renewal journey without assistance."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, because five out of six is 83%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage reports on six research participants; the statement makes a claim about "most users of the service" — the entire user population. Six people are not the population, and the passage says nothing about how representative they were. So the statement can be neither confirmed nor contradicted from the passage: Cannot say. Option D is the seductive trap: the arithmetic is correct, but the leap from a small research sample to "most users" is exactly the overgeneralisation the question is testing. Notice how this mirrors real product judgement — the **Applying user-centred insights** skill includes deciding when you need more insight. Research findings tell you what happened in the sessions; treating them as automatic truths about all users is a habit assessments and good product managers both avoid.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Inference from a stakeholder email**

Passage (email extract): "Thanks for the demo. I remain concerned that the new eligibility checker could increase calls to our contact centre if people don't understand their result. Before we agree to a public launch, I'd like to see evidence from the private beta about how users respond to 'not eligible' outcomes."

Statement: "The stakeholder has refused to allow a public launch."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, until the contact centre is expanded

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Read what the email actually does: it sets a condition — evidence from private beta about responses to "not eligible" outcomes — "before we agree to a public launch". Setting a condition for agreement is not a refusal; the phrase "before we agree" signals that agreement is anticipated once the evidence is seen. So "has refused" is contradicted by the passage's plain meaning: False. Option D invents a remedy (expanding the contact centre) the email never mentions. This distinction — condition versus refusal — is one you will meet constantly in stakeholder relationship management. Misreading a conditional "yes, if" as a "no" can cause a team to abandon or escalate unnecessarily; part of building and reaching consensus with stakeholders is hearing exactly what would satisfy them, which this email states clearly.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Comparing accounts in meeting notes**

Passage (from sprint review notes): "The developer reported that the postcode lookup fails for around 2% of lookups, mainly for new-build addresses. The service manager noted that all failed lookups currently result in a support call. The team agreed to add a manual address entry option in the next sprint."

Statement: "Adding manual address entry will eliminate support calls about addresses."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, because the team agreed to it

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage establishes a chain of facts: some lookups fail; failed lookups currently lead to support calls; the team will add manual entry. It is reasonable to hope manual entry will reduce those calls — but "eliminate" is a strong claim the passage never makes, and nothing tells us whether manual entry will work for every affected user, or whether other address-related calls exist. Not contradicted, not confirmed: Cannot say. Option D confuses a team's decision to try a fix with evidence that the fix will fully succeed. The professional parallel is direct: when you write up a change for stakeholders, say "we expect this to reduce address-related calls, and we will measure it" rather than promising elimination. That is the **Managing product outcomes** habit of pairing every change with a measurable expectation, phrased honestly.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Precise reading of a policy constraint**

Passage: "Applicants may submit supporting documents digitally. Where an applicant cannot use the digital route, a paper alternative must be provided. Paper submissions must be processed within the same service level as digital submissions."

Statement: "Paper submissions may be processed more slowly than digital submissions, provided applicants are warned."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, during peak periods only

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage's final sentence requires paper submissions to be processed "within the same service level" as digital ones. The statement claims slower processing is permissible with a warning — directly contradicting the equal-service-level requirement, so it is False. No warning, and no peak-period exception (option D), appears anywhere in the passage; assessments frequently test whether you will accept an invented qualification because it sounds administratively plausible. The underlying policy point is one product teams in government genuinely handle: assisted digital and offline routes must not become second-class service, and when you ensure your team complies with appropriate standards, that includes the non-digital path. Precise reading here is user advocacy for the people least able to complain.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Best summary of a passage**

Passage: "The team proposed three options for the renewal reminder. Option 1, email only, is cheapest but excludes users without email. Option 2, email plus SMS, costs more but reaches nearly all users. Option 3, email plus post, reaches everyone but costs the most and is slowest. The recommendation is Option 2, with post available on request for users who need it."

Which sentence best summarises the passage?

- A) The team recommends the cheapest reminder option.
- B) The team recommends email plus SMS reminders, with postal reminders on request, balancing reach against cost.
- C) The team recommends postal reminders because they reach everyone.
- D) The team cannot decide between the three options.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A good summary preserves the decision and its reasoning without adding or losing substance. The passage recommends Option 2 (email plus SMS) with a postal fallback on request — exactly what option B states, including the balancing logic (reach versus cost) that runs through the passage. Option A is false: the cheapest option was Option 1, which was not recommended. Option C promotes the rejected Option 3. Option D contradicts the clear recommendation. Summary questions reward finding the passage's conclusion sentence and checking each candidate against it. In your work, this is the show-and-tell skill: compressing a decision into one faithful sentence for stakeholders — and note how the passage models good product thinking too, pairing a mainstream channel choice with an inclusive fallback rather than pretending one channel fits everyone.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Identifying an assumption**

Passage (from a business case): "Moving the guidance content from PDFs to web pages will improve task completion, because users will no longer need to download files on mobile devices."

Which unstated assumption does this argument rely on?

- A) That a meaningful number of users are on mobile devices and find downloads a barrier to completing tasks.
- B) That PDFs are cheaper to produce than web pages.
- C) That the guidance content is currently accurate.
- D) That users prefer longer documents.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Strip the argument to its skeleton: change (PDFs → web pages) leads to outcome (better completion) via mechanism (no more downloads on mobile). The mechanism only produces the outcome if downloads on mobile are actually a barrier for enough users to move the completion figure — which the argument assumes but never states. That is option A. Options B, C, and D may or may not be true, but the argument would stand or fall without them: cost is not part of the claim, accuracy affects quality rather than the download mechanism, and nothing involves document length preference. Assumption-spotting is a high-value skill in your role: when you review a story's "so that" clause or a benefits claim, asking "what would have to be true for this to work?" turns you from a note-taker into a genuine contributor to evidence-based decisions — and it usually points directly at what user research should test next.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Two-condition rule with an exception**

Passage: "All new features must have analytics tracking agreed before development begins, unless the feature is classified as a security fix. Security fixes must instead be reviewed by the security team before release. Feature F began development last week. Analytics tracking for Feature F has not been agreed."

Statement: "Feature F is classified as a security fix."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say — unless we also know Feature F complied with the rules
- D) The passage contradicts itself

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Follow the logic carefully. The rule says: for any feature that is not a security fix, tracking must be agreed before development begins. Feature F began development without agreed tracking. If Feature F followed the rules, it must be a security fix (the only route that permits starting without tracking). But the passage never says Feature F followed the rules — teams do sometimes breach process. Two possibilities remain consistent with the passage: F is a security fix and compliant, or F is an ordinary feature and non-compliant. Since both are possible, we cannot say — option C, with its clarifying clause, is exactly right. This is the same reasoning shape as real governance conversations: an apparent rule breach might instead be a legitimate exception, so the professional move is to check before concluding. Concluding "True" here is the assessment version of accusing a team of non-compliance without first checking the exemptions — bad logic and bad stakeholder management alike.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Clear-writing judgement**

Your product manager asks you to draft a status update for stakeholders about a delayed feature. Which sentence communicates most clearly and honestly?

- A) "Due to unforeseen circumstances impacting deliverables, timelines have been recalibrated going forward."
- B) "The document upload feature will now launch in June rather than April, because accessibility testing found issues with the file picker that we are fixing first."
- C) "There is a possibility that certain functionality may potentially experience some degree of rescheduling."
- D) "The feature is delayed. More soon."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Judge each sentence against three tests: does it say what changed, why, and what happens next — in words a busy stakeholder can absorb in one reading? Option B passes all three: the specific feature, the old and new dates, the concrete reason (accessibility issues found in testing), and the implicit next step (fixing first). Option A is jargon that conveys no facts — no feature, no dates, no reason. Option C stacks hedge upon hedge until nothing is claimed at all. Option D is honest but so thin that it invites a wave of follow-up questions, which is the opposite of managing stakeholder communication well. Notice too that option B quietly models good values: it treats an accessibility fix as a legitimate, nameable reason for delay rather than something to obscure. Tailoring communication to stakeholders' needs starts with respecting their time and intelligence — plain, specific, and honest wins.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Inference across a research summary**

Passage: "Across ten interviews, participants who had used the old paper process described the new online service as faster. However, three participants who had never used the paper process found the online identity step confusing, and two of them abandoned it during the session. All participants who completed the identity step went on to finish the whole journey."

Statement: "The identity step is the only part of the journey where participants abandoned during the sessions."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True for paper-experienced participants only

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** This requires combining two sentences. First, two participants abandoned at the identity step. Second — the key sentence — "all participants who completed the identity step went on to finish the whole journey." If everyone who got past identity finished, then nobody abandoned at any later step. Could anyone have abandoned before the identity step? The passage describes abandonment only "during it", and the final sentence's structure (completing identity guarantees finishing) means the identity step is the only recorded point of loss in these sessions. So within the sessions described, the statement is True. Be careful about scope, though: the statement says "during the sessions", keeping the claim inside the passage's world — had it said "the identity step is the only problem with the service", the answer would be Cannot say, because ten interviews cannot establish that. Reading the scope of a claim ("in the sessions" versus "in the service") is the mark of a careful analyst, and it is exactly how you should write up research findings yourself.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Reconciling a strategy statement with a request**

Passage (strategy extract): "Our organisation prioritises reducing failure demand — contact caused by services not working well — over adding new functionality. Teams should demonstrate how proposed work reduces avoidable contact before committing to new features." A stakeholder then asks your team to build a new document-download feature "because another department has one".

Statement: "According to the strategy, the team should ask what avoidable contact the download feature would reduce before committing to build it."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, but only if the other department's feature reduced contact

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Map the request onto the strategy's rule. The strategy requires teams to "demonstrate how proposed work reduces avoidable contact before committing to new features". The download feature is proposed new functionality; therefore, before committing, the team should establish its effect on avoidable contact. That is precisely what the statement says: True. Option D smuggles in an irrelevant condition — what happened in another department does not change what this strategy requires of this team; "another department has one" is not a demonstration of reduced contact. This question rehearses your **Strategic ownership** skill directly: explaining how you make decisions that are aligned to the strategy of your organisation. At associate level you are not expected to refuse a stakeholder single-handedly — but you are expected to notice when a request has skipped the strategy's test, and to raise it with your product manager using the strategy's own words. The document gives you both the authority and the language.

### Preparation tips

- Master the three-way distinction first: True means the passage guarantees it; False means the passage contradicts it; Cannot say means neither. Before answering, ask "could the statement be false while everything in the passage stays true?" — if yes, it is not True.
- Answer from the passage alone. You will sometimes know from experience that a statement is realistic — that knowledge is a trap. The test measures reading, not background knowledge.
- Watch for quantifiers and scope words: all, only, must, may, some, most, "in the sessions", "of the service". Most hard questions turn on one such word.
- Read the statement before re-reading the passage. Knowing what you are checking for makes your second read focused and fast.
- Practise on real work documents: take a paragraph from the Service Standard or a research summary and write one True, one False, and one Cannot say statement about it. Composing the traps teaches you to see them.
- Build your clear-writing eye by editing your own updates: cut hedges, name specifics, and check every sentence answers "what, why, what next".

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- Marking a statement True because it is plausible or matches your experience. Plausible-but-unstated is the definition of Cannot say.
- Marking a statement False because the passage does not mention it. Silence is Cannot say; contradiction is False. This single confusion causes more lost marks than any other.
- Overlooking an exception clause ("unless", "provided that", "where the audience is specialist"). Exceptions transform what a rule forbids; always read to the end of the sentence.
- Generalising from a research sample to all users. Six participants tell you about six participants; watch for statements that quietly widen the scope.
- Accepting invented qualifiers in the options ("during peak periods", "if applicants are warned"). If the qualifier is not in the passage, the option is wrong however reasonable it sounds.
- Skimming stakeholder-email questions. Tone words ("concerned", "before we agree") carry the meaning; a condition is not a refusal, and a hope is not a commitment.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment presents realistic workplace scenarios and asks you to judge how effective different responses would be. Unlike the other assessments in this guide, there is rarely a single "provable" answer; instead, responses are scored against what experienced practitioners and the profession's behavioural frameworks consider effective. Common formats include: pick the most effective response; pick the most and least effective; rank all responses from best to worst; or rate each response independently on an effectiveness scale.

The typical format is an online test of 20 to 40 minutes with 10 to 20 scenarios. It is often untimed or generously timed, because it measures judgement rather than speed. There are no trick questions, but there are tempting wrong answers — usually responses that are fast, conflict-avoiding, or impressive-looking rather than genuinely effective.

Why do employers use situational judgement tests for associate product managers? Because your role is defined as much by behaviours as by knowledge: advocating for user needs throughout the organisation, working with multidisciplinary teams, communicating with stakeholders, learning product approaches, and knowing when to handle something yourself versus when to involve your product manager. At an entry level, the judgement being tested is rarely "can you solve this alone?" — it is "do you take ownership of what is yours, seek support at the right moment, stay honest under pressure, and keep users at the centre?" The scenarios below are calibrated for exactly that: day-to-day situations where you have real responsibility and real support available. As you practise, look for the recurring principles in the explanations — user focus, transparency, collaboration, proportionate escalation, and inclusion — because those principles, not memorised answers, are what the real assessment measures.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **User focus scenarios** map to your duty to **work with product managers to advocate for user needs throughout the organisation** and to **Applying user-centred insights**: the effective response keeps user evidence in the conversation, especially when it is inconvenient.
- **Team collaboration scenarios** map to **Product leadership** and your duty to **work with multidisciplinary teams**: effective responses respect the expertise of designers, developers, and researchers, and bring views together rather than overruling them.
- **Prioritisation and ownership scenarios** map to **Product management** and **making decisions on priorities for your teams**: effective responses take ownership of what is genuinely yours at associate level, and are honest about trade-offs.
- **Escalation scenarios** map to your level's design of **managing a smaller product with support**: knowing when to inform or involve your product manager is a skill, and both over-escalating and under-escalating are marked down.
- **Stakeholder scenarios** map to **Stakeholder relationship management**: effective responses communicate clearly, early, and honestly, and build consensus using evidence rather than avoidance or capitulation.
- **Standards and inclusion scenarios** map to **ensuring teams comply with appropriate standards**: accessibility and assisted digital are treated as non-negotiable user needs, not optional extras.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — A stakeholder asks you to skip research**

You look after a small feature within a larger product. A stakeholder tells you: "We already know what users want — just build the new form and skip the research this time. It'll be quicker." Your user researcher believes at least a short round of testing is needed. What is the most effective response?

- A) Agree to skip research — the stakeholder knows the policy area better than the team.
- B) Refuse outright and tell the stakeholder that research is mandatory, ending the conversation.
- C) Acknowledge the time pressure, explain what a small, fast round of testing would cover and what risks it removes, and agree with your product manager and researcher a proportionate approach.
- D) Say yes to the stakeholder in the meeting, then quietly arrange the research anyway.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Option C demonstrates the behaviours the profession looks for at your level: user focus (research stays in the plan), collaboration (the researcher and product manager are involved), stakeholder relationship management (the time pressure is acknowledged and addressed, not dismissed), and proportionality (a small, fast round rather than a maximal one). Option A abandons user advocacy — the stakeholder may know the policy, but policy knowledge is not the same as evidence about user behaviour. Option B may protect users but damages the relationship unnecessarily; "refuse and end the conversation" fails the consensus-building part of your role, and at associate level you should also be bringing your product manager into a disagreement like this rather than issuing verdicts alone. Option D is the worst: it is dishonest, and discovered dishonesty destroys the trust every future conversation depends on. The pattern to remember: acknowledge the pressure, offer a proportionate path, involve the right people, stay honest.

**Question 2 (easy) — You spot your own mistake**

You prepared the sprint board on Monday, and on Wednesday you realise you accidentally marked a story as "Done" that has not actually passed its accessibility checks. The show-and-tell, where the team will demo "done" work to stakeholders, is tomorrow. What is the most effective response?

- A) Say nothing — it will probably pass the checks eventually.
- B) Correct the board immediately, tell the team and your product manager what happened, and check whether the story can complete its checks before the show-and-tell or should be excluded from the demo.
- C) Quietly fix the board but tell no one, hoping nobody noticed.
- D) Wait until after the show-and-tell to fix it, to avoid awkward questions.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This scenario tests ownership and transparency — two behaviours entry-level assessments probe heavily, because how you handle your own mistakes predicts how safe you will be with bigger responsibilities. Option B does everything right: fix the error at once, tell the people affected, and manage the consequence (the demo) proactively. Options A and D both allow stakeholders to be shown untrue information — a story presented as done that is not — which turns a small honest slip into a credibility problem for the whole team, and D compounds it by making the concealment deliberate. Option C fixes the artefact but hides the learning: the team cannot adjust the demo, and if anyone did notice the change, silent editing looks worse than open correction. There is also an inclusion dimension worth noticing: the check being skipped is accessibility, and treating that as "will probably pass" is exactly how services quietly fail users with access needs. Teams that make mistakes visible fix them faster; be the person who models that.

**Question 3 (easy) — Conflicting instructions**

Your product manager asked you on Monday to spend this week preparing the backlog for next sprint. On Wednesday, a senior stakeholder from another team emails you directly, asking you to spend the next two days pulling together a detailed report on your product's usage statistics. You cannot do both. What is the most effective response?

- A) Drop the backlog work — the stakeholder is more senior than your product manager.
- B) Ignore the stakeholder's email until next week.
- C) Reply to the stakeholder acknowledging the request, then talk to your product manager the same day about the conflict so you can agree the priority together and give the stakeholder a realistic answer.
- D) Try to do both by working the weekend, telling no one there is a conflict.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** At associate level, resolving a genuine priority conflict between a senior stakeholder and your product manager is not a decision you should make alone — and the assessment is checking whether you know that without either capitulating (A) or hiding (B, D). Option C shows the full effective pattern: acknowledge promptly so the stakeholder is not left in silence, surface the conflict to your product manager the same day, and return with a realistic, agreed answer. Option A lets seniority override an agreed plan without the plan's owner even knowing — and teaches stakeholders that emailing you directly bypasses prioritisation. Option B damages the relationship and wastes days in which the conflict could have been resolved. Option D is the most tempting wrong answer for conscientious people: it feels helpful, but it hides a real demand-versus-capacity problem, sets an unsustainable precedent, and means nobody ever decides which piece of work actually mattered more. Making conflicts visible is not weakness; it is how prioritisation is supposed to work.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Developers disagree with the priority**

In sprint planning, you propose that the team's next priority is fixing the address-lookup problems, based on analytics and support-call data. Two developers argue the next priority should be upgrading a software framework, warning that the old version loses security support in four months. The discussion is going in circles. What is the most effective response?

- A) Insist on the address fix — product priorities are set by product people, and the data supports you.
- B) Give in to the developers to keep the peace, and schedule the address fix for "sometime later".
- C) Ask the developers to help you understand the upgrade's urgency and size, share your user data, and look for a plan that addresses both — for example sequencing the upgrade to start within its four-month window while the address fix proceeds — checking the proposal with your product manager.
- D) End the meeting and add both items to the backlog unranked, to be decided another time.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** This scenario tests how you balance user needs and business needs inside a multidisciplinary team — the heart of your **Product leadership** skill. The developers are not obstructing; they are contributing essential information (a security deadline) that your analytics cannot see. Option C treats the disagreement as missing information on both sides: you learn the upgrade's real urgency and cost, they see your user evidence, and the four-month window creates room for sequencing rather than either/or. Bringing the proposal to your product manager is right at your level for a decision with security implications. Option A wins the argument and loses the team: overruling technical risk advice by role authority is precisely the behaviour that later produces "we told you so" incidents. Option B avoids conflict by silently deprioritising evidenced user harm — "sometime later" is where user needs go to be forgotten. Option D postpones without a mechanism for deciding, so the circling discussion simply recurs. Effective product behaviour is neither commanding nor capitulating: it is integrating views into a sequenced, evidence-based plan.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Research findings are unwelcome**

Usability testing of a feature your team has spent six weeks building shows that most participants could not complete the journey. The team is deflated, and one colleague suggests presenting only the positive findings at tomorrow's show-and-tell "to keep morale up". What is the most effective response?

- A) Agree — morale matters, and the negative findings can come out later once fixes are underway.
- B) Suggest presenting the findings in full, framed as the research doing its job: what users struggled with, what the team will try next, and what was learned — and discuss with the researcher how to present it fairly.
- C) Present only the negative findings, to shock stakeholders into giving the team more time.
- D) Cancel the show-and-tell.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Show-and-tells exist to create transparency, and the moment they filter out inconvenient evidence they stop protecting the team and start misleading stakeholders. Option B is effective on every axis: honesty (full findings), user focus (the struggles users had are the headline, because they are what the product must fix), collaboration (the researcher co-shapes the telling), and morale handled the right way — by framing, not by omission. "The research worked: it found the problems before live did" is both true and genuinely morale-protecting. Option A is selective reporting; when the full picture emerges later — and it always does — stakeholders will wonder what else has been filtered, which is far worse for the team than one uncomfortable session. Option C is manipulation in the other direction, spending the team's credibility to engineer a reaction. Option D avoids the conversation entirely, failing the openness that Agile working depends on. A useful rule for scenarios like this: any option whose mechanism is controlling what others are allowed to know is rarely the effective one.

**Question 6 (moderate) — An accessibility issue late in the sprint**

Two days before a planned release, a tester finds that the new form cannot be completed using a keyboard alone — a barrier for users who cannot use a mouse, and a failure against accessibility standards. Fixing it will delay the release by about a week. A stakeholder urges: "Release now, fix it in the next iteration — hardly anyone uses keyboard-only navigation." What is the most effective response?

- A) Release on time — the stakeholder has accepted the risk, and the fix is coming anyway.
- B) Explain that the barrier excludes users and breaches the standards the team must meet, recommend delaying the release, and raise it promptly with your product manager so the decision is made at the right level with full information.
- C) Release on time but add a note to the site apologising to keyboard users.
- D) Refuse to discuss any release until every known defect of any kind is fixed.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Accessibility in government services is not a preference to be traded away by a single stakeholder's risk appetite — it is a legal obligation and a core standard your role is explicitly charged with upholding ("ensure teams comply with appropriate standards"). Option B does three things the assessment looks for: it advocates clearly (naming the exclusion and the standard, not just "there's a bug"), it recommends rather than unilaterally decides (right for associate level on a release decision), and it escalates promptly so the real decision-maker has full information before the deadline forces the issue. Option A treats "hardly anyone" as evidence; it is a guess, and the people excluded are among those who may have no alternative channel. Option C ships a known exclusion with an apology attached — an apology is not an alternative route. Option D overcorrects: not every defect blocks release, and refusing all nuance makes you an obstacle rather than an advocate. The discriminating principle: barriers that exclude classes of users are release-blocking by default; cosmetic defects are not; and the decision belongs where the accountability sits, made with honest information you supply.

**Question 7 (moderate) — A quiet voice in the team**

You notice over several sprints that a junior content designer rarely speaks in planning meetings, but afterwards often shares sharp, useful observations with you one to one. In today's planning session, the team is about to commit to wording for an important results page, and you suspect the content designer has concerns. What is the most effective response?

- A) Say nothing — if the concerns mattered, the content designer would raise them.
- B) After the meeting, tell the content designer they need to speak up more.
- C) In the meeting, create space directly: "Before we commit, I'd like to hear the content view — [name], what's your read on this wording?" — and separately explore with them how they'd prefer to feed in.
- D) Raise their concerns yourself in the meeting, presenting them as your own observations.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** This scenario tests inclusion and collaboration — behaviours that situational judgement tests for product roles probe because product managers shape how team voices get heard. Option C acts at the moment of decision (before commitment, when input can still change the outcome), gives the expert a direct, respectful opening, and follows up privately to find a sustainable pattern — some people contribute best asynchronously or in writing, and a good product person adapts the process to get the expertise, not the performance. Option A mistakes silence for absence of views; meeting dynamics, seniority, and confidence all suppress contributions, and the evidence in the scenario (sharp observations afterwards) says the views exist. Option B locates the entire problem in the individual and arrives after the decision is already made. Option D gets the content heard but erases its author — the designer loses recognition, the team loses the chance to engage the actual expert, and if questions arise you cannot answer them at their depth. Bringing together the views of a multidisciplinary team, which your role summary names directly, means designing moments where every discipline's insight reaches the decision.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Asked a question beyond your knowledge**

At a show-and-tell, a senior stakeholder asks you directly: "What would it cost to extend this feature to the legacy casework system?" You have no idea — integration with the legacy system has never been scoped, and your product manager is on leave. What is the most effective response?

- A) Give a rough figure off the top of your head so you look competent, and refine it later.
- B) Say you don't know and move quickly to the next demo item.
- C) Say the integration has not yet been scoped, so any figure now would be a guess; offer to take the question away, involve the technical lead, and come back with a considered estimate by a stated date.
- D) Suggest the stakeholder ask your product manager when they return from leave.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Assessments at entry level frequently test the "I don't know" moment, because the wrong instinct — improvising an answer to save face — causes real damage: an off-the-cuff number (option A) will be remembered, repeated in business cases, and defended long after everyone has forgotten it was a guess. Option C is the professional pattern: honest about the current state ("not yet scoped"), clear about why guessing is harmful, and — crucially — it converts the question into an owned action with the right expertise (the technical lead) and a committed date. That final step is what separates C from B: option B is honest but drops the stakeholder's genuine need on the floor. Option D is technically an escalation but reads as deflection — your product manager being on leave does not prevent you taking ownership of the follow-up; it makes doing so more valuable. Note the general principle: "I don't know, and here is exactly how and when you'll get a reliable answer" builds more stakeholder confidence than any improvised figure ever could.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Pressure to inflate a benefits estimate**

You drafted a benefits estimate for a proposed feature: around £40,000 per year in reduced support costs, based on call-volume data. A stakeholder reviewing the draft says: "Can you make it £100,000? The bid will land better, and these numbers are always fuzzy anyway." What is the most effective response?

- A) Change it to £100,000 — estimates are indeed uncertain, and the stakeholder owns the bid.
- B) Keep £40,000 in the draft, explain the evidence behind it, offer to show a range with stated assumptions (including any genuine upside the stakeholder can identify evidence for), and flag the conversation to your product manager.
- C) Compromise at £70,000 without new evidence, since it is between the two positions.
- D) Refuse and report the stakeholder to their line manager for attempted misconduct.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This is an integrity scenario, and integrity scenarios have a firm floor: you do not present figures you know to be unsupported. Option A crosses that floor — "fuzzy" describes uncertainty around an evidence-based estimate, not permission to invent one two and a half times larger; if the inflated bid succeeds, the product will be held to a benefit it cannot deliver, and your name is on the estimate. Option C fails in the same way while feeling more reasonable: splitting the difference without evidence is still fabrication, just politer. Option B holds the evidential line while staying genuinely collaborative — a range with explicit assumptions is the honest way to express uncertainty, and inviting the stakeholder to surface real upside evidence turns a pressure conversation into an analytical one. Telling your product manager is proportionate at your level: not an accusation, simply ensuring the person accountable knows the estimate was challenged. Option D leaps past every proportionate step to the most adversarial one; the stakeholder tested a boundary in a common, low-grade way, and escalation to their manager as a first move destroys the relationship the role requires you to build. Firm on the number, warm with the person, transparent with your manager — that is the pattern.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Most and least effective**

Your team's velocity has dropped for three consecutive sprints. In the retrospective, the developers say the real cause is that stories keep arriving without clear acceptance criteria, so work bounces back and forth. You write many of those stories, having recently taken on backlog preparation. Rank the responses: which is MOST effective and which is LEAST effective?

1. Acknowledge openly that story quality is part of the problem, ask the team to help define what "ready" should mean, and agree a definition of ready you will apply — reviewing together in two sprints whether bounce-backs have fallen.
2. Point out that developers should ask questions earlier instead of letting stories bounce.
3. Say nothing in the retrospective, but privately resolve to write better stories.
4. Suggest the team stops estimating velocity so the drop is no longer visible.

- A) Most effective: 1; least effective: 4
- B) Most effective: 2; least effective: 3
- C) Most effective: 1; least effective: 2
- D) Most effective: 3; least effective: 4

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Response 1 is clearly the most effective: it models ownership without self-flagellation, converts criticism into a team-owned improvement (a definition of ready), and — the mark of real Agile practice — builds in measurement, so in two sprints the team will know whether the change worked. That is your **Agile and Lean practices** skill and continuous improvement in one move. For least effective, compare 2, 3, and 4. Response 2 deflects blame and will sour the retrospective, but it at least engages with the problem's mechanics; asking questions earlier is even a fragment of a real improvement, badly delivered. Response 3 is silent but the private resolution might genuinely improve stories — weak on transparency, not destructive. Response 4 is qualitatively worse than both: it does not fail to fix the problem, it dismantles the instrument that revealed it. Hiding a signal because it is uncomfortable is the anti-pattern behind most delivery disasters, and it corrupts the team's transparency for every future issue too. So the pairing is most: 1, least: 4 — option A. In most/least format, judge the least-effective candidate by asking which response actively makes things worse, not merely which is unimpressive.

**Question 11 (challenging) — A shortcut with users' data**

Your team wants quick feedback on a prototype. A colleague suggests emailing the prototype link to a list of real service users whose email addresses appear in the live support system, "since they've contacted us anyway". You are fairly sure those addresses were collected only for handling support queries, and your researcher usually recruits participants through a consented panel. What is the most effective response?

- A) Send the emails — the users did contact the service, and feedback benefits everyone.
- B) Pause the idea, say you think using support contact details for research may go beyond what the data was collected for, and check with the user researcher and, where needed, the team responsible for data protection before proceeding — offering the consented panel as the faster compliant route.
- C) Send the emails but add an unsubscribe link.
- D) Anonymously report your colleague to the information commissioner.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Personal data collected for one purpose (support) being reused for another (research recruitment) is exactly the situation data protection rules exist to control, and government services are rightly held to the highest standard on this. You are not expected, at associate level, to be the data protection expert — and option B does not require you to be. It requires you to do three things assessments consistently reward: notice the issue, name it plainly without accusing anyone, and route it to the people whose job it is to judge (the researcher, and data protection colleagues if needed), while offering a compliant alternative that still meets the underlying need for fast feedback. That last element matters: the colleague's goal is legitimate, and effective product people solve the need, not just block the method. Option A treats "they contacted us" as consent, which it is not. Option C decorates the problem instead of resolving it — an unsubscribe link does not fix an improper basis for the contact. Option D, like all leap-to-maximum escalations, fails proportionality absurdly: nothing has even been sent yet, and internal routes exist precisely for this moment. Standards compliance is part of your role; the skill is raising it early, calmly, and constructively.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Your product manager is the problem**

Your product manager — who is supportive and whom you like — has verbally promised a delivery date to a senior stakeholder for a feature your team estimates will take at least twice that long. The team is now being pressed to "make it work", corners are being discussed (skipping usability testing, deferring accessibility checks), and morale is dipping. You are the most junior product person in the room. What is the most effective response?

- A) Keep your head down — date commitments are your product manager's business, and challenging them could harm your relationship.
- B) Speak to your product manager privately: share the team's estimate and the specific corners being discussed, ask how you can help re-plan or re-scope, and say plainly that you are worried about skipping accessibility and usability checks.
- C) Email the senior stakeholder directly to say the date is impossible and was promised in error.
- D) Tell the team to ignore the date and work at a sustainable pace, and say you will take responsibility.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This is the hardest kind of scenario for an entry-level assessment: the person you would normally escalate to is the source of the problem, and every incorrect option maps to a recognisable failure mode. Option B threads it correctly: the conversation happens privately (preserving your product manager's standing and your relationship), it leads with evidence (the team's estimate) rather than blame, it offers help (re-plan, re-scope — perhaps delivering a smaller slice by the promised date), and it is specific about the non-negotiables being eroded, naming accessibility and usability rather than a vague "quality". Speaking up to your own manager with evidence and respect is a professional behaviour, not insubordination — and supportive managers, like this one, generally want the early warning. Option A is the bystander failure: you have material information (the estimate, the corner-cutting) that the accountable person may not have fully absorbed, and silence makes you complicit in the outcome. Option C goes around your product manager to their stakeholder — a trust-destroying move that also exceeds your standing. Option D quietly countermands your manager and makes a promise ("I'll take responsibility") that your role cannot honour. If the private conversation fails and corners are actually cut on accessibility, then raising it further — openly, with your product manager aware — becomes the right next step. Escalation is a ladder, and the effective professional climbs it one rung at a time.

### Preparation tips

- Learn the principles, not the answers. Across every scenario above, the effective responses share a fingerprint: keep users (especially excluded users) in the decision, be honest even when it is uncomfortable, involve the right people at the right moment, offer alternatives rather than bare refusals, and act at the moment a decision can still change.
- Rehearse the escalation ladder for your level: handle it yourself → inform your product manager → involve your product manager → involve specialists (researcher, security, data protection) → wider escalation. Most wrong answers sit at the extremes of that ladder.
- When two options both look good, pick the one that adds transparency or measurement — telling the affected people, checking back in two sprints, committing to a date for an answer. Follow-through is what separates good intentions from effective behaviour.
- When choosing the least effective response, look for the option that hides a signal, deceives someone, or makes the situation actively worse — not merely the option that is timid.
- Reflect on your real week. After a tricky moment at work, ask yourself what the "option A to D" versions of your response would have been, and which one you actually chose. This turns everyday work into free practice.
- Read your organisation's values and the Service Standard before the test. Situational judgement scoring keys are built from exactly those documents, and the vocabulary will anchor your instincts.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- Choosing the response that avoids conflict rather than the one that resolves it. Giving in to keep the peace, staying silent in a retrospective, and hiding bad news are all conflict-avoidance in different costumes — and all score poorly.
- Choosing the maximal escalation. Reporting colleagues to regulators or senior managers as a first step almost always fails the proportionality test. Escalation is a ladder; start on the lowest effective rung.
- Overplaying your authority. At associate level, unilaterally overruling developers, refusing releases single-handedly, or countermanding your product manager exceeds the role. The effective versions recommend, advocate, and involve.
- Underplaying your responsibility. The mirror-image error: deflecting to "ask my product manager" when the follow-up is squarely yours to own. Being junior does not mean being passive.
- Treating honesty as optional under pressure. Any option involving inflated figures, filtered findings, or quiet concealment is wrong, however sympathetic the motive. Integrity questions have a floor, and the assessment is checking you know where it is.
- Forgetting the users who are not in the room. Accessibility, assisted digital, and data protection scenarios all test whether you notice harms to people who cannot advocate for themselves. Make them visible in your answer, as in your work.

In the numeric section, you worked through the arithmetic of product value: completion rates, velocity averages, percentage changes, weighted satisfaction scores, cost-per-transaction trends, and benefit estimates built step by honest step.
