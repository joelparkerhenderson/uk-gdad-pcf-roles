## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures how well you think with the actual materials of your job, rather than with abstract puzzles. Instead of shapes and number sequences, you will see sprint boards, dependency logs, team calendars, and simple delivery rules, and you will be asked to reason about them quickly and accurately.

The typical format is an online, timed test lasting 15 to 30 minutes, with somewhere between 15 and 30 questions. The questions cover pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective: your answers are compared with a large group of previous test takers, called a norm group. Many modern platforms are adaptive, which means the questions get slightly harder when you answer correctly and slightly easier when you do not — so do not panic if the test feels like it is getting more difficult. That usually means you are doing well. Most tests offer a few ungraded practice questions at the start so you can get used to the screen layout without any pressure.

Employers use cognitive assessments for delivery roles because so much of delivery management is careful noticing. An associate delivery manager who spots that two cards on the board describe the same task, or that a dependency has quietly slipped past its date, saves the team days of confusion. The test simulates that noticing in miniature. It rewards a calm, methodical approach: read carefully, check the rule, apply it, and move on.

One more reassurance: at your level, nobody expects you to reason like a head of delivery. The scenarios in this section are deliberately drawn from the day-to-day life of a small Agile team — stand-ups, boards, simple plans, and supervised work — because that is your world right now.

### How this assessment maps to your role

Each dimension of the cognitive assessment lines up with a named skill in your role summary:

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **Agile and Lean practices** and your duty to visualise and make visible the work of the team. Spotting that cards on a board follow (or break) a convention, or that the same blocker keeps returning sprint after sprint, is pattern recognition applied to delivery.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Maintaining delivery momentum**. When you track, manage, escalate and communicate dependencies, you constantly reason in if-then steps: if the API is late, the integration story cannot start; if the integration story cannot start, the demo needs to change.
- **Error checking** maps to **Making a process work** and **Agile and Lean practices**. Helping the team establish a process only works if the process is followed consistently, and that means noticing when a card skips a column or a definition of done is missed.
- **Prioritisation** maps to **Maintaining delivery momentum** and **Planning**. Actively addressing risks and issues, and knowing when to escalate them, depends on judging what matters most right now.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Planning** and **Team dynamics and collaboration**. When the sprint plan meets reality — someone is ill, a dependency slips, a meeting clashes — you help the team find a workable answer.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern recognition on a sprint board**

Your team's board has five columns, and every card must move through them in this order: Backlog, Ready, In progress, Review, Done. During stand-up you look at the history of four cards from yesterday:

1. Card A moved from Ready to In progress.
2. Card B moved from In progress to Review.
3. Card C moved from Ready to Review.
4. Card D moved from Review to Done.

Which card broke the board's rule?

- A) Card A
- B) Card B
- C) Card C
- D) Card D

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The rule says every card moves through the columns in order, one step at a time: Backlog, then Ready, then In progress, then Review, then Done. Cards A, B, and D each moved exactly one column to the right. Card C jumped from Ready straight to Review, skipping In progress. That is the broken pattern. In real life, a card that skips a column often means work was done without being visible on the board — perhaps someone reviewed a change that was never marked as started. Part of your role is to visualise and make visible the work of the team, so spotting a skipped column is not nit-picking: it protects the honesty of the board, which is what makes stand-ups and planning trustworthy.

**Question 2 (easy) — Logical deduction about a dependency**

Your team is building a form for a government service. The team's plan includes this rule: "The form cannot go to user testing until the content designer has approved the question wording." At stand-up, the user researcher says: "The form is going to user testing on Thursday." Which one of the following must be true if the plan's rule is being followed?

- A) The content designer approved the wording before Thursday's testing.
- B) The content designer will approve the wording on Friday.
- C) The user researcher has made a mistake.
- D) The form does not need content approval.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The rule is a simple condition: no user testing until approval happens. If the rule is being followed and testing happens Thursday, then the approval must have happened first — that is option A. Option B puts the approval after the testing, which breaks the rule. Options C and D invent facts that the question does not give you: nothing says the researcher is wrong, and nothing says the form is exempt. This is the discipline that assessments reward, and it is also the discipline your role rewards. When you track and communicate dependencies, you reason strictly from what the plan and the facts actually say — not from what you assume. If you were unsure in real life, the right move would be a friendly check: "Just confirming — has content sign-off happened for Thursday's testing?"

**Question 3 (easy) — Error checking a stand-up summary**

After stand-up, you write a summary message for the team channel. Before sending, you re-read it:

"Stand-up summary, Tuesday: 1) Priya finished the payment page and it is now in Review. 2) Sam is blocked waiting for test data from the Data team. 3) Jo will demo the prototype at Thursday's show and tell. 4) Sam finished the payment page and moved it to Done."

Which two points cannot both be true as written?

- A) Points 1 and 2
- B) Points 1 and 4
- C) Points 2 and 3
- D) Points 3 and 4

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Read each pair. Point 1 says Priya finished the payment page and it is in Review. Point 4 says Sam finished the payment page and it is in Done. The same piece of work cannot have been finished by two different people and be sitting in two different columns at once. Points 2 and 3 are about different things (test data and a demo) and do not clash with anything. The likely real-world cause is a copy-and-paste slip or two cards accidentally created for one task. Catching this before you press send matters, because your summaries are how you act as an advocate for the team externally — if your updates contradict themselves, stakeholders lose confidence in the team's information. A ten-second re-read is one of the cheapest quality habits you can build.

**Question 4 (easy) — Prioritisation of morning tasks**

You arrive at 9:00 and find four things waiting for you:

1. The stand-up starts at 9:30 and the board has not been updated with yesterday's changes.
2. A supplier has emailed asking you to confirm a meeting time for next week.
3. The delivery manager you support has asked for the risk log to be tidied by the end of the week.
4. A developer has messaged: "The test environment is down — the whole team is blocked."

What should you do first?

- A) Update the board so stand-up runs smoothly.
- B) Reply to the supplier to be polite and prompt.
- C) Start tidying the risk log while the morning is quiet.
- D) Find out more about the test environment problem and start getting help.

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Prioritisation questions ask you to weigh urgency and impact together. Item 4 is both urgent and high impact: the whole team is blocked right now, which means every hour costs the team an hour of progress. Your role says you actively remove or minimise risks, issues or dependencies where possible, and know when to escalate — a downed environment usually means quickly confirming the facts, raising it with the right support team, and telling your delivery manager. Item 1 matters but stand-up can start with a two-minute board update, or the team can update cards as they speak. Item 2 is polite housekeeping with a deadline of "next week". Item 3 has an end-of-week deadline. Options A, B, and C all put routine tasks ahead of a live blocker affecting the whole team, which inverts impact and urgency.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Pattern recognition across retrospectives**

You are preparing notes for the team's retrospective. Looking back at the last four retro boards, you list the top "what slowed us down" item from each:

- Sprint 1: "Waited three days for security review."
- Sprint 2: "Story unclear — had to rework after demo."
- Sprint 3: "Waited four days for security review."
- Sprint 4: "Waited two days for security review."

What is the most useful observation to bring to the retrospective?

- A) The team complains a lot and should focus on positives.
- B) Waiting for security review is a repeating pattern across three of four sprints and is worth investigating as a team.
- C) Sprint 2 proves the real problem is unclear stories.
- D) The waits are getting shorter, so the problem is solving itself.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Three of the four sprints name the same slowdown: waiting for security review. That is a recurring pattern, and recurring patterns are exactly what retrospectives exist to surface. Option A dismisses the data. Option C promotes a single occurrence above a repeated one. Option D is tempting — three days, then four, then two — but read carefully: the numbers do not steadily fall (they went up between sprints 1 and 3), and even two days of waiting each sprint is a real cost. The honest summary is "this keeps happening; let's understand why." Bringing a pattern rather than a complaint helps the team have a constructive conversation, perhaps about booking reviews earlier or agreeing a service-level expectation with the security team. This is your **Agile and Lean practices** skill in action: encouraging reflective practice with evidence, and helping the team see its own patterns.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Logical deduction about sprint scope**

Your team has a working agreement with three rules: (i) a story can only be brought into the sprint if it has been estimated; (ii) any story larger than 8 points must be split before it is estimated; (iii) the product owner must agree before a new story is added mid-sprint. On Wednesday, mid-sprint, a stakeholder asks for a new 13-point story to be added to the sprint immediately. Following the working agreement, what is the minimum that must happen before this story can enter the sprint?

- A) The product owner agrees, and the story is added as it is.
- B) The story is split, the resulting stories are estimated, and the product owner agrees.
- C) The story is estimated at 13 points and added with the product owner's agreement.
- D) Nothing can be done; stories can never be added mid-sprint.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Walk through the rules one at a time. The story is 13 points, which is larger than 8, so rule (ii) says it must be split before estimation. After splitting, rule (i) says the pieces must be estimated before entering the sprint. Because it is mid-sprint, rule (iii) says the product owner must agree. So the full chain is: split, estimate, agree — which is option B. Option A skips splitting and estimating. Option C estimates a story that rule (ii) says is too big to estimate as it stands. Option D invents a ban that the working agreement does not contain — mid-sprint additions are allowed with agreement. Assessments often test whether you apply all the given rules, not just the first one you remember. In your role, providing a clear, open and transparent framework in which teams can deliver means knowing the team's agreements well enough to apply them calmly when a stakeholder is pressing for speed.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Error checking a dependency log**

You keep the team's dependency log. An extract reads:

| ID | We need | From | Needed by | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| D1 | API test credentials | Platform team | 12 May | Received 10 May |
| D2 | Welsh translation | Content team | 20 May | In progress |
| D3 | Accessibility audit slot | Audit team | 18 May | Booked for 25 May |
| D4 | Sign-off on privacy notice | Legal team | 30 May | Not yet requested |

Today is 14 May. Which dependency most needs your attention today?

- A) D1
- B) D2
- C) D3
- D) D4

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Check each row against today's date, 14 May. D1 is complete — received before its date. D2 is needed on 20 May and is in progress; worth watching, but not alarming. D3 is needed by 18 May but is booked for 25 May — it is already guaranteed to be a week late unless something changes. That is a live problem today: the earlier you act, the more options you have, such as asking the audit team for an earlier slot, checking whether the 18 May date can move, or escalating to your delivery manager. D4 is not yet requested, but its date is 30 May, over two weeks away — it needs action soon, though it is not the most pressing. The skill being tested is comparing promised dates with needed-by dates, which is the heart of your duty to track, manage, escalate and communicate dependencies. A log is only useful if someone reads it the way you just did.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Prioritisation with a hidden dependency**

Your delivery manager is away today. Four items are on your list:

1. Prepare the slides for tomorrow's show and tell (about an hour of work).
2. Chase the Platform team about test credentials the developers need tomorrow morning.
3. Book a room for next month's planning day.
4. Write up Monday's retrospective actions before people forget the context.

Which order is most defensible?

- A) 1, 2, 3, 4 — the show and tell is the most visible event
- B) 2, 1, 4, 3 — unblock tomorrow's work first, then prepare tomorrow's event, then capture fading context, then the distant booking
- C) 3, 4, 1, 2 — quick tasks first to clear the list
- D) 4, 2, 1, 3 — retro actions are about team improvement, which always comes first

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The key insight is that item 2 has a hard dependency deadline: if the credentials do not arrive today, developers are blocked tomorrow morning, and chasing takes only minutes but may need the rest of the day for the other team to act. Sending that chase first gives it the longest possible time to land. Item 1 is due tomorrow and takes an hour, so it comes next. Item 4 loses value as memories fade, so it beats item 3, which is weeks away. Option A prioritises visibility over unblocking the team. Option C uses the "quick wins first" habit, which feels productive but leaves the highest-impact item until last. Option D overvalues an important-but-not-urgent task. Notice the general technique: send anything that starts a clock on someone else's desk as early as possible. That is delivery momentum thinking, straight from your **Maintaining delivery momentum** skill.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Applied problem solving with team capacity**

Your team of four developers planned 40 points for this two-week sprint, based on everyone being available every day. On day two, one developer is called away for the rest of the sprint, and a bank holiday means everyone loses one further day. The team asks you to help think through the impact. Roughly what share of the planned capacity remains, and what is the most sensible immediate step?

- A) About 90% remains; no change needed.
- B) About 70% remains; talk to the product owner about which stories matter most.
- C) About 50% remains; cancel the sprint.
- D) Exactly 40 points remain; capacity does not depend on people.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Estimate the capacity roughly, which is all the situation needs. Four developers for ten days is 40 developer-days. Losing one developer for eight of the ten remaining days removes about 8 developer-days; the bank holiday removes 1 day for each of the remaining three developers plus would have affected the fourth too — call it around 3 to 4 more days lost. Roughly 11 to 12 of 40 developer-days are gone, so about 70% remains. The sensible step is a conversation with the product owner about priorities, so the team protects the most valuable stories rather than half-finishing everything. Option A ignores real losses. Option C overreacts — reduced capacity is normal, and cancelling a sprint is a rare, drastic step. Option D confuses the plan with reality. Your role summary says you help the team set a sustainable cadence and support planning; this question shows what that looks like: quick arithmetic, honest communication, and a priority conversation instead of quiet overcommitment.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Multi-rule deduction about a release**

Your team's release checklist has these rules: (i) a release can only happen on a Tuesday or Wednesday; (ii) no release may happen on the last two working days before a public holiday; (iii) every release needs a completed accessibility check, which takes two full working days and must finish before release day; (iv) the accessibility checker is only available Monday to Wednesday. A public holiday falls on Friday of week 1. The accessibility check has not started as of Monday morning of week 1. What is the earliest release day?

- A) Tuesday of week 1
- B) Wednesday of week 1
- C) Tuesday of week 2
- D) Wednesday of week 2

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Work through the rules in order. The check takes two full working days and the checker works Monday to Wednesday, so the earliest it can finish is end of Tuesday (Monday and Tuesday of week 1). Rule (iii) says the check must finish before release day, so the earliest possible release day is Wednesday of week 1. Now apply rule (ii): the public holiday is Friday of week 1, so the last two working days before it are Wednesday and Thursday — releases are banned on both. That rules out Wednesday of week 1. The next allowed days under rule (i) are Tuesday and Wednesday of week 2. Tuesday of week 2 satisfies every rule: the check finished the previous week, it is a permitted weekday, and it is not adjacent to the holiday. So the answer is Tuesday of week 2. The common error is stopping after rules (i) and (iii) and choosing Wednesday of week 1, forgetting the holiday rule. Assessments — and release planning — reward checking every rule, every time.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Spotting the real blocker**

Your team's last three sprints show this pattern: stories move quickly to Review, then sit in Review for four to six days before moving to Done. The developers say they are working hard. The product owner is frustrated that "nothing is finishing." The Review column currently holds seven cards; the In progress column holds two. What is the most accurate diagnosis, and the most constructive first step for you as associate delivery manager?

- A) The developers are too slow; ask them to work faster.
- B) Work is piling up at the Review step; bring this pattern to the team and ask what would help reviews happen sooner.
- C) The product owner is too impatient; ask them to wait.
- D) The board has too many columns; remove the Review column so cards reach Done faster.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Look at where the work actually accumulates. Cards reach Review quickly, then wait four to six days; there are seven cards in Review and only two in progress. The bottleneck is the reviewing step, not the developing step — so option A blames the wrong stage, and would make things worse by pushing even more cards into the queue. Option C dismisses a legitimate concern: work genuinely is not finishing. Option D is the classic mistake of deleting the signal instead of fixing the problem — removing the column hides the queue without speeding up reviews. Option B does what your role asks: you visualise and make visible the work of the team, and you help the team find a process that works for them. You bring the observable pattern to the team without blame and let them propose answers — perhaps pairing on reviews, a working agreement to review before starting new work, or limiting how many cards Review can hold. Diagnosing from where the queue forms, rather than from who seems busy, is one of the most valuable habits in delivery.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Prioritising an escalation decision**

You are supporting a delivery manager who is on leave until Monday; it is Thursday. Three situations have developed today:

1. A supplier has said their deliverable will now arrive two weeks late, which will delay the team's beta assessment.
2. Two developers had a tense disagreement in stand-up about a technical approach; they have agreed to discuss it tomorrow with the tech lead.
3. The show and tell scheduled for tomorrow has a room double-booking.

For which situation is it most important to escalate beyond the team today, rather than handling it yourself or waiting for Monday?

- A) Situation 1, because a supplier delay affecting a beta assessment has consequences beyond the team and needs senior awareness now.
- B) Situation 2, because conflict is always an emergency.
- C) Situation 3, because tomorrow's event is the nearest deadline.
- D) None; everything should wait for the delivery manager's return on Monday.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Escalation questions test whether you can tell the difference between "I can handle this," "the team can handle this," and "someone senior needs to know now." Situation 1 crosses two boundaries: it involves a contracted supplier — and your role names you as a point of contact for contracted suppliers, with an understanding of the appropriate internal contacts — and it threatens a beta assessment date, which matters to people outside the team, including governance and possibly commercial colleagues. Delaying that news until Monday costs two working days of response time. Situation 2 already has a sensible next step arranged with the tech lead; watchful patience is right, and treating every disagreement as an emergency would undermine the team's ability to resolve things themselves. Situation 3 is squarely within your own power: rebook a room. Option D confuses "my manager is away" with "decisions pause" — your role explicitly includes knowing when to escalate. The skill is calibration: handle what you can, let the team handle what it can, and raise promptly what others need to know.

### Preparation tips

- **Practise with your own board.** Spend five minutes each day reading your team's board as if you were an assessor: does every card follow the rules? Are any dates inconsistent? This makes error checking a habit rather than an exam skill.
- **Say your reasoning out loud.** For deduction questions, practise walking through rules one at a time: "Rule one says... rule two says... so the only thing that must be true is..." Speaking it slows you down just enough to be accurate.
- **Time yourself gently.** Try answering three practice questions in five minutes. Timed practice reduces the shock of the clock in the real test — but start untimed until you are comfortable.
- **Learn the difference between "must be true" and "could be true."** Many cognitive questions turn on this. Only choose an answer that is guaranteed by the given facts.
- **Sleep and setup matter.** Take real tests rested, in a quiet spot, with a stable connection, and with the practice questions completed. These simple things measurably improve scores.
- **Treat wrong answers as findings.** When you miss a question, write one sentence about why. Patterns in your errors (rushing, skipping a rule, misreading a table) are the most useful preparation data you will ever collect.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Answering from experience instead of the question.** Your team might handle a situation differently, but the test asks about the rules and facts on the screen. Use only what is given.
- **Stopping at the first rule.** Multi-rule questions (like the release question above) punish people who apply one rule and answer. Check every rule against your candidate answer before committing.
- **Confusing busy with blocked.** In diagnosis questions, look at where work queues up, not at who appears to be working hard.
- **Rushing the easy questions.** Early questions are where careless errors happen because you relax. Bank the easy marks with a careful read.
- **Panicking when questions get harder.** On adaptive tests, harder questions mean you are doing well. Keep your pace steady.
- **Leaving answers blank.** Unless the instructions say otherwise, an educated guess beats a blank. Eliminate the options you can, then choose.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures how confidently and accurately you work with the numbers your role genuinely produces. For an associate delivery manager, that means sprint velocity, story points, team capacity in days, percentages of work completed, burndown charts, simple budgets, and supplier day rates. You will not be asked to do advanced mathematics. The arithmetic is percentages, ratios, averages, differences, and simple rates — the kind you can do with a pen, paper, and (usually) an on-screen calculator.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 20 to 30 minutes with 15 to 25 questions. Each question presents some data — a table, a short chart description, or a few figures in a sentence — and asks you to extract the right numbers and do a short calculation. Scoring is objective, and most platforms report both accuracy and speed. Many allow a basic calculator; check the instructions before you start, and practise both with and without one so you are ready either way.

Employers use numeric assessments for delivery roles because delivery is full of small, consequential sums. "Can we finish this by the sprint review?" is an arithmetic question about capacity. "Are we on track against the burndown?" is a comparison of two rates. "What does this supplier extension cost?" is a multiplication. An associate delivery manager who can do these sums quickly and calmly helps the team plan honestly, spot problems early, and understand the financial impact of the work — which your role summary explicitly names.

A reassurance before you begin: the numbers in delivery are friendly numbers. Sprints are two weeks, teams are small, budgets are round-ish. What the test rewards is care — reading the table correctly, choosing the right figures, and writing the steps down. Every worked answer below shows the arithmetic step by step so you can check your method, not just your answer.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Percentages and proportions** map to **Agile and Lean practices** and visualising the work of the team: "the team completed 34 of 40 planned points" is a percentage waiting to be calculated and communicated.
- **Averages and rates** map to **Planning**: understanding the importance of planning and forecasting starts with averaging past velocity and using it to sense-check the next sprint's plan.
- **Capacity arithmetic** maps to **Maintaining delivery momentum**: setting a sustainable team cadence means knowing how many working days the team really has, after leave, bank holidays, and ceremonies.
- **Budget and cost calculations** map to **Financial management** and **Commercial management**: understanding the financial impact of the work you do, and acting as a point of contact for contracted suppliers, both involve day rates, totals, and simple comparisons of cost.
- **Reading tables and charts** maps to **Communicating between the technical and non-technical**: you often translate a table of delivery data into a sentence a stakeholder can act on, and that translation must be numerically right.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Percentage of sprint completed**

Your team planned 40 story points for the sprint and completed 30 of them. What percentage of the planned points did the team complete?

- A) 70%
- B) 75%
- C) 80%
- D) 85%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Percentage completed = completed points ÷ planned points × 100.

Step 1: 30 ÷ 40 = 0.75.
Step 2: 0.75 × 100 = 75%.

The team completed 75% of its plan. When you report this at a review, the percentage is more meaningful than the raw numbers to non-technical stakeholders, because "three quarters of the plan" is instantly understandable. Be careful with the direction of the division: completed over planned, not planned over completed. Dividing the wrong way gives 40 ÷ 30 = 1.33, or "133%", which is a classic rushed-test error — if your answer to a "how much of the plan was done" question is over 100% when work was left over, you have divided the wrong way round.

**Question 2 (easy) — Average velocity**

Your team's velocity over the last four sprints was 28, 32, 30, and 34 points. What is the team's average velocity?

- A) 30 points
- B) 31 points
- C) 32 points
- D) 33 points

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Average = sum of values ÷ number of values.

Step 1: 28 + 32 = 60.
Step 2: 60 + 30 = 90.
Step 3: 90 + 34 = 124.
Step 4: 124 ÷ 4 = 31.

The average velocity is 31 points. In planning, an average like this is a starting point for a conversation, not a promise: your role summary says you should understand the importance of planning and forecasting, and a four-sprint average is one of the simplest, most honest forecasting tools a team has. If the team plans 40 points next sprint against an average of 31, that is a gap worth gently pointing out.

**Question 3 (easy) — Team capacity in days**

Your team has 5 members. The next sprint is 10 working days long. One member is on leave for 3 days, and one bank holiday falls in the sprint (affecting everyone). How many person-days of capacity does the team have?

- A) 40 person-days
- B) 42 person-days
- C) 45 person-days
- D) 47 person-days

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Start with the maximum and subtract what is lost.

Step 1: Maximum capacity = 5 people × 10 days = 50 person-days.
Step 2: Bank holiday: everyone loses 1 day = 5 person-days lost. Running total: 50 − 5 = 45.
Step 3: Leave: one member loses 3 further days = 3 person-days lost. Running total: 45 − 3 = 42.

The team has 42 person-days. A subtle check: could the leave overlap the bank holiday? The question says leave of 3 days plus a bank holiday affecting everyone; treat them as separate unless told otherwise. In real sprint planning you would ask that exact question — "is any of your leave on the bank holiday?" — which is a nice example of how numeric care and good planning conversation are the same habit. Capacity sums like this one are the foundation of a sustainable cadence, which your **Maintaining delivery momentum** skill asks you to protect.

**Question 4 (easy) — Reading a burndown**

A two-week sprint starts with 40 points. The ideal burndown reduces the remaining work by the same amount each of the 10 working days. At the end of day 5, how many points should ideally remain?

- A) 15 points
- B) 20 points
- C) 25 points
- D) 30 points

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The ideal line falls evenly from 40 to 0 over 10 days.

Step 1: Daily ideal reduction = 40 ÷ 10 = 4 points per day.
Step 2: After 5 days, ideal reduction = 4 × 5 = 20 points.
Step 3: Remaining = 40 − 20 = 20 points.

Halfway through the sprint, half the work should remain: 20 points. Burndown charts are one of the simplest ways to visualise and make visible the work of the team. If the actual line sits well above the ideal line at day 5 — say 30 points remaining — that is an early, factual prompt for a team conversation, long before the last day surprise. Knowing the ideal value at any day lets you describe the gap precisely: "we are 10 points behind the ideal line" is much more useful than "we seem a bit behind."

**Question 5 (moderate) — Supplier day-rate cost**

A contracted supplier provides one specialist at £650 per day. The specialist is booked for 3 days per week for 4 weeks. What is the total cost of the booking?

- A) £6,500
- B) £7,150
- C) £7,800
- D) £8,450

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Total cost = day rate × days per week × weeks.

Step 1: Days booked = 3 × 4 = 12 days.
Step 2: Total = £650 × 12.
Step 3: £650 × 12 = £650 × 10 + £650 × 2 = £6,500 + £1,300 = £7,800.

The booking costs £7,800. As a point of contact for contracted suppliers, you will see figures like this in emails and purchase orders, and being able to check them mentally protects your team and the public purse. Notice the calculator-free trick in step 3: multiplying by 12 is multiplying by 10 and by 2 and adding. Small mental methods like this keep you quick under test conditions and sharp in supplier meetings.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Percentage change in velocity**

Your team's velocity was 25 points in sprint 8 and 30 points in sprint 9. What was the percentage increase?

- A) 5%
- B) 17%
- C) 20%
- D) 25%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Percentage change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100.

Step 1: Change = 30 − 25 = 5 points.
Step 2: 5 ÷ 25 = 0.2.
Step 3: 0.2 × 100 = 20%.

Velocity rose by 20%. The most common error is dividing by the new value: 5 ÷ 30 ≈ 16.7%, which rounds to option B — the test writers put it there deliberately. Always divide by the value you started from. A communication note for your role: a 20% velocity rise over one sprint is pleasing but not yet a trend, and stating it with that caution ("one good sprint — let's see if it holds") is exactly the balanced reporting that builds stakeholder trust in the team's numbers.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Story completion ratio**

At the end of a sprint, the team completed 12 stories, carried over 4 stories, and removed 2 stories from the sprint by agreement with the product owner. Of the stories that stayed in the sprint, what fraction was completed?

- A) 2/3
- B) 3/4
- C) 4/5
- D) 12/18

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Read carefully: the question asks about stories that stayed in the sprint, so the 2 removed stories are excluded.

Step 1: Stories that stayed = completed + carried over = 12 + 4 = 16.
Step 2: Fraction completed = 12 ÷ 16.
Step 3: Simplify: 12/16 = 3/4.

Three quarters of the remaining stories were completed. Option D (12/18) is what you get if you wrongly include the removed stories: 12 + 4 + 2 = 18. This question rewards precise reading of which population the question is about — the same care you need when reporting sprint results, because "we finished 75% of what we committed to after the agreed scope change" and "we finished 67% of the original scope" are both true statements about different things, and a good update says which one you mean.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Budget remaining**

Your team's budget for this quarter is £120,000. So far it has spent £45,000 on staff time, £18,000 on a supplier, and £7,000 on software licences. What percentage of the budget remains?

- A) 41.7%
- B) 50%
- C) 58.3%
- D) 70%

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** First find total spend, then the remainder, then the percentage.

Step 1: Total spend = £45,000 + £18,000 + £7,000 = £70,000.
Step 2: Remaining = £120,000 − £70,000 = £50,000.
Step 3: Percentage remaining = 50,000 ÷ 120,000 = 0.4166…
Step 4: 0.4166… × 100 ≈ 41.7%.

The remaining share is 41.7%. Now look at option C, 58.3% — it is sitting there because it is the percentage spent (70,000 ÷ 120,000 = 58.3%), and under time pressure many candidates compute the spend percentage and pick it without checking which direction the question asked. Whenever a question says "remains," "left," or "still available," pause and confirm whether your figure is the spent share or the remaining share. They always add to 100%, so a one-second check — "41.7 + 58.3 = 100, and I want the remaining share, which is the smaller one here" — saves the mark. In your **Financial management** duty to understand cost and budgets, the same check protects you in real reports: telling a stakeholder the team has 58% of its budget left when it has 42% is a painful error that one careful read prevents.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Cycle time average with an outlier**

You measure how many days five recent stories took from "In progress" to "Done": 2, 3, 3, 4, and 13 days. What are the mean and the most representative typical value to share with the team?

- A) Mean 5; the mean best represents a typical story.
- B) Mean 5; the median of 3 better represents a typical story because of the outlier.
- C) Mean 3; there is no outlier.
- D) Mean 13; the largest value dominates.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Compute the mean first.

Step 1: Sum = 2 + 3 + 3 + 4 + 13 = 25.
Step 2: Mean = 25 ÷ 5 = 5 days.
Step 3: Order the values: 2, 3, 3, 4, 13. The middle (third) value — the median — is 3 days.

The mean is 5, but four of the five stories took 4 days or fewer; the 13-day story is an outlier pulling the mean up. Saying "a typical story takes about 3 days, though one recently took 13 and it is worth asking why" gives the team both the honest typical value and the interesting exception. Option A reports a mean that no story is actually near. Options C and D miscalculate. This is a gentle introduction to a big delivery idea: averages can mislead when the data is skewed, and part of helping teams measure and visualise outcomes (your **Agile and Lean practices** skill) is choosing the number that tells the truth.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Forecasting with average velocity**

The product owner asks: "There are 93 points of work left for this feature. When might it be done?" The team's last three sprint velocities are 29, 31, and 30 points, and sprints are 2 weeks long. Using average velocity, how many sprints of work remain, and roughly how many weeks is that?

- A) 2 sprints; about 4 weeks
- B) 3 sprints; about 6 weeks
- C) 4 sprints; about 8 weeks
- D) 5 sprints; about 10 weeks

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Work step by step.

Step 1: Average velocity = (29 + 31 + 30) ÷ 3 = 90 ÷ 3 = 30 points per sprint.
Step 2: Sprints needed = 93 ÷ 30 = 3.1 sprints.
Step 3: You cannot deliver in a fraction of a sprint, so round up: 4 sprints.
Step 4: 4 sprints × 2 weeks = about 8 weeks.

The honest forecast is about 4 sprints, roughly 8 weeks. The trap is step 3: 3.1 rounds up, not down, because after 3 sprints (90 points) there are still 3 points left, which need a fourth sprint. Choosing "3 sprints" is the optimistic error that creates missed expectations. When you share a forecast like this, add the health warning your **Planning** skill implies: it assumes velocity stays stable and no new work is added — a forecast is a compass, not a contract.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Comparing two suppliers**

Two suppliers can provide a tester for an 8-week engagement, 5 days per week. Supplier X charges £480 per day. Supplier Y charges £520 per day but offers a 10% discount on the total if the engagement is 6 weeks or longer. Which supplier is cheaper for this engagement, and by how much?

- A) X is cheaper by £400
- B) X is cheaper by £480
- C) Y is cheaper by £480
- D) Y is cheaper by £400

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Calculate each total.

Step 1: Days = 8 weeks × 5 days = 40 days.
Step 2: Supplier X total = £480 × 40 = £19,200.
Step 3: Supplier Y before discount = £520 × 40 = £20,800.
Step 4: The engagement is 8 weeks, which is 6 or longer, so the discount applies: 10% of £20,800 = £2,080.
Step 5: Supplier Y total = £20,800 − £2,080 = £18,720.
Step 6: Compare: £19,200 (X) vs £18,720 (Y). Y is cheaper by £19,200 − £18,720 = £480.

Despite the higher headline day rate, Supplier Y is cheaper by £480 once the discount applies. The deeper lesson sits in step 4 — conditional discounts are where supplier comparisons go wrong, because under time pressure candidates either forget the discount (making Y look £1,600 dearer) or apply it when its condition is not met. As the team's point of contact for contracted suppliers, you will meet exactly these structures in quotes: day rates, thresholds, and discounts. Always ask: what is the total before adjustments, does the condition for each adjustment actually apply, and what is the final comparison? Write the two totals side by side before declaring a winner.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Reading a delivery data table**

The table shows four teams' planned and completed points over a programme increment:

| Team | Planned | Completed |
|---|---|---|
| Red | 60 | 48 |
| Blue | 80 | 68 |
| Green | 50 | 45 |
| Yellow | 70 | 49 |

Which team had the lowest completion percentage, and what was it?

- A) Red, at 80%
- B) Blue, at 85%
- C) Yellow, at 70%
- D) Green, at 90%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Compute each percentage: completed ÷ planned × 100.

Step 1: Red = 48 ÷ 60 = 0.80 → 80%.
Step 2: Blue = 68 ÷ 80 = 0.85 → 85%.
Step 3: Green = 45 ÷ 50 = 0.90 → 90%.
Step 4: Yellow = 49 ÷ 70 = 0.70 → 70%.
Step 5: The lowest is Yellow at 70%.

Every option in this question states a true percentage — the test is whether you answer the actual question, which asks for the lowest. Yellow completed the most points of the bottom two teams in raw terms (49 vs Green's 45), yet has the weakest percentage because its plan was larger. This distinction — raw numbers versus proportions — is one you will use constantly when comparing teams or sprints of different sizes. And a role note: if you presented this table at a programme review, the constructive framing is not "Yellow is the worst team" but "Yellow's plan may have been ambitious relative to its capacity — worth a conversation," which is how an associate delivery manager reports numbers without harming team dynamics.

### Preparation tips

- **Write every step down.** The worked answers above model this: one line per operation. On paper or the test's notepad, written steps catch slips that mental arithmetic hides.
- **Master the four core operations of delivery arithmetic:** percentage of a total, percentage change, averages (and when the median is fairer), and rate × time (day rates, velocity × sprints). They cover almost every question you will meet.
- **Practise with your team's real numbers.** Compute last sprint's completion percentage, the three-sprint average velocity, and the burndown's ideal line. Real data makes the methods stick.
- **Check the direction of every division.** "Completed over planned" and "change over original" — say the phrase before you divide.
- **Round up when forecasting whole sprints.** Part-sprints of remaining work still need a whole sprint on the calendar.
- **Rehearse with the calculator you will actually have.** If the test provides an on-screen calculator, practise with a basic one; if it does not, practise the ×10-and-adjust mental tricks shown above.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Answering the spent share when asked for the remaining share** (or the reverse). The two always sum to 100% — use that as a one-second check.
- **Dividing by the new value in percentage-change questions.** Divide by the original value, always.
- **Including excluded items in the denominator.** Read exactly which stories, days, or costs the question counts.
- **Forgetting conditions on discounts or rules.** Apply an adjustment only when its stated condition is genuinely met.
- **Trusting the mean when the data is skewed.** One 13-day story among 3-day stories makes the mean unrepresentative; consider the median.
- **Rounding too early.** Keep full precision through the steps and round only at the end; early rounding compounds into wrong options.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately you read, understand, and draw conclusions from written material — using the kinds of documents your role actually involves. For an associate delivery manager, that means extracts from the GOV.UK Service Manual, working agreements, stakeholder emails, meeting notes, supplier messages, and short reports. The test presents a passage, then asks questions about it.

The most common question style is "true / false / cannot say": given only the passage, is a statement definitely true, definitely false, or impossible to determine? Other styles include choosing the best summary of a passage, identifying what can properly be inferred, and judging which of several sentences communicates a point most clearly. The typical format is online and timed — usually 15 to 25 minutes for 15 to 30 questions — with the clock encouraging you to read efficiently but carefully.

Employers use verbal reasoning tests for delivery roles because so much of the job is reading and relaying. You read a policy note and tell the team what it means for their work. You read a supplier email and decide whether anything needs escalating. You read the Service Manual and help the team meet the Service Standard. Getting these readings right — not adding assumptions, not missing conditions, not over-claiming — is a professional skill, and the test measures it directly.

The single most important rule, worth learning before anything else: answer only from the passage. In verbal reasoning, "cannot say" is not a weak answer — it is the correct answer whenever the passage does not settle the question, even if your general knowledge or workplace experience suggests an answer. This is the same discipline you use when you pass on information at work: you report what was actually said, and you are honest about what you do not know. Practising that habit here will make you a more trustworthy communicator on the job.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Reading comprehension** maps to **Communicating between the technical and non-technical**: before you can translate between audiences, you must extract the exact meaning of what a developer, policy colleague, or supplier has written — no more and no less.
- **True/false/cannot-say discipline** maps to **Maintaining delivery momentum**: when you communicate risks, issues and dependencies, the difference between "the supplier said the API will be late" and "the supplier might be late" is the difference between a fact and a guess, and teams plan on what you relay.
- **Inference** maps to **Life cycle management**: Service Manual guidance often states principles rather than instructions, and explaining what each phase of the life cycle is for means drawing sound inferences about what the guidance implies for your team.
- **Clear-writing judgement** maps to your duty to be an advocate for the team externally and to support discussions within a multidisciplinary team: choosing the clearest sentence in a test mirrors choosing the clearest sentence in a stand-up summary or stakeholder update.
- **Careful reading of conditions** maps to **Commercial management** and **Making a process work**: contracts, processes, and working agreements are written in conditions ("if", "unless", "only when"), and applying them correctly starts with reading them correctly.

### Practice questions

Passage for Questions 1–4:

"Government services move through phases. In the discovery phase, the team learns about users and their needs; no service is built during discovery. In the alpha phase, the team builds prototypes to test hypotheses with users. A service moves from alpha to beta only after passing a service assessment. In the public beta phase, the service is available to real users while the team continues to improve it. Teams should not treat beta as a finished state: a service remains in beta until it meets the Service Standard criteria for live."

**Question 1 (easy) — True, false, or cannot say**

Statement: "During discovery, the team builds an early version of the service."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True only for large services

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage says directly: "no service is built during discovery." The statement claims the opposite, so it is definitely false. This is the simplest kind of verbal question — a direct contradiction — but it still rewards careful reading, because the passage does say the team builds things in alpha (prototypes), and a skim-reader can blur the phases together. Option D invents a distinction the passage never makes; when an option adds a condition that appears nowhere in the text, it is almost never right. In your role, keeping the phases straight is part of your **Life cycle management** skill: explaining what each phase is for starts with knowing that discovery is for learning, not building.

**Question 2 (easy) — True, false, or cannot say**

Statement: "A service must pass a service assessment before moving from alpha to beta."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) The passage does not mention assessments

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The passage states: "A service moves from alpha to beta only after passing a service assessment." The statement rephrases this accurately — "only after passing" means passing is required before the move, which is what "must pass before" says. So it is true. Option D is factually wrong about the passage. Notice the small but important act of translation here: the passage uses "only after," and the statement uses "must ... before." Verbal tests constantly rephrase, and your job is to check whether the rephrasing preserves the meaning. This is exactly what you do when you turn a formal document into a plain-English summary for your team: the words change, the meaning must not.

**Question 3 (moderate) — True, false, or cannot say**

Statement: "Most services spend at least six months in public beta."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) False, because beta is usually shorter

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Search the passage for anything about how long beta lasts. There is nothing: the passage says a service remains in beta until it meets the live criteria, but gives no durations and no information about "most services." You might have real-world knowledge or opinions about typical beta lengths — set them aside. The passage alone cannot confirm or deny the statement, so the answer is "cannot say." Option D is a trap for people who answer from experience rather than the text. This discipline has a direct workplace parallel: if a stakeholder asks "how long will beta take?" and your only source is this guidance, the honest answer is that the guidance does not say — it depends on meeting the criteria. Saying "cannot say" gracefully, in tests and in meetings, is a strength, not a weakness.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Best summary**

Which option best summarises the passage?

- A) Services should leave beta as quickly as possible.
- B) Services progress through learning, prototyping, and improving phases, with defined conditions for moving between them.
- C) The discovery phase is the most important phase.
- D) Real users only encounter a service once it is live.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A good summary covers the passage's main shape without adding or distorting. The passage describes a sequence of phases (discovery for learning, alpha for prototypes, beta for real use and improvement) and conditions for movement (assessment before beta; Service Standard criteria before live) — which is option B. Option A adds advice the passage never gives; if anything, the passage warns against rushing by saying beta is not a finished state. Option C invents a ranking of importance. Option D contradicts the passage, which says beta services are "available to real users." Summary questions mirror one of your most common real tasks: a colleague asks "what does that guidance actually say?" and you answer in one sentence. The best sentence is the one that is faithful, complete at the right level, and free of your own additions.

Passage for Questions 5–7:

An email from a supplier's account manager:

"Hi — quick update on the search component work. Our developer completed the build on Friday and it has passed our internal tests. However, we have not yet run the accessibility checks, which are scheduled for Wednesday. We would therefore recommend not deploying to your beta environment before Thursday. Separately, please note that our invoice for May remains unpaid; our finance team will apply a late payment charge if payment is not received within 10 working days of this email. Could you confirm receipt and let us know your intended deployment date?"

**Question 5 (easy) — Fact finding**

According to the email, which of the following has already happened?

- A) The accessibility checks
- B) The deployment to the beta environment
- C) The build and the supplier's internal tests
- D) The late payment charge

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Sort the email's contents by time. Completed: the build ("completed the build on Friday") and internal tests ("has passed our internal tests"). Scheduled but not done: accessibility checks (Wednesday). Recommended for the future: deployment (not before Thursday). Conditional and future: the late payment charge (only if payment is not received within 10 working days). Only option C names things that have actually happened. This sorting — done, planned, conditional — is precisely how you should read any supplier or stakeholder message before relaying it at stand-up. A crisp relay would be: "Build done and internally tested; accessibility checks Wednesday; they advise deploying Thursday at the earliest; and there is a finance action for us."

**Question 6 (moderate) — True, false, or cannot say**

Statement: "The supplier will apply a late payment charge."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, because the invoice is unpaid

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The email says the charge will be applied "if payment is not received within 10 working days." That is a conditional statement, and the email gives no information about whether payment will be made in time. If your department pays within the window, there is no charge; if not, there is. Since the passage cannot settle which will happen, the answer is "cannot say." Option D wrongly treats the current unpaid status as settling a future conditional — but the condition is about payment within the next 10 working days, not about the situation today. Conditionals are everywhere in commercial correspondence, and as a point of contact for contracted suppliers you need to relay them precisely: "there will be a charge" and "there will be a charge unless we pay within 10 working days" are very different messages to pass to your finance contacts, and only the second is true.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Inference and action**

Which of the following is the best-supported inference from the email?

- A) The supplier's work is behind schedule.
- B) Deploying to beta on Tuesday would go against the supplier's recommendation.
- C) The search component has failed accessibility checks.
- D) The invoice dispute means the component cannot be deployed.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Test each option against the text. A: the email describes progress and schedules but never compares them to a plan, so "behind schedule" is unsupported. B: the supplier recommends "not deploying ... before Thursday"; Tuesday is before Thursday, so a Tuesday deployment would indeed go against the recommendation — this follows directly. C: the accessibility checks have not run yet, so they cannot have been failed; this contradicts the text. D: the email treats the invoice as a separate matter ("Separately...") and never links payment to deployment. Inference questions reward the smallest step beyond the text that the text fully supports. The same care applies at work: inferring "they're behind schedule" from this email and saying so at stand-up would spread a claim the supplier never made — a small inaccuracy that can sour a commercial relationship.

Passage for Questions 8–10:

From a team working agreement:

"Stand-up is at 9:30 each working day and lasts no more than 15 minutes. Team members who cannot attend should post a written update in the team channel before 9:30. Discussions that need more than a brief exchange must be taken offline: the people involved arrange a follow-up conversation after stand-up. The board must be updated before stand-up, not during it. Visitors are welcome to observe stand-up but should not contribute unless invited by the facilitator."

**Question 8 (easy) — True, false, or cannot say**

Statement: "A team member who cannot attend stand-up should post a written update before 9:30."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, but only with the facilitator's permission

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** This is stated almost word for word: "Team members who cannot attend should post a written update in the team channel before 9:30." The statement matches, so it is true. Option D adds a permission requirement that appears nowhere — the agreement attaches the facilitator only to visitor contributions, a different rule entirely. When two rules live near each other in a document, tests (and tired readers) blend them; keep each rule attached to its own subject. Working agreements are a core artefact of your **Making a process work** skill — supporting teams in establishing a process includes being the person who can quote the agreement accurately when a question comes up.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Applying the rules**

During stand-up, two developers begin a detailed debate about database indexing. According to the working agreement, what should happen?

- A) The debate continues, because technical topics take priority.
- B) The facilitator invites a visitor to give an outside view.
- C) The people involved take the discussion offline and arrange a follow-up after stand-up.
- D) Stand-up is extended by 15 minutes to finish the debate.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The agreement says: "Discussions that need more than a brief exchange must be taken offline: the people involved arrange a follow-up conversation after stand-up." A detailed indexing debate is clearly more than a brief exchange, so option C applies the rule exactly. Option A invents a priority the agreement does not contain. Option B misapplies the visitor rule, which is about who may speak, not about resolving debates. Option D breaks the explicit 15-minute cap. In practice, this is one of the moments an associate delivery manager earns their keep: a friendly "sounds like a good one to take offline — can you two grab time after this?" keeps stand-up short while making sure the discussion still happens. The test verifies you can find the applicable rule; the job asks you to apply it kindly.

**Question 10 (moderate) — Clear-writing judgement**

You need to tell stakeholders that the team will not finish a promised feature this sprint. Which sentence communicates this most clearly and professionally?

- A) "Due to unforeseen circumstances impacting velocity, the deliverable timeline has experienced slippage."
- B) "We will not finish the payments feature this sprint. It will be completed next sprint, by 21 June. The delay is due to a dependency on test data that arrived late."
- C) "Unfortunately things have been crazy and the feature isn't done, sorry!!"
- D) "The feature is delayed. More information will follow at some point."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Judge each option against three tests: is it clear, is it specific, does it help the reader act? Option B passes all three — it says plainly what is not happening, gives a new concrete date, and explains the cause in one factual sentence. Option A hides the message in abstract jargon ("deliverable timeline has experienced slippage") and never names the feature or a date; this style erodes trust because readers sense they are being cushioned. Option C is honest but unprofessional and information-poor: no feature name, no new date, no cause. Option D is clear but unhelpfully vague about what comes next. Your role summary asks you to communicate effectively with technical and non-technical stakeholders and to be an advocate for the team externally — and the best advocacy when news is bad is calm, specific, plain English. Stakeholders forgive delays far more readily than they forgive fog.

**Question 11 (challenging) — True, false, or cannot say with a subtle condition**

Passage: "The department's spend approval process requires prior approval for any single purchase over £5,000. Purchases of £5,000 or less may be approved retrospectively by a budget holder. Software subscriptions are treated as a single purchase covering the full contract term."

Statement: "A £450-per-month software subscription with a 12-month term may be approved retrospectively."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, because £450 is under £5,000

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The trap is treating the subscription as twelve small purchases. The passage's final sentence closes that door: a subscription is "a single purchase covering the full contract term." So compute the single-purchase value: £450 × 12 = £5,400. Step by step: £450 × 12 = £450 × 10 + £450 × 2 = £4,500 + £900 = £5,400. That exceeds £5,000, so the purchase requires prior approval and may not be approved retrospectively — the statement is false. Option D is the trap made explicit: it applies the threshold to the monthly figure instead of the contract value. Notice how this question blends verbal and numeric care: the decisive move was noticing which sentence changes the unit of analysis. Real procurement rules work exactly like this, and your **Financial management** awareness — understanding how cost and budgets are created — includes reading thresholds against the right total.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Identifying the author's purpose**

Passage, from a delivery report: "Sprint 14 completed 27 of 32 planned points. The two carried-over stories both depend on the identity platform team, whose release was postponed for the second consecutive sprint. We have raised this dependency at the programme board and requested a committed date. If no date is received by 12 July, we will re-plan the quarter's roadmap to sequence non-dependent work first."

What is the primary purpose of this passage?

- A) To blame the identity platform team for missed work.
- B) To report progress, flag a recurring external dependency, and set out a clear conditional plan.
- C) To announce that the quarter's roadmap has been re-planned.
- D) To request more developers for the team.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Purpose questions ask what the text is doing, not just what it says. Walk through its moves: it reports results (27 of 32 points), identifies the cause of the shortfall factually (a postponed dependency, twice running), describes the escalation already taken (raised at programme board), and states a conditional next step with a date trigger (re-plan if no date by 12 July). That is option B. Option A misreads tone — the passage attributes cause without blame language, which is an important distinction you should carry into your own reports. Option C confuses a conditional future action with a completed one: the re-plan happens only "if no date is received." Option D has no basis in the text. This passage is also a model worth copying for your own updates: numbers, cause, action taken, and a conditional plan with a trigger date. Reports written like this are what "track, manage, escalate and communicate dependencies" looks like in prose.

### Preparation tips

- **Read the passage first, questions second — then re-read the relevant lines.** Skimming questions first tempts you to hunt for keywords and miss conditions like "only," "unless," and "before."
- **Practise "cannot say" until it feels comfortable.** Take any workplace document, write three statements about it — one true, one false, one undetermined — and check yourself. The undetermined ones teach you the most.
- **Circle condition words.** "If," "unless," "only after," "no more than," "within 10 working days" — these small words decide most answers. Train your eye to stop on them.
- **Answer from the passage, never from your experience.** Your workplace knowledge is an asset in the job and a liability in the test. Before choosing "true" or "false," find the sentence that proves it.
- **Read good models.** The GOV.UK Service Manual and well-written team reports are ideal practice material: accurate, plain, and full of the conditional structures that tests use.
- **When judging clear writing, apply three tests:** clear message, specific facts (names, dates, causes), and a next step for the reader. The option that passes all three wins.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Importing outside knowledge.** The most common verbal reasoning error at every level. If the passage does not say it, you cannot conclude it.
- **Blending neighbouring rules.** Keep each rule attached to its own subject — the facilitator rule was about visitors, not absentees.
- **Treating conditionals as certainties.** "Will charge if unpaid" is not "will charge." Relay conditions with their conditions attached.
- **Missing a sentence that changes the unit.** One line ("a subscription is a single purchase for the full term") can flip an answer. Read to the end.
- **Choosing summaries that add advice or judgement.** A summary states what the passage says; the moment an option adds "should," check whether the passage actually said it.
- **Confusing tone with content.** A politely worded email can contain hard deadlines and charges; a blunt one can be harmless. Judge the facts, not the mood.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement test (SJT) presents realistic workplace scenarios and asks you to judge possible responses. Unlike the other assessments in this guide, there is often no single mathematically provable answer; instead, responses are scored against what experienced practitioners and occupational psychologists agree is more or less effective behaviour for the role. Common formats include: choose the most effective response, choose the least effective response, pick the best and worst from four options, or rate each response on a scale.

The typical format is online, 20 to 30 minutes, with 10 to 20 scenarios. SJTs are often untimed or generously timed, because they measure judgement rather than speed. There are no trick questions: the scenarios are drawn from the real dilemmas of the role — in your case, the dilemmas of an associate delivery manager on a small Agile team, learning on the job and supporting a more experienced delivery manager.

Employers use SJTs for delivery roles because judgement is the heart of the job. Delivery management is not about following a script; it is about choosing well when values pull in different directions — transparency versus diplomacy, team autonomy versus intervention, handling something yourself versus escalating. At your level, the assessment also looks for a healthy relationship with guidance and mentorship: knowing what you can handle, what the team should own, and when to involve your delivery manager is a strength, never a weakness.

As you practise, look for the recurring principles behind good answers: focus on users and outcomes, be honest and transparent, respect and empower the team, address problems early and directly but kindly, escalate facts rather than blame, and include everyone. When two options both seem reasonable, the better one usually acts sooner, involves the right people, and preserves trust.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Team-focused scenarios** map to **Team dynamics and collaboration**: the test probes whether you understand team dynamics, value feedback, and support an environment where a team can deliver — including when dynamics get difficult.
- **Blocker and escalation scenarios** map to **Maintaining delivery momentum**: actively addressing risks and issues and knowing when to escalate them is a judgement call the SJT simulates repeatedly.
- **Stakeholder scenarios** map to **Communicating between the technical and non-technical**: managing differing stakeholder perspectives, hosting discussions with difficult dynamics, and advocating for the team externally all appear as dilemmas about what to say, to whom, and when.
- **Supplier scenarios** map to **Commercial management**: acting as the point of contact for contracted suppliers means judging when a supplier issue is routine and when it needs internal contacts or escalation.
- **Process scenarios** map to **Making a process work** and **Agile and Lean practices**: guiding teams to focus on outputs rather than process, and helping them find a process that works for them, requires judgement about when to intervene and when to let the team decide.
- **Learning scenarios** map to your role level itself: you learn on the job, potentially working alongside a delivery manager under guidance and mentorship — so the test rewards asking, admitting uncertainty, and using your mentor well.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — A mistake in your own work**

Yesterday you sent the weekly delivery update to stakeholders. This morning you realise you reported the wrong sprint completion figure: you wrote 90% but the true figure was 75%. Nobody has commented on it. What is the most effective response?

- A) Say nothing; the next update will quietly contain the right figure.
- B) Send a short correction to the same audience today, with the right figure, and tell your delivery manager what happened.
- C) Wait to see whether anyone notices, and correct it only if asked.
- D) Correct the figure only in the version stored on the team drive.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The principles at stake are honesty, transparency, and ownership. Option B corrects the record promptly with the same audience that received the error, and keeps your delivery manager informed — which is not confessing a crime, it is giving them accurate situational awareness, since they may have repeated your figure onwards. Options A, C, and D all leave stakeholders holding a wrong number for longer, and each risks the far more damaging moment when someone discovers the error and wonders what else was inaccurate. At your level, nobody expects error-free work; they expect errors to be surfaced fast. A calm correction ("Correction to yesterday's update: sprint completion was 75%, not 90% — apologies for the error") actually builds credibility, because it shows your reports are checked and honest. This is ownership behaviour: the mistake was yours, and so is the fix.

**Question 2 (easy) — A quiet team member**

In the last three retrospectives, you have noticed that one developer, who joined the team recently, has said almost nothing, while two other members do most of the talking. What is the most effective response?

- A) Do nothing; some people are just quiet, and participation is optional.
- B) In the next retro, put the new developer on the spot: "You've been quiet — what do you think?"
- C) Change the retro format to include silent writing time before discussion, and separately, privately check in with the new developer about how they are finding the team.
- D) Ask the two talkative members to stop talking so much, in front of the team.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The principle is inclusion — making it easy for everyone to contribute — combined with respect for individual difference. Option C changes the structure so quieter voices have a route in (silent writing before discussion is a well-known facilitation technique that levels the field between quick talkers and reflective thinkers) and pairs it with a private, low-pressure conversation to understand rather than assume. Option A abandons a new colleague who may have useful observations or unspoken concerns; noticing and acting on team dynamics is explicitly part of your role. Option B forces contribution publicly, which usually makes quiet people quieter and can feel humiliating. Option D publicly criticises engaged team members, punishing enthusiasm rather than redesigning the format. Notice the general SJT pattern: the strongest options change the system kindly and talk to individuals privately; the weakest options single people out in public.

**Question 3 (easy) — Asked something you do not know**

In a meeting with stakeholders, a senior manager asks you directly: "What is the team's plan if the identity platform dependency slips again?" You do not know the answer. What is the most effective response?

- A) Improvise a plausible-sounding plan so the team looks prepared.
- B) Say: "I don't want to guess — I'll check with the team and get you an answer by tomorrow," and then do exactly that.
- C) Say the question would be better directed to someone senior, and move on.
- D) Stay silent and hope someone else answers.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The principles are honesty and follow-through. Option B is honest about the limit of your knowledge, commits to a specific action and deadline, and — crucially — is completed. Nobody credible expects an associate delivery manager to know everything; they do expect reliable information handling. Option A is the most dangerous choice on the card: an improvised plan may be repeated upwards, become an expectation, and collide with reality later, damaging both you and the team. Option C deflects a question you are well placed to carry back to the team, and wastes the chance to be a useful conduit — being an advocate for the team externally includes taking questions to them. Option D avoids the moment but leaves the stakeholder unserved. "I'll find out and come back to you by [date]" is one of the most powerful sentences in delivery management at any level; at your level it is practically a superpower, provided you always come back.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Pressure to inflate a report**

A programme manager preparing a slide for their director asks you to describe your team's progress as "on track" in this week's report. Looking at the data, the team is behind: a key dependency has slipped and the current forecast shows the milestone landing two weeks late. The programme manager says, "It'll probably sort itself out — no need to worry the director." What is the most effective response?

- A) Agree; the programme manager is senior and owns the report.
- B) Refuse angrily and tell the team the programme manager tried to cook the report.
- C) Say you are not comfortable reporting "on track" against the current forecast, propose accurate wording with the mitigation actions underway, and tell your delivery manager about the conversation.
- D) Report "on track" but keep a private note that you disagreed.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The principles are honesty, transparency, and appropriate escalation. Option C does three things well: it holds the line on accurate reporting without hostility, it offers a constructive alternative (accurate status plus mitigations reads far better to a director than a surprise later), and it informs your delivery manager — the right move at your level when someone senior pressures you to misreport, because your delivery manager can support you and may need to address the pattern. Option A makes you complicit in misleading a director; when the milestone lands late anyway, the report trail shows your team said "on track," and the trust cost lands on your team. Option B protects the truth but poisons relationships and gossips about a senior colleague to the team — escalate facts through the right channel instead. Option D is quiet complicity; a private note protects no one and changes nothing. Honest reporting with mitigation is almost always the best-scoring pattern in SJT questions about status pressure, because it serves users, decision makers, and the team simultaneously.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Two seniors, two instructions**

The product owner asks you to arrange the sprint review for Thursday so a visiting policy team can attend. An hour later, the delivery manager you support messages: "Keep the review on Friday as usual — the team's rhythm matters." Both have good reasons; both expect you to act. What is the most effective response?

- A) Book Thursday; the product owner asked first.
- B) Book Friday; the delivery manager is your line of guidance.
- C) Tell each of them what the other asked for, and get the two of them (with you) to agree the date quickly, sharing what each option affects.
- D) Book both slots and decide on the day.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The principle is collaboration over silent obedience: when two reasonable instructions conflict, the effective move is to surface the conflict to the people who can resolve it, not to arbitrate it secretly yourself. Option C makes the disagreement visible cheaply and fast — often a two-line message ("PO would like Thursday so policy can attend; DM prefers Friday for team rhythm — can you two agree? Happy to set up either") resolves it within minutes, and it teaches both parties that you handle conflicting asks transparently. Options A and B each pick a side using an arbitrary rule (first-come, or hierarchy), leaving one senior colleague surprised and the underlying disagreement unresolved — and you carry the blame for a choice that was never yours to make alone. Option D wastes the team's calendar and defers rather than resolves. Managing differing stakeholder perspectives is named in your role summary, and this is its everyday form: not grand diplomacy, just prompt, even-handed transparency.

**Question 6 (moderate) — The struggling supplier contact**

You are the day-to-day contact for a small supplier building a reporting component. Their developer has missed two informal check-ins and their last update was vague. The formal deliverable is due in three weeks. Your delivery manager is busy with a service assessment this week. What is the most effective response?

- A) Wait until the deliverable date; judging progress before then is unfair.
- B) Email the supplier a firm warning that contract penalties will apply.
- C) Arrange a short call with the supplier this week to get a specific progress picture, ask what they need from your side, and give your delivery manager a brief heads-up note.
- D) Ask a developer on your team to secretly start rebuilding the component in-house, just in case.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The principles are early engagement, fairness, and keeping your mentor informed without dumping the problem on them. Option C treats the missed check-ins and vague update as a signal worth investigating now, while three weeks still allow recovery; it approaches the supplier as a partner first (asking what they need — the blocker may be on your side, such as missing access or data); and the heads-up note respects your delivery manager's bandwidth while ensuring no surprise later. Option A confuses fairness with passivity — vague updates are exactly when good contract management leans in, and waiting converts a recoverable slip into a crisis. Option B jumps to contractual threats you may not have authority to make (penalty decisions typically involve commercial colleagues — the "appropriate internal contacts" your role summary mentions) and damages the relationship on incomplete information. Option D wastes team capacity on duplicated secret work and signals mistrust. The escalation ladder at your level runs: engage directly, inform your delivery manager, and involve commercial specialists if the facts warrant — in that order.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Retro actions going stale**

You notice that the last two retrospectives produced action lists, but almost none of the actions have been done. The team's retros are pleasant and people enjoy them. What is the most effective response?

- A) Stop holding retrospectives, since the actions are not happening anyway.
- B) At the next retro, present the undone actions without blame, and ask the team how they want to make actions stick — perhaps fewer actions with named owners and a check at stand-up.
- C) Personally do all the outstanding actions yourself this week.
- D) Email the team a list of who has failed to do which action.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The principle is continuous improvement with team ownership. Retros that generate undone actions slowly teach a team that reflection is theatre — the fix is to make the follow-through visible and let the team redesign its own mechanism, which is exactly what option B does. The specific suggestions (fewer actions, named owners, a recurring visibility point) are proven, but note the phrasing: you ask the team how they want to fix it, because a process imposed by you is your process, and your role summary is explicit that you help the team find a process that works for them. Option A discards the practice instead of repairing it. Option C hides the problem and sets an unsustainable precedent — you become the team's action-completion service. Option D is public blame by email, the format most likely to create defensiveness and least likely to create change. Watch for this SJT pattern: options that make a problem visible to the team and hand the team the pen usually beat options where you either give up, do it all yourself, or name and shame.

**Question 8 (moderate) — An excluded voice in a technical discussion**

In sprint planning, the developers debate two technical approaches at speed, using heavy jargon. You notice the content designer — whose work the choice directly affects — has stopped following and looks disengaged. The discussion is productive and the developers are enjoying it. What is the most effective response?

- A) Let it run; interrupting productive technical discussion damages morale.
- B) Pause the discussion briefly: "Before we go further — this affects content. Could someone recap the two options in plain terms so we can get the whole picture?"
- C) After the meeting, apologise privately to the content designer.
- D) Ask the developers to email their decision to the content designer afterwards.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The principle is inclusion in real time, which is also good decision-making: the content designer holds information the developers need (how each option affects content and users), so their exclusion is not just unkind, it degrades the decision. Option B intervenes lightly and without blame — asking for a plain-terms recap frames the pause as improving the discussion, not policing it, and it models exactly the behaviour your role summary describes: supporting and hosting discussions within a multidisciplinary team, and communicating between the technical and non-technical. Option A protects momentum at the cost of a worse-informed decision and a colleague learning that meetings run past them. Option C treats a live, fixable problem as an after-the-fact apology; the decision will already have been made without their input. Option D makes the designer a recipient of a decision rather than a participant in it. Multidisciplinary teams are the foundation of government digital delivery, and the facilitator who notices who has fallen out of a conversation — and gently reopens the door — is doing the role's most valuable quiet work.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Overheard concern about burnout**

You overhear a developer telling another that they have worked the last three weekends to keep a story on track, and that they are exhausted but "don't want to let the team down." Sprint commitments have been met, and nobody else seems aware. What is the most effective response?

- A) Do nothing; commitments are being met and the developer chose to work weekends.
- B) Announce at stand-up that nobody is allowed to work weekends.
- C) Check in privately with the developer with care and without revealing surveillance ("How are you finding the workload lately?"), and raise the sustainability pattern with your delivery manager; look at whether the team's commitments match its real capacity.
- D) Publicly praise the developer's weekend work at the sprint review to recognise their dedication.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The principle is sustainable pace — named in your role summary as setting a cadence and tempo that is sustainable — plus care for individuals. The green sprint metrics are concealing their own cost: the plan is only being met through invisible overwork, which means the team's velocity data is quietly inflated and future planning will bake the overwork in. Option C acts on both levels: a private, gentle check-in that lets the developer speak without feeling watched, and a systemic look (with your delivery manager, since wellbeing patterns are squarely a thing to raise with your mentor) at whether commitments exceed capacity. Option A mistakes silence for consent; "don't want to let the team down" signals pressure, not free choice. Option B addresses the symptom with a blunt public rule that may embarrass the developer who was overheard. Option D is actively harmful: publicly celebrating weekend work tells the whole team that overwork is what recognition looks like, institutionalising the problem. SJTs often hide the best answer behind the least dramatic option: quiet care plus a systemic question.

**Question 10 (challenging) — A blocker with no owner**

A story is blocked because the team needs a firewall change. The network team says it needs a security sign-off; the security team says the request form must come from the network team; each is waiting for the other, and this has consumed four days. Your delivery manager is on leave until next week. What is the most effective response?

- A) Wait for your delivery manager to return; cross-team blockers are above your level.
- B) Get the two teams into one short call (or one shared thread), state the impact plainly, and ask them to agree who does what by when; if they cannot agree, escalate to your delivery manager's cover or the service owner with a factual summary.
- C) Tell the developers to make the firewall change without the sign-off, since the process is clearly broken.
- D) Raise a formal complaint about both teams to their heads of profession.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The principle is active blocker removal with legitimate means. This is a classic circular dependency — each side is behaving reasonably by its own rules, and no one owns the loop. The highest-leverage move is simply to collapse the loop by getting both parties into the same conversation, which is option B: state the impact ("four days blocked, sprint goal at risk"), ask for owner and date, and only escalate — factually, to the designated cover — if the loop survives contact. This is your role summary made concrete: actively address risks and issues, and know when to escalate them; a delivery manager being on leave does not pause the team's momentum, and there is almost always a cover arrangement. Option A costs another week for a problem you are equipped to at least attempt. Option C trades a process problem for a security breach — bypassing security controls in government is never the effective answer, however broken the process feels; the legitimate version of that instinct is challenging the process afterwards, which your **Making a process work** skill encourages. Option D leaps to the heaviest escalation before trying a conversation, spending relationship capital that a 20-minute call would have saved.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Credit taken for your work**

At a programme meeting, a colleague from another team presents a dependency-tracking spreadsheet you designed and describes it as "something we've put together," with no mention of you or your team. Several managers praise it. You feel stung. What is the most effective response?

- A) Interrupt the meeting to state that the spreadsheet is yours.
- B) Say nothing ever; credit does not matter in the civil service.
- C) Afterwards, speak to the colleague privately and directly ("I was surprised the tracker was presented without mentioning where it came from — can we make sure joint work is credited accurately in future?"), and mention the situation to your delivery manager if it recurs.
- D) Email the meeting's attendees a correction with version history attached as proof.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The principles are direct communication, proportionality, and protecting working relationships. Option C addresses the issue with the person who caused it, privately, promptly, and specifically — the combination most likely to change behaviour without creating a public conflict. It also sets a boundary for the future rather than relitigating the applause, and keeps your delivery manager in the loop only if a pattern emerges, which is proportionate escalation. Option A creates a public confrontation over attribution in a programme meeting; even when you are right, the room mostly remembers the awkwardness. Option D turns a repairable interpersonal slight into a written, distributed accusation — almost always an over-response as a first move. Option B is wrong in a subtler way: while grace about credit is often wise, systematically never addressing misattribution teaches colleagues it is cost-free, and accurate attribution genuinely matters for how work (and people) get recognised and developed. Note the SJT rhythm again: private and direct beats public and dramatic; proportionate beats maximal; and one incident earns a conversation, a pattern earns an escalation.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Choosing least effective: the slipping sprint goal**

Mid-sprint, it becomes clear the sprint goal will probably not be met: a story turned out much larger than estimated. The team is working well and morale is good. Which of the following is the LEAST effective response?

- A) Facilitate a quick conversation with the team and product owner about options: split the story, reduce scope, or accept the miss and learn from the estimate.
- B) Update the relevant stakeholders early with the honest forecast and what the team is doing about it.
- C) Say nothing outside the team and quietly encourage the team to work extra hours so the goal is met and nobody needs to know.
- D) Make sure the estimation surprise is raised at the retrospective so the team learns from it.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** This is a least-effective format — read carefully, because three options are good practice and you are hunting the harmful one. Option C combines two damaging behaviours: concealment (stakeholders plan on what they know; hiding a probable miss until it happens converts a manageable adjustment into a surprise) and unsustainable pressure (extra hours to protect appearances is precisely the overwork pattern from Question 9, now actively encouraged by you). Options A, B, and D form the healthy pattern in three parts: adapt the plan with the right people (A — the product owner owns scope trade-offs, and involving them respects that), communicate early and honestly (B — bad news ages badly; early bad news comes with options, late bad news comes with apologies), and learn (D — estimation misses are the retro's natural food). If you chose A, B, or D as "least effective," re-read the question stem: least-effective questions are where careless reading loses the most marks, in tests and in life. A sprint goal missed honestly, adjusted openly, and learned from is a normal, healthy sprint; a goal met through hidden overwork is a quiet failure dressed as success.

### Preparation tips

- **Learn the principles, not the answers.** User focus, honesty, early communication, team empowerment, inclusion, proportionate escalation, sustainable pace. Before choosing, ask: which option best serves these?
- **Use the "sooner, together, openly" heuristic.** When torn between two decent options, the better one usually acts sooner, involves the right people, and is more transparent.
- **Read the question stem twice.** Most-effective and least-effective questions look identical at a glance. Underline which one you are answering.
- **Practise the escalation ladder for your level:** handle it yourself → help the team handle it → inform or involve your delivery manager → involve specialists (commercial, security) or senior cover. The right rung is the lowest one that actually resolves the issue without hiding it.
- **Rehearse hard conversations in low-stakes moments.** The private, direct, specific conversation (Questions 2, 9, 11) is a learnable skill; practising its phrasing makes it available under pressure.
- **Reflect on your real week.** After the test-practice, pick one real situation from your week and ask what the four options would have been and which you chose. SJT skill and job skill are the same skill.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Choosing the dramatic option.** Public confrontations, formal complaints, and unilateral bans feel decisive but almost always score poorly. Calm, private, proportionate actions win.
- **Choosing the passive option.** "Wait and see" and "it will sort itself out" score badly when the scenario contains a live signal (a slipped date, a vague update, an exhausted colleague). Passivity is the quiet twin of drama.
- **Escalating everything — or nothing.** At your level, both extremes are penalised. Handle what you can; never hide what others need to know.
- **Protecting appearances over accuracy.** Any option that keeps a report, board, or stakeholder impression looking better than reality is a trap, however kindly it is framed.
- **Solving team problems by decree.** Options where you impose a fix on the team usually lose to options where you make the problem visible and facilitate the team's own fix.
- **Forgetting the "least effective" flip.** In least-effective questions, the three good options are the distractors. Slow down and re-read the stem.
