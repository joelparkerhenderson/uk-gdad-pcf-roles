## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive ability assessment measures the exact mental processes your role demands, using practical workplace scenarios rather than abstract puzzles. For a head of function, that means reasoning about systems of work rather than single artefacts: standards and their exception paths, assurance regimes and their incentives, workforce models and their dependencies, portfolios and their contradictions.

Typical format: an online, timed test lasting 15 to 30 minutes, with questions covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective against a norm group; most modern platforms adapt difficulty to your performance, so a test that feels progressively harder is usually going well. Employers typically receive speed and accuracy separately, and at senior levels they often weight accuracy: a head of function who is slowly right is far more valuable than one who is quickly wrong, because nobody downstream re-checks the head's reasoning.

For a head of business analysis, cognitive assessment is relevant in a distinctive way. Your function exists to bring structured thinking to the organisation's hardest problems, and you are its exemplar and final quality gate. You determine how complex programmes are represented across the organisation; you spot when two programmes' models, two directorates' plans, or a policy intent and a delivery design quietly contradict each other; you design the standards and governance through which dozens of analysts reason on your behalf; and you make resourcing and prioritisation decisions whose logic must survive challenge at board level. The skills under test — holding multiple structures in mind, deducing exactly what follows from constraints, detecting the inconsistency everyone else's review missed, ranking competing demands defensibly — are not adjacent to that work. They are that work, sampled in miniature.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The assessment dimensions map directly to the named skills in your role profile:

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **Business modelling** and **Enterprise and business architecture (business analyst)**: determining the scope and methodology for representing complex programmes across an organisation, and helping define capabilities and operating models, both mean recognising structural patterns — and structural contradictions — across many models, plans and directorates at once.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Context, problem and option analysis** and **Consultancy**: advising on options analysis, feasibility and impact assessment, and leading the definition of guidance for the whole organisation, require valid reasoning from constraints to consequences — with the discipline to state assumptions and deduce nothing more.
- **Error checking** maps to **Requirements definition and management** and **Testing (business analysis)**: directing the approach to requirements management and reviewing test and assurance reporting across a function means detecting the defect that survived everyone else's review — because after you, there is no one.
- **Prioritisation** maps to **Adapting to delivery methodologies** and **Stakeholder relationship management**: setting standards for how teams deliver, directing stakeholder strategy, and allocating your function's scarce capacity across an enterprise portfolio are all ranking problems under competing, legitimate pressures.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Business process improvement** and **Systems analysis**: leading the identification of large-scale improvement opportunities, and understanding how processes, systems and measurements interact across an organisation, both mean decomposing tangled enterprise problems into parts that can be reasoned about, evidenced and recombined into decisions.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern recognition across a standards library**

Your function's standards library contains four documents. Standard A: "Requirements informing decisions above £1m must be independently reviewed." Standard B: "All discovery outputs must be peer-reviewed within the producing team." Standard C: "Business cases above £5m must use the approved benefits model." Standard D: "All models informing decisions above £1m must be independently reviewed, except models produced under emergency protocols." A new analyst asks which single structural feature most distinguishes Standard B from the other three. What is the best answer?

- A) Standard B is the only one without a financial threshold — it applies to all discovery outputs regardless of decision value.
- B) Standard B is about discovery, and discovery matters less.
- C) Standard B is the shortest.
- D) Standard B is the only one that cannot be breached.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Standards A, C and D all scope their obligations by financial threshold (£1m, £5m, £1m); B alone applies universally to a class of artefact, and its review is internal rather than independent. Spotting that the library mixes two scoping philosophies — value-thresholded independent review versus universal peer review — is exactly the pattern a head of function must see, because mixed philosophies generate boundary confusion ("this discovery informs a £3m decision — which standard governs it?") and that confusion is where assurance fails. Option B smuggles in a value judgement no document states; C is trivially true and structurally irrelevant; D is false — any standard can be breached. When you lead the definition of guidance for an organisation, auditing your own library for structural consistency is the first act of quality assurance, and it is a pattern-recognition act.

**Question 2 (easy) — Logical deduction from a delegation scheme**

Your delegation scheme states: "The head of business analysis may approve methodology exemptions lasting up to one quarter. Exemptions exceeding one quarter require the digital director's approval." A programme requests a two-quarter exemption. The digital director is on leave for three weeks and the programme says the work cannot wait. Which conclusion must be true under the scheme as written?

- A) You may approve the two-quarter exemption because the director is unavailable.
- B) You may approve an exemption of up to one quarter now; the two-quarter request as such requires the director's approval, which the scheme does not transfer in her absence.
- C) The programme must halt until the director returns.
- D) The scheme permits you to approve the exemption if you inform the director afterwards.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The scheme gives you authority up to one quarter and reserves anything longer to the director; nothing in the text creates an urgency exception or a deputising rule, so A and D read permissions into silence — the classic delegation error that audit reports are made of. C overreaches in the other direction: the programme's work is not required to halt; the scheme constrains exemption approval, not delivery, and B's observation that you can lawfully approve one quarter now gives the programme a compliant path while the longer request awaits the director. The head-level habit this rehearses: when a governance document is silent, the answer is "the document does not provide for that", not "so presumably I can" — and the practical skill is finding the compliant move that keeps work flowing inside the authority you actually hold.

**Question 3 (easy) — Error checking a capability matrix**

A draft workforce paper includes this capability matrix: "Function total: 24 analysts. By level: 3 leads, 8 seniors, 10 analysts, 4 associates. By specialism: 12 service analysis, 7 data-focused analysis, 6 process analysis." What is wrong?

- A) Nothing — both breakdowns are internally consistent.
- B) The level breakdown sums to 25, not 24.
- C) The specialism breakdown sums to 25, not 24; one person is double-counted or the total is wrong.
- D) Both breakdowns sum incorrectly.

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Check each sum in writing, term by term. Levels: 3 + 8 = 11; 11 + 10 = 21; 21 + 4 = 25. Specialisms: 12 + 7 = 19; 19 + 6 = 25. Both breakdowns sum to 25 against the stated total of 24 — option D. The trap is stopping at the first discrepancy you find: a checker who verifies the level breakdown, finds 25, and immediately answers B has caught one error and missed the more informative pattern. Because *both* breakdowns independently reach 25, the most economical single explanation is that the stated total is wrong and headcount is actually 25 — one consistent story rather than two coincidental double-counts. That diagnosis changes the correction you make and the question you ask the paper's author. The head-level point: a workforce paper whose numbers disagree with themselves will be destroyed at the first board question, taking your funding request with it. Arithmetic self-consistency checks — do the parts sum to the whole, everywhere the whole is stated — are the cheapest assurance your function performs, and the most embarrassing to skip. Finish the systematic pass before you answer; in artefact review as in this question, the second finding is often the one that matters.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Prioritisation across a head of function's week**

Monday morning. Four demands compete:

1. Wednesday's executive committee will decide your function's budget for next year; your paper is drafted but its workforce numbers came from the flawed matrix in Question 3 and need re-verification.
2. A lead analyst has resigned; her programme director wants an urgent conversation about cover for a gate review in three weeks.
3. The cross-government heads-of-profession network meets Friday; you are due to present your capability framework, which colleagues in two departments plan to adopt.
4. An analyst has raised, via your open-door route, a concern that a supplier-run discovery is presenting fabricated user research quotes; the discovery reports to its programme board in two weeks.

Which sequencing is most defensible?

- A) 1, 4, 2, 3 — budget paper verification, then triage the fabrication concern, then cover planning, then the presentation
- B) 4, 1, 2, 3 — fabrication first because integrity issues always come first
- C) 1, 2, 3, 4 — the order the demands arrived
- D) 3, 1, 4, 2 — external reputation first

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Two items carry this-week consequence: the Wednesday budget decision (wrong numbers in an executive paper are a self-inflicted wound with a hard deadline) and the fabrication allegation (an integrity issue, but with a two-week runway to its decision point). The discriminating judgement is what "first" means for item 4: the correct Monday action is a same-day, short triage — hear the analyst, secure the evidence, and commission a discreet verification with the supplier's raw research materials — not the full investigation, which proceeds in parallel through the week. Option B's "integrity always first" would be right if the board were imminent or evidence at risk; with a two-week runway, launching the investigation before verifying your own executive paper trades a certain Wednesday failure against no gain in the fabrication timeline — A sequences the same-day triage of item 4 immediately after the paper is made safe. Item 2 is genuinely urgent to the director but is a planning conversation you can hold Tuesday or Wednesday; item 3 is prepared material needing rehearsal, not creation. C mistakes arrival order for priority; D puts external presentation ahead of both a budget decision and an integrity concern — reputation follows substance, not the reverse. Head-level prioritisation is rarely "which one matters"; it is designing the week so that every hard-deadline item gets its minimum effective action in time.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Deduction about an assurance regime's incentives**

Your assurance regime scores programmes red, amber or green on analysis quality. You observe: (i) programmes self-assess before your team's review; (ii) a programme whose self-assessment matches your review outcome faces no follow-up, while mismatches trigger a deep-dive; (iii) over the year, self-assessments have converged almost perfectly with your reviews — and both have drifted greener. Which conclusion does the evidence best support?

- A) Analysis quality has genuinely improved across the portfolio.
- B) The regime rewards agreement rather than accuracy: programmes minimise deep-dive risk by predicting your review rather than assessing themselves, and reviewers face no counterweight to grade inflation — so convergence-plus-greening is exactly what gaming would produce, and independent evidence is needed to distinguish it from genuine improvement.
- C) Your reviewers are colluding with programmes.
- D) Self-assessment should be abolished.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Trace the incentive structure. Mismatch triggers cost (deep-dive); match is free — so rational programmes optimise for predicting the reviewer, not for honest self-assessment. Meanwhile nothing in the described regime penalises drift toward green if both sides drift together. The observed pattern — convergence plus collective greening — is therefore consistent with gaming and cannot, by itself, evidence improvement; distinguishing the two requires evidence from outside the loop (defect escape rates, post-decision benefit realisation, spot deep-dives on matched greens). That is precisely B, and note what B does not claim: not that gaming *is* occurring (A's mirror error), merely that the regime cannot tell — deducing the limits of what a measurement system can establish is the head-level skill. C leaps to misconduct when structure explains everything; D throws away self-assessment's real value (it builds the reflective habit) instead of repairing the incentive — for instance by randomly deep-diving a sample of matches. Designing measurement regimes whose incentives survive contact with rational actors is the systems-analysis heart of running a function.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Pattern recognition in escaped defects**

Your function tracks "escaped defects" — analysis errors found after baselining. This year's 40 escapes classify as: ambiguous requirement wording, 8; missed stakeholder impact, 6; incorrect policy interpretation, 22; missing non-functional requirement, 4. Three years ago, policy interpretation escapes were 15% of the total; they are now 55%. During the same period your organisation's legislative programme has doubled and your two policy-specialist senior analysts have both been promoted into generalist lead roles. What is the strongest reading?

- A) Analysts have become careless with policy work and need reminding of the standard.
- B) The rise in policy-interpretation escapes tracks rising policy complexity and the loss of specialist capacity to promotion — a capability-supply problem the function's structure created; the response is structural: rebuild policy-analysis specialism (roles, training pathway, review support), not exhortation.
- C) The other categories have improved, which is what matters.
- D) The classification scheme is probably wrong.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Read the pattern against its context. One category has quadrupled in share while its known drivers moved in the same direction: demand for policy interpretation doubled (legislative programme) while specialist supply fell to zero (both specialists promoted out, their expertise diluted into generalist roles). Concentrated growth in one defect class, co-timed with a demand-supply shift in exactly that class, points to a structural capability gap — and structural gaps do not respond to reminders, which is why A fails: exhortation addresses motivation, and nothing here evidences a motivation problem. C is complacent arithmetic — the other categories' stability is real but the total is being driven by the one that matters. D is the evasion of last resort; the classification would need to have changed to explain the trend, and nothing suggests it did. The head-level lesson is double: defect data read against workforce data yields diagnoses neither yields alone, and promotion decisions are capability decisions — when you promoted both specialists, the function silently traded its policy depth, and building business analysis capability for the organisation means noticing such trades before the escape rate announces them.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Error checking a board paper's logic**

A paper you are quality-assuring for the investment board argues: "Our user research shows 78% of surveyed users want feature X. Feature X costs £400k. Our competitor department delivered a similar feature for £250k, proving our supplier is overcharging. Therefore we should insource the build." Which is the most serious logical defect?

- A) The 78% figure is from surveyed users, who may not represent all users.
- B) The conclusion (insource) does not follow from the premises: even granting the survey and the price gap, the £250k comparison at most supports renegotiating or re-procuring — the paper never assesses insourcing's own cost, capacity or feasibility, and "similar feature" elsewhere does not establish comparability.
- C) User wants are not user needs.
- D) The paper does not state the survey sample size.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Several defects are real — A, C and D all name genuine weaknesses — but the question asks for the most serious, and B identifies the argument's structural failure: the conclusion is unsupported by its own premises. Walk it through: premise one (users want X) supports building X somehow; premise two (competitor paid less) supports, at most, that a better price may exist — via negotiation or re-procurement — and even that rests on an unexamined comparability claim ("similar feature" hides scope, integration and quality differences). Insourcing is a third option that appears in the conclusion having never been assessed: no internal capacity check, no cost estimate, no delivery risk. This is the surviving-option fallacy's aggressive cousin — the conclusion-swap, where evidence against one option is treated as evidence for a specific alternative. Papers reaching investment boards fail this way constantly, and the head of function's review exists to catch exactly this: not the weak premise (reviewers at every level catch those) but the invalid inference from acceptable premises. When you advise on options analysis to ensure best practice, insist that every recommended option was itself appraised — not merely that its rivals were wounded.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Applied problem solving: designing a triage rule**

Demand for your function exceeds supply by roughly 40%. Requests arrive as: statutory or legally driven work (about 15% of demand), executive-sponsored strategic initiatives (25%), programme delivery support (45%), and speculative "come and help us think" requests (15%). You must publish a triage rule your leads can apply without you. Which rule is best designed?

- A) First come, first served — it is objective and cannot be accused of favouritism.
- B) Statutory work is always accepted; strategic and delivery work is accepted against published criteria (decision value, user impact, analytical leverage) with scores visible to requesters; speculative requests get a time-boxed one-day scoping conversation rather than an engagement; and the rule itself is reviewed quarterly against what was declined and what the declines cost.
- C) Executive-sponsored work is always accepted, since executives set organisational priority.
- D) All requests are accepted with effort scaled down proportionately — everyone gets 60% of what they asked for.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Evaluate each rule as a system under load. A is objective and blind — it spends scarce specialist capacity on whatever arrived first, decoupling allocation from value entirely; queues are fair only when items are interchangeable, and analysis requests are not. C converts sponsorship into priority, which teaches the organisation to escalate sponsorship rather than improve cases — and 25% of demand would crowd out the statutory 15% whenever executives outbid the law, which they must never be allowed to do by default. D is the peanut-butter fallacy: spreading 100% of capacity across 140% of demand under-resources everything, and analysis below a critical mass produces confident-looking work too shallow to be safe — the most dangerous output a function can ship. B is designed: a genuine bright line only where the law puts one; published criteria that make trade-offs visible and contestable (transparency is what actually defends against favouritism, not queuing); a cheap product (scoping day) for speculative demand, which preserves the option value of early conversations without open-ended commitment; and — the head-level signature — the rule audits itself, because a triage system's real quality is only visible in what it declines. Guiding tactical decision making with published, self-correcting rules is how you scale your judgement across a function.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Multi-constraint workforce planning**

You must place four new hires — two seniors (S1, S2) and two analysts (A1, A2) — into four teams (Casework, Payments, Data, Policy), one hire per team. Constraints: (i) Policy must receive a senior, given the policy-defect problem; (ii) A1, a former caseworker, cannot join Casework this year under the conflict-of-interest rule, but is the only candidate with payments-platform experience; (iii) S2 is the only candidate qualified to lead Data's regression testing work, which is a senior-level duty; (iv) each team gets exactly one hire. Which placement satisfies all constraints?

- A) S1→Policy, S2→Data, A1→Payments, A2→Casework
- B) S1→Data, S2→Policy, A1→Payments, A2→Casework
- C) S1→Policy, S2→Casework, A1→Payments, A2→Data
- D) S1→Payments, S2→Data, A1→Policy, A2→Casework

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Derive forced placements from the tightest constraints. From (iii), S2 must go to Data — the qualification is unique and the duty is senior-level, so no one else can cover it: eliminate B (S2→Policy) and C (S2→Casework). From (i), Policy needs a senior; with S2 fixed at Data, that senior must be S1: eliminate D (which sends A1, an analyst, to Policy). Check A fully: S1→Policy satisfies (i); S2→Data satisfies (iii); A1→Payments satisfies (ii) twice over — it avoids Casework and deploys the only payments-platform experience where it is needed; A2→Casework breaches nothing, as the conflict rule binds only A1. All four teams receive exactly one hire. The method — fix what is forced, then propagate — is the same discipline as the scheduling problems your programmes face, applied to the workforce decisions that are now your core work: placement puzzles with qualification, conflict and development constraints are a weekly reality for a head of function, and solving them explicitly (on paper, constraints listed) also produces the audit trail that makes placements defensible when someone asks why they didn't get the senior.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Deduction about a target operating model's arithmetic of accountability**

A proposed operating model states: (i) every programme will have a named analysis lead accountable for analysis quality; (ii) analysis leads must be at senior analyst level or above; (iii) no individual may be analysis lead for more than two programmes; (iv) the portfolio will run 11 programmes next year. Your function has 3 leads and 4 senior analysts; one senior analyst will be on parental leave for the full year. Which conclusion must be true?

- A) The model is workable as stated.
- B) Available senior-or-above headcount is 6, giving a maximum of 12 programme-lead slots against 11 needed — arithmetically feasible only if every available lead and senior takes the maximum two programmes with a single exception, leaving no slack for attrition, leave or the duties those roles otherwise perform.
- C) The model requires hiring at least two more seniors.
- D) Constraint (iii) should be relaxed to three programmes each.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Do the arithmetic of accountability. Eligible individuals: 3 leads + 4 seniors − 1 on leave = 6. Maximum slots under constraint (iii): 6 × 2 = 12. Required: 11. So the model is feasible in pure arithmetic — but only at 11/12 of absolute saturation: five people carrying two programmes each and one carrying one, with zero capacity for a resignation, a new programme, further leave, or the fact that leads and seniors have entire day jobs (coaching, assurance, community, their own analysis) that the model's arithmetic silently prices at zero. B states exactly this: feasible, and fragile to the point of planning malpractice. A ignores the fragility that the deduction exposes; C and D are recommendations, not deductions — each is a *possible* response to B's finding, but neither "must be true", and the question asks what must. The head-level pattern: operating models fail not at the arithmetic boundary but in the unmodelled load — and the person who computes 12-versus-11 *and then asks what the twelve slots assume about everything else those six people do* is doing the deduction the board needs. Helping define operating models that meet business objectives means stress-testing their headcount logic before agreeing to be accountable for it.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Prioritising the function's improvement portfolio**

You can fund two of four internal improvement initiatives this year:

1. A requirements-quality toolchain (automated traceability checking) — benefits your largest programmes, cost high, payback strong but dependent on adoption by teams already complaining of tool fatigue.
2. A policy-analysis capability programme (recruitment, training pathway, review support) — directly addresses your dominant escaped-defect category, benefits realised over 18–24 months.
3. A function-wide dashboard of analysis metrics for executive visibility — cheap, fast, popular with your director, measures mostly what is easy to count.
4. An early-engagement protocol with the policy directorate, embedding analysts in policy design before positions harden — cheap, addresses the *source* of the policy-defect category, requires the policy director's cooperation, which is probable but not certain.

Which pair is the strongest investment?

- A) 1 and 3 — tooling plus visibility
- B) 2 and 4 — attack the dominant defect category from both the supply side (capability) and the demand side (upstream engagement)
- C) 3 and 4 — the two cheap ones
- D) 1 and 2 — the two big ones

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Start from the evidence you hold (Question 6's pattern): policy interpretation is 55% of escaped defects and rising, driven by a demand surge and a specialist-supply collapse. Initiatives 2 and 4 attack that single dominant problem from both ends — 4 reduces the number of hard interpretations reaching analysts (upstream prevention, cheap, fast if the cooperation lands) while 2 rebuilds the capacity to handle those that still arrive (slow, durable). Paired, they hedge each other: if 4's cooperation falters, 2 still grows supply; while 2's 18-month ramp is covered by 4's earlier effect. Initiative 1 is real but aims at the *ambiguous wording* category — 20% of escapes and stable — and its adoption risk among tool-fatigued teams discounts its strong payback. Initiative 3 is the seductive distractor: cheap, fast, director-pleasing, and measuring what is easy rather than what is failing — a dashboard of the wrong numbers creates confident visibility of nothing, and "popular with your director" is a reason to explain your choice to the director, not a reason to build it. C picks by price; D picks by size; A picks by optics. B picks by evidence — allocating the function's improvement budget against its own defect data is business process improvement applied to yourself, which is the most credible advertisement a head of analysis can run.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Applied problem solving: contradictory strategic mandates**

Your organisation's digital strategy commits to "channel shift: 90% of transactions digital by 2028". Its customer strategy commits to "no user left behind: assisted routes maintained at current service levels throughout the strategy period". The finance settlement assumes £6m savings from contact-centre reduction starting next year — savings only achievable by reducing the assisted routes the customer strategy protects. All three documents are board-approved. A programme asks your function which mandate governs their business case. What is the strongest analytical response?

- A) Advise the programme to follow the most recently approved document.
- B) Advise the programme to follow the finance settlement, since funding constraints are the binding reality.
- C) Surface the three-way contradiction explicitly to the board level that owns all three documents — with the arithmetic that proves the conflict (the £6m assumes reductions the customer strategy forbids), scenario options showing what each resolution implies for the other two commitments, and an interim analytical position for the programme so it is not paralysed while the board resolves it.
- D) Advise the programme to draft its case ambiguously enough to be defensible under any of the three readings.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The contradiction is real, board-owned, and arithmetic: £6m of contact-centre savings cannot coexist with maintained assisted service levels unless something else gives (digital uptake beyond forecast, efficiency inside the assisted channel, or a smaller saving). No programme-level choice among A, B or D resolves it — A's recency rule and B's funding-first rule each silently repeal a board commitment without the board's knowledge, which is a decision above everyone's pay grade being made by default; D institutionalises the contradiction by making the business case unfalsifiable, producing exactly the ambiguous analysis your standards exist to prevent. C does the head-of-function job in full: it converts a felt tension into a demonstrated arithmetic conflict (contradictions survive as long as they stay qualitative — the proof is what forces resolution); it brings scenario options, because boards resolve trade-offs, not complaints; and it gives the programme an interim position (for example, casework the assisted-channel assumptions both ways with the divergence flagged) so delivery continues while governance catches up. Ensuring all streams of work align to the organisation's strategic context sometimes means being the person who proves the strategic context does not align with itself — and saying so where the owners of all three documents can hear it together.

### Preparation tips

- **Practise on systems, not artefacts.** Take a standard, an assurance process or a delegation scheme you own and stress-test it the way these questions do: where are its silences, what do its incentives reward, what happens at its boundaries? Ten minutes of this daily builds exactly the reasoning the test samples — and improves the instruments themselves.
- **Do the arithmetic of every plan you sign.** Headcounts, slots, sums that should equal totals, capacity against saturation. Head-level papers fail on self-consistency more often than on strategy, and the checking habit must be automatic before it can be fast.
- **Name assumptions and silences aloud.** For deduction questions, practise the sentence "the document does not provide for that". Distractors — and delegation disasters — are built from permissions inferred from silence.
- **Derive forced moves first in constraint problems.** List constraints, fix what is uniquely determined, propagate, then check candidates. Never test options by feel; the method is faster and it leaves an audit trail, in tests and in workforce decisions alike.
- **Interrogate measurement regimes.** For any metric or assurance pattern, ask: what behaviour does this reward, and what would gaming produce? If gaming and success look identical, the regime cannot distinguish them — and neither can the question's distractors.
- **Protect accuracy over speed.** At your level, employers weight being right. A steady, written, sanity-checked pace beats a brilliant blur — in the test and in the committee room.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Inferring authority from silence.** Governance documents grant what they grant. "It doesn't say I can't" is the pitfall; "it doesn't provide for that" is the discipline.
- **Reading convergence as improvement.** When self-assessments and reviews agree ever more warmly, check the incentive structure before celebrating. Agreement is cheap; accuracy is expensive.
- **Fixing structure with exhortation.** Defect trends driven by capability supply and demand do not respond to reminders. Match the intervention's type to the cause's type.
- **The conclusion-swap.** Evidence against one option is not evidence for a specific alternative. Insist the recommended option was itself appraised — in board papers and in test questions alike.
- **Feasible-but-saturated planning.** Arithmetic that "just fits" with zero slack is a finding, not a reassurance. State the fragility, not merely the fit.
- **The easy-to-count dashboard.** Measures chosen for availability rather than importance create confident visibility of nothing. In prioritisation questions, the cheap popular option that measures the wrong thing is a designed distractor.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures your ability to interpret, manipulate and draw sound conclusions from quantitative information of the kind your role genuinely handles. The arithmetic stays within percentages, ratios, averages, rates and projections; the difficulty lives in judgement — extracting the right figures, choosing the right operation under time pressure, and recognising precisely what a number does and does not establish.

Typical format: an online, timed test of 20 to 40 minutes with 15 to 25 questions anchored to tables, charts and short data-rich passages. Calculators and rough paper are normally permitted; many platforms adapt difficulty upward as you succeed; and employers usually receive accuracy and speed separately.

For a head of business analysis, the numbers are distinctive in two ways. First, they are function-scale: budgets, pay bills, day rates, utilisation, capability investment cases, benefits portfolios — you are now the owner of the kind of business case you once wrote for others. Second, and more importantly, they arrive pre-packaged: finance summaries, supplier reports, programme dashboards and workforce returns land on your desk already aggregated, already averaged, already framed — and your role is the last analytical checkpoint before they become decisions. The errors that survive to your level are structural rather than arithmetical: wrong denominators, unweighted averages, mix effects, annualisation games, survivorship, false comparability. Employers assess numeric reasoning at head level because the function's credibility rests on its leader being faster and more precise with a suspicious number than anyone in the room — including the person presenting it.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Budget and unit-cost arithmetic** maps to **Consultancy** and **Context, problem and option analysis**: leading the definition of guidance on how the organisation uses digital and data skills effectively includes making — and defending — the numeric case for your own function: pay bill, contractor mix, cost per engagement, benchmarking against alternatives.
- **Weighted averages, distributions and mix effects** map to **Business modelling** and **Systems analysis**: models supporting strategic planning must survive compositional questions — what the average conceals, what the mix shift manufactures — or they misdirect the decisions built on them.
- **Rates, capacity and workforce arithmetic** map to **Adapting to delivery methodologies** and **Enterprise and business architecture (business analyst)**: setting standards for how teams deliver, and shaping operating models, both require converting demand, headcount, utilisation and ramp-up assumptions into plans that hold.
- **Benefits and investment appraisal** map to **Business process improvement**: leading the identification of large-scale improvement opportunities means owning benefit-cost logic — phasing, ramp-up, decay, attribution — for a portfolio, including the improvement investments you make in your own function.
- **Reading packaged data critically** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management** and **Testing (business analysis)**: influencing senior stakeholders and reviewing assurance reporting both mean re-deriving, sometimes live in the meeting, the aggregates someone else prepared — because after your sign-off, nobody else checks.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Function budget shares**

Your function's annual budget is £2.4 million: £1.68 million on permanent staff pay, £480,000 on contractors, and the remainder on training, tools and travel. What percentage of the budget is the non-pay remainder?

- A) 8%
- B) 10%
- C) 12%
- D) 20%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Remainder = £2,400,000 − £1,680,000 − £480,000 = £240,000. Share = 240,000 ÷ 2,400,000 = 0.10 = 10%. A useful cross-check: permanent pay is 1,680 ÷ 2,400 = 70%, contractors 480 ÷ 2,400 = 20%, and 70% + 20% + 10% = 100%. Budget-share fluency matters because these three numbers — 70/20/10 — are the opening frame of every conversation you will have with finance about your function's shape, and because the contractor share (20%) is the figure most often challenged: knowing it, and its denominator, without looking down is the difference between commanding a budget discussion and surviving one.

**Question 2 (easy) — Cost of a vacancy gap**

A senior analyst post (total employment cost £68,000 per year) has been vacant for 4 months, covered by a contractor at £520 per day, who has worked 82 days in that period. How much more has the cover cost than the vacant post would have?

- A) £19,973
- B) £22,667
- C) £42,640
- D) £15,400

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Contractor cost = 82 × £520 = £42,640. The post's cost for 4 months = £68,000 × 4 ÷ 12 = £68,000 ÷ 3 = £22,667 (rounded). Premium = £42,640 − £22,667 = £19,973. Option C is the contractor total mistaken for the premium; B is the salary fraction mistaken for it; D has no valid derivation. The vacancy-premium calculation — contractor spend minus avoided salary, per post, per period — is the workhorse of workforce papers: it converts "we have vacancies" into "each month of this vacancy costs roughly £5,000 over plan", which is the form of words that unlocks recruitment decisions. Heads of function who can produce that number in the meeting, correctly based, get their requisitions approved; those who quote the gross contractor cost get corrected by finance in public.

**Question 3 (easy) — Utilisation rate**

Your function of 20 analysts recorded 3,540 billable-equivalent days last year against a theoretical maximum of 4,400 available days (after leave and bank holidays). The target utilisation is 78%. Did the function meet the target, and what was actual utilisation?

- A) No — 76.4%
- B) Yes — 80.5%
- C) Yes — 78.0% exactly
- D) No — 74.2%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Utilisation = 3,540 ÷ 4,400 = 0.8045… = 80.5% (one decimal place), which exceeds the 78% target: option B. Quick sense-check: 78% of 4,400 = 3,432 days, and 3,540 > 3,432, confirming the target was passed by about 108 days. Two head-level footnotes worth internalising with the arithmetic. First, always confirm what the denominator excludes — a "maximum" already net of leave produces a very different rate from a gross-days base, and utilisation claims are only comparable on a stated base. Second, over-target utilisation is not unambiguously good news: the 20% non-billable remainder funds training, community, standards work and slack for surge — the capability-building duties your role exists to protect — and a function run hot on utilisation is quietly liquidating them. Read the number, then read what the number is spending.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Weighted cost per engagement**

Last year your function completed 30 engagements: 18 small (averaging 20 analyst-days each), 9 medium (averaging 60 analyst-days), and 3 large (averaging 180 analyst-days). At a blended cost of £450 per analyst-day, what was the average cost per engagement across the portfolio?

- A) £13,500
- B) £21,600
- C) £39,000
- D) £27,000

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Total analyst-days = 18 × 20 + 9 × 60 + 3 × 180 = 360 + 540 + 540 = 1,440 days. Total cost = 1,440 × £450 = £648,000. Average per engagement = £648,000 ÷ 30 = £21,600. Option A prices the unweighted mean of the three size-averages incorrectly; D is the simple mean of the three engagement sizes (20+60+180)÷3 = 86.7 days × £450 = £39,000 — which is option C, another trap: the unweighted mean of sizes ignores that small engagements are six times as numerous as large ones. The structural insight the weighting reveals: the 3 large engagements (10% of engagements) consumed 540 of 1,440 days — 37.5% of capacity — and the 18 small ones consumed 25%. When your director asks "what does an engagement cost?", the honest answer is a distribution, not a number; but if one number must serve, it is the weighted £21,600, and you should volunteer the concentration finding with it, because portfolio-shaping decisions (should we do fewer, larger engagements?) live in exactly that skew.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Benchmarking with unlike bases**

A benchmarking report states: "Department X's analysis function costs £96,000 per analyst per year; yours costs £120,000 — you are 25% more expensive." Investigation shows X's figure covers salary and employer costs only, while yours additionally includes training, tools, travel and a share of corporate overheads totalling £21,000 per analyst. What is the like-for-like comparison?

- A) You cost £99,000 to X's £96,000 on X's basis — about 3.1% more, not 25%.
- B) The report is right: 25% more expensive.
- C) You are cheaper than X by £3,000.
- D) No comparison is possible.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Strip the elements X's figure excludes: £120,000 − £21,000 = £99,000 on the salary-plus-employer-costs basis. Like-for-like gap = £99,000 − £96,000 = £3,000; relative = 3,000 ÷ 96,000 = 3.125% ≈ 3.1%. So the headline "25% more expensive" is almost entirely a basis mismatch — unless X's analysts genuinely receive no training, tools or travel, in which case the *un*-comparable £21,000 is buying capability X's figure hides the absence of. Option B accepts the mismatched bases; C subtracts in the wrong direction; D overcorrects — comparison is possible once bases align, and "no comparison is possible" is the refuge of people who dislike the comparison. Benchmarks arrive at head level pre-framed, usually by someone with a conclusion in mind; the reflex this question drills — *what does each figure include?* before *which is bigger?* — is the single highest-value numeric habit for defending your function's cost base honestly.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Capability investment payback**

Your policy-analysis capability programme costs £180,000 in year 1 (recruitment, training, backfill). Escaped policy-interpretation defects currently cost the organisation £600,000 per year (20 defects at an average remediation cost of £30,000 each — rework, delay, re-decision). The programme is forecast to reduce annual defect costs by 30% in year 1 and by 60% from year 2 onward. Ignoring other benefits and costs, what is the programme's net position by the end of year 2?

- A) £540,000 net benefit
- B) £360,000 net benefit
- C) £180,000 net benefit
- D) £0 — break-even

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Work year by year from the baseline. Annual defect cost = 20 × £30,000 = £600,000. Year 1 saving at 30%: £600,000 × 0.30 = £180,000. Year 2 saving at 60%: £600,000 × 0.60 = £360,000. Total saving over two years = £180,000 + £360,000 = £540,000. Net of the £180,000 programme cost: £540,000 − £180,000 = £360,000 net benefit. Each distractor encodes a specific omission: A forgets the programme cost entirely; C counts only year 2's saving against the cost (£360,000 − £180,000), dropping year 1's contribution; D notices that year 1's saving exactly offsets the cost and stops there, ignoring year 2. Note also the pleasing structure worth quoting in the real paper: the programme pays for itself within year 1 (saving equals cost), and everything thereafter is return. This is the defect-cost arithmetic that converts a capability programme from a "nice to have" into an investment case — quantify the baseline cost of the problem, phase the reduction honestly, and net off the spend. Making that case for your own function's development is the numeric core of building business analysis capability for the organisation.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Demand forecast for workforce planning**

Analysis demand has grown from 1,150 analyst-weeks (two years ago) to 1,265 (last year) to 1,392 (this year). If the growth rate continues, what is a sound forecast for next year, and roughly how many additional analysts does it imply at 44 productive weeks per analyst?

- A) About 1,460 analyst-weeks; 1 additional analyst
- B) About 1,531 analyst-weeks; 3 additional analysts
- C) About 1,620 analyst-weeks; 5 additional analysts
- D) About 1,392 analyst-weeks; 0 additional analysts — forecasting growth is speculative

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Establish the growth pattern: 1,265 ÷ 1,150 = 1.10; 1,392 ÷ 1,265 = 1.10 — consistent 10% annual growth, so the sound continuation is multiplicative: 1,392 × 1.10 = 1,531.2 ≈ 1,531 analyst-weeks. Increment = 1,531 − 1,392 = 139 analyst-weeks; at 44 weeks per analyst, 139 ÷ 44 = 3.16 ≈ 3 additional analysts. Option A applies linear growth of about +70 (halving the observed increments for no reason); C compounds at roughly 16%; D refuses to forecast at all — and "no forecast" is itself a forecast of zero growth, the one rate the data most clearly rules out. Two disciplines to carry from the arithmetic: verify the growth is genuinely proportional before compounding it (two consistent ratios justify a working assumption, and you should say "if the 10% trend holds" in the paper), and convert capacity to people explicitly, showing the 44-week divisor — because the difference between "we need 139 analyst-weeks" and "we need 3 more analysts, recruited by Q2 to be productive by Q4" is the difference between a statistic and a workforce plan.

**Question 8 (moderate) — The contractor conversion case**

Six contractors each cost £520/day and work 210 days per year. Converting all six roles to permanent posts at £72,000 total employment cost each would incur a one-off conversion cost (recruitment, onboarding, knowledge transfer) of £90,000, and permanent recruitment would realistically fill only 4 of the 6 posts in year 1, with the remaining 2 covered by contractors for the full year. What is the year 1 net saving (or cost) of the conversion programme?

- A) £223,200 saving
- B) £58,800 saving
- C) £58,800 cost
- D) £148,800 saving

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Build both scenarios in full. Baseline (no conversion): each contractor costs 210 × £520 = £109,200 per year, so 6 × £109,200 = £655,200. Conversion scenario, year 1: 4 permanent posts = 4 × £72,000 = £288,000; the 2 unfilled posts remain on contractors = 2 × £109,200 = £218,400; one-off conversion cost = £90,000. Scenario total = £288,000 + £218,400 + £90,000 = £596,400. Year 1 net saving = £655,200 − £596,400 = £58,800. Cross-check by increments: each converted post saves £109,200 − £72,000 = £37,200; four conversions save 4 × £37,200 = £148,800 gross; minus the £90,000 one-off = £58,800 net ✓. The distractors are the standard conversion-case errors: D is the gross saving with the one-off cost forgotten; C is a sign slip that would have you arguing against your own case; and A — the important one — is the *steady-state* annual saving (6 × £37,200 = £223,200) presented as if it arrived in year 1. Conversion cases are won in year 2: honest papers show year 1 thin (ramp-limited recruitment, one-off costs, residual contractor cover) and year 2 strong, rather than claiming steady state immediately — and finance committees, who see the optimistic version weekly, fund the honest one.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Portfolio benefits attribution**

Three programmes claim benefits from the same process improvement your function designed: Programme P claims £400,000, Programme Q claims £350,000, Programme R claims £250,000 — total claimed £1,000,000. Finance's bottom-up measurement of the actual improvement finds £640,000 of realised benefit. The three programmes' claims were based on the same baseline and their scopes do not overlap in transactions, but each programme applied its own uplift assumptions. If realised benefit is allocated pro-rata to claims, what does each programme book, and what is the more important head-level observation?

- A) P: £256,000; Q: £224,000; R: £160,000 — and the claims exceeded reality by 56%, meaning the uplift assumptions systematically inflated, which needs a common benefits standard more than it needs an allocation formula.
- B) P: £400,000; Q: £240,000; R: £0 — first come, first served.
- C) Each books £213,333 — equal shares.
- D) P: £256,000; Q: £224,000; R: £160,000 — and the allocation formula is the important thing.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Pro-rata arithmetic first: realisation ratio = 640,000 ÷ 1,000,000 = 0.64. P: £400,000 × 0.64 = £256,000; Q: £350,000 × 0.64 = £224,000; R: £250,000 × 0.64 = £160,000 (check: 256 + 224 + 160 = £640,000 ✓). Options A and D share the correct arithmetic and differ only in judgement — which is the question's real test. The allocation formula is administrative; the signal is that three non-overlapping claims on a common baseline totalled 156% of measured reality, meaning every programme's independent uplift assumptions inflated in the same direction. That is not three errors; it is one missing control — no common benefits-modelling standard, no independent challenge of uplift assumptions before claims are booked. The head-of-function response is to fix the system: a standard benefits model (your Business process improvement skill's "methods, tools and standards" clause), assured assumptions, and claims reconciled to measurement as routine. B invents a queue no principle supports; C ignores the claims entirely. The habit to build: whenever an allocation question arrives, answer it — then ask what the need for allocation reveals about the controls upstream.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Survey evidence for a board paper**

Your annual stakeholder survey of the function reports: "87% of stakeholders rate the function good or excellent (up from 79%)." Details: surveys sent to 240 stakeholders; 98 responded (41% response rate); last year, 180 were sent and 122 responded (68%). Response is voluntary; this year's survey followed a well-publicised success on a flagship programme. Which statement should govern how you use the headline in your board paper?

- A) Report the improvement as evidence of rising function performance.
- B) The 87% describes this year's 98 self-selected respondents; with response down from 68% to 41% and a salient recent success likely influencing both who responded and how, the year-on-year comparison is not like-for-like — report the figure with response rates alongside, note the non-response risk, and avoid claiming a measured improvement.
- C) Suppress the figure entirely as statistically meaningless.
- D) Weight the responses to correct for non-response and report the adjusted figure as definitive.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Compute what changed beneath the headline: respondents fell from 122 to 98 while the population grew from 180 to 240 — so the visible sample is a shrinking, self-selecting fraction (41%) of a changing population, surveyed in the afterglow of a publicised success. The 8-point rise could be genuine improvement, response-composition shift, salience effect, or any mixture; the data cannot separate them. B handles this the way your own analysis standards would demand of any programme: report the number with its base and response rate, name the non-response risk, claim only what is supported. A is the flattering over-claim — and a head of analysis whose own board reporting violates the function's evidential standards has spent the credibility that enforces them everywhere else; your papers are read as exemplars, which doubles the cost of every shortcut. C overcorrects: a 41%-response survey is weak evidence, not no evidence, and suppressing inconvenient caveated data is its own integrity failure. D promises more than the method delivers — non-response weighting requires knowing how non-respondents differ, which is exactly what is unknown. The meta-lesson is the head-level one: you are the person who must apply the function's skepticism to the function's own good news.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Mix effect in function performance**

Your function reports average engagement duration as a health metric. Year 1: 40 engagements averaging 45 days. Year 2: 50 engagements averaging 39 days — celebrated as "13% faster". Segmented, year 2 comprises 35 standard engagements averaging 30 days (year 1: 28 standard at 32 days) and 15 complex engagements averaging 60 days (year 1: 12 complex at 75 days). What does the segmentation show?

- A) The celebration is legitimate: both segments genuinely improved — standard engagements by 6.3% (32→30 days) and complex by 20% (75→60 days) — and with the complex share of the portfolio unchanged at 30%, the aggregate's 13% gain reflects real segment-level improvement rather than mix shift, with the complex segment's 20% the standout result.
- B) The improvement is entirely mix shift; both segments actually slowed.
- C) The aggregate and the segments tell the same story, so segmentation added nothing.
- D) Complex engagements should be excluded from the metric.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Verify each piece. Year 1 aggregate: (28 × 32 + 12 × 75) ÷ 40 = (896 + 900) ÷ 40 = 1,796 ÷ 40 = 44.9 ≈ 45 ✓. Year 2: (35 × 30 + 15 × 60) ÷ 50 = (1,050 + 900) ÷ 50 = 1,950 ÷ 50 = 39 ✓. Segments: standard 32 → 30 days (−6.3%); complex 75 → 60 days (−20%). Now check the mix: complex share was 12 ÷ 40 = 30% in year 1 and 15 ÷ 50 = 30% in year 2 — unchanged. So the aggregate's 13% gain is a clean reflection of genuine segment improvements, weighted stably: both segments improved, the mix held steady, and the aggregate faithfully summarises real gains — with the complex segment's 20% improvement the standout. This question inverts the usual Simpson's-paradox trap: having learned that aggregates can lie, the sophisticated error is assuming they always do (option B is the distractor for readers pattern-matching on paradox). Segmentation's value is symmetric — it can vindicate an aggregate as well as indict one, and C is wrong because you only know the aggregate is trustworthy *because* you segmented. D discards your hardest work from measurement, creating the incentive to avoid complex engagements. Verify, segment, then conclude — in that order, every time.

**Question 12 (challenging) — The savings target arithmetic**

A spending review requires your function to model a 15% budget reduction on its £2.4m base (£360,000) and present options. Fixed commitments: £140,000 of the budget is contractually committed tooling and licences (reducible only at contract break in 18 months); the training budget is £120,000; travel £40,000; contractor spend £480,000; the rest is permanent pay (£1.62m, averaging £67,500 per post across 24 posts). Eliminating all training and travel would save £160,000. Which options honestly reach £360,000 in-year, and what should the paper say?

- A) Cut training and travel entirely (£160,000) and cut contractor spend by £200,000 (42% of it) — total £360,000 — presenting the delivery impact of losing roughly 385 contractor days and all capability development.
- B) Cut tooling (£140,000), training (£120,000) and travel (£40,000), plus £60,000 of contractor spend.
- C) Declare the target unachievable and refuse to model it.
- D) Reduce permanent headcount by 5.3 posts to save £360,000.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Test each option's arithmetic against the constraints. B founders immediately: the £140,000 tooling is contractually committed for 18 months, so it cannot contribute in-year — B's arithmetic reaches £360,000 only by counting money that cannot move. D's arithmetic works in a spreadsheet (£360,000 ÷ £67,500 = 5.33 posts) but not in a year: redundancy consultation, notice periods and exit costs mean in-year savings from headcount reduction are far below the annualised figure — and fractional posts signal a paper that never met an HR process. C refuses the commission — heads of function model unpalatable options; refusing to model is not the same as recommending against, and it forfeits your seat in the decision. A is the honest reachable combination: training and travel (£160,000, genuinely stoppable) plus £200,000 of the £480,000 contractor spend — and A's decisive feature is that it *prices the consequences*: £200,000 ÷ £520 ≈ 385 lost contractor days, and the complete loss of capability development, stated in the paper as delivery and capability impacts for the board to weigh. The head-level standard for savings papers: every option's arithmetic must be achievable within the year and within the contracts, and every option must carry its consequences on its face. An option that reaches the number by cutting what cannot be cut is not an option; it is a future correction wearing a paper's clothes — and your recommendation can still be that the £360,000 does lasting harm, argued from A's own consequence pricing.

### Preparation tips

- **Drill the workforce arithmetic set.** Vacancy premiums, conversion cases, utilisation bases, weeks-to-people conversions — these five or six calculation patterns cover most head-of-function numeric work. Practise each until the structure (baseline, scenario, delta, phasing) is automatic.
- **Align bases before comparing anything.** Benchmarks, utilisation rates and unit costs are only comparable on identical inclusions. Make "what does each figure include?" your first question, in the test and in every meeting.
- **Verify, segment, then conclude.** Recompute presented aggregates from their components, break them by segment, and only then interpret. Segmentation both catches lying averages and vindicates honest ones — you cannot know which until you look.
- **Phase everything through time.** In-year versus annualised, ramp-up, contract breaks, recruitment lag: most savings and benefits errors are timeline errors. Draw the quarters before trusting any total.
- **Ask who is missing from every sample.** Response rates, survivorship, self-selection — packaged evidence at your level is pre-filtered, and the filter is usually the finding.
- **When your figure and theirs disagree slightly, trace, don't assume.** Small discrepancies usually mean a hidden rounding or basis choice. The question "can we confirm the baseline?" is both the correct analysis and the correct politics.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Quoting gross where net is the question.** Contractor costs against vacancy savings, savings targets against committed spend — the head-level errors are netting errors more often than multiplication errors.
- **Averaging engagement sizes instead of weighting by count.** Portfolio averages must be built from totals. The unweighted mean of small/medium/large is a designed distractor — and a common real defect in function reporting.
- **Accepting a benchmark's frame.** The 25% cost gap that becomes 3% on aligned bases is the standard shape of hostile benchmarking. Re-base first, respond second.
- **Claiming steady state in year 1.** Conversion cases, capability programmes and process improvements all ramp. The steady-state figure presented as the first-year figure is the most common flattering error in business cases — including cases about your own function.
- **Pattern-matching on paradox.** Having learned that aggregates mislead, do not assume every aggregate misleads. Segment to find out; conclude from the segments.
- **Applying weaker evidential standards to your own good news.** The survey uplift, the utilisation win, the celebrated speed improvement — test your function's numbers exactly as hard as a supplier's. Everyone notices when you don't.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately you extract meaning from written material of the kind your role handles daily — spending review guidance, machinery-of-government announcements, cross-departmental agreements, professional standards, ministerial correspondence, consultation papers — and how soundly you reason from what a text actually says rather than what your experience predicts it should say.

Typical format: an online, timed test of 15 to 25 minutes. Each short passage carries a cluster of questions in familiar styles: True / False / Cannot say (is the statement guaranteed, contradicted, or unaddressed by the passage alone?); best-supported inference; and clear-writing judgement (which version most faithfully and clearly conveys the meaning?). The governing rule is absolute: answer from the passage alone. This rule is hardest for the most senior candidates, because seniority supplies confident context the passage may deliberately not share — and the test, like a select committee, cares only about what the document in front of you says.

At head-of-function level, verbal precision is an instrument of governance. The standards you author bind analysts you will never meet; a "must" that should have been "should" generates a hundred unnecessary escalations, and the reverse generates a hundred silent exceptions. The agreements you negotiate — with other departments, professions and suppliers — will be read against you in their least generous construction at the worst possible moment. Your briefings to executives are quoted onward without your caveats unless the caveats are structurally impossible to detach. And your reading of what a minister's office, a spending review letter or a consultation response *actually commits* determines what your organisation builds. Employers assess verbal reasoning at this level because your misreadings become organisational positions, and your drafting habits become the profession's culture.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Precise comprehension** maps to **Enterprise and business architecture (business analyst)** and **Requirements definition and management**: translating business drivers, goals and constraints into objectives starts with reading strategies, settlements and mandates exactly — including their qualifiers, defined terms, silences and scope boundaries.
- **True/False/Cannot-say discipline** maps to **Context, problem and option analysis**: separating what evidence states from what it suggests — and holding "not established" as a finding — is the verbal form of the options and impact rigour you require of every analysis your function signs.
- **Inference judgement** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management** and **Consultancy**: reading senior correspondence for its commitments, hedges and deliberate silences — without converting your informed guess into their stated position — is the daily craft of directing stakeholder strategy at executive level.
- **Clear-writing judgement** maps to your standards-authorship and **Business modelling** duties: guidance you lead the definition of, and models supporting strategic planning, must be unambiguous to readers who cannot ask you what you meant — and reviewing others' drafting to a teachable standard is how capability scales.
- **Reading binding documents** maps to **Adapting to delivery methodologies** and **Testing (business analysis)**: methodology standards, assurance frameworks and acceptance regimes bind exactly what their words bind; finding the gap between assumed and actual obligation — before a dispute finds it — is a recurring head-level task.

### Practice questions

For questions 1 to 5, use this passage.

> **Extract from a spending review settlement letter to the department**
>
> The settlement provides £42 million over three years for the digital transformation portfolio, of which no more than £6 million may be spent on external consultancy and contractor resource. Release of funding beyond year one is conditional on the portfolio achieving the milestones set out in Annex B and on the department demonstrating, at the year-one review point, a credible plan to grow internal digital and data capability. The Treasury notes the department's intention to expand its business analysis and architecture functions and expects this to be reflected in the capability plan. Funding may not be transferred between the transformation portfolio and other departmental programmes without prior Treasury consent. Nothing in this letter constitutes approval of individual programme business cases, which remain subject to existing approval processes.

**Question 1 (easy) — True / False / Cannot say**

Statement: "The department may spend £8 million of the settlement on contractors if consultancy spend is zero."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The cap is expressed as a single combined limit: "no more than £6 million may be spent on external consultancy and contractor resource". The two categories share one ceiling, so £8 million on contractors breaches it regardless of consultancy spend being zero — the statement is contradicted by the letter, hence False. The distractor logic invites you to read "consultancy and contractor" as two separate allowances, or to assume unspent consultancy headroom transfers to contractors beyond the cap. Combined-versus-separate limits is among the most consequential small readings in settlement letters — a head of function planning workforce mix against this letter needs the combined reading on day one, because a contractor-heavy growth plan that assumed separate caps would fail at its first Treasury checkpoint.

**Question 2 (easy) — True / False / Cannot say**

Statement: "If the portfolio misses an Annex B milestone, year-two funding will be withheld."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The letter makes milestone achievement (plus a credible capability plan) a *condition* of releasing later-year funding — but it does not state what happens when a condition is missed. Automatic withholding is one possibility; others include partial release, renegotiated milestones, or release with enhanced conditions — settlement practice knows all four, and the letter chooses none. So the statement is neither guaranteed nor contradicted: Cannot say. The distinction being tested — a condition exists versus a specified consequence follows — is the same one that separates "exit requires Schedule 4 compliance" from "non-compliance invalidates notice" in contract reading. Heads of function brief executives on letters like this within hours of receipt; the accurate briefing says "release is conditional; the consequence of a miss is not specified and would be negotiated", and that second clause is where your negotiating room lives.

**Question 3 (moderate) — True / False / Cannot say**

Statement: "The Treasury has approved the expansion of the department's business analysis function."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Read the sentence's actual verbs: the Treasury "notes the department's intention" to expand the functions and "expects this to be reflected in the capability plan". Noting an intention and expecting its reflection in a plan is not approval — and the letter's final sentence removes any doubt about the letter's approving force generally: "Nothing in this letter constitutes approval of individual programme business cases". The statement attributes to the Treasury an act (approval of the expansion) the letter's language deliberately withholds, so it is False rather than Cannot say — the letter speaks to exactly this subject and speaks short of approval. The head-level stakes are concrete: recruiting against "the Treasury approved our expansion" when the truth is "the Treasury expects our expansion to appear in a plan it will review" is how functions grow ahead of their authority and shrink in public. Noted is not approved; expected is not funded.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Inference**

Which of the following is best supported by the passage?

- A) The Treasury regards growing internal capability as a means of reducing dependence on the capped external resource.
- B) The Treasury believes the department's business cases are weak.
- C) The department's other programmes are forbidden from using consultants.
- D) The year-one review will be conducted by the Treasury itself.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The letter's architecture supports A as a modest inference: it caps external consultancy and contractor spend, conditions later funding on a credible plan to grow *internal* capability, and explicitly links the expansion of analysis and architecture functions to that plan. Capping the external resource while requiring growth of its internal substitute is a coherent policy with an evident purpose — the elements are placed in relation to each other, and inferring the connecting rationale is the small, supported step. B converts the reservation of business-case approval to existing processes (a routine jurisdictional clause) into a quality judgement nowhere expressed. C misapplies the cap's scope — it governs the transformation portfolio's £42 million, and the letter says nothing about other programmes' consultancy habits, only about fund transfers. D invents an actor: a "year-one review point" is named, its conductor is not. The discriminating skill: inferring purpose from a document's *structure* is legitimate when the structure is this deliberate; inferring facts the document does not contain never is.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Clear-writing judgement**

You must brief your directorate's leadership on the letter in one paragraph. Which briefing is best?

- A) "Good news: £42m secured over three years, and the Treasury has backed our function's expansion."
- B) "We have £42m over three years, but with a £6m combined cap on consultancy and contractors, milestone-conditional release after year one, a required capability plan in which our function's expansion must credibly feature, no virement without Treasury consent — and individual business cases still need approval as before. Practical implications: workforce growth must be permanent-heavy, year-one milestones are now portfolio-critical, and our capability plan becomes a funding document."
- C) "The settlement letter is broadly positive; details available on request."
- D) "£42m over three years. Note that the Treasury has capped our contractor spend at £6m per year."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** B reproduces every operative element — amount, combined cap, conditionality, the capability plan's required content, the virement restriction, the business-case reservation — and then does what head-level briefing exists to do: converts each provision into its consequence for the audience (permanent-heavy growth, milestone criticality, the capability plan's promotion from HR document to funding document). A commits Question 3's error in celebratory form and drops every condition. C is the abdication option — "details available on request" delegates comprehension to people who will not request them. D contains a factual corruption worth dwelling on: it renders the £6 million three-year combined cap as "£6m per year" and as contractor-only — two silent expansions of spending room that would surface as a £12 million problem in year three. The test's briefing questions, like your real ones, reward completeness, faithfulness and consequence-translation; the designed traps are cheerfulness (A), vagueness (C) and plausible corruption of a figure's basis (D).

For questions 6 to 8, use this passage.

> **Letter from the permanent secretary to heads of profession**
>
> Following the efficiency review, I am asking every head of profession to identify options for a 10% reduction in their function's running costs by the end of the month. I want to be clear about what this exercise is and is not. It is a request for costed options with impacts stated; it is not a decision to implement any of them. No option should assume recruitment freezes across the board — I would rather see targeted choices than uniform restraint. Options that trade short-term savings for larger long-term costs should be identified as such, not excluded. Where a function believes a reduction of this scale would compromise a statutory obligation or create unacceptable risk, the head of profession should say so explicitly in their return, with evidence. I expect returns to be honest rather than tactical, and I will treat them accordingly.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Comprehension**

What has the permanent secretary asked heads of profession to deliver?

- A) Implementation of a 10% reduction in running costs by month end.
- B) Costed options for a 10% reduction, with impacts stated — including, where believed, evidenced statements that the scale would compromise statutory obligations or create unacceptable risk — by month end, with no decision yet taken.
- C) A defence of current budgets against any reduction.
- D) A uniform recruitment freeze pending decisions.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The letter is unusually explicit about its own status: "a request for costed options with impacts stated ... not a decision to implement". B captures the deliverable (options, costed, impacts), the deadline (month end), the status (no decision), and the letter's built-in dissent channel (evidenced statements of statutory or risk concerns belong *in* the return). A converts an options exercise into an implementation instruction — the misreading that triggers premature cuts and the exact confusion the permanent secretary pre-empted. C mistakes the dissent channel for the exercise's purpose: the letter invites evidence of unacceptable risk as part of an honest return, not as an alternative to producing options. D asserts what the letter expressly discourages ("no option should assume recruitment freezes across the board"). Reading a tasking letter for deliverable, deadline, status and channel — the four things your leads will ask you within the hour — is the comprehension discipline this question samples.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Inference**

What can most safely be inferred about the permanent secretary's expectations of the returns?

- A) They anticipate that some functions may submit inflated impact claims to protect their budgets, and are signalling that such tactics will be recognised.
- B) They have already decided which functions will bear the reductions.
- C) They believe all functions can absorb 10% without harm.
- D) They will exempt any function that claims a statutory obligation.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The closing sentence — "I expect returns to be honest rather than tactical, and I will treat them accordingly" — is a pre-emptive signal: naming the tactical return as a category, contrasting it with honesty, and attaching an unspecified consequence ("treat them accordingly") is the language of someone who has seen budget-defence gamesmanship and is warning against it. Inferring that anticipation is the small, supported step: A. B has no textual basis — the letter's whole design (options, impacts, evidence) is a decision-input process, and inferring a pre-made decision contradicts its stated status. C is contradicted by the letter's own provision for statutory and risk exceptions — a writer who believed 10% universally harmless would not build that channel. D overreads the channel into an exemption rule: the letter asks for explicit, evidenced claims and promises nothing about their success. For a head of profession composing a return, inference A is operationally decisive: your statutory-risk claims must be conservative and evidenced precisely because the reader has announced they will be scrutinised as possible tactics.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Clear-writing judgement**

Your return must flag that a 10% reduction would compromise your function's assurance of legally significant casework analysis. Which formulation best matches the letter's request?

- A) "A 10% reduction is impossible for this function and we decline to model it."
- B) "We have modelled the requested options (Annex A). Option 2 reaches 10% but would remove independent review of analysis supporting legally significant casework decisions — the control that caught 14 defects last year, three in cases that proceeded to litigation. We assess this as creating unacceptable legal risk, evidenced in Annex B, and recommend capping this function's reduction at 6% (Option 1) unless the risk is explicitly accepted at board level."
- C) "Any reduction would compromise our statutory obligations."
- D) "We can achieve 10% with no material impact."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Map the letter's requirements onto each option. The permanent secretary asked for: costed options (B provides them, including the full 10% option — the letter wants risky options *identified*, "not excluded"), impacts stated (B names the specific control lost and its evidenced track record — 14 defects, three litigation-relevant), explicit evidenced risk claims (B's Annex B and the "unacceptable legal risk" assessment), and honesty over tactics (B recommends 6%, not 0%, and routes the residual decision to the level that owns the risk). A refuses the commission — exactly the tactical return the letter warned about, and it forfeits your influence over options others will then model for you. C is the unevidenced universal claim that inference-question A predicted would be recognised as budget defence: "any reduction" is almost never true and marks the whole return as tactical. D is dishonest in the optimistic direction — if 10% truly had no material impact, the function was 10% overfunded, which is not the admission you intend. B is what "honest rather than tactical" looks like in practice: model everything, price the danger precisely, evidence it, and make the decision-holder decide with eyes open.

For questions 9 to 12, use this passage.

> **Extract from the government analysis profession's draft assurance standard (issued for consultation)**
>
> This standard applies to analysis that materially informs a decision of a public body. Analysis materially informs a decision if the decision maker relies on it, or could reasonably be expected to rely on it, in reaching the decision. All such analysis must have a named analytical owner. Analysis rated business-critical must additionally undergo independent assurance proportionate to the decision's impact and reversibility; departments must maintain a register of business-critical analysis. The standard does not define "business-critical": departments must publish their own definitions, which must at minimum capture analysis where error could lead to significant financial loss, legal challenge, or harm to individuals. Departments may exceed this standard. Departures below the standard must be approved by the department's analysis governance board and recorded, with rationale, in the departmental register. This standard takes effect for new analysis from 1 April; existing analysis must be brought into compliance within two years.

**Question 9 (moderate) — True / False / Cannot say**

Statement: "Analysis that a decision maker did not actually rely on can still fall within the standard's scope."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The scope test is disjunctive: analysis materially informs a decision if the decision maker "relies on it, *or could reasonably be expected to rely on it*". The second limb captures analysis a decision maker could reasonably be expected to use even where, in fact, they did not — so the statement is guaranteed by the passage: True. The drafting choice is deliberate and worth understanding as an author of standards yourself: an actual-reliance-only test would let a decision maker take analysis out of scope by claiming they ignored it, and would leave analysis unassured until the moment of use — too late for assurance to do its work. The "could reasonably be expected" limb makes scope determinable *in advance*, which is what an assurance regime requires. Reading disjunctions limb by limb — and asking what each limb is *for* — is how heads of profession both apply standards accurately and draft them well.

**Question 10 (moderate) — True / False / Cannot say**

Statement: "A department whose definition of business-critical analysis omits legal challenge as a criterion is compliant, provided its governance board approved the definition."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The standard sets a floor for departmental definitions: they "must at minimum capture analysis where error could lead to significant financial loss, legal challenge, or harm to individuals". A definition omitting legal challenge falls below that minimum. Could governance-board approval cure it? The board-approval mechanism exists for "departures below the standard" — but note what it requires: approval *and* recording with rationale in the register. The statement describes a board approving the definition, not a recorded departure with rationale — and more fundamentally, the departure mechanism governs departures in *applying* the standard; the minimum-content rule for definitions is expressed as an unqualified "must". On the passage's own terms, a definition below the minimum is non-compliant as described: False. The layered reading this rehearses — floor provisions, exception mechanisms, and exactly what each mechanism can and cannot excuse — is the daily grammar of assurance regimes, and the difference between a defensible register and an audit finding.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Applying the standard to a scenario**

On 1 June (after the standard takes effect), your department's benefits model — built three years ago, updated quarterly, and used by the investment board to approve programme funding — has a named owner but has never had independent assurance. Your department's published definition rates analysis informing investment decisions above £10m as business-critical; the model informs decisions well above that. Under the standard, which statement is most accurate?

- A) The model is non-compliant and its use must cease immediately.
- B) The model is out of the standard's scope because it predates 1 April.
- C) The model falls within scope (it materially informs decisions), is business-critical under the department's definition (so requires proportionate independent assurance and registration), and as existing analysis it benefits from the two-year transition — so continued use is lawful under the standard while it is brought into compliance, though its unassured status on decisions of this size argues for prioritising it early in the transition plan.
- D) The quarterly updates make it "new analysis" from each update, so it required full compliance from 1 April.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Apply the standard clause by clause. Scope: the investment board relies on it — materially informs, in scope. Classification: it informs decisions above the department's £10m threshold — business-critical, so independent proportionate assurance plus registration are required. Timing: it is existing analysis (built three years ago), and "existing analysis must be brought into compliance within two years" — so its current unassured state is not a breach *yet*; the transition provision exists for exactly this case. C assembles all three readings and adds the judgement the clause-reading supports: two years is a ceiling, not a target, and analysis carrying this much decision weight belongs at the front of the transition queue. A ignores the transition provision — the over-strict error, which at organisational scale would halt investment decisions the standard never required halting. B misreads the transition provision as an exemption: existing analysis is inside the standard with a compliance deadline, not outside it. D is the ingenious wrong answer: if quarterly updates made analysis "new", the two-year transition would be meaningless for any maintained model — a reading that destroys a provision's purpose is almost never the intended construction, in standards or in statutes. This clause-by-clause walk, ending in a prioritisation judgement, is precisely what your function will do with every regime that lands on the profession.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Clear-writing judgement in a consultation response**

Your consultation response wants the standard to define "proportionate" independent assurance more precisely. Which drafting proposal is strongest?

- A) "Proportionality should be left to professional judgement, as now."
- B) "Replace 'proportionate to the decision's impact and reversibility' with: 'Assurance intensity must be determined by the department's published assurance matrix, which must map at least three tiers of assurance activity against decision impact and reversibility, and must specify the minimum tier for business-critical analysis. Where the matrix's application to a specific analysis is unclear, the analysis governance board determines the tier and records the rationale.'"
- C) "All business-critical analysis should receive maximum assurance regardless of proportionality."
- D) "The word 'proportionate' is too vague and should be deleted."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** A consultation response's drafting proposal succeeds if it makes the obligation determinable — someone reading it can tell what compliance requires — while preserving the flexibility the original word was doing real work to provide. B achieves both: it keeps proportionality's substance (tiers mapped to impact and reversibility) but relocates it from each analyst's private judgement to a published, auditable instrument (the matrix), sets floor content for that instrument (three tiers minimum, a specified minimum for business-critical), and — the mark of practised drafting — provides the residual-ambiguity route (board determines, rationale recorded), because every matrix meets a case it did not anticipate. A defends the status quo the consultation exists to improve: unstructured "professional judgement" is precisely what produces inconsistent assurance and indefensible registers. C abolishes proportionality in the name of rigour — maximum assurance on everything is a resourcing impossibility that would either bankrupt assurance capacity or, more likely, be quietly ignored, and standards that will be ignored are worse than weaker standards that will be followed. D identifies the vagueness and proposes nothing — deletion without replacement leaves the obligation *less* determinable. The pattern to internalise for your own authorship: vague standard words are repaired by published instruments with floor content and a recorded-judgement fallback — not by deletion, maximalism, or trust.

### Preparation tips

- **Rehearse the three-verdict discipline on governing documents.** True = guaranteed; False = contradicted; Cannot say = unaddressed. Practise on settlement letters, standards and framework agreements — the genre you read professionally is the genre the test samples, and the False/Cannot-say boundary decides most marks.
- **Read verbs of authority precisely.** Notes, expects, approves, provides, conditions, reserves — settlement and governance documents are built from these verbs, and each grants something different. Practise restating a paragraph as a list of who-may-do-what.
- **Check every limit's structure.** Combined or separate? Per year or total? Floor or ceiling? A cap's basis is as important as its number, and corrupted bases are the test's favourite briefing-question trap — and real life's.
- **Trace conditions to their stated consequences — and stop.** "Conditional on X" tells you X matters; it does not tell you what a miss triggers. Practise writing "consequence unspecified" as a finding.
- **For drafting questions, apply the determinability test.** Could a stranger tell what compliance requires? Does the draft keep the flexibility the vague word was providing? Is there a route for the unanticipated case? The strongest option passes all three.
- **Purpose-check your constructions.** A reading that makes a provision pointless (updates resetting "new analysis"; exemptions swallowing rules) is almost never the intended one. When two readings compete, ask which one lets the document do its evident job.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Upgrading "noted" to "approved".** The most expensive misreading in government correspondence. Match your briefing's verbs to the document's verbs exactly.
- **Splitting combined caps.** "£6m on consultancy and contractors" is one ceiling, not two allowances. Limits questions turn on exactly this.
- **Inventing consequences for conditions.** Conditionality without a specified consequence is negotiating room, not automatic forfeiture — and claiming either certainty is an error.
- **Reading dissent channels as exemption rules.** "Say so explicitly, with evidence" invites a claim; it promises nothing about the claim's success.
- **Constructions that destroy purpose.** If your reading makes the transition period, the exception mechanism or the minimum-content rule meaningless, it is wrong — in the test and before any tribunal of construction.
- **Answering from seniority instead of the passage.** You have read fifty letters like the passage; this one may differ in the clause that matters. The most senior candidates lose marks by recognising the genre and skipping the words.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment (SJT) presents realistic workplace dilemmas and asks you to judge the effectiveness of possible responses. There is rarely a provably correct action; responses are scored against a behavioural key built with experienced practitioners — for a head-of-function role, behaviours such as strategic influence, integrity under political pressure, capability stewardship, proportionate governance, inclusion, and care for the profession beyond your own organisation.

Typical format: an online assessment of 25 to 45 minutes presenting 8 to 15 scenarios. Response formats include choosing the most effective response; choosing the most *and* least effective; ranking all responses; or rating each independently. The overall assessment is timed but individual scenarios usually are not. For senior UK government digital roles, the key is strongly informed by the Service Standard's user-centred ethos, Civil Service values — honesty, integrity, objectivity, impartiality — and the leadership behaviours expected of heads of profession.

For a head of business analysis, the SJT is the closest thing psychometrics offers to a day in your actual job. Your calendar is a sequence of judgement calls with no analytically correct answer: a director general who wants the evidence to say something else; a function that must shrink, or grow, or defend its existence; a standard that a crisis programme wants waived; a talented lead whose ambition exceeds your vacancies; a fellow head of profession whose failing programme your analysts can see from the inside. What distinguishes head-level judgement is that every choice is also a precedent: your function watches how you handle pressure and learns what the real standards are, whatever the written ones say. SJTs sample exactly this, and practising them builds the articulated reasoning that lets you not only make such calls but explain them — to boards, to your leads, and to the profession you develop.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Strategic influence and negotiation** map to **Stakeholder relationship management** and **Consultancy**: scenarios test whether you influence and negotiate with the most senior stakeholders — protecting evidence, relationships and progress simultaneously, and knowing which to spend when they conflict.
- **Evidence integrity as organisational infrastructure** maps to **Context, problem and option analysis** and **Business process improvement**: scenarios probe whether the analysis your function signs stays honest when boards, ministers' offices or spending reviews need it warmer — because your personal line *is* the function's line.
- **Building capability, not dependency** maps to your organisational capability-building mandate and **Requirements definition and management**: talent, succession, standards-adoption and function-design scenarios test whether you grow systems that outlast you rather than heroics that require you.
- **Proportionate governance** maps to **Adapting to delivery methodologies** and **Testing (business analysis)**: setting and iterating standards — and deciding when they bend, with what record — is tested through exemption, assurance and crisis scenarios where both rigidity and capitulation are designed distractors.
- **User focus and inclusion at portfolio scale** map to **User experience analysis**, **Systems analysis** and **Business modelling**: scenarios include the constituencies with no seat at your governance tables — excluded users, frontline staff, future analysts — and reward the head who structurally represents them.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — The director general and the inconvenient baseline**

Your function's analysis of a flagship programme shows its benefits baseline is unachievable — a finding due at the programme board next week. The director general, the programme's sponsor, asks you to "hold the finding back until after the spending review announcement in three weeks; the timing could cost us the settlement". What is the most effective response?

- A) Hold the finding back as asked; three weeks changes little and the settlement matters more.
- B) Publish the finding at the board regardless, without further discussion.
- C) Decline to suppress the finding, but work with the DG on its presentation: the board sees it on schedule with the corrective options your function proposes, framed as the programme self-correcting — and offer to help prepare lines that show the Treasury a programme that finds and fixes its own problems.
- D) Tell the DG you will hold it back, then brief the board chair privately.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The DG's request has a legitimate core — settlement risk is real — wrapped around an illegitimate ask: suppressing analysis from the governance board that exists to receive it. C refuses the suppression cleanly while taking the legitimate concern seriously: the finding lands on schedule (a three-week-old known-and-withheld finding, discovered later, is a scandal; the same finding presented with corrective options is management), and the reframing — a programme that catches its own baseline problems is *stronger* at a spending review, not weaker — gives the DG a better strategy than concealment, which is what turns a refusal into an alliance. A makes you complicit and teaches the DG that your function's timing is negotiable, which repriced every future finding. B wins the integrity point and burns the relationship needlessly — presentation, framing and preparation are legitimately negotiable even when timing is not, and C's collaboration costs nothing in substance. D is the worst of all: a lie to the DG plus a back-channel, either half of which, discovered, ends the trust your role runs on. Scored behaviours: integrity with statecraft, converting a suppression request into a presentation collaboration, and protecting governance without humiliating the sponsor.

**Question 2 (easy) — The exemption precedent**

A crisis programme with genuine statutory deadlines requests exemption from your analysis assurance standard. Your deputy recommends granting it informally — "just this once, no paperwork, it sets no precedent if it isn't written down". What is the most effective response?

- A) Grant the exemption informally as recommended; discretion avoids precedent.
- B) Refuse any exemption; the standard exists precisely for pressure like this.
- C) Grant a formal, recorded exemption: scoped to the statutory-deadline work, time-boxed, with the minimum assurance that still protects the legally significant elements, the rationale documented, and a review date — and correct your deputy's theory: unrecorded exemptions set the strongest precedents, because they teach people that the register is optional.
- D) Escalate the decision to the digital director to avoid owning the precedent.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The scenario's real subject is your deputy's theory of precedent, which is exactly backwards: written, scoped, time-boxed exemptions create a governed exception path — the record *is* the containment, showing every future requester that exceptions exist, cost paperwork, carry conditions and expire. Unwritten exemptions create the ungoverned kind: invisible to review, infinitely citable ("you did it for them"), and corrosive precisely because deniable. C grants what the crisis genuinely needs (proportionate relief, fast), protects what cannot be relieved (the legally significant elements — a statutory-deadline programme is the *last* place to waive assurance of legal-risk analysis), and uses the moment to coach your deputy, which is how the function's next generation learns governance. A buys short-term ease with the register's credibility. B treats the standard's letter as its purpose; standards that cannot flex proportionately under genuine statutory pressure get abolished, not respected. D delegates upward a decision squarely within your authority — the director should hear about the exemption, not make it. Scored behaviours: proportionate governance, record-keeping as protection, coaching in the moment, and owning your delegations.

**Question 3 (easy) — The vanishing pipeline**

Reviewing your workforce data, you find your function has hired no one below senior level for three years — every vacancy was filled with experienced contractors or senior permanent hires "because delivery pressure never left room to grow juniors". Your leads privately agree the function has become excellent, expensive and demographically narrow, with no pipeline. What is the most effective response?

- A) Continue the senior-heavy model; it delivers, and juniors are a luxury for calmer times.
- B) Commit the function to a rebuilt pipeline as a strategic change: ring-fenced junior and apprenticeship posts in the workforce plan, mentoring loads built into seniors' objectives, entry routes designed to widen the intake beyond the usual sources, and the delivery cost of supervision priced honestly into engagement planning — presented to your board as the cost of the function existing in ten years.
- C) Convert two contractor posts to junior posts next quarter and see how it goes.
- D) Ask HR to run a graduate recruitment campaign for you.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Three years of senior-only hiring is not a hiring pattern; it is a strategic decision made by default — the function has been consuming capability the profession grew elsewhere while growing none, and narrowing its own intake demographics in the process. B treats the correction at the level the problem lives: structural (ring-fenced posts survive the next delivery squeeze, which unprotected good intentions never do), systemic (mentoring in objectives makes growing juniors part of seniors' jobs rather than charity), inclusive by design (entry routes are where workforce diversity is actually determined — apprenticeships and non-graduate routes widen it; senior-only hiring compounds narrowness), and honest about cost (supervision time is real and pricing it prevents the pressure that killed the pipeline last time). A eats the future — "building business analysis capability for the organisation" is your role's defining sentence, and a pipeline-free function fails it by definition. C is a gesture where a strategy is needed: unprotected and unresourced, it repeats the original failure at smaller scale. D outsources the *what* along with the *how*: HR can run campaigns, but entry-route design, supervision economics and capability strategy are yours. Scored behaviours: long-term stewardship, inclusion as structure rather than sentiment, honest costing, and treating workforce shape as strategy.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Two programmes, one analyst, and the truth between them**

Programme X and Programme Y are in open conflict over a shared platform's requirements; the dispute is escalating toward the portfolio board. Your senior analyst embedded in X tells you, in confidence, that X's leadership is knowingly overstating its user-volume forecasts to win the argument — she has seen the internal numbers. What is the most effective response?

- A) Do nothing with the information; it was shared in confidence and embedding depends on trust.
- B) Pass the internal numbers to Programme Y so the argument is fair.
- C) Have your function formally validate both programmes' forecasts ahead of the portfolio board — a legitimate step for any escalated requirements dispute over shared infrastructure — so the overstatement surfaces through independent analysis rather than through your analyst, whom you protect by never citing her; and separately, be clear with her that she was right to bring it to you.
- D) Report Programme X's leadership to the portfolio board for misconduct on the strength of her account.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Three obligations collide: the decision must not rest on falsified forecasts; your analyst must be protected (embedded analysts who become known as leak channels lose all access, and your embedding model dies with their trust); and X's leadership deserves due process, not conviction on a private account. C satisfies all three through the move heads of function uniquely possess: instituting independent validation as *standard practice for escalated shared-infrastructure disputes* — a step so procedurally normal it needs no justifying source — which surfaces the truth through methodology rather than testimony. The closing affirmation to your analyst matters as much: she took a risk on your judgement, and how you handle it determines whether the next embedded analyst speaks up. A privileges one obligation (trust) by abandoning the decision to known-bad evidence — and "in confidence" cannot mean "the board decides on numbers I know are false". B converts you into a rival's intelligence service — a betrayal in the opposite direction that also leaves the falsification unexamined. D spends her confidence and reframes an evidence question as a conduct trial you cannot yet win. Scored behaviours: protecting sources while acting on substance, due process, procedural creativity, and reinforcing the speak-up culture your function depends on.

**Question 5 (moderate) — The methodology mandate from above**

A new government-wide mandate requires all departments to adopt a standardised discovery framework your own evidence (from two instrumented pilots) shows works poorly for complex casework services. As head of profession in your department, colleagues across government are watching your response. Meanwhile your central-department counterpart, who owns the mandate, has publicly called early criticism "resistance to consistency". What is the most effective response?

- A) Adopt fully and silently; the mandate is decided and your counterpart is defensive.
- B) Adopt the framework in good faith across your portfolio while sharing your pilot evidence privately with your counterpart first — offered as implementation learning, with proposed amendments for casework contexts — then through the framework's formal review channel with your counterpart's knowledge; and tell your own community exactly what you are doing and why: complying, evidencing, and proposing improvement through the front door.
- C) Publicly present your pilot evidence at the next cross-government event to correct the record on "resistance to consistency".
- D) Adopt the framework nominally while quietly telling your casework programmes to keep their existing approach underneath compliance-coloured reporting.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The complicating fact is your counterpart's public defensiveness — evidence they will experience as attack will be fought, not weighed, and the mandate's improvement depends on its owner adopting your amendments. B sequences for adoption: private first (letting your counterpart own the improvement rather than absorb a public correction converts a defender into a sponsor — influence at head level is largely the art of giving others the credit for your evidence), formal channel second (so the evidence enters the record with the owner's knowledge, not around them), and full transparency with your own community third — which is what makes B principled rather than merely diplomatic: everyone can see you comply, evidence, and propose through the front door. A wastes the only instrumented evidence in government — silent compliance by the best-placed critic is stewardship failure. C wins the room and loses the framework: publicly falsifying the mandate-owner's "resistance" line entrenches the defensiveness that blocks amendment. D is the disqualifying option: compliance-coloured reporting is institutional dishonesty that, discovered, ends your standing to enforce any standard of your own — and it will be discovered, because frameworks get audited. Scored behaviours: evidence-based dissent, sequencing influence for adoption, transparency with your community, and refusing hypocrisy even when it is operationally convenient.

**Question 6 (moderate) — The failing peer**

A fellow head of profession — a peer you work with closely — leads a function whose weak requirements work your analysts keep encountering downstream: three programmes this quarter have absorbed rework caused by his function's outputs. He is respected, defensive about his function, and unaware (you believe) of the pattern. The portfolio director has begun asking pointed questions about "where all this rework comes from". What is the most effective response?

- A) Answer the portfolio director's question fully and first; the data is the data.
- B) Take the evidence to your peer privately before anyone else: the three programmes, the rework pattern, offered as one professional to another — with an offer of practical help (joint review standards, shared training, a loaned senior) — and agree with him how the pattern gets addressed and how it gets represented to the portfolio director, while being clear you will not misrepresent it if asked directly.
- C) Say nothing to anyone; his function is not your jurisdiction.
- D) Raise it at the heads-of-profession forum so the correction is collective rather than personal.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The head-level reading: this is a peer-stewardship problem wearing a data problem's clothes. B goes to the person who can fix it, first and privately — the professional courtesy you would want reversed, and the approach with the best odds, since defensiveness feeds on ambush and starves on private evidence plus offered help. The help matters substantively: joint standards and loaned capability fix the *cause*, where a governance intervention would fix the blame. And B's closing clause carries the integrity line — you will support his repair, not his concealment; asked directly by the portfolio director, you answer honestly, and he knows that going in, which is itself motivating. A is honest but graceless: first disclosure to the director, before the peer has heard the evidence, converts a fixable professional problem into a political one and teaches every peer that your data reaches their boss before it reaches them. C confuses jurisdiction with responsibility — the rework lands on programmes you serve, and professions steward each other. D makes the correction maximally public while pretending not to: a forum ambush is A with witnesses. Scored behaviours: peer stewardship, cause-fixing over blame-fixing, courage in private, honesty when asked, and the long game of cross-functional trust.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Inclusion versus the delivery narrative**

Your organisation's transformation story — repeated by executives, celebrated in internal comms — is that the new digital service is "loved by users, 92% satisfaction". Your function's deeper analysis shows the 92% comes from post-completion surveys of digital-channel users only; assisted-channel users (18% of the base, skewed old, disabled and poor) were never surveyed, and their proxy indicators — complaints, abandonment, repeat contact — are worsening. The comms team is about to publish the figure externally in a case study the chief executive will front. What is the most effective response?

- A) Let publication proceed; the figure is technically true and the case study is already written.
- B) Brief the chief executive's office and comms urgently: the figure as framed will not survive scrutiny (state the survey's actual base), the excluded 18% show worsening indicators, and provide corrected framing options — publish the digital-channel figure accurately labelled, alongside a stated commitment on assisted-channel improvement — so the story becomes honest without becoming a retraction.
- C) Contact the journalists likely to cover the case study and correct the record directly.
- D) Publish your function's fuller analysis internally the same week, letting the contradiction speak for itself.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The figure is the dangerous kind of true: accurate within a base nobody will state, deployed to describe a population it excludes — and the excluded population is precisely the one public services exist not to leave behind. B treats it as what it is, a briefing emergency with a fixable frame: the CE's office hears the exposure risk (externally published, front-person attached, one FOI or select committee away from "92% of whom, exactly?"), gets the accurate labelling that preserves most of the story, and gets the assisted-channel commitment that converts the weakness into an agenda. That last element is what makes B inclusion rather than pedantry — the worsening indicators of 18% of users become a stated executive priority instead of a suppressed footnote. A lets your organisation's leadership walk into a misleading claim you could have corrected — "technically true" is the phrase that precedes every reputational analysis failure. C is a loyalty and process breach that ends your role's access — corrections travel inside before outside, always. D stages an internal ambush: publishing the contradiction without briefing first optimises for being provably right over the users' outcome and the CE's trust, and achieves neither. Scored behaviours: inclusion as evidence discipline, protecting principals from their own narratives, speed proportionate to exposure, and correcting through the people who can act.

**Question 8 (moderate) — The board seat**

A reorganisation proposal would move your function's reporting line: instead of you attending the digital and data board, your function would report through the new chief delivery officer, who "will represent analysis interests". Colleagues advise you to fight for your seat as a matter of status. What is the most effective response?

- A) Fight the change on status grounds; heads of profession belong at the board.
- B) Accept the change gracefully; structures matter less than relationships.
- C) Make the case in functional, not status, terms: identify the specific board decisions where analysis evidence changed the outcome in the last year, the assurance and integrity questions that require a professional voice independent of delivery interests (a CDO accountable for delivery pace cannot neutrally represent evidence that slows delivery), and propose structural safeguards if the line moves anyway — a standing evidence slot, direct escalation rights on integrity issues, and your attendance for business-critical analysis items.
- D) Accept the line change but negotiate a personal invitation to attend board meetings informally.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The proposal's flaw is not that it demotes you; it is a structural conflict of interest: analysis exists partly to tell delivery leadership things delivery leadership does not want to hear, and routing the evidence function *through* the delivery function filters exactly the messages that matter most — the CDO becomes the judge of which inconvenient findings reach the board, however honourable the individual. C argues this functionally, with the receipts (last year's outcome-changing evidence), and — the mark of a negotiator rather than a defender — comes with fallback safeguards that preserve the substance (independent escalation on integrity, direct voice on business-critical items) even if the org chart moves. That converts a losable status fight into a winnable design conversation. A fights on the weakest available ground: status claims invite the org-design retort ("everyone thinks their seat is essential") and concede the functional argument by not making it. B mistakes graciousness for stewardship — relationships change with postholders; structures outlast them, which is why this fight is worth having properly. D personalises the safeguard: an informal invitation protects you-this-year, not the function or your successor, and evaporates with the first calendar squeeze. Scored behaviours: arguing from function not status, structural thinking about independence, negotiating with fallbacks, and stewardship of the role beyond your own tenure.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Most and least effective: the spending review squeeze**

The efficiency exercise concludes: your function must lose 15% of its budget. You can shape how. Your analysis shows the least-harmful path cuts two vacant posts, ends the contractor premium through conversions, and — the painful part — withdraws embedded analysts from four low-value programmes whose directors are vocal and well-connected. The alternative path, favoured by your director because it is quieter, spreads the cut evenly: every programme keeps its analyst, every analyst loses training, community time and assurance backup. Identify the MOST and LEAST effective responses.

- A) Implement the least-harmful path: present the evidence for concentrating the cut, absorb the four directors' anger by meeting each personally with the prioritisation rationale and a defined re-entry route when their programmes' analysis needs change, and protect the function's training, standards and assurance core as non-negotiable infrastructure.
- B) Implement the even spread your director favours; political capital matters more than operating model purity.
- C) Implement the least-harmful path but let the four directors hear it through the reorganisation announcement rather than from you.
- D) Delay the decision by commissioning further analysis of the options.

**Correct answer: Most effective A; least effective B**

**Explanation:** The even spread is the quiet catastrophe: it keeps every flag on the map while hollowing out what makes the flags worth having — training, community, assurance backup are the infrastructure that turns individuals into a function, and cutting them converts your operating model into a body shop whose quality decays invisibly for two years and then all at once. That is why B is least effective despite being the politically painless option your director prefers: it trades the function's actual capability for the appearance of universal service — the exact inversion of your role's purpose — and does so silently, so the decay is discovered only through the escaped defects it causes. A takes the harder, honest path and does the leadership work that makes it survivable: evidence for the concentration, personal delivery of bad news to the four directors (anger absorbed in person converts to grudging respect at a rate ambush never matches), a re-entry route that makes withdrawal a prioritisation rather than a rejection — and the explicit framing of the function's core as infrastructure, which is the argument your director needs to defend the choice upward. C is A minus the courage, and the omission is not cosmetic: directors who learn of withdrawal from an announcement become permanent enemies of the function, where directors who got the meeting often become its advocates. D spends money you no longer have to avoid a decision the analysis has already made — delay is itself a choice, the even spread by default. Scored behaviours: evidence-led hard choices, protecting capability infrastructure, personal courage with affected stakeholders, and managing upward with arguments rather than compliance.

**Question 10 (challenging) — The whistleblower and the flagship**

A junior analyst — two years in post, in a team three levels below you — uses your open-door route to tell you the flagship programme's benefits reporting, signed by her lead and presented by her director, double-counts a major saving across two workstreams. She has raised it twice in her team and been told "the model has been through governance". She is visibly frightened about consequences. What is the most effective response?

- A) Thank her, verify the claim through the reporting chain — asking her lead and director to walk you through the model.
- B) Thank her and make three commitments you then keep: the substance will be independently checked (a targeted model review by an analyst outside that programme's chain, on a routine-assurance footing that does not identify a trigger); she will hear back from you personally on the outcome either way; and any concern about how her raising it is treated comes straight to you — then, if the double-count is confirmed, treat it as two findings: the model error, and the team culture that answered a specific technical challenge with "it's been through governance" twice.
- C) Send her back to raise it through her line management, the correct route, with your encouragement.
- D) Immediately inform the programme director that an allegation has been made about the benefits model and ask him to investigate.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Every element of B is load-bearing. The routine-assurance footing protects her — a targeted review triggered visibly by a junior's complaint identifies her as surely as naming her would, in a team that has already rebuffed her twice; independent-of-that-chain protects the check itself from the interests that signed the model. The personal-outcome commitment and the retaliation channel are what "open door" means if it means anything: she risked her standing on your route, and what you do next determines whether anyone below senior level ever uses it again — your function's early-warning system is being calibrated in this meeting. The two-findings framing is the head-level move: a confirmed double-count is an error, but "it's been through governance" as an answer to a specific technical challenge — twice — is a culture defect that will generate the *next* error, and it belongs in your findings as explicitly as the arithmetic. A and D each route the verification through the people who signed the model — the reporting chain investigating itself, with her exposure as the likely first casualty. C is procedurally tidy and practically cruel: the correct route has failed her twice, which is why she is in your office; sending her back teaches the function that escalation routes are circular. Scored behaviours: protecting the discloser structurally rather than rhetorically, independence of verification, closing the loop personally, and reading culture findings out of technical ones.

**Question 11 (challenging) — The definition of done for your own reform**

Eighteen months ago you launched your function's flagship reform: a new engagement model with published triage criteria, tiered assurance and embedded quality metrics. Your board sponsor calls it a success and wants it showcased cross-government. Your own review honestly finds: triage works well; tiered assurance works where adopted but a third of programmes negotiated bespoke arrangements; and the quality metrics exist but nobody uses them for decisions. What is the most effective response?

- A) Showcase the reform as your sponsor wishes; momentum and reputation help the function, and the gaps are normal implementation noise.
- B) Decline the showcase until all three components are fully working.
- C) Showcase it honestly as a partial success: present triage as proven practice others can adopt now, the bespoke-arrangement pattern as a live finding about what drives assurance adoption (with your countermeasures), and the unused metrics as an open problem you are working — and tell your sponsor before the event that this is the version you will present, and why it serves them better than the polished one.
- D) Quietly fix the two weak components first and showcase in a year.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The scenario tests whether you apply your function's evidential standards to your function's own story — under the gentlest and therefore most corrosive pressure: a sponsor's enthusiasm. C showcases *and* tells the truth, and the combination is stronger than either alone: proven practice travels (triage genuinely works — sharing it is the community obligation of a head of profession), live findings teach (the bespoke-negotiation pattern is the most useful thing in the talk — every function attempting tiered assurance will hit it, and your countermeasures are the transferable asset), and open problems recruit (someone in the audience has cracked metrics-for-decisions, and honesty is how you find them). The pre-brief to your sponsor is the professional courtesy that keeps the relationship: they hear the framing from you, with the argument — a leader showcasing honest partial results is more credible cross-government than one showcasing polish, and audiences of practitioners detect polish instantly. A spends your evidential credibility on your own product — the one place you can least afford it, since your authority to challenge others' 92%-satisfaction stories rests on never running your own. B and D confuse honesty with perfectionism: withholding proven components while you perfect the rest serves your comfort, not the community — and D's "quietly" adds a concealment flavour to the delay. Scored behaviours: applying your own standards reflexively to yourself, community stewardship, managing sponsors with candour, and modelling how professionals present partial results.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Most and least effective: the merger of functions**

A machinery-of-government change merges your department with a smaller one. Their business analysis function — 9 analysts to your 24 — has different standards, a different (older) methodology, lower average grades, and a head who is now your deputy-designate and applied unsuccessfully for your role. The executive wants "one function, one way of working, within six months". Identify the MOST and LEAST effective responses.

- A) Extend your existing standards and methodology to the incoming function on day one; yours are newer, your function is larger, and six months is short.
- B) Run a structured convergence: joint review of both functions' standards against evidence (yours are probably better, but "probably" is a hypothesis — their older methodology may embed domain knowledge yours lacks); mixed-team engagements from month one so integration happens through work rather than announcements; your deputy-designate given genuine ownership of a substantial workstream — the convergence design itself — with your visible backing; and the six-month milestone defined as "one standards framework, agreed and in use" rather than "everyone works like us".
- C) Let both functions keep their ways of working and revisit in a year once the dust settles.
- D) Ask the executive to extend the timeline to eighteen months before starting.

**Correct answer: Most effective B; least effective A**

**Explanation:** A is annexation, and its costs are precisely the ones that do not appear in a six-month plan: the incoming nine — who know things about their department's domain, users and policy landscape that your standards were never tested against — learn on day one that merger means erasure, your deputy-designate (already carrying the bruise of the failed application) becomes the natural leader of a resentful faction, and whatever their older methodology genuinely did better is discarded unexamined. "Ours are newer and we are bigger" is not evidence; it is the assumption A substitutes for the review B runs. B is the head-level design: evidence decides the standards question (and visibly so, which is what makes the outcome adoptable by the losing side of each point); mixed engagements integrate through shared work, the only mechanism that actually merges cultures; and the deputy-designate move is the masterstroke the scenario invites you to find — ownership of the convergence design converts your most dangerous potential opponent into the merger's co-author, with their standing invested in its success, while your visible backing settles the authority question without a confrontation. The six-month milestone is kept but defined honestly: one agreed framework in use, not uniformity of habit, which no six months ever produced. C fails the executive's legitimate requirement and lets two cultures harden into permanence — "revisit in a year" means "inherit a feud". D opens your tenure over the merged function with a request to be excused from its first test — sometimes timelines do need extending, but asking *before starting* spends credibility that running B for two months and returning with evidence would earn. Scored behaviours: evidence over incumbency, integration through work, converting rivals into owners, honest milestone definition, and the self-awareness that your own function's ways are also hypotheses.

### Preparation tips

- **Answer as the steward, not the incumbent.** The key is calibrated to a head of profession whose horizon is the function in five years and the profession beyond one department. Responses that protect your comfort, your status or this quarter's optics over that horizon are the designed distractors.
- **Find the option that converts opponents into owners.** At this level, the strongest responses routinely give the difficult person — the defensive counterpart, the rival deputy, the angry director — a genuine stake in the solution. Train yourself to look for the conversion move before the confrontation move.
- **Check every response for precedent load.** You are watched. Ask of each option: if this became known as "how the head handles it", what would the function learn? Unrecorded exemptions, quiet fixes and tailored truths all fail this test before they fail any other.
- **Protect people structurally, not rhetorically.** Disclosers, embedded analysts and junior staff are protected by process design — routine footings, independent channels, closed loops — not by promises. The option with the structural protection beats the option with the warm words.
- **Sequence influence for adoption.** Private before public, evidence before argument, the owner's credit before your own. When a scenario includes a defensive stakeholder, the winning option is usually the one that lets them adopt your evidence as theirs.
- **Rehearse on your own history.** List the five hardest calls of your leadership so far and write what the best-practice response would have been. The honest gaps are your syllabus — and the reflection doubles as preparation for any senior interview's leadership questions.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Sophisticated dishonesty.** Technically-true figures, compliance-coloured reporting, tailored versions for different audiences — at head level these are the engineered worst answers, because your personal integrity is the function's operating licence.
- **Rigidity dressed as principle.** Refusing all exemptions, declining every showcase until perfection, fighting every structural change — purity options typically rank second-worst, behind only concealment, because they abandon the outcome to defend the posture.
- **Routing verification through the interested chain.** Whenever a scenario involves checking a claim, notice who signed the original. Options that ask the signatories to investigate themselves — however senior, however convenient — are traps.
- **Fighting on status when function is the argument.** Board seats, reporting lines and mandates are defended by evidence of functional consequence, not by rank. The status-based defence is the losable version of a winnable case.
- **The quiet option.** Even spreads, unannounced withdrawals, decisions delivered by reorganisation memo — options that minimise this month's conflict usually maximise next year's damage, and the key knows it.
- **Forgetting you are the culture.** In every scenario, your function is learning what the real standards are from what you do under pressure. If an option would embarrass you read aloud at your own community of practice, it is not the answer.
