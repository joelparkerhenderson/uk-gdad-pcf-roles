Psychometric assessments are standardised exercises measuring the cognitive abilities and behavioural judgement that predict effectiveness in a role. At your level, well-designed assessments are unmistakably job-shaped. They draw on the artefacts you genuinely handle — multi-team dependency maps, business cases, contract schedules, benefits profiles, escalation papers, assessment evidence, budget envelopes negotiated across programmes — and they simulate the decisions that define seniority: creating and tailoring delivery approaches rather than applying them, deciding on evidence when a team should continue, pivot or stop, negotiating with suppliers, moderating high-risk discussions within constrained timescales, and acting as the main point of escalation when complexity and risk concentrate.

Why does this practice matter for you in particular? A senior delivery manager is accountable for the effective delivery of complex, high-risk products and services. The distance between you and a delivery manager is not effort but range: you have seen more of the life cycle, you carry greater accountability, and problems reach you precisely because they have defeated the usual mechanisms. That is a cognitive and behavioural profile assessments can measure: reasoning across systems of teams rather than single boards, numerical fluency at business-case and portfolio scale, reading dense governance and contractual text exactly, and exercising judgement where stakeholders are senior, timescales are constrained, and no option is clean.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the reasoning your role actually demands, using realistic delivery material rather than abstract items. At senior level, the material scales up: instead of one team's board, you reason over portfolios of teams, chains of cross-service dependencies, governance artefacts, and evidence bases for continue/pivot/stop decisions.

The typical format remains an online, timed test of 15 to 30 minutes — pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving — scored objectively against a norm group, frequently adaptive, and reported to employers as an accuracy-and-speed profile rather than a single number. What changes at your level is the norm group and the item design: senior candidates face denser stimuli (multi-column tables, layered rules, longer scenario stems) and items engineered so that surface-plausible answers fail under complete checking.

Employers use cognitive assessment for senior delivery managers because the role concentrates exactly this demand. You are the main point of escalation: what reaches you is the residue that simpler reasoning failed to resolve — the dependency knot with no owner, the metric that contradicts the narrative, the plan whose constraints cannot all hold. Optimising the delivery flow of several teams simultaneously requires seeing systemic patterns single-team eyes miss. Deciding on evidence whether a team should continue, change direction or stop requires separating signal from noise and inference from assumption at a standard that survives challenge from a director or an assessment panel.

Expect scenarios in this section involving multiple teams, ambiguous ownership, governance context, and strategic consequence — because that is the altitude at which your reasoning is deployed and therefore assessed.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **Agile and Lean practices** and **Maintaining delivery momentum**: optimising flow across teams means detecting portfolio-level signatures — synchronised slippage, recurring cross-team contention on a shared service, an innovation that improved one team and quietly taxed three others.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Planning** and **Life cycle management**: leading a continual planning process in a very complex environment, and using evidence to decide whether a team should continue, change direction or stop, are sustained exercises in valid inference under many constraints.
- **Error checking** maps to **Financial management** and **Commercial management**: business cases, benefits profiles, and contract schedules fail through internal inconsistency far more often than through arithmetic — and you are the person expected to catch it before signature or submission.
- **Prioritisation** maps to your accountability as the main point of escalation: when the most complicated risks, issues and dependencies arrive together, ranking them by impact-weighted urgency — including where ownership exists outside every team or nowhere — is the daily discipline.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Maintaining delivery momentum** ("identify innovative ways to unblock issues") and **Team dynamics and collaboration**: decomposing an organisationally tangled situation into a tractable intervention, then choosing the intervention that strengthens rather than substitutes for the teams involved.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern recognition across a portfolio**

You oversee four teams. Their sprint-over-sprint completion percentages for the last four sprints:

| Team | S1 | S2 | S3 | S4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Alpha | 92% | 90% | 91% | 93% |
| Bravo | 88% | 84% | 79% | 73% |
| Charlie | 85% | 94% | 78% | 90% |
| Delta | 90% | 89% | 88% | 90% |

Which team most warrants your attention, and why?

- A) Charlie — its results vary the most from sprint to sprint.
- B) Bravo — it shows a consistent, compounding downward trend.
- C) Alpha — its consistency suggests padded commitments.
- D) Delta — its stability suggests stagnation.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Distinguish variance from trend. Charlie oscillates (85, 94, 78, 90) around a healthy mean with no direction — worth understanding, not alarming; teams doing risky discovery-heavy work legitimately vary. Bravo declines monotonically: 88, 84, 79, 73 — roughly five points per sprint, four sprints running. A consistent directional trend is a system telling you something has changed and is still changing: attrition, accumulating technical debt, a growing external drag, or eroding morale. Options C and D manufacture pathology out of health — consistency is not evidence of padding, and stability is not stagnation; inventing problems where data shows none is its own reasoning failure, and a senior habit worth policing in yourself. Optimising the delivery flow of teams, plural, is your named skill: the portfolio view exists precisely so that a slope visible across sprints triggers inquiry before the team itself raises a hand — which declining teams, tellingly, often do not.

**Question 2 (easy) — Deduction from a stop/continue rule**

Your governance board has agreed: "A private beta may continue for a further quarter only if (a) its benefits profile has been re-validated in the last six months, and (b) either its user satisfaction is at or above 75%, or a remediation plan approved by the service owner is in place." Service X requests continuation. Its benefits profile was re-validated four months ago; user satisfaction is 68%; a remediation plan exists but the service owner has not yet approved it. What follows?

- A) X may continue; the remediation plan exists.
- B) X may not continue as things stand; condition (b) is unmet until the plan is approved.
- C) X may not continue under any circumstances this quarter.
- D) X may continue if satisfaction improves to 70%.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Test each condition exactly. Condition (a): re-validated four months ago, within six months — satisfied. Condition (b) is a disjunction: satisfaction ≥ 75% (68% — fails) or an approved remediation plan (a plan exists, but approval is absent — fails as stated). Both limbs of (b) currently fail, so continuation is not permitted as things stand — option B, with its precise qualifier. Option A ignores the approval requirement, and the distinction between "exists" and "approved" is exactly the kind that governance wording carries deliberately. Option C overshoots: the situation is curable — obtain the service owner's approval and (b) is met; "as things stand" and "under any circumstances" are different claims. Option D invents a threshold. Using evidence to decide whether a team should continue, change direction or stop is your named **Life cycle management** skill, and its foundation is unglamorous: reading compound conditions with the exactness their drafters intended, then telling the service team precisely which lever cures the failure rather than merely announcing it.

**Question 3 (easy) — Error checking a benefits profile**

A business case extract claims: "Automating the renewals process will release 12 FTE of caseworker effort. Total annual benefit: £780,000, based on a fully loaded cost of £65,000 per FTE. Of the released effort, 8 FTE will be redeployed to the complex-casework backlog and 6 FTE will be taken as headcount reduction." What is wrong?

- A) The £780,000 figure is miscalculated.
- B) The redeployment plan allocates 14 FTE of a 12 FTE benefit.
- C) £65,000 is an implausible fully loaded cost.
- D) Nothing is wrong.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Check each figure. The headline: 12 × £65,000 = £780,000 — arithmetic sound, so option A fails. £65,000 fully loaded (salary plus employer costs plus overheads) is unremarkable for caseworker grades, so option C fails. The defect is allocation: 8 FTE redeployed + 6 FTE reduced = 14 FTE dispensed from a 12 FTE benefit. Two FTE are being spent twice — the signature error of benefits profiles assembled by different authors for different audiences (the operational plan promising redeployment to one stakeholder, the finance case promising reduction to another). It matters beyond tidiness: benefits double-counting is among the most common findings when programmes are audited, and it silently overstates the case for investment. Your **Financial management** skill — writing or inputting into business cases and communicating business-value propositions — includes being the reader who reconciles the parts before submission. The technique generalises: in any benefits artefact, sum the dispositions and compare them to the claimed pool. Ten seconds; regularly decisive.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Prioritisation as the point of escalation**

Monday, 08:45. Four escalations await you:

1. Team Bravo's delivery manager reports their production release failed overnight; the service is degraded for users, and the team is mid-incident with the operations centre engaged.
2. A supplier's commercial director has emailed threatening to pause work Friday over an invoice dispute your finance team believes is the supplier's error.
3. The programme director wants your recommendation by Wednesday on whether Team Charlie's discovery should proceed to alpha.
4. Two of your delivery managers have separately raised the same complaint about a shared platform team's unresponsiveness, the third such complaint this month.

Which sequencing is most defensible?

- A) 1, 2, 4, 3 — confirm the incident is properly led and supported (minutes, not hours), then defuse the Friday supplier cliff-edge, then act on the pattern, then the Wednesday recommendation.
- B) 2, 1, 3, 4 — commercial threats outrank operational noise.
- C) 3, 4, 1, 2 — strategic decisions first, and incidents belong to teams.
- D) 1, 3, 2, 4 — deal with the incident fully yourself before anything else.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The senior version of prioritisation is allocating your attention, not just your task order — asking of each item: what does it need from me, and by when? Item 1 is live user harm, but a functioning incident response is already running; your role is verification and air cover — confirm the right people lead it, remove obstacles, agree the communication line — which costs minutes, and taking over (option D) would both duplicate the incident lead and stall everything else, the classic seniority failure of doing your old job during a crisis. Item 2 has a hard Friday cliff-edge with user-facing consequences if work pauses; with days rather than hours available, engaging early — facts from finance, a call to the commercial director, commercial colleagues briefed — is what taking responsibility for complex supplier relationships means, and Friday deadlines are defused on Monday, not Thursday night. Item 4 is a pattern: three complaints in a month about a shared dependency is a systemic flow problem — exactly what you exist to fix — but it is measured in days, not hours. Item 3 has a Wednesday deadline and needs reflective quality, so it is scheduled deliberately rather than squeezed first. Options B and C each rank by category (commercial, strategic) rather than by impact-weighted urgency, and option C's "incidents belong to teams" is true right up until the incident needed senior support nobody checked for.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Pattern recognition in a cross-team dependency map**

Your programme's dependency register shows, over the past quarter: Team Alpha waited on the Identity platform 3 times (total 11 days); Team Bravo waited on Identity twice (9 days) and on Team Charlie once (2 days); Team Delta waited on Identity twice (8 days); Team Charlie waited on Data Engineering once (6 days). Next quarter's plans show all four teams increasing their integration work with Identity. What is the most important conclusion?

- A) Team Charlie is the programme's main bottleneck.
- B) The Identity platform is a shared systemic constraint (7 waits, 28 days this quarter) and demand on it is about to rise — it needs a structural response now, not per-team chasing.
- C) The teams should each escalate their own Identity waits harder.
- D) Dependencies are normal in programmes; no action is needed.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Aggregate before diagnosing. Identity accounts for 7 of the 9 recorded waits and 28 of the 36 lost days — across three different consumer teams, which rules out "one team integrates badly" and establishes a shared constraint. The forward-looking fact seals the priority: all four teams plan more Identity integration next quarter, so the constraint is about to be hit harder precisely as its backlog compounds. Option B draws the structural conclusion: capacity conversation with Identity's management, a demand forecast from the four teams, possibly an interface roadmap, sequencing changes, or dedicated integration support — the register of remedies your skill "actively address the most complicated risks, issues and dependencies including where ownership exists outside the team" implies. Option A misreads the table (Charlie caused one 2-day wait; it mostly waits on others). Option C is the anti-pattern the question is engineered to expose: four teams independently escalating against one constrained platform raises the temperature and not the throughput — competitive escalation is how shared constraints get worse. Option D normalises a quantified, growing loss. The senior habit: read dependency data by the column that receives the arrows, not the rows that send them.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Deduction under layered planning constraints**

You are sequencing three go-lives — Payments (P), Accounts (A), and Notifications (N) — across four monthly windows (March, April, May, June). Constraints: (i) P requires the fraud-screening capability, which completes end of April; (ii) A must go live at least one month before N, which consumes A's outputs; (iii) the department has a change freeze in June; (iv) only one go-live per window. Which schedule is forced?

- A) A in March, P in April, N in May
- B) A in March, N in April, P in May
- C) P in March, A in April, N in May
- D) A in March, P in May, N in April

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Derive from the constraints rather than choosing the tidy-looking sequence. P requires fraud screening, which completes end of April, so P cannot go live before May. June is frozen (iii), leaving March, April, and May as the usable windows — so P must be May. A must precede N by at least a month (ii), and with only March and April remaining, A takes March and N takes April. The forced schedule is A in March, N in April, P in May — option B. Now audit the distractors: options A and C both place P before its required capability exists (the "natural order" of option A survives every constraint except the one requiring a date calculation); option D has N before A, violating (ii). The discipline it trains is the one senior planning actually requires: derive the schedule from the constraints, then verify each constraint against the result, rather than selecting the plausible pattern and rationalising it. In a real programme this is the difference between a roadmap that survives its first governance challenge and one that a director unpicks in the meeting — and leading a continual planning process in a very complex environment, your named **Planning** skill, means being the person whose schedules have already survived your own audit.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Error checking an escalation paper**

A delivery manager you mentor drafts an escalation paper for the programme board. Key lines: "Risk: the supplier's data-migration tooling is 4 weeks late, threatening our October go-live. Impact: go-live slips to November, delaying £150,000/month of benefits. Mitigation underway: we have re-sequenced testing to absorb 2 weeks. Ask: the board is asked to note the risk." What is the paper's most significant weakness?

- A) The benefits figure should be annualised.
- B) The ask is misaligned with the content: a live issue with a quantified residual impact needs a decision or support request, not a "note."
- C) The mitigation should not be disclosed, as it weakens the negotiating position with the supplier.
- D) Risks should never name suppliers.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Follow the paper's own logic: four weeks late, two absorbed by re-sequencing — a two-week residual remains, carrying (on the paper's own figures) roughly £75,000 of delayed benefit. Content of that shape demands an ask with teeth: a decision (accept a two-week slip now versus fund acceleration), support (commercial pressure on the supplier at board level), or a contingent authority ("if tranche 2 slips past the 14th, we recommend..."). "Asked to note" hands the board no verb — and boards given nothing to decide will nod, minute it, and later, when November arrives, observe that they were "only asked to note." Escalation papers are instruments for obtaining decisions, and the alignment check — does the ask match the impact? — is the first thing to teach the delivery managers you coach, which is precisely the mentoring relationship in the stem. Option A is cosmetic; monthly figures are often clearer. Option C confuses a governance paper with a negotiation document — boards need the true position. Option D is simply false; accountability requires naming. Your role as main point of escalation includes quality-controlling the escalations themselves: a well-formed ask is half the unblocking.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Problem solving where ownership is absent**

Three teams' services all consume a legacy reference-data system owned by a directorate outside your programme. It fails roughly monthly, each failure costing each team one to two days. The owning directorate acknowledges the fragility but has no funded remediation and no service-level commitment. Escalations from individual teams have returned sympathetic replies and no change for six months. As senior delivery manager, what is the strongest next move?

- A) Instruct the three teams to build independent workarounds immediately.
- B) Keep escalating monthly through the same route; persistence eventually wins.
- C) Aggregate the quantified cross-programme impact (roughly 3–6 team-days per month, converted to cost and delivery risk), take it jointly with the other affected seniors to the governance tier that spans both organisations, and present costed options: funded remediation, a supported workaround, or an accepted risk with explicit ownership.
- D) Accept it as legacy reality and pad all plans by two days per month.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Diagnose why six months of escalation achieved nothing: the escalations were fragmented (one team's occasional two-day loss is absorbable noise to a directorate with its own priorities) and routed to a tier that cannot fund or compel. Option C repairs both defects — aggregation converts noise into a quantified recurring cost that competes for attention honestly, and routing to the governance tier spanning both organisations reaches the only place that can allocate money or accept risk across the boundary. The three-option framing (fund, support a workaround, or formally accept the risk with a named owner) is the senior move: it forces a decision without dictating one, and "no clear ownership exists" — the exact phrase of your **Maintaining delivery momentum** skill — is cured only by making someone visibly choose to own or decline. Option A triples the cost of a workaround and abandons leverage before the decision-forcing move has been tried. Option B repeats a demonstrated failure. Option D deserves more respect than it usually gets — explicit contingency is sometimes the right answer — but accepting a risk is itself a decision that belongs at the level option C engages, not a default adopted by exhaustion. Innovative unblocking, at senior level, usually means redesigning the escalation, not the software.

**Question 9 (challenging) — The confounded improvement claim**

A delivery manager reports: "Since we moved to trunk-based development in April, our change failure rate has fallen from 18% to 9%. The practice works — the other three teams should adopt it now." You recall that in April the same team also gained a senior site-reliability engineer, and reduced its release frequency from daily to twice-weekly. As the recognised expert on Agile and Lean approaches, what is the soundest assessment?

- A) The halved failure rate vindicates the practice; mandate adoption across all teams.
- B) Three changes coincided (practice, staffing, release cadence), so attribution is currently impossible; moreover, the lower release frequency mechanically flatters the rate's interpretation. Treat trunk-based development as promising, examine which factor carries the effect, and let other teams trial it deliberately rather than by mandate.
- C) The improvement is probably the SRE alone; trunk-based development is irrelevant.
- D) Change failure rate is a flawed metric; ignore the claim.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Three candidate causes changed in the same window: the practice, the personnel, and the cadence. With confounding that thorough, the halving supports no single attribution — and there is a subtler distortion: failure rate is failures per change, and halving the number of changes alters the denominator's composition (fewer, larger, better-rested releases), so even an unchanged underlying capability could show a different rate. Option B holds the full position: credit the improvement as real, refuse the attribution as premature, identify how to learn more (the team's own history, comparison with teams that adopted only one of the changes, or a deliberate trial elsewhere), and — critically for your level — reject the mandate. Acting as a recognised expert and advocate for Agile and Lean approaches means your endorsements move organisations, which is precisely why they must be evidence-proof: an expert who mandates on confounded data spends the community's trust on a guess. Option A converts enthusiasm into policy. Option C commits the same attribution error in reverse. Option D throws away a genuinely useful (if imperfect) signal because it is imperfect. Experimenting with new ways of working to improve delivery across the organisation — your named skill — is exactly this: enthusiasm for the trial, rigour about the inference.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Deduction across a contract and a plan**

The supplier contract states: (i) acceptance of each milestone triggers the associated payment; (ii) if acceptance is not granted or refused within 15 working days of delivery, the milestone is deemed accepted; (iii) milestone 4 ("performance test evidence") is a precondition for milestone 5 commencing. The supplier delivered milestone 4 on 1 July. Your test team, short-staffed, has not examined it. Today is 21 July (14 working days later, given no holidays). The evidence pack is rumoured to be thin. What is the most important immediate implication?

- A) Nothing is urgent; acceptance is a formality.
- B) Tomorrow, milestone 4 will be deemed accepted — triggering payment and unlocking milestone 5 — unless acceptance is granted or refused today or tomorrow; an immediate review-or-refuse decision is required now.
- C) Milestone 5 cannot start for another 15 days regardless.
- D) The rumour should be investigated over the coming weeks.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Chain the clauses against the calendar. Delivery 1 July; deemed acceptance operates 15 working days later; today is day 14 — the guillotine falls tomorrow. Deemed acceptance is not a paperwork technicality: via clause (i) it triggers payment, and via clause (iii) it unlocks milestone 5, meaning the programme would be paying for and building upon performance evidence nobody has read — evidence rumoured to be inadequate. The immediate action set follows: divert reviewing capacity today, and if a proper review genuinely cannot complete in two days, issue a formal refusal or agreed extension before the deadline (a refusal on documented grounds preserves the position; silence surrenders it). Option A mistakes a default for a formality — deemed-acceptance clauses exist precisely to punish consumer-side inaction. Option C misreads (iii): milestone 5 awaits milestone 4's acceptance, which is about to occur by default. Option D's "coming weeks" is the trap phrase: the clock expires in one day. Getting good value out of contracts — your **Commercial management** skill — is substantially the discipline of knowing which clocks are running against you and never learning about them from the supplier's invoice.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Reading a cumulative flow signature across teams**

Two teams consume the same design authority for approvals. Team Alpha's cumulative flow diagram shows its "awaiting approval" band widening steadily over eight weeks; Team Bravo's equivalent band is stable. Alpha submits designs averaging 40 pages; Bravo submits average 6-page decision-focused summaries. The design authority's stated turnaround is 10 working days, which it meets for Bravo and misses for Alpha. Alpha's delivery manager wants you to escalate the authority's "failure to meet turnaround." What does the evidence best support?

- A) Escalate the design authority's under-resourcing; turnaround commitments are being missed.
- B) The differential pattern (one consumer served within standard, one not; submissions differing sevenfold in size) suggests Alpha's submission format is a major driver; coach Alpha to trial Bravo-style decision-focused submissions before escalating, while checking the authority's view of the workload.
- C) Alpha's designs are more complex, so nothing can be compared.
- D) Merge both teams' submissions to average the load.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The comparative structure is the evidence: a single shared service meeting its standard for one consumer and missing it for another, where the consumers differ visibly in what they submit. That pattern points the first inquiry at the differing input, not the shared service — 40-page documents plausibly cost the authority multiples of a 6-page decision summary, and Alpha's widening band is consistent with its own submissions saturating the reviewer. Option B sequences the response correctly: coach the controllable factor first (Alpha trials the leaner format — a cheap, fast, reversible experiment), verify with the authority (their view of the workload is one conversation away and might reveal genuine capacity limits too), and hold escalation in reserve. Escalating first (option A) on this evidence would likely return embarrassingly: "Bravo gets ten-day turnaround; have you looked at what Alpha sends us?" — and a senior delivery manager's escalations must be pre-audited precisely because they carry weight. Option C surrenders to an untested alibi; complexity may differ, which is what the trial and the conversation would surface. Option D is category confusion. The coaching layer matters doubly here: you coach and mentor delivery managers, and teaching Alpha's DM to interrogate their own pipeline before indicting someone else's is a lesson that outlasts this incident.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Continue, pivot, or stop**

A team eight months into private beta presents this evidence: completion rate for the core journey 84% (target 80%); cost per transaction £7.20 against a business-case target of £3.50, flat for four months; user satisfaction 82%; the projected volumes underpinning the business case have been revised down 40% by the policy team following a legislative change; the team proposes 6 more months of beta to "optimise costs." Applying evidence-based life cycle judgement, what is the soundest recommendation?

- A) Continue as proposed; the service works and users like it.
- B) Stop immediately; the cost target is missed.
- C) The decision hinges on the revised economics, not the service quality: with volumes down 40% and unit cost flat at more than double target, the original value proposition needs re-validation before more delivery spend — recommend a short, time-boxed re-examination of the business case (can costs plausibly reach viability at the new volumes? does a pivot — narrower scope, shared platform, different channel mix — restore the case?), with continue/pivot/stop decided on that analysis rather than defaulting to either extension or cancellation.
- D) Continue, but relabel the cost target as aspirational.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Separate the two questions the evidence answers differently. Does the service work? Yes: completion and satisfaction exceed targets — the team has built something users can use. Is it worth continuing to build? Unknown, and the balance has moved twice against it: unit cost is stuck at 2.1× target (flat for four months — the trend matters; a falling £7.20 would read differently), and the demand side has shifted structurally, not cyclically — a 40% volume reduction from legislative change alters the denominator every viability calculation rests on. Option C routes the decision through the only analysis that can settle it, time-boxed so that analysis does not become its own delay, and keeps all three outcomes live: the numbers may yet close (continue), a redesign may restore value (pivot — your skill explicitly includes "change direction"), or the case may have genuinely collapsed (stop — and stopping a well-built service whose economics have failed is a mark of evidence-led maturity, not defeat). Option A funds six more months on affection for a service whose business case may no longer exist; "optimise costs" with a four-month flat line is hope, not a plan. Option B destroys option value symmetric with A's wishfulness — the evidence warrants examination, not execution. Option D — relabelling the failed target — is the one genuinely corrosive choice: it doesn't change the economics, it changes the instrument that measures them. Guiding colleagues through the life cycle at senior level means being the person in the room who insists the continue/pivot/stop question be decided by the case, not the sunk cost.

### Preparation tips

- **Practise at portfolio altitude.** Take your real multi-team data — dependency registers, cross-team flow, escalation logs — and run the aggregation reads from Questions 5 and 11 on it monthly. The patterns in this section are all findable in your own artefacts.
- **Derive, then audit.** For any constraint-based question (or real plan), derive the answer from the constraints, then verify every constraint against the candidate answer. The plausible-pattern trap in Question 6 is the most common senior error precisely because experience supplies convincing patterns.
- **Reconcile the parts of every composite artefact.** Benefits profiles, business cases, escalation papers: sum the allocations, chain the dates, match the ask to the impact. Internal inconsistency is the highest-yield error class at your level.
- **Name the confound before crediting the cause.** When any metric moves, list everything that changed in the window — practice, people, cadence, measurement — before attributing. Your endorsements carry organisational weight; make them evidence-proof.
- **Know which clocks run against you.** Deemed acceptance, option expiry, freeze windows, assessment lead times. In tests and contracts alike, the expensive constraint is the one with a date attached.
- **Rehearse with a coaching lens.** Explaining a worked answer aloud — to a mentee or an empty room — exposes gaps that silent recognition hides, and doubles as preparation for the coaching that is itself part of your assessment at interview.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Reading variance as pathology and trend as noise.** Oscillation around a healthy mean is usually fine; a monotonic slope is usually a message. Confusing the two misdirects senior attention.
- **Accepting the plausible schedule without the date check.** The natural-looking sequence that violates one calculated constraint is the assessment's favourite senior trap — and the roadmap review's favourite real one.
- **Fragmented diagnosis of shared constraints.** Reading dependency data team-by-team hides the platform that is throttling everyone. Aggregate by the receiving column first.
- **Escalating on unaudited evidence.** An escalation that the receiving tier can puncture ("have you seen what Alpha submits?") costs more credibility than no escalation. Pre-audit your own case.
- **Letting service quality answer the viability question.** Users loving a service and the service being worth its cost are independent findings; continue/pivot/stop decisions conflate them at the taxpayer's expense.
- **Moving the measure instead of the number.** Relabelling targets, narrowing definitions, hiding dashboards — any option whose mechanism is changing the instrument rather than the reality is the wrong option, in tests and in programmes.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures fluency with the quantitative material your role genuinely handles — which, at senior level, means business-case arithmetic, budget negotiation, supplier cost structures, cross-team capacity and forecasting, benefits profiles, and trend interpretation robust enough to survive board scrutiny. The operations remain percentages, ratios, rates, averages, and weighted comparisons; the stimuli are denser, the traps more structural, and the consequences of the modelled errors larger.

Typical format: online and timed, 20 to 30 minutes for 15 to 25 questions, data presented in tables, chart descriptions, or prose, an on-screen calculator usually available, and objective scoring reported as accuracy and pace. Senior norm groups mean the comparison population is experienced: the discriminating questions are those where selecting the right figures and structure matters more than the arithmetic itself.

Employers weight numeric assessment heavily at your level because your numbers move real money and real decisions. You negotiate, influence or set budgets in complex environments; you write or input into business cases and communicate business-value propositions; you negotiate with suppliers and are accountable for getting good value from contracts. In each of those settings, the person across the table has their own numbers — and the senior delivery manager who can rebuild a supplier's quote, stress-test a benefits claim, or catch a business case double-count in the meeting rather than after signature is worth a great deal to a department. The test is a compressed rehearsal of exactly that.

Every worked answer shows its arithmetic step by step. Even where you compute correctly, study the step order: under pressure and in negotiation, the visible method is what makes your figure the one the room trusts.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Budget and variance arithmetic** maps to **Financial management**: negotiating, influencing or setting budgets in complex environments requires run rates, committed-versus-actual positions, and variance projections computed quickly and defended clearly.
- **Business-case and benefits calculations** map to **Financial management** ("write or input into business cases and communicate business-value propositions"): net present thinking in simplified form — costs, benefits, break-even, sensitivity to assumptions.
- **Supplier cost modelling** maps to **Commercial management**: negotiating with suppliers and getting good value out of contracts means decomposing quotes into rates, volumes, and conditions, and comparing structures rather than headline prices.
- **Cross-team forecasting and capacity arithmetic** map to **Planning** and **Maintaining delivery momentum**: leading continual planning across services requires throughput-based forecasts, net rates, and honest treatment of uncertainty and outliers.
- **Trend and distribution reading** maps to **Agile and Lean practices** and **Life cycle management**: evidence-based continue/pivot/stop recommendations rest on distinguishing trends from noise, means from medians, and rate changes from denominator changes.
- **Presenting figures** maps to **Communicating between the technical and non-technical**: the final step of every calculation in this role is the sentence a director can act on — accurate, sourced, and honest about its assumptions.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Portfolio budget share**

You oversee four teams with annual budgets: Alpha £1.2m, Bravo £0.9m, Charlie £1.5m, Delta £0.4m. What percentage of the total portfolio budget is Charlie's?

- A) 30%
- B) 33%
- C) 37.5%
- D) 40%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Share = component ÷ total × 100.

Step 1: Total = 1.2 + 0.9 + 1.5 + 0.4 = £4.0m.
Step 2: Charlie's share = 1.5 ÷ 4.0 = 0.375.
Step 3: 0.375 × 100 = 37.5%.

Charlie holds 37.5% of the portfolio. The round total (£4.0m) makes this an accuracy check rather than a difficulty test — which is exactly why it would open a real assessment: early items build the score cheaply for careful candidates and expensively for rushed ones. In portfolio conversations, shares matter because absolute budgets mislead across teams of different sizes; "Charlie is three-eighths of my portfolio spend" is the framing that makes the follow-up question — is it delivering three-eighths of the value? — askable.

**Question 2 (easy) — Day-rate blend for a negotiation**

A supplier proposes a team of 2 architects at £950/day and 4 engineers at £700/day. What is the blended (average) day rate per person?

- A) £783
- B) £800
- C) £825
- D) £850

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Blend = total daily cost ÷ headcount.

Step 1: Architects: 2 × £950 = £1,900.
Step 2: Engineers: 4 × £700 = £2,800.
Step 3: Total daily = £1,900 + £2,800 = £4,700.
Step 4: Blended rate = £4,700 ÷ 6 = £783.33 ≈ £783.

The blend is £783/day — not £825, the simple average of the two rates, which is the planted error: an unweighted average ignores that engineers outnumber architects two to one. Blended rates are negotiation currency: suppliers quote them because they compress the mix into one comparable number, and the buyer who can decompose the blend ("your blend rises £42 if the second architect stays — is that role genuinely needed for phase 2?") negotiates the mix, not just the rate. That decomposition habit is the numerical core of getting good value out of contracts.

**Question 3 (easy) — Benefits break-even**

A proposed automation costs £360,000 to build and £5,000 per month to run. It saves £45,000 per month in caseworker effort. Ignoring discounting, after how many months of operation does it break even?

- A) 8 months
- B) 9 months
- C) 10 months
- D) 12 months

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Break-even = build cost ÷ net monthly benefit.

Step 1: Net monthly benefit = £45,000 − £5,000 = £40,000.
Step 2: Months = £360,000 ÷ £40,000 = 9.

Break-even at 9 months of operation. The planted error is dividing by the gross saving (£360,000 ÷ £45,000 = 8 months), forgetting that running costs offset the benefit every month — the same net-versus-gross structure as queue-drain arithmetic, recurring here at business-case scale. When you input into business cases, state break-even with its assumptions attached: "nine months, assuming the £45k saving realises in full from month one" — because benefits that ramp up over six months push real break-even well past the headline, and the sensitivity sentence is what separates a business-value proposition from a sales pitch.

**Question 4 (easy) — Budget variance percentage**

Your programme's delivery budget for the year is £6.4m. The mid-year forecast projects an outturn of £6.88m. What is the projected overspend as a percentage of budget?

- A) 6.5%
- B) 7%
- C) 7.5%
- D) 8%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Variance % = (forecast − budget) ÷ budget × 100.

Step 1: Overspend = £6.88m − £6.4m = £0.48m.
Step 2: 0.48 ÷ 6.4 = 0.075.
Step 3: 0.075 × 100 = 7.5%.

A projected 7.5% overspend. The denominator is the budget, not the forecast (0.48 ÷ 6.88 ≈ 7% — the planted near-miss at option B). In most departments a variance of this size crosses a formal reporting threshold, and the timing is the actionable part: identified at mid-year, a £480k gap leaves six months to close through descoping, re-phasing, or a variance request — negotiating exactly this is what "negotiate, influence or set budgets in complex environments" means in practice. The same calculation surfaced in month eleven is not a negotiation; it is an apology.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Comparing supplier proposals with different structures**

Two suppliers bid for a 12-month engagement. Supplier P: fixed price £840,000, payable monthly in equal instalments. Supplier Q: £60,000 per month for the first 6 months, then £75,000 per month for the remaining 6, plus a one-off £30,000 transition fee in month 1. Which is cheaper over the full term, and by how much?

- A) P, by £30,000
- B) Q, by £30,000
- C) P, by £5,000
- D) They cost the same

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Total each structure.

Step 1: P = £840,000.
Step 2: Q's first half: 6 × £60,000 = £360,000.
Step 3: Q's second half: 6 × £75,000 = £450,000.
Step 4: Q's transition fee: £30,000.
Step 5: Q total = £360,000 + £450,000 + £30,000 = £840,000.

Identical totals — £840,000 each — so the price decides nothing and the structure decides everything. That is the real lesson: Q's back-loaded profile costs less in the first half (£390,000 including the fee, versus P's £420,000), which matters if budget phasing is tight this financial year; P's flat profile is simpler and transfers no escalation risk; Q's step-up would cost more if the engagement extended at the final rate. A senior negotiator reads equal totals as the start of the comparison — cash-flow phasing, exit points, extension rates — not the end. Assessments plant "same total, different shape" questions to catch candidates who stop at the headline; procurement plants them too.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Cost of delay across a portfolio**

Three initiatives compete for one team slot next quarter. Estimated monthly value once live: initiative X £120,000; Y £80,000; Z £50,000. Each takes the full quarter to build. Choosing X first delays Y and Z by three months each. What is the total cost of delay imposed on the unchosen initiatives if X is chosen first?

- A) £240,000
- B) £360,000
- C) £390,000
- D) £510,000

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Cost of delay = each delayed initiative's monthly value × months delayed.

Step 1: Y delayed 3 months: 3 × £80,000 = £240,000.
Step 2: Z delayed 3 months: 3 × £50,000 = £150,000.
Step 3: Total imposed = £240,000 + £150,000 = £390,000.

Choosing X first forgoes £390,000 from Y and Z — against which X's own three months of earlier value, 3 × £120,000 = £360,000, is the offsetting gain. Note what the comparison reveals: sequencing the highest-value item first is not automatically optimal when durations are equal and values differ this way; the full CD3-style analysis (value ÷ duration) matters when durations differ too. For this question the asked-for figure is £390,000 — read precisely what was asked (the cost imposed, not the net position, not X's gain). In portfolio prioritisation with directors, this arithmetic converts "my initiative is important" into a comparable number — the move that de-personalises sequencing fights, which is half its value.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Weighted average with a data correction**

Your quarterly report shows average cycle time across three teams, weighted by items completed: Alpha 24 items at 5 days average; Bravo 36 items at 8 days; Charlie 20 items at 4 days. After publication, Bravo reports its item count was actually 30, not 36. What is the corrected weighted average cycle time (to one decimal place)?

- A) 5.7 days
- B) 6.1 days
- C) 6.0 days
- D) 5.9 days

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Weighted average = Σ(items × days) ÷ Σ(items).

Step 1: Alpha: 24 × 5 = 120 item-days.
Step 2: Bravo (corrected): 30 × 8 = 240 item-days.
Step 3: Charlie: 20 × 4 = 80 item-days.
Step 4: Total item-days = 120 + 240 + 80 = 440.
Step 5: Total items = 24 + 30 + 20 = 74.
Step 6: Weighted average = 440 ÷ 74 = 5.945… ≈ 5.9 days.

Corrected average: 5.9 days. (The original, with 36 Bravo items: (120 + 288 + 80) ÷ 80 = 488 ÷ 80 = 6.1 — option B, the pre-correction figure left as a trap for those who recompute nothing.) Two disciplines here: weight by volume, never average the averages ((5+8+4)÷3 = 5.7, option A, is the unweighted error); and when source data is corrected, recompute the aggregate and reissue — a report known to contain a stale aggregate quietly spends the credibility of every future figure it carries. At senior level your numbers are quoted onwards without you in the room; their hygiene is your reputation.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Capacity for a coaching commitment**

You commit to coaching each of your five delivery managers fortnightly (one hour each), running a monthly community session (2 hours preparation, 1 hour delivery), and attending two governance boards monthly (each 1.5 hours plus 1 hour preparation). Over a 4-week month (20 working days at 7.2 hours), what percentage of your working time do these commitments consume (to the nearest whole percent)?

- A) 9%
- B) 12%
- C) 15%
- D) 18%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Total each commitment over the month.

Step 1: Coaching: 5 DMs × 1 hour × 2 sessions per month = 10 hours.
Step 2: Community: 2 + 1 = 3 hours.
Step 3: Governance: 2 boards × (1.5 + 1) = 2 × 2.5 = 5 hours.
Step 4: Total = 10 + 3 + 5 = 18 hours.
Step 5: Available = 20 × 7.2 = 144 hours.
Step 6: Share = 18 ÷ 144 = 0.125 = 12.5% ≈ 12%.

About 12% of the month — one working day in eight — before any delivery work, escalation, or planning occurs. The arithmetic is easy; the practice it models is rare: senior people chronically over-commit because each individual promise is small, and only the summed percentage reveals the true load. Running this calculation on your real diary quarterly is the difference between coaching that happens and coaching that gets rescheduled into fiction — and the same audit, run for your delivery managers, is a coaching gift: sustainable pace, your own skill language, applies to leaders too.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Interpreting a benefits realisation table**

A benefits realisation table shows planned versus actual monthly benefit (£000) for the two quarters since go-live:

| Month | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Planned | 40 | 40 | 60 | 60 | 80 | 80 |
| Actual | 20 | 28 | 40 | 52 | 68 | 82 |

Which statement best characterises the position at month 6?

- A) The programme has failed: cumulative actual (£290k) is well short of cumulative planned (£360k).
- B) Benefits are ramping slower than planned but converging: the monthly gap has narrowed from £20k to −£2k, with actual exceeding plan in month 6; the cumulative shortfall of £70k is real but the run rate has caught up.
- C) Benefits have exceeded plan, so realisation is complete.
- D) The numbers are too mixed to interpret.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Read both the levels and the trajectory.

Step 1: Cumulative planned = 40+40+60+60+80+80 = £360k.
Step 2: Cumulative actual = 20+28+40+52+68+82 = £290k.
Step 3: Cumulative shortfall = £70k.
Step 4: Monthly gaps (planned − actual): 20, 12, 20, 8, 12, −2 — narrowing overall, and negative (actual above plan) in month 6.

Option B is the only statement holding both truths: a genuine £70k cumulative shortfall (which option C's "exceeded plan" ignores — one good month does not repay five light ones) and a converged run rate (which option A's "failed" ignores — the trajectory is the strongest predictor of the next quarter). This dual read — cumulative position and current rate — is precisely what boards need from a benefits table and rarely get from a RAG colour: the honest sentence is "£70k behind cumulatively; now running at or above plan; shortfall recoverable by around month 10 if the current rate holds." Communicating business-value propositions includes communicating their recovery arithmetic — and the discipline of never letting either the good news or the bad news travel without the other.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Rebuilding a supplier's "savings" claim**

A supplier proposes replacing 3 contractors (each £680/day, 220 days/year) with their managed service at £400,000/year, claiming it "saves over £50,000 a year." Your own analysis notes the managed service covers only the work of 2.5 contractor-equivalents, and your team would need 0.5 FTE of a civil servant (fully loaded £70,000/year) to absorb the remainder. What is the true annual position?

- A) The claim is right: about £49,000 of savings — close enough to "over £50,000."
- B) Comparing like for like, the managed service plus absorption costs £435,000 against £448,800 for the contractors: a true saving of about £13,800, roughly a quarter of the claim.
- C) The managed service costs £35,000 more per year than the contractors.
- D) The proposal saves £84,000 a year.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Rebuild both sides on a like-for-like basis.

Step 1: Current contractor cost = 3 × £680 × 220 = 3 × £149,600 = £448,800/year.
Step 2: Supplier's implicit comparison: £448,800 − £400,000 = £48,800 — the "over £50,000" claim, rounded generously (and note it is actually under £50,000; the claim inflates twice).
Step 3: Like-for-like adjustment: the service delivers 2.5 of 3 contractor-equivalents, so your side must add 0.5 FTE at £70,000 × 0.5 = £35,000/year.
Step 4: True cost of the proposed arrangement = £400,000 + £35,000 = £435,000.
Step 5: True saving = £448,800 − £435,000 = £13,800/year.

The genuine saving is about £13,800 — real, but roughly a quarter of the claimed figure, and thin enough that a small rate rise or scope shortfall erases it. This is the negotiation-arithmetic skill in its purest form: supplier claims are usually not false, they are un-adjusted — the coverage gap and the absorption cost were simply left off the slide. The senior move in the meeting is to present step 4 as a question ("does the £400k cover all three roles' scope? because our analysis says 2.5") — which either extracts a scope commitment or re-prices the deal, and either outcome is worth more than £13,800. Getting good value out of contracts is this calculation, performed before signature, out loud.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Forecast with scope growth and an uncertainty range**

A programme backlog holds 480 points. Historical scope growth adds 5% of the original backlog per quarter (24 points per quarter, flat). Three teams contribute a combined 120 points per quarter, but one team (40 points/quarter) will be diverted elsewhere for the first quarter. Using these assumptions, in which quarter does the backlog complete, and what should accompany the point estimate?

- A) Quarter 4; no caveats needed with arithmetic this clear.
- B) Quarter 6, accompanied by the assumptions (flat scope growth, stable throughput after Q1) and the sensitivity — for example, scope growth at 10% instead of 5% pushes completion further out again.
- C) Quarter 6, because forecasts should always add a safety quarter to whatever the arithmetic says.
- D) Quarter 5, presented as a firm commitment to the board.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Model quarter by quarter, netting scope growth against throughput.

Step 1: Q1 throughput = 120 − 40 = 80; scope growth +24; net reduction = 80 − 24 = 56. Remaining: 480 − 56 = 424.
Step 2: Q2 onward throughput = 120; net reduction per quarter = 120 − 24 = 96.
Step 3: Q2: 424 − 96 = 328. Q3: 328 − 96 = 232. Q4: 232 − 96 = 136. Q5: 136 − 96 = 40.
Step 4: 40 points remain after Q5, so completion falls early in Q6.

The boundary is the trap: 424 ÷ 96 = 4.4 quarters after Q1, and the tempting rounding — "call it Q5" — leaves 40 points undelivered; option D makes that boundary error and then compounds it by promising the wrong quarter as a commitment. Note the difference between B and C, which state the same quarter: B reaches Q6 by modelling (net rates, quarter walk, remainder check) and presents it with assumptions and sensitivity; C reaches Q6 by adding an arbitrary safety quarter to an unexamined figure — right number, indefensible method, and the method is what a board will probe when the forecast is challenged. Two disciplines make this senior-grade: net the scope growth (throughput alone gives 480 ÷ 120 = 4 quarters, option A's error), and walk the final period explicitly — does the remainder actually reach zero? Forecasts at your level are models with stated assumptions, never firm commitments; converting one into the other is the exact failure your **Planning** skill exists to prevent.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Choosing the honest headline**

Post-launch, your service's monthly transactions grew: 10,000, 11,000, 12,100, 13,310, 14,641 (months 1–5). A colleague drafts the board headline: "Growth accelerating: monthly increases rose from 1,000 to 1,331 transactions." Which assessment is most accurate?

- A) The headline is correct: increases are growing every month.
- B) The absolute increases grow, but the growth rate is constant at 10% per month — the pattern is steady compounding, not acceleration; the honest headline is "sustained 10% monthly growth," and projecting it forward requires knowing when saturation effects will bend the curve.
- C) The growth is linear at roughly 1,150 per month on average.
- D) The data shows growth slowing in percentage terms.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Compute the rates, not just the differences.

Step 1: 11,000 ÷ 10,000 = 1.10 → +10%.
Step 2: 12,100 ÷ 11,000 = 1.10 → +10%.
Step 3: 13,310 ÷ 12,100 = 1.10 → +10%.
Step 4: 14,641 ÷ 13,310 = 1.10 → +10%.

Every month grows exactly 10%: constant-rate compounding. The absolute increments (1,000; 1,100; 1,210; 1,331) grow because the base grows — that is what compounding is, not what acceleration is (acceleration would be a rising rate). The distinction is not pedantry: "accelerating growth" invites a board to extrapolate an ever-steepening curve and to fund capacity for it, while "steady 10% compounding" invites the correct questions — how long can 10% hold, what is the addressable population, when does the S-curve flatten? Option C forces an exponential into a linear frame and will under-predict immediately. Option D is simply wrong on the computed rates. Communicating between the technical and non-technical, at senior level, includes being the person who keeps the mathematical adjectives honest — because the board's capacity decisions inherit whichever verb you let through.

### Preparation tips

- **Rebuild every claim from components.** Supplier savings, benefits totals, blended rates: recompute from the underlying figures before accepting any headline. The gap between claimed and rebuilt numbers is where negotiations are won.
- **Drill the six senior structures:** weighted averages (never average averages), net rates (benefit minus running cost, throughput minus scope growth), like-for-like adjustment, cost of delay, variance against the right denominator, and compounding versus linear growth.
- **Walk boundaries explicitly.** Break-even months, completion quarters, threshold crossings — compute the final period's remainder rather than rounding by feel; boundary errors are the most common senior slip.
- **Attach assumptions and sensitivity to every forecast.** Practise writing the one-sentence caveat ("assumes flat scope growth; at 10% growth, add a quarter") until it is reflexive. Boards trust the figure that arrives with its fragility stated.
- **Run the diary audit and the portfolio shares on your real data.** Your own budgets, cycle times, and commitments are the ideal practice set — and auditing them is directly useful work.
- **Present method, not just results, when practising aloud.** In negotiations and boards, the visible derivation is what makes your number the reference number in the room.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Averaging averages.** Weight by volume, always. The unweighted blend is the single most planted senior distractor.
- **Accepting un-adjusted comparisons.** Coverage gaps, absorption costs, transition fees, conditional discounts — the missing adjustment is usually on the other party's slide for a reason.
- **Using the wrong denominator for variance.** Overspend divides by budget, change divides by original; the near-miss option is always the other denominator.
- **Confusing compounding with acceleration, and increments with rates.** Compute the ratio between periods before choosing a verb.
- **Netting only one side.** Gross savings without running costs, throughput without scope growth, benefits without ramp-up — every forecast has a subtraction someone forgot.
- **Presenting point estimates as commitments.** A model is not a promise; the assessment penalises the conflation mildly, and governance penalises it severely.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment measures the precision with which you extract, test, and act on written meaning — using the document classes a senior delivery manager actually confronts: contract schedules and variation clauses, assessment reports, board minutes, policy and spend-control extracts, benefits narratives, and escalation correspondence. A passage is presented; questions probe what it states, implies, permits, requires, and — critically — leaves unsettled.

Formats: true/false/cannot-say against the passage alone; strongest-inference selection; best-summary; author's-purpose; and argument evaluation (does the evidence support the conclusion drawn?). Timing is typically 15 to 25 minutes for 15 to 30 questions. Senior variants use denser text with layered conditions, embedded exceptions, and rhetorical framing engineered to reward the reader who separates what a document says from what its author wants the reader to take away.

Employers weight verbal reasoning at your level because your reading has consequences and your relaying has reach. You mediate between people and represent positions at all levels; you moderate difficult discussions about high-risk topics within constrained timescales; you speak on behalf of the community to large audiences. Every one of those acts begins with exact comprehension: a misread contract clause becomes a commercial exposure, a misrelayed board condition becomes a programme working to the wrong gate, and an overclaimed inference repeated to a director becomes your credibility spent on someone else's imprecision. Senior people are also the quality control for others' reading — the delivery managers you coach will relay what you confirm.

The cardinal discipline is unchanged from every level: the passage is the universe. "Cannot say" is correct whenever the text does not settle the statement, however loudly experience votes. What changes at senior level is the sophistication of the temptations — plausible commercial instinct, familiar governance patterns, and well-written advocacy are all engineered into the distractors.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Precise comprehension of conditions** maps to **Commercial management**: identifying appropriate contractual frameworks, negotiating with suppliers, and getting good value from contracts all rest on exact reading of obligations, triggers, exceptions, and time limits.
- **True/false/cannot-say discipline** maps to **Communicating between the technical and non-technical**: mediating between people and managing expectations at all levels requires relaying exactly what was written or decided — with every qualifier intact — because your relays are treated as authoritative.
- **Inference calibration** maps to **Life cycle management** and your evidence-based continue/pivot/stop judgement: assessment reports and evaluation evidence state findings; deciding what they support without overclaiming is the senior reading skill.
- **Argument evaluation** maps to **Financial management** ("write or input into business cases"): business cases and escalation papers are arguments, and your role is to strengthen sound ones and catch overreaching ones before they reach boards.
- **Purpose and framing detection** maps to **Team dynamics and collaboration** and your mediation duties: in disputes, each party's account is advocacy; reading what is asserted versus what is framed is the precondition of fair mediation.
- **Summary fidelity** maps to speaking on behalf of, and representing, the community to large audiences: the community's positions must survive your compression intact.

### Practice questions

Passage for Questions 1–4 (a contract variation clause):

"Either party may propose a variation to the Services by written notice. No variation shall have effect unless agreed in writing by both parties' authorised representatives. Where a variation increases the Charges by more than 10% of the annual contract value, the Customer's approval must additionally be confirmed at its commercial board. The Supplier shall not unreasonably refuse a variation proposed by the Customer that reduces the scope of the Services, provided that the Customer remains liable for costs already reasonably incurred in respect of the descoped Services. For the avoidance of doubt, oral agreements, custom and practice, and conduct of the parties shall not constitute agreement to any variation."

**Question 1 (easy) — True, false, or cannot say**

Statement: "A variation agreed verbally by both parties' authorised representatives has contractual effect."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, if later documented

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Two clauses close this decisively: "No variation shall have effect unless agreed in writing," and the avoidance-of-doubt sentence expressly excludes oral agreements from constituting agreement. A verbal agreement — even between the correct representatives — therefore has no effect: false. Option D smuggles in a rescue ("if later documented") that changes the case: once documented in writing by authorised representatives it would be a written agreement, but the statement as put describes a verbal agreement, and statements are judged as put. The workplace force of this clause is worth internalising: suppliers' project managers and your delivery managers agree things on calls every week, and this clause means none of it varies the contract — which protects both sides from drift and means your governance habit ("confirming in writing what we agreed today...") is not bureaucracy but contract operation. Reading no-oral-variation clauses accurately is foundational to taking responsibility for complex supplier relationships.

**Question 2 (moderate) — True, false, or cannot say**

Statement: "A variation increasing the Charges by 8% of the annual contract value requires approval at the Customer's commercial board."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, because all variations need board approval

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The commercial-board requirement attaches only "where a variation increases the Charges by more than 10% of the annual contract value." An 8% increase does not cross the threshold, so the additional board confirmation is not required by this clause — the ordinary route (written agreement by authorised representatives) suffices. The statement asserts a requirement the clause does not impose: false. Note the reasoning shape: thresholds create two regimes, and the test is which side of the line the case falls — the same shape as spend-control delegation and severity triggers. One caution for real life, which the question's strict textual answer cannot carry: a department's own internal governance may impose stricter rules than the contract; the clause tells you what the contract requires, not what your organisation additionally demands. Distinguishing those two sources of obligation — and never asserting "the contract requires X" when you mean "our policy requires X" — is precisely the exactness your negotiating counterparties will test.

**Question 3 (moderate) — True, false, or cannot say**

Statement: "If the Customer proposes a descoping variation, the Supplier must accept it."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, provided incurred costs are paid

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The clause says the Supplier "shall not unreasonably refuse" a descoping variation — which is not an obligation to accept. A reasonable refusal remains permitted (the clause's structure presupposes it), and the proviso about incurred costs conditions the Customer's entitlement, not the Supplier's obligation to say yes. "Must accept" overstates "must not unreasonably refuse," so the statement is false — and option D fails for the same reason with the proviso bolted on: paying incurred costs removes one ground of reasonable refusal but does not extinguish the category. This distinction — absolute obligations versus qualified ones ("reasonable endeavours," "shall not unreasonably...") — is among the most commercially consequential in contract reading: negotiating with suppliers, your named skill, is frequently a negotiation about exactly which of these verb forms lands in the drafting. A senior delivery manager who relays "they have to accept our descope" to a programme board has converted a qualified right into an absolute one, and the correction will arrive at the least convenient moment.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Strongest inference**

Which of the following is best supported by the clause as drafted?

- A) The drafters anticipated disputes about informal variation and deliberately closed that route.
- B) The Supplier drafted the clause to disadvantage the Customer.
- C) Variations below 10% do not require written agreement.
- D) The Customer can descope without any cost consequence.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Work the options. C is contradicted: the writing requirement applies to all variations; the 10% threshold adds a further step, it does not remove the base one. D is contradicted by the express proviso preserving liability for incurred costs. B asserts authorship and motive — nothing in the text identifies who drafted it, and the clause distributes protections both ways (the Customer gets protection from drift and a qualified descoping right; the Supplier gets cost protection and refusal grounds). A is the calibrated inference: an "avoidance of doubt" sentence expressly excluding oral agreement, custom and practice, and conduct is drafting that exists because informal-variation disputes are common — the clause's belt-and-braces structure is itself evidence of anticipated failure modes. This is inference at the right altitude: from textual features to drafting purpose, without inventing facts. Reading contracts for why clauses exist — not just what they say — is what lets you predict where a supplier relationship will generate friction, which is the anticipatory layer of commercial management.

Passage for Questions 5–8 (a service assessment report extract):

"The panel was impressed by the team's user research practice and its clear articulation of user needs. However, the service did not demonstrate that it can meet demand at scale: performance testing was conducted against 40% of projected peak volume, and the team could not evidence a tested failover arrangement. The panel also noted that two of the six team members responsible for operating the live service are contractors whose engagements end within three months, with no documented knowledge-transfer plan. The panel's decision is not met. The service may not progress to public beta until a reassessment demonstrates: (1) performance at projected peak volume; (2) a tested failover arrangement; (3) a credible operational staffing and knowledge-transfer plan. The panel encourages the team to seek reassessment promptly and commends the strength of its user-centred practice."

**Question 5 (easy) — True, false, or cannot say**

Statement: "The service failed the assessment primarily because of weaknesses in user research."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, since research is always decisive

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The report praises user research twice — the panel was "impressed by the team's user research practice" and closes by commending "the strength of its user-centred practice." The not-met decision rests on three stated grounds: unproven performance at scale, no tested failover, and an operational staffing risk without a knowledge-transfer plan. The statement inverts the report's structure, so it is false. Easy as this is, it models a real relaying hazard: summaries of assessment outcomes travel fast and lossy ("we failed the assessment" → "the panel didn't like the service" → informal blame landing on the wrong practice), and the senior delivery manager's job — contributing to the assessment of other teams and guiding colleagues through phases — includes keeping the failure's actual grounds attached to the outcome as it travels. A team whose genuine strength gets blamed for a failure it did not cause loses both morale and the incentive that praise was meant to create.

**Question 6 (moderate) — True, false, or cannot say**

Statement: "If the team demonstrates performance at projected peak volume at reassessment, the service may progress to public beta."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, because performance was the main concern

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The report sets three numbered conditions conjunctively: peak-volume performance, a tested failover arrangement, and a credible staffing and knowledge-transfer plan. Meeting one of three does not satisfy a conjunction — the statement's "if... then may progress" claims sufficiency for a condition the report makes merely necessary. False. The near-trap is option C: could one argue the report doesn't say meeting one condition wouldn't suffice? No — "may not progress until a reassessment demonstrates: (1)... (2)... (3)..." makes all three the gate; the text settles it. Necessary-versus-sufficient confusion is the single most common misreading of governance conditions, and its workplace cost is concrete: a team that sprints on performance testing alone arrives at reassessment to fail on failover and staffing, burning an assessment slot and a quarter. When you guide teams through life cycle gates, the first coaching act is often just this: converting a conditions list into a plan that covers every numbered item, not the most technically interesting one.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Strongest inference**

Which is best supported by the report?

- A) The contractors are underperforming and should be replaced.
- B) The panel regards operational continuity — not just current capability — as within the assessment's scope.
- C) Performance testing at 40% of peak is contrary to government policy in all cases.
- D) The team will pass reassessment within three months.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The panel made staffing tenure and knowledge transfer a formal condition of progression — treating who will operate the service in three months, and whether their knowledge survives their departure, as assessable. That is direct evidence for B: the panel's scope includes continuity of operation, not merely the service's present state. Option A converts a tenure-and-transfer risk into a performance judgement the report never makes — the contractors may be excellent, which is precisely why their departure without knowledge transfer is a risk. Option C generalises one panel's expectation into universal policy on no textual basis. Option D predicts an outcome the report cannot underwrite (it encourages prompt reassessment; encouragement is not forecast). The senior reading skill here is scope inference: assessment reports reveal, through what they condition on, what the assessing regime considers itself entitled to examine — and a senior delivery manager preparing other teams for assessment (your role explicitly includes contributing to such assessments) reads reports partly to map that scope, so the next team evidences continuity before the panel asks.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Summary fidelity under compression**

You must summarise this outcome in one message to the programme director. Which is most faithful and useful?

- A) "Assessment failed. Major concerns across the board. Team needs support."
- B) "Not met, on three specific grounds: performance untested at peak (only 40%), no tested failover, and an operational staffing/knowledge-transfer gap as two contractor engagements end within three months. User-centred practice was explicitly commended. Reassessment requires all three fixed; team is planning a prompt return. Support that would help: [X]."
- C) "Mostly positive — user research was praised. A few technical items to tidy before beta."
- D) "The panel penalised us for using contractors, which is unfair and worth challenging."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Compression tests fidelity in both directions. Option A deflates: "concerns across the board" contradicts the report's explicit commendation, and a director reading it will misallocate worry — possibly toward the research practice the panel praised. Option C inflates: a not-met decision with three gating conditions is not "a few technical items," and a director soothed by this summary will be ambushed by the reassessment timeline and any dependent milestones. Option D converts a condition (documented knowledge transfer) into a grievance (penalised for contractors) — the report does not object to contractors; it objects to their departing with undocumented knowledge — and proposes spending the director's capital challenging a misreading. Option B carries the decision, all three grounds with their specifics, the commendation (which matters: it protects the team's strength from the lossy-summary effect of Question 5), the forward plan, and an ask — because a message to a director should end with what would help. This is your representation duty in miniature: when you speak on behalf of teams to senior audiences, both the bad news and the good must survive your compression intact, and the message should always leave the recipient knowing what to do with it.

Passage for Questions 9–10 (a board minute):

"The Board considered the paper on the consolidation of the two case-management platforms. The Director of Operations expressed strong support. The Finance Director noted that the stated savings of £1.4m over three years depended on decommissioning timelines that had slipped twice before, and asked that the savings profile be re-baselined. The Board endorsed the direction of travel, requested a re-baselined savings profile and an independent technical assurance review before the business case is submitted for approval, and agreed that no procurement activity should commence in the interim. Action: Programme SRO to return to the January Board."

**Question 9 (moderate) — True, false, or cannot say**

Statement: "The Board approved the consolidation business case."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True in principle

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The minute's verbs are doing precise work: the Board "endorsed the direction of travel" — and simultaneously established that the business case has not yet been submitted for approval ("before the business case is submitted for approval"), requested two preconditions (re-baselined savings, independent assurance), and prohibited interim procurement. A business case that has not been submitted cannot have been approved, and the minute says so in terms: false. Option D ("true in principle") is the seductive one — "endorsed the direction of travel" feels like approval-in-principle — but the statement says "approved the business case," a specific governance event with specific consequences (spending authority, procurement commencement), and the minute expressly blocks those consequences. Governance language is a vocabulary of graduated commitment — noted, discussed, endorsed, agreed, approved — and each grade authorises different action. Relaying "the Board approved it" after this meeting could start procurement the Board explicitly forbade; the senior delivery manager is the person in the room whose relays preserve the grade.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Reading advocacy inside the record**

Comparing the minute's treatment of the two directors, which reading is best supported?

- A) The Finance Director opposes consolidation.
- B) The record shows support qualified by a specific, evidenced concern: the FD challenged the savings profile's credibility (citing two prior slips) without opposing the direction — and the Board's conditions adopted the FD's concern almost exactly.
- C) The Director of Operations' support was decisive in the Board's endorsement.
- D) The Board considered the savings figure of £1.4m to be overstated.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Track what each participant is minuted as doing, and what the Board then did. The FD did not oppose: they "noted" a dependency (savings resting on twice-slipped decommissioning timelines) and "asked" for re-baselining — challenge to the evidence base, not the proposal. The Board's own conditions (re-baselined profile, assurance review, procurement pause) map almost one-to-one onto that concern, which is the textual basis for B's final clause. Option A converts qualified scrutiny into opposition — a misreading that, repeated in a corridor, manufactures a conflict the record does not contain and poisons the FD's future scrutiny by making it "opposition." Option C claims causal weight ("decisive") the minute cannot support — it records the support's existence, not its influence. Option D overreaches similarly: requesting re-baselining treats the figure as insufficiently evidenced, not as overstated; the re-baseline could confirm £1.4m. This is the mediation-grade reading your role requires: in disputes and in governance, parties' positions must be carried at their actual strength — neither inflated into conflict nor flattened into consensus — because you will be the one representing those positions to the people who were not in the room.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Argument evaluation in an escalation paper**

An escalation paper argues: "The shared platform team's API delays have now impacted three of our four service teams, costing an estimated 22 team-days this quarter. The platform team's own roadmap shows their capacity fully committed for two quarters. Unless the programme intervenes, these delays will continue and worsen as integration demand grows. We therefore request that the programme board direct the platform team to prioritise our four integration requests above its other commitments."

Which is the most accurate critical assessment?

- A) The argument is sound and the request should be granted as asked.
- B) The evidence establishes a real, quantified, likely-worsening problem, but the requested remedy does not follow from it: the paper never examines what the platform team's other commitments are, so "prioritise us above everything" may simply relocate the harm to other consumers — the evidence supports a programme-level prioritisation decision across all demands, not a directed answer to it.
- C) The argument fails because 22 team-days is a trivial cost.
- D) The argument fails because platform teams cannot be directed by programme boards.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Separate the argument's descriptive and prescriptive layers. Descriptively it is strong: multi-team impact (three of four), quantified cost (22 team-days), a capacity fact from the platform's own roadmap, and a credible worsening mechanism (growing integration demand) — this is a well-evidenced problem statement. Prescriptively it leaps: the remedy assumes the four integration requests outrank everything else on the platform's roadmap, yet the paper presents no information about what those other commitments are or whom they serve — the platform's capacity may be committed to another programme's statutory deadline. The evidenced conclusion is that a cross-demand prioritisation decision is needed at the tier that can see all the demands; the requested conclusion pre-empts that decision in the requester's favour. Option B catches exactly this gap. Option A misses it. Options C and D fail on their own terms: 22 team-days is a material quantified cost, and the governance question of what boards can direct is not addressed by the passage either way — inventing a rule is the same sin as inventing a priority. The senior relevance is double: you write papers like this (and should write them to B's standard — evidence the problem, request the decision forum, resist pre-empting the answer), and you receive them from the delivery managers you coach, where spotting the evidence–remedy gap before the board does is the coaching.

**Question 12 (challenging) — True, false, or cannot say across two texts**

Text 1 (policy extract): "Departments must obtain Cabinet Office approval for any digital or technology spend over £1m, unless the spend falls within a pre-agreed exemption category."
Text 2 (email from a programme office colleague): "Good news — the £1.3m platform renewal falls within our pre-agreed hosting exemption, so no Cabinet Office approval is needed. I've confirmed the exemption category covers hosting renewals up to £1.5m."

Statement: "The £1.3m platform renewal requires Cabinet Office approval."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) False, because the colleague has confirmed the exemption applies

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Chain the texts carefully — and notice what each can and cannot establish. Text 1 sets the rule: over £1m requires approval unless a pre-agreed exemption applies. £1.3m exceeds £1m, so everything turns on the exemption. Text 2 asserts the exemption applies — but Text 2 is a colleague's email making claims ("falls within," "I've confirmed... covers hosting renewals up to £1.5m"), not the exemption instrument itself. The passages give you a rule plus a testimony about the rule's exception; whether the renewal is genuinely hosting, whether the claimed category exists as described, and whether the confirmation is authoritative are all outside what the texts establish. The statement can be neither confirmed (the exemption may well apply exactly as claimed) nor denied (the email may be wrong — misclassified spend, stale threshold, informal "confirmation"): cannot say. Option D is the designed trap, and its design is the lesson — it treats an assertion within the evidence as settlement of the evidence, the precise error senior people are paid to catch. In real spend-control practice, the difference between "our programme office believes the exemption covers this" and "the exemption instrument, checked, covers this" is the difference between assurance and hope — and a senior delivery manager signing a submission on the strength of the email has adopted its risk. Verification of load-bearing claims, before relaying them upward, is the reading discipline this entire assessment exists to measure.

### Preparation tips

- **Read verbs at governance grade.** Noted, endorsed, agreed, approved; shall, shall not unreasonably, reasonable endeavours; met, not met, conditional. Build the habit of quoting the exact verb when relaying — it is the meaning.
- **Map conditions as conjunctions or disjunctions before answering.** Numbered lists after "until" are almost always conjunctive; "either... or" and "unless" create alternative routes. Mark which structure you are in.
- **Separate the text from testimony about the text.** A claim quoted inside a passage (a colleague's confirmation, a supplier's assurance) is evidence that the claim was made, not that it is true. Statements resting on such claims are usually "cannot say."
- **Practise argument surgery on real papers.** Take an escalation paper or business case and draw the line where evidence ends and remedy begins; ask whether the remedy follows or merely benefits the author. This is simultaneously test practice and board preparation.
- **Compress real documents to one message, then audit against the original.** Every decision, every condition, every commendation, correct verb grade, and an ask. Your summaries are load-bearing; train them under audit.
- **When a threshold appears, compute the case against it explicitly.** 8% versus 10%, £1.3m versus £1m — write the comparison down; threshold questions are lost to assumption, not arithmetic.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Converting qualified obligations into absolute ones.** "Shall not unreasonably refuse" is not "must accept"; "endorsed the direction" is not "approved the case." The overclaimed relay is the senior reader's signature error.
- **Treating one met condition as satisfying a conjunctive gate.** Count the numbered conditions; plan and answer against all of them.
- **Accepting in-passage assertions as established facts.** Especially confirmations, assurances, and "I've checked" claims — the test (and the audit) distinguishes testimony from verification.
- **Reading scrutiny as opposition.** A challenge to the evidence base is not resistance to the proposal; relaying it as such manufactures conflict and chills future scrutiny.
- **Letting a well-evidenced problem carry an unexamined remedy.** Grade the descriptive and prescriptive layers separately; the gap between them is where escalation papers fail.
- **Importing commercial or governance experience as fact.** Your instincts about how exemptions, panels, and suppliers usually behave are hypotheses; the passage is the evidence. "Cannot say" is a senior answer, not a hedge.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement test (SJT) presents realistic role dilemmas and asks you to identify the most effective response, the least effective, best-and-worst pairs, or to rate each option's effectiveness. Scoring reflects the consensus of experienced practitioners and occupational psychologists on effective professional behaviour — user focus, honest information flow, proportionate escalation, empowerment over substitution, inclusion, and sustainable delivery — calibrated to the seniority of the role.

Typical format: online, 20 to 35 minutes, 10 to 20 scenarios, generously timed. At senior level the scenarios change character: the dilemmas involve senior stakeholders, contracted suppliers, cross-organisational ambiguity, and the second-order consequences of your choices — because at your level, every response is also a precedent, a signal to the delivery managers who watch how you handle things, and a data point in your organisation's culture.

Employers weight SJTs heavily for senior delivery managers because the role is defined by its judgement calls. You are the main point of escalation: what reaches you is what process could not resolve. You mediate between people, moderate difficult discussions about high-risk topics within constrained timescales, coach delivery managers through their own dilemmas, and take responsibility for supplier relationships when they strain. There is rarely a clean option; the assessment measures whether you consistently find the response that is honest, proportionate, early, and capability-building — under conditions engineered to make the flattering, dramatic, or avoidant options tempting.

A senior-specific note: several scenarios test the boundary between your accountability and others' — when to act, when to coach the person whose job it is to act, and when to route a decision to the tier that owns it. Getting that boundary right is the difference between a senior leader and a very busy one.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Escalation and unblocking scenarios** map to **Maintaining delivery momentum**: addressing the most complicated risks, issues and dependencies — including where ownership sits outside every team or nowhere — and finding innovative ways to unblock.
- **Coaching scenarios** map to your accountability to coach and mentor delivery managers: the SJT tests whether you build their capability and let them grow through difficulty, or absorb their problems and cap their development.
- **Supplier scenarios** map to **Commercial management**: taking responsibility for complex supplier relationships, negotiating firmly and fairly, and knowing when the commercial machinery — and which tier of it — should engage.
- **Stakeholder and mediation scenarios** map to **Communicating between the technical and non-technical**: mediating between people, strengthening relationships, managing expectations, and moderating high-risk discussions within constrained timescales.
- **Life cycle and evidence scenarios** map to **Life cycle management**: holding the evidence line on continue/pivot/stop decisions and phase gates, under pressure from sunk cost, enthusiasm, and dates.
- **Team-system scenarios** map to **Team dynamics and collaboration**: reading team health signals (including Agile health checks), rectifying dynamics, choosing the right feedback mode, and accelerating team development without forcing it.
- **Financial scenarios** map to **Financial management**: budget pressure, business-case integrity, and the escalation judgement when money and honesty intersect.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — The delivery manager who brings you everything**

A delivery manager you line-manage, six months in post and capable, brings you every non-trivial decision: escalations they could run, stakeholder messages they could send, process choices their team could make. Each individual request is reasonable; the pattern is not. What is the most effective response?

- A) Keep deciding for them; it is faster and the decisions are sound.
- B) Tell them to stop bringing you things.
- C) Name the pattern kindly and explicitly, reset the contract — "bring me your proposed decision and reasoning, not the decision itself; I'll add value where I disagree" — and hold the line by asking "what would you do?" whenever they revert, while making it safe to get some calls wrong.
- D) Escalate the dependency problem to your own manager.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The pattern is a coaching failure in the making, and both blunt options entrench it differently: option A trains the dependency deeper (each rescued decision confirms that decisions live with you), while option B removes the support without building the capability, teaching them only to hide uncertainty. Option C restructures the interaction: the propose-first contract keeps you informed and available while relocating the cognitive work — and the deciding act — back where it belongs; the "what would you do?" reflex converts each regression into a rep; and the explicit safety ("some calls will be wrong, that's the cost of you growing") addresses the fear that usually drives the pattern. This is the literal content of your role — coach and mentor delivery managers — and the deeper senior lesson: your capacity is the organisation's bottleneck, and every decision you retain that someone else could make is flow you have stolen from yourself. Option D outsources a development conversation that is squarely yours. Watch the SJT signature: the best coaching options combine naming the pattern, restructuring the mechanism, and preserving safety — not choosing between kindness and standards.

**Question 2 (easy) — The director's corridor commitment**

At a leadership away day, a director tells you enthusiastically that they have "agreed with the minister's office" that your programme's flagship service will launch in March — three months earlier than your evidence-based plan supports. Nothing is in writing yet. What is the most effective response?

- A) Congratulate them and re-plan to March; the commitment is made.
- B) Promptly and privately give the director the evidence picture: what March would require, what the current plan supports, the specific risks of the gap, and options (descoped March launch, phased launch, June with confidence) — so they can manage the expectation they have created while it is still soft.
- C) Ignore it until it arrives through formal channels.
- D) Tell your teams to start working weekends now to make March feasible.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The commitment is real as a political fact and soft as a planning fact — nothing is in writing, which means the director still has room to shape what "launch in March" turns out to mean, but only if they get the evidence before the expectation hardens. Option B moves at the speed the situation rewards: private (the director must be able to adjust without losing face), evidence-based (what March requires, what the gap is), and option-rich — the descoped or phased March launch is often the move that satisfies the political need and the delivery reality simultaneously, and bringing it shows you are solving the director's problem, not just defending your plan. Option A converts an unexamined corridor remark into a plan your evidence contradicts — and when June arrives anyway, the failure will be attributed to delivery, not to the corridor. Option C gambles that the commitment will die quietly; commitments to ministers' offices do not — they compound. Option D spends your teams' health on a date no evidence supports, before even testing whether the date is negotiable. Managing stakeholder expectations and moderating difficult discussions about high-risk topics within constrained timescales — your named skill — is precisely this: reaching the expectation while it is still wet.

**Question 3 (easy) — The health check you administered**

You run quarterly Agile health checks across your teams. This quarter, one team's scores dropped sharply on "confidence in leadership" — and the free-text comments make clear the leadership in question is their delivery manager, whom you coach and rate highly. The delivery manager has seen the scores and is visibly shaken. What is the most effective response?

- A) Reassure the delivery manager that surveys exaggerate and the team will come round.
- B) Share the scores with the team and ask them to be more constructive.
- C) Support the delivery manager through it without dismissing the data: help them read the signal seriously but proportionately, plan how they will respond to the team (acknowledgement, listening session, visible changes), and increase your coaching support through the response — treating the episode as recoverable and developmental.
- D) Begin quietly planning the delivery manager's move to another team.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Two duties collide: care for a person you rate and coach, and respect for a team's clearly expressed signal — and the effective response refuses to sacrifice either. Option C holds both: the data is treated as real (dismissing it, option A, would coach the delivery manager into the exact defensiveness that deepens leadership-confidence problems), the response is theirs to lead (which is what rebuilds the team's confidence — a rescue by you would confirm the team's doubt), and your role intensifies as coach precisely when coaching is most valuable: helping them design the acknowledgement, the listening, and the visible change, and steadying them emotionally so they can do it non-defensively. Your skill language is exact here: identify issues through Agile health checks and help to stimulate the right responses — the instrument worked; the response is the skill. Option B weaponises the survey against its authors and would end honest responses permanently. Option D converts one bad quarter into a verdict, skipping the recovery that — handled well — often becomes the delivery manager's most formative episode and the team's proof that feedback works. The senior pattern: when someone you sponsor receives hard feedback, your credibility is now attached to how seriously they take it — sponsor the response, not the denial.

**Question 4 (moderate) — The supplier negotiation with an information gift**

During a contract extension negotiation, the supplier's delivery lead — with whom you have an excellent working relationship — privately lets slip that their firm has priced the extension assuming your department has no realistic alternative, and that their account director "expects you to push back to about 8%, so there's room." What is the most effective use of this information?

- A) Use it fully and immediately: open by demanding far more than 8%, citing what you were told.
- B) Use the substance without burning the source: sharpen your negotiating position with independent evidence (benchmarks, alternative options, scope levers) that justifies the stronger push on its own merits, and never reveal or rely on the slip — while noting privately that the relationship's informality now needs more careful boundaries on your side too.
- C) Report the delivery lead to their account director for unprofessional disclosure.
- D) Ignore the information entirely; using it at all would be improper.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Weigh three things: negotiating duty, source protection, and your own professional boundaries. The information is not stolen or procured — it was volunteered — and your duty to obtain good value from contracts (your named skill) does not permit you to un-know that the price contains acknowledged headroom. But option A converts the slip into ammunition in a way that identifies its source within one conversation ("cited what you were told" has exactly one possible origin), destroying a working relationship, the delivery lead's standing, and every future channel of informal candour — a catastrophic exchange rate for one negotiation round. Option B captures the value legitimately: knowing headroom exists tells you to push harder and hold longer, but the push is built on independently defensible evidence — benchmarks, alternatives, descoping levers — so the position stands on its own merits and the source is never load-bearing. The final clause matters at your level: a counterpart who leaks their own side's position once will assume reciprocity; the relationship needs warmth and firmer boundaries. Option C punishes candour with betrayal. Option D confuses fastidiousness with ethics — you cannot unhear it, and negotiating deliberately below your information is not neutrality, it is a donation of public money. Negotiating with suppliers, done at senior level, is exactly this: full use of your position, zero spending of people.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Mediating the product-architecture standoff**

A programme-critical decision is deadlocked: the lead product manager insists on shipping a politically visible feature set by September; the lead architect insists the platform migration must complete first or the feature work will be built on infrastructure being decommissioned. Both have escalated to you, each asking you to overrule the other. Their working relationship has deteriorated into copied-in emails and meeting avoidance. What is the most effective response?

- A) Rule for the architect; technical foundations outlast political moments.
- B) Rule for the product manager; user-facing value outranks internal plumbing.
- C) Refuse the overrule framing: get them in a room, establish the shared facts (what does September actually require? what does the migration actually block, and when?), surface the options neither has explored under adversarial framing (partial migration, feature subset on new infrastructure, thin September release), facilitate toward a joint recommendation — and separately name the email-and-avoidance pattern to both as unacceptable for leaders, whatever the outcome.
- D) Send the decision to the programme board with both positions attached.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Adversarial escalation has collapsed a multi-dimensional trade-off into a binary — and both binary options (A and B) share the deeper flaw: whichever side you crown, you teach both leaders that deadlock summons a verdict, guaranteeing you every future dispute, and the loser's expertise disengages exactly when implementation needs it. Option C does the senior mediation work your role names (mediate between people and strengthen relationships): re-establishing shared facts first, because deadlocked parties are usually arguing from divergent factual pictures — "the migration blocks everything" and "September needs everything" are both typically softer than advertised — and the option space between them (phased migration, subset releases) only becomes visible when the frame shifts from winning to solving. The behavioural clause is not decoration: copied-in emails and meeting avoidance among senior leaders is a culture signal their teams are already metabolising, and addressing the conduct separately from the content — with both parties symmetrically — is what strengthening relationships means when relationships have degraded. Option D has a legitimate core (some trade-offs genuinely belong at board level) but deployed here it is premature: sending up an unmediated dispute exports noise, not a decision — the board will ask what you tried, and "nothing" is the answer option D contains. Escalate the decision if mediation surfaces a genuine strategic fork; never escalate the mediation itself.

**Question 6 (moderate) — The evidence against the flagship**

Your department's flagship transformation — publicly announced, ministerially sponsored, 18 months in — shows accumulating evidence of trouble in the teams you oversee: benefits assumptions invalidated by policy change, unit costs double the case, and two consecutive assessments passed only narrowly with conditions. Your programme director, who champions the programme, asks you to "keep the noise down while we get through the spending review." What is the most effective response?

- A) Comply; the spending review matters more than internal reporting hygiene.
- B) Refuse loudly and copy the SRO.
- C) Tell the programme director you cannot suppress evidence-based reporting, but offer what they legitimately need: a rigorous, fair account — problems, their causes, the recovery options, and what the programme has demonstrably achieved — so the spending review case rests on defensible ground; and if pressure to suppress continues, escalate your concern through your management line or the SRO with the evidence, not the grievance.
- D) Keep reporting accurately but more quietly, in documents few people read.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Parse what is being asked before choosing how to refuse it: "keep the noise down" spans a spectrum from the illegitimate (suppress the evidence) to the legitimate (stop unstructured alarm, present the position rigorously) — and option C answers the whole spectrum: it refuses the suppression cleanly while giving the director the professionally honourable version of what they want, which is a case that survives scrutiny. This reframe is frequently the move that dissolves the pressure: a spending review case built on "no problems here" is fragile to the first Cabinet Office question, while "problems identified, understood, costed, with recovery underway" is both true and stronger. The escalation clause is calibrated correctly: it triggers on continued suppression pressure, routes through the line or the SRO, and carries evidence — the senior escalation discipline throughout this guide. Option A makes you a participant in information suppression during exactly the process (a spending review) that exists to allocate public money on accurate information. Option B escalates the conflict before attempting the conversation, and "loudly" converts a resolvable pressure into a public rupture. Option D is the subtle failure worth naming: technically accurate reporting engineered not to be read is suppression with an audit trail — the form of honesty without its function. Your role's language — moderate difficult discussions about high-risk topics, even within constrained timescales — describes precisely this conversation with the director; the constrained timescale is the spending review, and the difficult discussion is the one option C has.

**Question 7 (moderate) — The innovation that worked, and its cost**

One of your delivery managers has piloted an AI-assisted triage tool that cut their team's backlog processing time by 40%. They are rightly proud and want to roll it out across all your teams next month. Reviewing the pilot, you find: the results are real; the tool was procured on a corporate card as software-of-low-value, skirting procurement thresholds for what a full rollout would cost; and no data protection assessment was done, though the triage data includes personal information. What is the most effective response?

- A) Halt everything, admonish the delivery manager, and file the tool under lessons learned.
- B) Roll out next month on the pilot's momentum; governance can catch up.
- C) Protect the innovation and fix its foundations: celebrate the result publicly, be straight with the delivery manager privately about the two compliance gaps and why they are not optional (personal data and procurement thresholds are legal exposure, not bureaucracy), and sequence the rollout through the necessary DPIA and procurement route — using your seniority to accelerate those processes rather than bypass them.
- D) Report the procurement and data issues to compliance and distance yourself from the pilot.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The scenario embeds a real tension of senior delivery leadership: you are explicitly charged with experimenting with new and innovative ways of working to improve delivery across the organisation — and with the judgement to know which corners are cuttable. The pilot's two gaps are in the uncuttable category: processing personal data without assessment is legal exposure to the department and to actual users, and threshold-skirting procurement at rollout scale is the kind of finding that ends careers and freezes innovation programmes wholesale — a compliance failure discovered later would not just kill this tool, it would poison the well for every future experiment. Option C holds the full position: the celebration is genuine and public (the behaviour you want repeated is evidence-generating experimentation — that part succeeded), the correction is private and educational (the delivery manager's gap is knowledge of where guardrails bind, which is coachable), and the sequencing converts "no" into "yes, via" — with your seniority spent on accelerating the legitimate route, which is exactly what senior sponsorship of innovation is for. Option A executes the correction and the celebration's opposite: it teaches your community that initiative is punished, and the 40% improvement dies with the lesson. Option B adopts the legal exposure at scale, knowingly — momentum is not a governance category. Option D is self-protection wearing a compliance costume: reporting without supporting, and distancing from a pilot you oversee, fails both the innovation and the person. The senior pattern: guard the guardrails and champion the experiment — refusing to choose is the job.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Choosing the feedback mode**

Sprint reviews across your teams have surfaced a pattern: one experienced delivery manager routinely presents their team's work impressively but interrupts and speaks over their own team members — twice this month visibly deflating a junior developer mid-demo. Their team's health check shows declining "voice" scores. The delivery manager seems unaware. You have a scheduled one-to-one tomorrow and a written quarterly review due next week. What is the most effective approach?

- A) Cover it in the written quarterly review, where it becomes part of the formal record.
- B) Raise it in tomorrow's one-to-one: specific observed instances, their visible effect, the corroborating health-check data — as a development conversation with concrete alternatives (letting team members present their own work) — and keep the written review for themes, not ambushes.
- C) Mention it lightly at the next team retro so the whole team can discuss it.
- D) Say nothing specific but model better behaviour in your own meetings.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Your skill framework names this precisely: engage in varying types of feedback, choosing the right type at the appropriate time, and ensuring the discussion and decision stick. Audit each mode against the situation. The behaviour is specific, recent, observed, and unconscious — the profile that responds best to prompt, private, evidenced conversation: tomorrow's one-to-one is the venue, and option B's construction (instances, effects, data, alternatives) is the difference between feedback that lands and criticism that triggers defence; the health-check corroboration matters because it converts "I noticed" into "your team is telling you too." Option A's failure is timing and temperature: a written formal document is where feedback becomes record — appropriate for patterns already discussed and unaddressed, but as a first raising it reads as an ambush, and the week's delay costs another review cycle of the behaviour. Option C outsources a leadership conversation to the team least safe to have it — juniors critiquing their manager's conduct in a retro is an unfair ask, and "lightly" guarantees it lands as either nothing or humiliation. Option D is the fantasy that modelling substitutes for feedback; unconscious behaviour, by definition, is not cured by examples the person does not know they need. The stick-the-landing clause: agree what changes (team members present their own work), and watch the next review — feedback without follow-through is commentary.

**Question 9 (challenging) — The assessment you contribute to**

You are asked to contribute to another programme's service assessment as a peer reviewer. The service belongs to a directorate whose head is influential and known to react badly to criticism; your own programme will need that directorate's cooperation on a shared platform next quarter. The evidence you review is clear: the service's performance data is cherry-picked (three good months presented, two bad ones omitted), and its operational support model is unstaffed beyond month two. A colleague on the panel suggests "landing the concerns softly — maybe as recommendations rather than conditions — given the relationships involved." What is the most effective response?

- A) Agree; recommendations preserve the relationship and the substance survives.
- B) Contribute your honest assessment with the evidence, support conditions where the evidence warrants conditions, and be rigorous about tone — factual, specific, respectful, no blindsiding: ensure the service team hears the concerns and their basis directly. Treat the relationship risk as real but not purchasable with assessment integrity, and manage it separately through your engagement on the shared platform.
- C) Withdraw from the panel, citing the conflict of interest.
- D) Soften the written report but tell the directorate head the full truth privately.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Name what is actually being proposed: converting evidence-warranted conditions into recommendations is not diplomacy of tone but alteration of substance — conditions gate progression, recommendations request consideration, and a service with cherry-picked performance data and a support model that evaporates in month two is exactly what conditions exist for (the omitted months are not a presentational quibble; they are the difference between evidence and advocacy). Option B holds the assessment's integrity while doing everything legitimate for the relationship: rigour of tone, specificity, no ambush, direct communication of the basis — because most relationship damage from assessments comes not from the findings but from how they arrive. The separation clause is senior-grade thinking: the relationship risk is real, and it is managed in the relationship (your platform engagement, where cooperation is built) — not in the evidence record, where its price is paid by the service's future users and by every future assessment's credibility. Your role explicitly includes contributing to the assessment of other teams, providing guidance and support — the guidance and support are the tone; the assessment is the substance. Option A trades users' interests for organisational comfort. Option C mistakes an influence concern for a conflict of interest — withdrawal removes the panel's most independent voice precisely because independence has a cost, and if actual conflicts existed the time to declare them was at appointment. Option D is the worst construction: a written record that understates and a private channel that contradicts it — the report is the instrument of record, and building a divergence between what is written and what is known is how institutions learn to trust neither.

**Question 10 (challenging) — The budget raid**

Mid-year, your finance partner alerts you: the portfolio board will consider reallocating £600k from your programme's remaining budget to a struggling sister programme, on the argument that your programme is underspending. The underspend is real but deliberate — it is the phased buffer for your Q4 supplier payments and the contingency for a known-fragile migration. The paper goes to board Friday; you are not on the attendee list. What is the most effective response?

- A) Accept the outcome; boards allocate, delivery managers deliver.
- B) Get the true position in front of the decision before it is made: brief your programme director immediately with the evidence (the underspend's composition — committed Q4 phasing plus risk-based contingency, and the specific consequence of losing each), request attendance or ensure a written response accompanies the paper, and propose constructive alternatives if the sister programme's need is real — a smaller sum, a loan against Q4, or jointly approaching the portfolio's actual contingency.
- C) Spend the buffer quickly before Friday so there is nothing to reallocate.
- D) Lobby sympathetic board members informally to vote the paper down.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The decision is legitimate in forum and flawed in evidence: "underspend" as presented conflates unallocated money with phased commitments and priced risk — and the correction must reach the decision, through the front door, before Friday. Option B is the complete senior response: speed (briefing the director immediately — decisions are shaped in the pre-reads, and a rebuttal after the minute is worth a fraction of a sentence before it), evidence (the composition argument, concretely: £X contracted for Q4 supplier milestones, £Y held against the migration whose fragility is documented — "our underspend is your Q4 invoice" is a sentence boards understand), presence (attendance or a written response attached to the paper — silence at the table is agreement in the minute), and statesmanship: the constructive-alternatives clause acknowledges the sister programme's need may be real and the portfolio's problem genuine, offering the board a decision that solves it without manufacturing a Q4 crisis in your programme — negotiating and influencing budgets in complex environments, your named **Financial management** skill, at its most literal. Option A abdicates: input to resource decisions is not insubordination, it is the information the board is entitled to. Option C is the classic perverse incentive — spending public money defensively to protect an allocation is a misuse that also happens to validate the raid's premise. Option D routes influence through the back channel: even when it works, it teaches the portfolio that decisions are lobbied rather than evidenced — and your case is strong precisely because it does not need the back channel.

**Question 11 (challenging) — The exhausted high performer**

Your strongest delivery manager — running your most complex, highest-risk service — has for two months been first online and last off, visibly thinner-skinned in meetings, and this week snapped uncharacteristically at a junior colleague, then apologised profusely. Their service is mid-migration; they have twice deflected your "how are you doing?" with "fine, just a big quarter." A director has just asked for them by name to also take on an incoming crisis programme, "since they're clearly your best." What is the most effective response?

- A) Pass them the crisis programme with extra thanks; high performers rise to demand, and declining the director is politically costly.
- B) Decline the director on their behalf without telling them, to protect them.
- C) Have the real conversation, past the deflection — name the specific changes you have observed and that you are concerned, not evaluating; jointly re-examine their load with actual relief on the table (what you will take, delegate, or delay); and handle the director's request honestly — the crisis programme gets a properly supported alternative, not your best person's last reserves — while making clear to the delivery manager that protecting their sustainability is a leadership decision you own, not a verdict on their capability.
- D) Schedule them a resilience course and revisit next quarter.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Read the signal stack: extended hours, thinning composure, an uncharacteristic public snap, and — most diagnostic — repeated deflection, which in high performers is not reassurance but the burnout pattern itself (the identity that makes them excellent makes admitting overload feel like failure). Option C's construction answers each layer. The conversation names observations, not conclusions ("you've been first on and last off for two months; the snap this week wasn't like you") because specific observation is harder to deflect than "how are you doing?" and "concerned, not evaluating" addresses the fear that keeps the deflection running. The relief is concrete and jointly designed — abstract permission to slow down changes nothing while the load is unchanged; understanding the psychology of a team, your skill's language, includes the psychology of its leaders. The director's request is handled with honesty and an alternative — the political cost of declining (option A's fear) is real but purchasable, whereas spending your best person's last reserves on a second crisis is how organisations convert their strongest asset into a long-term absence and a resignation letter; and note option A's tell, "clearly your best," which is how the reward-for-overwork spiral advertises itself. Option B protects the person by removing their agency — deciding their capacity in secret infantilises a senior professional and, when discovered, reads as a career intervention made behind their back. Option D outsources a load problem to a training course: resilience is not the deficit; the quarter is. The senior pattern, worth stating plainly: sustainable pace is something you enforce for your people against the organisation's appetite — and occasionally against their own.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Choosing least effective: the failing supplier at the gate**

Your critical supplier has missed two milestones; the recovery plan you agreed is now also slipping. The contract allows termination for material breach after the next missed milestone, in five weeks. Retendering would take six months; the supplier's product is deeply integrated; and their delivery team — as distinct from their management — is competent and candid. Which response is LEAST effective?

- A) Intensify structured pressure now: weekly senior-to-senior reviews with the supplier's account director, explicit recovery criteria for the five-week window, and commercial colleagues engaged on the termination-versus-continuation analysis so the decision at the gate is prepared, not improvised.
- B) Begin contingency work in parallel: honestly assess the six-month retender path, interim options, and the true cost of each, so that five weeks from now the department chooses between real options rather than hostage positions.
- C) Signal privately to the supplier's account director that termination "isn't really on the table given the integration," to keep the relationship collaborative through the recovery.
- D) Tell the supplier plainly that the next milestone is decision-critical, that both recovery and exit paths are being prepared, and that their delivery team's candour is valued and should continue.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Least-effective format: three options form a coherent senior playbook, one dismantles it. Options A, B, and D are the triad — pressure with structure and criteria (A), genuine alternatives so the gate decision is free (B), and honest signalling that keeps the supplier's incentives sharp while protecting the working-level channel (D). Option C destroys the mechanism the other three build: privately conceding that termination is off the table converts your one hard lever into a known bluff — account directors price exactly this, and a supplier who knows the gate has no consequence has no reason to fund the recovery with their best people; the missed milestones that got you here were themselves priced against your revealed reluctance. Note the seduction in C's framing: "keep the relationship collaborative" — but collaboration purchased by disarming yourself is not a relationship, it is a dependency with meetings, and the genuinely collaborative move is D's, which pairs candour about consequences with respect for the people performing well. The integration depth and retender cost are real (they are why B exists — an empty threat is worse than none, and B is what makes the threat real), but they are constraints to be priced into your options, not confessions to be handed to the counterparty. Getting good value out of contracts and suppliers, your named skill, has a compressed senior form: never pay for a relationship with your leverage — the supplier's management is watching what you do with the gate, and so, five weeks from now, is your own.

### Preparation tips

- **Audit each option for second-order effects.** At senior level the question behind the question is: what does this response teach — the delivery managers watching, the supplier pricing your next move, the team learning whether feedback is safe? The best option usually wins on the second order.
- **Practise the boundary triage:** act, coach the person whose job it is to act, or route to the owning tier. Before choosing, identify which of the three the scenario calls for — most senior SJT errors are boundary errors in one direction or the other.
- **Separate substance from tone deliberately.** Many scenarios (assessments, escalations, feedback) tempt you to buy relationships with substance. The strong options are rigorous in substance and generous in tone; any option trading the first for the second is a distractor.
- **Rehearse the honest-reframe move.** "You cannot have suppression, but you can have a rigorous, defensible account" (Q6); "not your best person, but a properly supported alternative" (Q11). Converting an illegitimate ask into its legitimate cousin is the most repeated senior pattern in this section.
- **Check every information decision against one rule:** does this response move accurate information toward the people entitled to act on it, or away from them? Options that quietly starve a decision — softened reports, unread documents, absent voices at boards — are systematically penalised.
- **Use your real caseload as the practice set.** For each scenario here, find its nearest real equivalent from your last two quarters and compare your actual response to the modelled one. The residual gap, honestly logged, is your development plan — and excellent material for your own coaching conversations upward.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Ruling when you should mediate.** Crowned verdicts in expert disputes buy speed and cost you every future dispute plus the loser's engagement. Facilitate the joint answer; escalate genuine strategic forks only.
- **Rescuing when you should coach.** Absorbing your delivery managers' decisions, responses, and recoveries caps their growth and bottlenecks the organisation on your capacity — the seniority trap with the best intentions.
- **Purchasing relationships with integrity.** Softened assessments, suppressed reporting, conceded leverage: each converts a permanent asset into a temporary comfort, and the SJT prices the exchange correctly even when the scenario's characters do not.
- **Confusing the political cost of honesty with its wisdom.** Declining directors, correcting corridor commitments, and attending hostile boards all carry real cost — and the scenarios are built so the honest option's cost is visible and its alternative's larger cost is deferred. Choose against deferral.
- **Protecting people by removing their agency.** Secret declining, quiet workarounds, and unshared concerns infantilise capable colleagues. Care at senior level is done with people, not to them.
- **Misreading the least-effective stem.** The strongest distractor set is three good options and one seductive saboteur; polarity misreads throw away the section's easiest marks. Read the stem twice, especially when tired — which is also sound advice for the job.
