## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive ability assessment measures the exact mental processes your role demands, using practical workplace scenarios rather than abstract puzzles. Rather than testing you on shapes and sequences, it presents the materials of enterprise-level analysis — conceptual models, cross-programme dependency maps, operating model comparisons, portfolio prioritisation frameworks, governance papers — and asks you to reason quickly and accurately about them.

Typical format: an online, timed test lasting 15 to 30 minutes, with questions covering pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective against a norm group, and most modern platforms are adaptive: correct answers raise the difficulty of what follows, so a test that feels progressively harder is usually a test going well. Employers typically receive speed and accuracy separately, so a deliberate, correct pace beats a fast, careless one. Short ungraded practice items normally precede the scored section.

For a lead business analyst, cognitive assessment is relevant in a particular way: your role is defined by complexity that exceeds any single artefact. You determine scope and methodology for representing complex programmes across an organisation; you frame problems so they can be understood by people who will never see the underlying detail; you spot when two programmes' models quietly contradict each other; and you troubleshoot situations that arrive tangled, political and half-described. The cognitive skills under test — holding multiple structures in mind, deducing what must follow from a set of constraints, detecting the inconsistency someone else's review missed, ranking competing demands — are the load-bearing machinery of that work. Employers assess them at this level because the people you advise rarely check your reasoning; they act on it.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The assessment dimensions map directly to the named skills in your role profile:

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **Business modelling** and **Enterprise and business architecture (business analyst)**: determining the scope and methodology for representing complex programmes, and helping define capabilities and operating models, both mean recognising recurring structures — duplicated capabilities, parallel processes, repeated dependency shapes — across multiple products and services at once.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Context, problem and option analysis** and **Consultancy**: advising on options analysis, feasibility and impact assessment, and providing recommendations from specialist knowledge, requires valid reasoning from constraints and evidence to consequences — and the discipline to deduce nothing more.
- **Error checking** maps to **Requirements definition and management** and **Testing (business analysis)**: directing the approach to requirements management and reviewing test plans and outcome reports means detecting inconsistencies between artefacts produced by many hands across many teams — inconsistencies no single author can see.
- **Prioritisation** maps to **Adapting to delivery methodologies** and **Stakeholder relationship management**: negotiating delivery priorities, directing stakeholder strategy, and choosing where your own scarce attention goes across multiple products are all ranking problems under competing pressures.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Business process improvement** and **Systems analysis**: leading the identification of large-scale improvement opportunities, and bringing business perspectives and technical concepts together into recommendations, both mean decomposing tangled enterprise problems into parts that can be reasoned about, tested and recombined.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern recognition across programme models**

Three programmes have each modelled their case-handling process. You are reviewing them for a cross-government shared capability proposal. Programme A: Receive → Validate → Assess → Decide → Notify. Programme B: Receive → Validate → Assess → Decide → Notify → Archive. Programme C: Receive → Assess → Validate → Decide → Notify. Which observation is most significant for a shared capability design?

- A) Programme B has one more step than the others.
- B) Programme C validates after assessment rather than before, which may reflect a genuinely different control philosophy — or an error — and must be resolved before the processes can share a capability.
- C) All three programmes use five or six steps, so they are essentially identical.
- D) Programme A lacks an Archive step, so it must be non-compliant with records requirements.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The consequential difference is not step count but step order. Programmes A and B share an identical core sequence (B merely appends archiving, a compatible extension). Programme C inverts validation and assessment — meaning it invests assessment effort in cases that may later fail validation, or it deliberately uses assessment output to inform validation. Either way, a shared capability cannot serve both orderings without configuration, so the inversion is the design question to resolve first. Option A notices the trivial difference; C confuses superficial similarity with structural equivalence; D leaps from an absent step in a summary model to a compliance verdict — models omit detail, and absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. Spotting which differences between models are structural and which are cosmetic is the core pattern skill of determining scope and methodology for representing complex programmes across an organisation.

**Question 2 (easy) — Logical deduction from an assurance rule**

A portfolio assurance rule states: "A programme may proceed past its alpha gate only if its business analysis products have been peer-reviewed by an analyst from outside the programme." You learn that Programme X proceeded past its alpha gate last month. Which conclusion must be true?

- A) Programme X's analysis products were of high quality.
- B) Programme X's analysis products were peer-reviewed by an external-to-programme analyst, assuming the rule was followed.
- C) Programme X's analysis products were peer-reviewed by an external-to-programme analyst.
- D) The peer review found no defects.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The rule makes external peer review a necessary condition for passing the gate. If the rule was followed, passing the gate entails the review happened — but nothing in the facts confirms the rule was followed, so the deduction must carry that assumption explicitly, which is what B does and C silently omits. A and D confuse the occurrence of review with its outcome: a review can happen, find defects, and the products still pass. The habit of surfacing the hidden assumption — "assuming the rule was followed" — separates lead-level reasoning from plausible reasoning. In assurance work, governance rules are exactly the things that sometimes get waived under pressure, and the analyst who asks "was the rule actually applied?" before relying on its consequences is the analyst whose advice survives audit.

**Question 3 (easy) — Error checking a conceptual model**

A conceptual model you commissioned for strategic planning shows: "Each service is owned by exactly one directorate. Each directorate owns at least one service. The Licensing service is owned by both the Regulation directorate and the Commercial directorate." What is the correct characterisation?

- A) The model is internally consistent.
- B) The third statement contradicts the first; the model cannot be baselined until the ownership of Licensing is resolved to a single directorate or the cardinality rule is deliberately changed.
- C) The second statement contradicts the first.
- D) The model is consistent provided the two directorates cooperate.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The first statement asserts a one-to-one ownership cardinality from service to directorate; the third asserts dual ownership of Licensing; they cannot both be true. The second statement (each directorate owns at least one service) is independent and conflicts with nothing. Option D mistakes an organisational workaround for logical consistency — cooperation between directorates does not make "exactly one" compatible with "both". The resolution is a decision, not a redrawing: either Licensing's ownership is genuinely shared, in which case the cardinality rule must change model-wide (with consequences for every accountability built on it), or a single owner must be named. Conceptual models documented to support strategic planning and decision making are only as trustworthy as their internal consistency, and dual-ownership contradictions are among the most common — and most politically loaded — defects they contain.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Prioritisation across an enterprise portfolio**

Monday morning. Four demands compete for your week:

1. The investment committee meets Thursday to decide between two operating model options; your impact assessment is the key paper and is 80% complete.
2. A lead analyst in another department has asked you to speak at a cross-government community event in three weeks; the agenda deadline is Friday.
3. One of your programmes reports that a supplier's requirements interpretation will, if built, breach a data-sharing constraint; build starts Monday next.
4. Your quarterly capability review of the eight analysts you manage is due in two weeks; none is drafted.

Which sequencing is most defensible?

- A) 1, 3, 2, 4 — committee paper, then the constraint breach, then the agenda, then the reviews
- B) 3, 1, 2, 4 — the constraint breach first because breaches are always first, then the paper, then the rest
- C) 1, 2, 3, 4 — strict deadline order
- D) 3, 1, 4, 2 — breach, paper, reviews, community

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Two items carry hard, high-impact deadlines: the Thursday committee paper and the Monday-next build start. The discriminating question is which to progress first, and the answer turns on completion state and intervention cost: the paper is 80% complete and feeds an enterprise-level decision on Thursday — finishing it is the scarce, non-delegable work only you can do this week. The constraint breach is urgent but its Monday deadline leaves the week; more importantly, your first action on it is cheap and immediate — a same-day instruction to pause that requirement's build slot and a delegation to the programme's senior analyst to document the interpretation gap, with your review midweek. Lead-level prioritisation is not just sequencing your effort; it is choosing what to delegate with direction versus what to do. B's "breaches are always first" is a slogan; handled as above, the breach is contained Monday without displacing the committee paper. C lets an event agenda outrank a legal constraint. D buries the community commitment that your role — leader in the community across government — explicitly carries, when it needs only a short reply by Friday. The strongest answer secures both hard deadlines and honours the smaller commitment cheaply.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Deduction across dependency chains**

Your portfolio dependency map records: (i) Service P's re-platforming requires the shared payments capability; (ii) the shared payments capability go-live requires Programme Q's API gateway; (iii) Programme Q's API gateway has slipped by two quarters; (iv) Service R's re-platforming requires no shared capabilities. Which conclusion must be true?

- A) Service P's re-platforming will slip by two quarters.
- B) Service P's re-platforming cannot complete earlier than the delayed payments capability allows; Service R's re-platforming is unaffected by the slip, so far as these dependencies show.
- C) Service R will now re-platform before Service P.
- D) The payments capability should be descoped from Service P.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Trace the chain: Q's gateway slip delays the payments capability, which constrains P — but "constrains" is the precise word. P inherits a later earliest-possible date; whether P slips by exactly two quarters depends on how much float existed between the payments go-live and P's plan, which the facts do not state. So A overstates. R has no shared-capability dependencies, so this slip does not touch it through the mapped dependencies — though B's careful qualifier "so far as these dependencies show" matters, because unmapped dependencies may exist. C converts a constraint into a race prediction: nothing states either service's actual dates. D is a recommendation, not a deduction, and an unmotivated one. This is the discipline of enterprise dependency reasoning: propagate constraints exactly — earliest dates, not certain slips — and flag the limits of the map you are reasoning from. Advising on impact assessments so best practice is followed means teaching exactly this distinction to the programmes you oversee.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Pattern recognition in demand data**

Across your organisation's twelve services, you review "requirements churn" — the percentage of baselined requirements changed within three months. Nine services sit between 8% and 14%. Three services show 31%, 29% and 34% — and you notice all three take their requirements from the same policy directorate. What is the strongest analytical move?

- A) Conclude the three teams' analysts are weak and arrange training.
- B) Conclude policy-driven churn is inevitable and exclude the three services from churn reporting.
- C) Treat the clustering as a signal of a shared upstream cause — investigate how that directorate's policy positions are communicated and stabilised, and whether analysis is engaging with policy early enough — before attributing anything to the teams.
- D) Average all twelve services and report 16% churn as within tolerance.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The pattern is not just three outliers — it is three outliers with a common upstream source, which shifts the probable cause from the teams (three independent failures would be a coincidence) to the shared input. The right investigation targets the interface: are policy positions arriving unstable, is analysis engaging before positions harden, is there a feedback loop from delivery back to policy? Option A punishes the visible node in the system rather than the causal one; B capitulates — churn from late policy change is common but not immutable, and excluding it from reporting removes the very signal needed to improve it; D launders a bimodal distribution into a meaningless mean, hiding the finding entirely. Reading a system-level pattern and locating the cause at the right level of the system is the essence of leading the identification of large-scale opportunities to deliver business performance improvements — the opportunity here is not in any team; it is in the policy-to-delivery interface.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Error checking an options appraisal's logic**

An options appraisal you are quality-assuring argues: "Option 1 (extend the legacy system) is rejected because the legacy platform exits support in 2027. Option 2 (single new build) is rejected because no single supplier can meet all requirements. Option 3 (modular build on the shared platform) is therefore the right choice." What is the principal logical weakness?

- A) The appraisal does not state Option 3's costs.
- B) The argument eliminates alternatives without ever positively assessing Option 3 against the requirements, risks and constraints — survival by elimination is not evidence of adequacy, and the option set itself may be incomplete.
- C) Option 1's rejection is invalid because support contracts can be extended.
- D) Three options is too few for a compliant appraisal.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The structure is a process-of-elimination argument, and its flaw is structural: even if both rejections are sound, nothing shown demonstrates that Option 3 is feasible, affordable or acceptable — it merely remains standing. Elimination arguments also depend on the option set being exhaustive, which is asserted nowhere; a fourth option (for example, extending legacy briefly while building modularly) is not considered. Option A names a symptom of the deeper gap B describes. C challenges a premise on outside knowledge the appraisal may have addressed elsewhere — a fair review question, but not the *principal* logical weakness. D appeals to a rule that does not exist; appraisal quality is about coverage of the decision space, not option count. When you advise on approaches to options analysis to ensure best practice is followed, this is among the most common defects you will catch: the recommended option that was never actually appraised, only left over.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Applied problem solving: framing an ambiguous request**

A director general tells you: "Casework is too slow, morale is poor, and the board wants it fixed by spring. Have a look at it." Casework spans three services, two case management systems and 400 staff. What is the strongest first analytical move?

- A) Commission a full current-state process map of all three services to a uniform level of detail.
- B) Define and agree the problem frame first: what "too slow" means measurably and to whom, how slowness and morale might be related, what "fixed" would look like by spring, and which slices of the casework system the evidence says to examine first — then scope analysis to that frame.
- C) Procure a casework software replacement, since two systems for one function is the obvious root cause.
- D) Survey all 400 staff about morale before any process analysis.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The request is an executive symptom report, not a problem definition — three claims ("too slow", "morale is poor", "board wants it fixed") with undefined terms, an implied causal link, and a deadline. Framing before analysing is what your Consultancy skill names directly: frame problems so they can easily be understood. Concretely, B forces the questions that make spring achievable: slow against what benchmark, for which case types, at which steps? Is morale a cause of slowness, an effect, or a separate issue sharing a cause? What movement, by when, would the board accept as "fixed"? The agreed answers then let evidence — performance data, complaint themes, staff exit interviews — select where deep analysis pays. Option A spends the whole runway producing uniform detail across a system whose problems are almost certainly non-uniform; C decides the answer before any analysis, on a premise (two systems = root cause) that is merely a hypothesis; D elevates one strand to a prerequisite when it can run in parallel inside the frame. At enterprise scale, the scarce resource is analytical attention, and framing is how you point it.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Multi-constraint design of a review programme**

You must schedule deep-dive reviews of four programmes — Alpha, Bravo, Charlie, Delta — across four consecutive weeks, one review per week. Constraints: (i) Alpha's review must precede Charlie's, because Charlie consumes Alpha's shared component; (ii) Bravo's review cannot be in week 1, as its lead analyst returns from leave mid-week 1; (iii) Delta must be reviewed the week immediately after Bravo, sharing its review panel; (iv) Charlie cannot be reviewed in week 4, because its gate decision is at the start of week 4. Which schedule satisfies all constraints?

- A) Alpha, Bravo, Charlie, Delta
- B) Alpha, Bravo, Delta, Charlie
- C) Bravo, Delta, Alpha, Charlie
- D) Alpha, Charlie, Bravo, Delta

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Test the constraints systematically rather than checking options by feel. From (iii), Bravo and Delta occupy consecutive weeks in that order; from (ii), Bravo is not week 1, so Bravo–Delta can sit at weeks 2–3 or 3–4. From (iv), Charlie is not week 4; from (i), Alpha precedes Charlie. If Bravo–Delta take weeks 2–3, then Alpha and Charlie take weeks 1 and 4 — but Charlie cannot be week 4, contradiction. So Bravo–Delta must take weeks 3–4, leaving weeks 1–2 for Alpha then Charlie (Alpha before Charlie, satisfied; Charlie in week 2, permitted). That is exactly option D: Alpha (week 1), Charlie (week 2), Bravo (week 3), Delta (week 4). Checking the others: A places Delta after Charlie, breaking (iii)'s adjacency to Bravo? — in A, Bravo week 2, Charlie week 3, Delta week 4: Delta is not immediately after Bravo, breaking (iii). B places Charlie in week 4, breaking (iv). C places Bravo in week 1, breaking (ii). The method — derive what must be true from the tightest constraints before testing candidates — is precisely how you untangle real multi-programme scheduling, where panel availability, gate dates and dependency order interact.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Deduction about operating model consequences**

A target operating model states: (i) all initial customer contact moves to a single national contact centre; (ii) regional offices retain casework but lose their local contact teams; (iii) cases requiring local knowledge are flagged by the contact centre and routed to the relevant regional office. Regional managers report that today, roughly 30% of contacts are resolved on the spot by local contact teams using local knowledge, without ever becoming cases. If the model is implemented as stated, which consequence must follow?

- A) The 30% of contacts previously resolved locally on the spot must either be resolved by the national centre — without the local knowledge that resolved them — or be flagged and routed as cases to regional offices, increasing regional casework.
- B) Customer satisfaction will fall by 30%.
- C) Regional offices will refuse to accept the routed cases.
- D) The national contact centre will need 30% more staff than planned.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Follow the structure. The model removes local contact teams (ii) and gives initial contact to the national centre (i). The 30% of contacts that local teams currently resolve using local knowledge must now land at the national centre, which by hypothesis lacks that knowledge; the model's only mechanism for local-knowledge contacts is flagging and routing to regional offices as cases (iii). So each such contact either gets resolved nationally without the ingredient that previously resolved it, or becomes a routed case — swelling regional casework with work that previously never became casework at all. That disjunction is forced by the model's own statements; it is conclusion A and nothing more. B invents a quantified satisfaction prediction; C predicts behaviour; D assumes a staffing baseline the facts do not give. The lead-level point: operating model documents state structures, and the analyst's job — helping define capabilities and operating models that meet business objectives — is to derive the workload and knowledge-flow consequences those structures entail, and put the forced disjunction in front of decision makers before implementation makes it a discovery.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Prioritising analytical investment under uncertainty**

Your function can staff two of four proposed discovery investigations this quarter:

1. A minister-favoured idea for a new digital service; political enthusiasm is high, but a comparable service in another department failed user research testing badly last year.
2. Consolidation of three overlapping internal case systems; unglamorous, with a strong prima facie benefits case and low uncertainty.
3. A data-sharing initiative that two directorates each assume the other is analysing; nobody owns it, and a statutory deadline sits eight months out.
4. A proof of concept for an AI triage tool a supplier has offered to run "free of charge" using live case data.

Which pair represents the strongest analytical investment?

- A) 1 and 4 — highest political and innovation value
- B) 2 and 3 — the strong benefits case plus the orphaned statutory deadline
- C) 1 and 2 — balance political demand with solid benefits
- D) 3 and 4 — the two nobody else is doing

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Work each option's risk-adjusted value. Investigation 3 is the sleeper priority: a statutory deadline with no owner is the highest-risk item on the list precisely because everyone assumes it is covered — eight months is short for anything statutory, and discovery is the mechanism that surfaces its true size; passing on it risks a legal failure no one chose. Investigation 2 is a strong, low-uncertainty benefits case — the reliable return that justifies the function's cost. Investigation 1's political enthusiasm is real, but the comparable failure is evidence that the idea needs reshaping before a full discovery — the proportionate response is a short, cheap evidence review of the other department's research (days, not a discovery slot), which either kills the idea or improves the eventual discovery; recommending that is better stewardship than spending a slot on enthusiasm. Investigation 4's "free" proof of concept carries non-free costs — live case data raises data protection and information governance questions that need resolving before, not after, a supplier touches it. Option B therefore maximises risk-adjusted value while A, C and D each leave the statutory orphan unowned. The behaviours exercised — guiding tactical decision making, influencing strategic plans, ensuring streams of work align to organisational goals — are your Context, problem and option analysis skill operating at portfolio altitude.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Applied problem solving: reconciling contradictory evidence**

Two evidence sources about the same service contradict each other. The performance dashboard shows average end-to-end processing time falling steadily for six months. Frontline staff and stakeholder complaints both insist the service is getting slower. Investigation confirms both sources are accurate as measured. Which hypothesis best reconciles the contradiction and should be tested first?

- A) Staff are resistant to change and their perception is simply wrong.
- B) The dashboard's average is being pulled down by compositional change — for example, a growing share of simple, fast cases — while complex cases are actually getting slower; test by segmenting processing time by case type over the six months.
- C) The dashboard has a calculation bug.
- D) Complainants are a vocal minority and should be discounted.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** When two accurate measurements contradict, the resolution is almost always that they measure different things. An overall average can fall while every subgroup worsens if the mix shifts toward fast cases — a form of Simpson's paradox, and common in services where a new digital channel absorbs simple cases. Frontline staff experience the case-level reality (complex cases slowing); the dashboard reports the mix-weighted average. Hypothesis B explains both observations simultaneously and is cheaply testable: segment the six-month trend by case type and channel. Options A and D resolve the contradiction by discarding one evidence source — the move that feels decisive and is almost always wrong, because the stem states both sources are accurate; C contradicts the stem directly. The lead-level lesson: your organisation's dashboards aggregate, your stakeholders experience, and the analyst who can name the compositional mechanism reconciling them turns a credibility war between "the data" and "the frontline" into a shared, testable finding. That is systems analysis in its deepest sense — understanding how processes, systems and measurements interact — and it is why leaders bring you the contradictions no one else can untangle.

### Preparation tips

- **Practise at portfolio altitude.** Take two or three real programme artefacts from different teams and hunt for cross-artefact inconsistencies — contradictory cardinalities, incompatible process orderings, dependency chains that no single document shows. This is the distinctive lead-level cognitive skill, and ten minutes a day builds it.
- **Name your assumptions aloud.** For deduction questions, practise stating the hidden assumption a conclusion needs ("assuming the rule was followed", "so far as the map shows"). It slows you by seconds and protects you from the strengthened conclusions that distractors are built from.
- **Drill constraint problems on paper.** For scheduling and sequencing questions, derive forced placements from the tightest constraints first, then test candidates. Write the constraint list down; working memory is precious under a clock.
- **Study elimination arguments.** Options appraisals, business cases and supplier proposals constantly argue by elimination. Practise asking two questions of any such argument: was the surviving option positively assessed, and is the option set actually exhaustive?
- **Rehearse compositional reasoning.** Averages that move against subgroup trends, mix shifts, and denominator changes are favourite hard-question territory. Work through one Simpson's-paradox-style example until the mechanism is intuitive.
- **Protect your test conditions.** Book a quiet slot at your sharpest time of day, prepare rough paper, and treat rising difficulty as the adaptive engine confirming progress rather than a signal of trouble.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Concluding beyond the constraint.** A delayed dependency sets an earliest date, not a certain slip. Distractors — and real programme reports — routinely convert constraints into predictions.
- **Attributing systemic patterns to individuals.** When several teams sharing an input all show the same anomaly, the input is the prime suspect. Blaming the visible node is quick, satisfying and usually wrong.
- **Accepting the surviving option.** "The others were rejected" is not an appraisal of what remains. Insist on positive assessment — in tests, and in every recommendation you sign off.
- **Analysing before framing.** Ambiguous executive requests reward a framing move first; the distractor that commissions comprehensive analysis of everything is a trap of diligence.
- **Discarding one of two accurate sources.** When evidence conflicts, look for the mechanism that makes both true. The answer that dismisses the frontline, or the dashboard, is nearly always a distractor.
- **Doing everything yourself in prioritisation questions.** At lead level, the strongest sequencing options include delegation with direction. Answers where you personally serialise all the work are usually second-best.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures your ability to interpret, manipulate and draw sound conclusions from quantitative information of the kind your role genuinely handles. The arithmetic itself stays within percentages, ratios, averages, rates and projections; what is really under test is judgement — extracting the right figures from tables and charts, choosing the right operation under time pressure, and recognising what a number does and does not establish.

Typical format: an online, timed test of 20 to 40 minutes with 15 to 25 questions, each anchored to a table, chart or short data-rich passage. Calculators and rough paper are normally permitted; many platforms are adaptive; and employers usually see accuracy and speed separately, sometimes with a "distractor susceptibility" indicator showing whether your errors fall on the plausible-looking traps.

At lead level, the numbers you handle are consequential in a specific way: they are usually someone else's numbers. Suppliers present benefits models; programmes present velocity and churn statistics; finance presents unit-cost comparisons; directors quote figures in committees that someone must be able to verify in real time. You direct the approach to requirements and analysis across multiple products, which means the quantitative claims that reach investment committees pass through your quality assurance — and the ones that mislead tend to mislead structurally: wrong denominators, unweighted averages, mix effects, annualisation games, survivorship in samples. Employers assess numeric reasoning at this level because the person who signs off the analysis must be faster and more precise with a suspicious number than the person who produced it.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Percentages and percentage change** map to **Business process improvement**: leading the identification of large-scale improvement opportunities means comparing baselines, targets and actuals across services without conflating points, percentages and bases.
- **Ratios, unit costs and weighted comparisons** map to **Context, problem and option analysis** and **Consultancy**: feasibility and impact assessments turn on cost per case, benefit-cost ratios and like-for-like normalisation across options of different scales.
- **Averages, distributions and mix effects** map to **Business modelling** and **Systems analysis**: conceptual and quantitative models that support strategic planning must survive compositional questions — what happens to the average when the mix changes — or they will misdirect the decisions built on them.
- **Rates, capacity and forecasting** map to **Adapting to delivery methodologies** and **Enterprise and business architecture (business analyst)**: negotiating delivery priorities and shaping operating models both require converting demand, capacity and productivity assumptions into feasible plans.
- **Reading tables and charts under scrutiny** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management** and **Testing (business analysis)**: influencing and negotiating with senior stakeholders means verifying — sometimes live in the meeting — the figures on which agreement is about to be reached, and reviewing test outcome reports means re-deriving their aggregates.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Share of a portfolio**

Your portfolio contains 45 active analysis engagements: 18 in citizen-facing services, 12 in internal operations, 9 in data and reporting, and the rest in policy support. What percentage of engagements are in policy support?

- A) 6%
- B) 13.3%
- C) 15%
- D) 20%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Policy support engagements = 45 − 18 − 12 − 9 = 6. Share = 6 ÷ 45 = 0.1333… = 13.3% (to one decimal place). The available trap is option A, which reports the count (6) as if it were the percentage — a surprisingly common slip when working fast. A quick proportional sense-check helps: 6 out of 45 is a bit more than 1 in 8, and 1 in 8 is 12.5%, so 13.3% is plausible. Portfolio composition figures like these open most function-level reports you write, and getting the routine ones effortlessly right buys you time for the harder questions.

**Question 2 (easy) — Percentage change with the correct base**

A service you advise reduced its average cost per case from £48 to £42 over a year. Its director claims "a 14% efficiency gain". Is the claim accurate?

- A) Yes — the reduction is 14.3%, which rounds to 14%.
- B) No — the reduction is 12.5%; the director has divided by the new cost instead of the original.
- C) No — the reduction is 6%.
- D) No — the reduction is 87.5%.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Reduction = £48 − £42 = £6. Percentage reduction = 6 ÷ 48 = 0.125 = 12.5%. The director's 14% comes from 6 ÷ 42 = 14.3% — dividing by the *new* value, which overstates the improvement. Option C halves the correct figure by an arithmetic slip; D is the new-to-old ratio (42/48 = 87.5%), which describes what remains, not what was saved. Base errors flatter improvements systematically, which is why they survive so long in performance narratives: nobody is motivated to catch them. When you quality-assure claims heading for committees, re-derive every percentage change from its base — a ten-second check that regularly earns its keep.

**Question 3 (easy) — Reading a two-way table**

A quarterly report shows requirements produced and defects found in review, by team. Team North: 120 requirements, 18 defects. Team South: 200 requirements, 24 defects. Which team has the higher defect rate per requirement, and what is it?

- A) South — 24 defects is more than 18
- B) North — 15% versus South's 12%
- C) North — 18% versus South's 15%
- D) They are equal at 12%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Compute rates, not counts. North: 18 ÷ 120 = 0.15 = 15%. South: 24 ÷ 200 = 0.12 = 12%. North's rate is higher even though South's count is larger — the elementary rate-versus-count distinction that option A ignores. Option C miscalculates both figures; D is South's rate applied to both. In function-level quality reporting, raw defect counts punish the most productive teams; rates compare fairly. As the person who sets the direction for analysis standards across teams, insisting that quality metrics are expressed as rates — with the denominator stated — is a small act of measurement governance with large cultural effects.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Weighted average across services**

You are consolidating user satisfaction for a committee paper. Service A: 82% satisfied from 1,500 respondents. Service B: 68% satisfied from 500 respondents. A draft paper reports "average satisfaction 75%". What is the correct combined satisfaction rate?

- A) 75.0%
- B) 76.5%
- C) 78.5%
- D) 80.0%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Weight by respondents. Service A satisfied: 1,500 × 0.82 = 1,230. Service B satisfied: 500 × 0.68 = 340. Combined: (1,230 + 340) ÷ (1,500 + 500) = 1,570 ÷ 2,000 = 0.785 = 78.5%. The draft's 75% is the unweighted mean of 82 and 68, which treats a 500-person sample as equal to a 1,500-person one and understates the true figure by 3.5 points. Notice that the error's direction is not always conservative — unweighted means flatter whichever side has the smaller sample, so they can inflate as easily as deflate. Before any average of averages reaches a committee under your name, re-derive it from counts. This is exactly the discipline your Testing skill applies to pass-rate aggregation and your Consultancy skill applies to every evidence pack you endorse.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Benefit-cost ratio comparison**

Two improvement proposals compete for funding. Proposal 1: costs £600,000, delivers benefits of £1,500,000 over five years. Proposal 2: costs £250,000, delivers benefits of £750,000 over five years. The available budget is £700,000 and the proposals are independent (both could run if affordable). What does benefit-cost analysis support?

- A) Fund Proposal 1 only — it has the largest total benefit.
- B) Fund Proposal 2 only — it has the higher benefit-cost ratio (3.0 versus 2.5).
- C) Fund neither until ratios exceed 3.0.
- D) Fund Proposal 2 first on ratio, but note that the £700,000 budget cannot cover both (£850,000); the residual £450,000 cannot fund Proposal 1, so the committee must either fund 2 alone, fund 1 alone, or seek £150,000 more to capture both.

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Compute the ratios: Proposal 1: 1,500 ÷ 600 = 2.5. Proposal 2: 750 ÷ 250 = 3.0. Ratio ranking favours 2 — but ratio ranking alone answers the wrong question when a budget constraint binds. Total cost of both = £850,000 > £700,000, so the real decision space is: 2 alone (cost £250k, benefit £750k), 1 alone (cost £600k, benefit £1.5m), or find £150,000 more and take both (benefit £2.25m). Note the tension D surfaces: 1 alone delivers double the benefit of 2 alone and still fits the budget — so "fund the higher ratio first" would, applied mechanically, leave £450,000 idle and £750,000 of benefit unclaimed. The analyst's job is to lay out exactly this trade-off, not to apply one decision rule silently. A ranks by numerator; B by ratio; C invents a threshold. Framing the affordable combinations, with their totals, is what advising on options analysis to ensure best practice looks like in numbers.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Capacity modelling for a function**

Your business analysis function has 10 analysts. Each has 44 productive weeks per year after leave and training. Portfolio demand next year is: programme work 320 analyst-weeks, discovery investigations 60 analyst-weeks, and assurance reviews 40 analyst-weeks. You also hold 5% of gross capacity in reserve for unplanned requests. How many analyst-weeks short (or spare) is the function?

- A) 2 weeks short
- B) 20 weeks spare
- C) 2 weeks spare
- D) 42 weeks short

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Step 1 — gross capacity: 10 × 44 = 440 analyst-weeks. Step 2 — reserve: 5% × 440 = 22 analyst-weeks, leaving 440 − 22 = 418 plannable. Step 3 — demand: 320 + 60 + 40 = 420. Step 4 — balance: 418 − 420 = −2, i.e. 2 analyst-weeks short. Option B forgets the reserve (440 − 420 = 20 spare) — the flattering error; C flips the sign; D double-counts the reserve against net capacity. Two weeks short on 440 sounds trivial, but the honest statement is that the plan has no headroom and negative slack before the year begins, with the reserve already spoken for by its purpose. Leads who present capacity plans to senior stakeholders should present exactly this arithmetic — gross, reserve, net, demand, balance — because the credibility of every subsequent prioritisation conversation rests on it, and because negotiating delivery priorities honestly (your Adapting to delivery methodologies skill) starts from a truthful capacity number.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Trend versus seasonality**

A service's application volumes for the last eight quarters were: 10,2k, 8,1k, 9,0k, 12,3k, 11,0k, 8,9k, 9,8k, 13,4k (in order, Q1 to Q8; the service's year runs Q1–Q4 and Q5–Q8). A colleague's paper states: "Volumes fell 19% between Q4 and Q6, indicating declining demand." What is the sounder reading?

- A) The colleague is right: 12.3k to 8.9k is a material decline.
- B) Compare like quarters across years: Q1→Q5 rose 7.8% (10.2k to 11.0k), Q2→Q6 rose 9.9% (8.1k to 8.9k), Q3→Q7 rose 8.9% (9.0k to 9.8k), Q4→Q8 rose 8.9% (12.3k to 13.4k) — demand is growing roughly 8–10% year on year with a seasonal peak in Q4-equivalents; the "fall" is seasonality.
- C) Demand is flat because the mean of all eight quarters is stable.
- D) The data is too noisy to interpret.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The colleague compared a seasonal peak (Q4-equivalent, the highest quarter in both years) with an off-peak quarter, manufacturing a decline from the seasonal cycle itself. The correct technique compares each quarter with its counterpart one year earlier: 11.0 ÷ 10.2 = 1.078 (+7.8%); 8.9 ÷ 8.1 = 1.099 (+9.9%); 9.8 ÷ 9.0 = 1.089 (+8.9%); 13.4 ÷ 12.3 = 1.089 (+8.9%). Four out of four year-on-year comparisons show growth in a tight 8–10% band — a consistent underlying trend with a stable seasonal shape. Option C's flat mean hides the growth (means across a trend understate the endpoint); D surrenders to noise that the year-on-year technique fully resolves. Demand forecasts drive operating model sizing and channel strategy — enterprise decisions your role informs — and mistaking seasonality for decline (or growth) is among the most expensive simple errors an organisation can make.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Unit cost normalisation across options**

An impact assessment compares outsourced and in-house options for document processing. Outsourced: £2.10 per document, with a minimum annual charge of £630,000. In-house: fixed annual cost £400,000 plus £0.80 per document. Annual volume is uncertain: the low scenario is 250,000 documents, the high scenario 500,000. What does the cost comparison show?

- A) Outsourced is cheaper in both scenarios.
- B) In-house is cheaper in both scenarios: £600k versus £630k at low volume, and £800k versus £1,050k at high volume.
- C) In-house is cheaper at high volume only.
- D) Outsourced is cheaper at low volume only.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Compute each cell. Low volume (250k docs): outsourced = 250,000 × £2.10 = £525,000, but the minimum charge lifts this to £630,000; in-house = £400,000 + 250,000 × £0.80 = £400,000 + £200,000 = £600,000. In-house cheaper by £30,000. High volume (500k docs): outsourced = 500,000 × £2.10 = £1,050,000 (above the minimum, so no floor applies); in-house = £400,000 + 500,000 × £0.80 = £800,000. In-house cheaper by £250,000. So B — and note the structure of the trap: without the minimum-charge clause, outsourced would win the low scenario (£525k < £600k), which is exactly why such clauses exist and exactly why analysts must read the commercial terms, not just the headline rate. The gap also widens with volume (in-house's marginal cost is £0.80 versus £2.10), so growth strengthens the in-house case. Reading fixed/variable structures and contractual floors into a like-for-like comparison is core feasibility-assessment craft — the numeric heart of your Context, problem and option analysis skill.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Time-phased benefits with attrition**

A process automation is forecast to release 12,000 staff-hours per year at £28 per hour, from go-live at the start of Q3 of year 1 (years have four equal quarters). Realisation ramps: 40% of the full rate in the first live quarter, 70% in the second, 100% thereafter. Additionally, benefits decay: from the start of year 3, the annual figure erodes by 10% per year (of the original full-year value) as volumes shift to a new channel. What benefit does the forecast give for year 1 and year 3 respectively?

- A) Year 1: £92,400; Year 3: £302,400
- B) Year 1: £168,000; Year 3: £336,000
- C) Year 1: £92,400; Year 3: £336,000
- D) Year 1: £336,000; Year 3: £302,400

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Full annual value = 12,000 × £28 = £336,000; full quarterly value = £84,000. Year 1: Q1 and Q2 pre-go-live = £0; Q3 at 40% = £84,000 × 0.4 = £33,600; Q4 at 70% = £84,000 × 0.7 = £58,800. Year 1 total = £33,600 + £58,800 = £92,400. Year 3: the ramp completed during year 2, so year 3 would be the full £336,000, but decay of 10% of the original full-year value applies from the start of year 3: £336,000 − £33,600 = £302,400. So A. Option B annualises year 1 at 50% without quarter-phasing and forgets decay; C phases year 1 correctly but ignores decay; D swaps the years. The two mechanisms — ramp-up and decay — are the standard honesty adjustments that separate credible benefits models from optimistic ones, and both are simple multiplication once the timeline is drawn. When you quality-assure a business case, draw the quarter-by-quarter line before checking any totals: most benefits errors are timeline errors wearing arithmetic disguises.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Sample composition and survivorship**

A supplier reports: "92% of users who completed our post-transaction survey rated the new journey good or excellent (n = 1,204)." Operational data shows 48,000 users attempted the journey in the period, of whom 31,200 completed it; the survey was offered only on the completion page. Which statement best characterises what the 92% establishes?

- A) 92% of all users rate the journey good or excellent.
- B) The journey is performing well; 92% is an excellent score on a large sample.
- C) Among completers who chose to answer (1,204 of 31,200, a 3.9% response rate), 92% rated the journey positively; the figure says nothing about the 16,800 users (35% of attempts) who never completed — the group most likely to have had a poor experience — so it cannot support claims about users overall.
- D) The survey is worthless and should be ignored.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Trace the funnel of who could answer. Attempters: 48,000. Completers: 31,200 — so 16,800 (48,000 − 31,200 = 16,800, i.e. 35%) abandoned and were never surveyed, since the survey lives on the completion page. Respondents: 1,204 of 31,200 = 3.9%, self-selected. The 92% is therefore a statement about satisfied-enough-to-finish-and-respond users — doubly filtered by survivorship (completion) and self-selection (response). It is not meaningless (D overcorrects): it is evidence about completers' sentiment, useful alongside other measures. But A and B extend it to populations it never sampled — and the missing 35% are precisely where the journey's problems live. At lead level you will meet this structure constantly in supplier reporting and internal dashboards alike; the discipline of asking "who could not appear in this number?" is the numeric core of reviewing evidence before it reaches decision makers, and of ensuring services genuinely meet user needs rather than surveyed-user needs.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Mix effect in a performance comparison**

Two years of casework data. Year 1: simple cases 40,000 at an average of 5 days each; complex cases 10,000 at an average of 25 days each. Year 2: simple cases 55,000 at 6 days; complex cases 5,000 at 30 days. A report claims: "Average processing time improved from 9 days to 8 days." Verify the averages and choose the best characterisation.

- A) The claim is arithmetically wrong.
- B) The averages are right: Year 1 = (40,000×5 + 10,000×25) ÷ 50,000 = 450,000 ÷ 50,000 = 9 days; Year 2 = (55,000×6 + 5,000×30) ÷ 60,000 = 480,000 ÷ 60,000 = 8 days. But both case types got slower (5→6 days, +20%; 25→30 days, +20%); the "improvement" is entirely mix shift toward simple cases. Performance worsened for every case a user actually experiences.
- C) The averages are right and performance genuinely improved.
- D) Complex cases should be excluded as outliers, showing simple-case performance of 6 days.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Verify: Year 1 total days = 40,000 × 5 + 10,000 × 25 = 200,000 + 250,000 = 450,000; ÷ 50,000 cases = 9.0 days. Year 2 = 55,000 × 6 + 5,000 × 30 = 330,000 + 150,000 = 480,000; ÷ 60,000 = 8.0 days. The claim's arithmetic is correct — and its implication is false. Segment: simple cases slowed 20% (5→6); complex cases slowed 20% (25→30). Every case type deteriorated; the headline improved only because the caseload mix moved from 20% complex (10k/50k) to 8.3% complex (5k/60k). This is Simpson's paradox in production data, and it is the single most important numeric pattern for a lead analyst to recognise on sight, because it makes true numbers tell false stories in both directions. The corrective is always the same: report segment-level trends alongside the aggregate, and state the mix explicitly. Options A and C each get one half right; D discards the segment where users wait longest — the opposite of user focus.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Reconciling finance and operational counts**

Finance reports the service processed £8.40 of cost per transaction last year (total cost £4,200,000). The operations dashboard reports 560,000 transactions. Investigation finds: finance's transaction count includes only completed transactions; the dashboard counts attempts; 12% of attempts fail before completion and cost, on average, half as much as a completed transaction. Using the dashboard's attempt count, what is the approximate cost per completed transaction implied by consistent accounting?

- A) £7.50
- B) £8.04
- C) £8.40 — finance's figure is already on that basis
- D) £8.93

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Read the reconciliation carefully before computing anything. Finance's £8.40 = £4,200,000 ÷ (finance's completed-transaction count), which gives finance's count as 4,200,000 ÷ 8.40 = 500,000 completed transactions. Check consistency with the dashboard: attempts 560,000 × (1 − 0.12) = 492,800 completions — close to finance's 500,000 (timing and definitional differences plausibly explain the ~1.4% gap). So the two sources broadly agree once their counting bases are aligned, and finance's figure is already cost per *completed* transaction: the answer is C. The failed-attempt cost detail is a live distractor: it would matter if you were asked to *reallocate* cost between failures and completions, but the question asks what finance's figure already represents — and it represents total cost (failures included) spread over completions, which is the standard basis. Options A, B and D all result from performing an adjustment the question does not call for. The lead-level lesson is that reconciliation questions are read-carefully questions: the most senior numeric skill is not calculating faster but establishing precisely what each source counts before touching the calculator — the same discipline your Systems analysis skill applies to any two systems that "disagree".

### Preparation tips

- **Re-derive before you rely.** Build the habit of recomputing any percentage, average or ratio you are about to repeat to someone senior. Most of this test — and much of your assurance role — is exactly that habit under a clock.
- **Draw the timeline for anything time-phased.** Ramp-ups, mid-year go-lives, decay and seasonality all become easy once the quarters are drawn and hard while they stay in prose. Sketch first, multiply second.
- **Interrogate every denominator and every sample.** Who is counted, who is excluded, and who never had the chance to appear? Survivorship and self-selection questions are where strong candidates separate from average ones.
- **Practise segmenting aggregates.** Take any average you encounter this week and ask what the two or three underlying segments are doing. Mix-effect fluency — spotting Simpson's paradox quickly — is learnable and rare, and it is tested at exactly this level.
- **Rehearse fixed-plus-variable cost structures.** Minimum charges, tiered rates and fixed/marginal splits are standard in option comparisons. Practise finding the crossover volume where the cheaper option changes.
- **Keep arithmetic on paper.** Write intermediate results. Under time pressure, chained mental arithmetic is where accurate people become inaccurate — and the test scores the answer, not the elegance.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Dividing by the new value.** Percentage change takes the original as its base. The flattering error survives because no one wants to catch it; catch it anyway.
- **Averaging averages.** Any time two rates from different-sized groups meet, weight by size or re-derive from counts. Unweighted means are the most common defect in papers you will quality-assure.
- **Trusting an aggregate across a mix shift.** A moving average over a changing case mix can contradict every segment. Always ask what the segments did.
- **Ranking by ratio when a budget binds.** Benefit-cost ratios order options; they do not solve the knapsack. Lay out the affordable combinations with totals.
- **Comparing peak to trough.** Seasonal data demands like-for-like comparisons (year on year), not adjacent-quarter drama.
- **Adjusting what needs no adjustment.** Reconciliation questions often include a detail that invites an unnecessary calculation. Establish what each figure already represents before computing anything.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately you extract meaning from written material of the kind your role handles daily — policy papers, legislative summaries, governance terms of reference, supplier contracts, ministerial correspondence, standards and strategies — and how soundly you reason from what a text actually says rather than what experience predicts it will say.

Typical format: an online, timed test of 15 to 25 minutes. Each short passage carries a cluster of questions in familiar styles: True / False / Cannot say (is the statement guaranteed, contradicted, or unaddressed by the passage alone?); best-supported inference; and clear-writing judgement (which version most faithfully and clearly conveys the meaning?). The governing rule is absolute: answer from the passage alone, setting aside outside knowledge — a discipline that is harder, not easier, for experts, because expertise supplies confident assumptions the passage may not share. Adaptive versions raise passage density as you succeed.

At lead level, verbal precision operates at organisational stakes. You direct the approach to requirements management, which means the wording conventions you set — must, should, may; defined terms; scope clauses — propagate through every team you influence. You negotiate with senior stakeholders where a hedged sentence ("we support this direction in principle, subject to affordability") must be read for exactly what it commits and withholds. You provide consultancy where your written advice will be quoted, sometimes selectively, in papers you never see. And you coach analysts whose drafting you review: the ability to articulate *why* a sentence is ambiguous — not merely to sense it — is what makes your feedback transferable. Employers assess verbal reasoning at this level because your reading errors become organisational positions, and your writing habits become organisational culture.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Precise comprehension** maps to **Requirements definition and management** and **Enterprise and business architecture (business analyst)**: translating business drivers, goals and constraints into objectives starts with reading the source documents — strategies, legislation, mandates — with exactness, including their qualifiers, definitions and scope limits.
- **True/False/Cannot-say discipline** maps to **Context, problem and option analysis**: separating what evidence states from what it suggests, and holding "not established" as a legitimate finding, is the verbal form of sound options and impact analysis.
- **Inference judgement** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management** and **Consultancy**: influencing and negotiating with senior stakeholders requires reading correspondence for its commitments, hedges and deliberate silences — and never confusing your informed guess with their stated position.
- **Clear-writing judgement** maps to **Business modelling** and your coaching accountabilities: conceptual models and advisory papers that support strategic planning must be unambiguous to readers who cannot query the author, and reviewing others' drafts to a teachable standard is how you enable others across the function.
- **Reading standards and contracts** maps to **Testing (business analysis)** and **Adapting to delivery methodologies**: acceptance obligations, quality gates and methodology standards bind only what their words actually bind — and finding the gap between assumed and actual obligations is a recurring lead-level task.

### Practice questions

For questions 1 to 5, use this passage.

> **Extract from a cross-government shared services framework agreement**
>
> Departments may adopt the shared casework platform on either the standard or the enhanced tier. The standard tier includes hosting, maintenance and quarterly releases. The enhanced tier additionally includes a dedicated integration team and monthly releases. Departments adopting either tier must complete a data protection impact assessment before migration and must nominate a senior responsible owner. Requests for functionality changes are considered by the cross-government design board, which meets monthly; the board prioritises requests that benefit multiple departments. A department may exit the platform with six months' notice, provided all data extraction obligations in Schedule 4 are met. Nothing in this agreement transfers statutory accountability for casework decisions from the adopting department to the platform provider.

**Question 1 (easy) — True / False / Cannot say**

Statement: "A department on the standard tier receives releases every month."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage assigns quarterly releases to the standard tier and reserves monthly releases as an enhanced-tier addition. The statement contradicts the tier definition, so it is false — not "cannot say", because the passage directly specifies the standard tier's release cadence. The pattern to notice: two-tier structures generate distractors that quietly transfer a feature from one tier to the other, exactly as commercial conversations do in real life ("we assumed monthly releases were included"). Reading tier and scope boundaries precisely is the verbal foundation of the platform adoption advice your role provides.

**Question 2 (easy) — True / False / Cannot say**

Statement: "A department must nominate a senior responsible owner before adopting either tier."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Look closely at what the passage requires and when. It says departments adopting either tier "must complete a data protection impact assessment before migration and must nominate a senior responsible owner". The DPIA carries an explicit timing condition ("before migration"); the SRO nomination carries none — it is mandatory, but the passage does not say it must precede adoption. The statement adds a timing requirement ("before adopting") that the passage neither guarantees nor contradicts. So the answer is "cannot say". If you chose A, you experienced how naturally the mind distributes a nearby qualifier across a whole sentence — the exact mechanism by which requirements acquire unintended constraints. When you direct requirements standards, this is why you teach analysts that each obligation carries only its own conditions, and why contract lawyers punctuate the way they do.

**Question 3 (moderate) — True / False / Cannot say**

Statement: "If a department exits the platform without meeting Schedule 4's data extraction obligations, its six months' notice is invalid."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage states a department "may exit ... with six months' notice, provided all data extraction obligations in Schedule 4 are met" — making Schedule 4 compliance a condition of the exit right. But the statement asserts a specific legal consequence ("notice is invalid") that the passage does not spell out: failing the proviso might invalidate the notice, delay the exit, create a breach claim, or trigger some Schedule 4 remedy — the passage simply does not say which. We know exit is conditional; we do not know the mechanism of failure. That gap between "conditional" and "this specific consequence" is where Cannot say lives. Lead analysts meet this constantly in contract and policy work: knowing that a condition exists is different from knowing what happens when it fails, and pretending the second knowledge is contained in the first is how organisations discover surprises during disputes.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Inference**

Which of the following is best supported by the passage?

- A) The design board will reject a functionality request that benefits only one department.
- B) The framework's authors anticipated that departments might be held responsible for casework decisions made using the platform, and wished to prevent any doubt that this responsibility stays with departments.
- C) The enhanced tier is better value for money than the standard tier.
- D) Schedule 4 requires data to be extracted within six months.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The final sentence — "Nothing in this agreement transfers statutory accountability ... from the adopting department to the platform provider" — is a clarification with a purpose: drafters include such provisions precisely because the transfer might otherwise be argued or assumed. Inferring authorial intent from the presence of a defensive clause is a modest, well-supported step: B. Option A overreads "prioritises requests that benefit multiple departments" — prioritising multi-department requests does not entail rejecting single-department ones; a priority order is not a rejection rule. C imports a value judgement with no textual basis. D invents Schedule 4's content from the coincidental six-month notice period — the passage never describes what Schedule 4 contains beyond "data extraction obligations". The discriminating skill: distinguishing what a text is *doing* (foreclosing an argument) from what it merely mentions, while refusing to let two unrelated details (six months; Schedule 4) fuse into fabricated content.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Clear-writing judgement**

A department asks you to summarise the exit provision for its board. Which summary is most accurate and clear?

- A) "We can leave whenever we like with six months' notice."
- B) "We can exit on six months' notice, but only if we meet the data extraction obligations in Schedule 4 — so exit planning must include Schedule 4 compliance from the start."
- C) "Exit is essentially impossible; the agreement locks us in via Schedule 4."
- D) "Exit requires six months' notice and design board approval."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** B reproduces both elements of the provision — the notice period and the Schedule 4 proviso — and adds one planning implication that follows directly from the conditional structure (a board summarising an exit right needs to know the condition is load-bearing). A drops the proviso, giving the board a rosier right than it holds; C editorialises the proviso into an impossibility the passage nowhere supports; D imports the design board from a different provision — an error of contamination between clauses, which is among the most common summarisation defects in governance papers. The standard you are practising — every element of the source, nothing not in the source, plus only implications that follow necessarily — is the standard your consultancy outputs are held to when boards act on your summaries without reading the underlying agreement.

For questions 6 to 8, use this passage.

> **Letter from a director general to the lead business analyst**
>
> Thank you for the discovery report on casework consolidation. I have read the summary and recommendations. I am minded to accept the recommendation to consolidate onto a single platform, subject to two conditions. First, the transition must not reduce service levels for vulnerable users at any point; if your implementation analysis cannot evidence this, I would rather phase more slowly. Second, the trade union consultation must begin before any public announcement. I have not yet discussed this with ministers, and until I do, please treat the direction of travel as provisional. I would like your implementation analysis by the 20th. My office will arrange time with you and the service owners in the first week of next month.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Comprehension**

Which statement most accurately captures the director general's position?

- A) The consolidation recommendation is approved and can be announced once union consultation begins.
- B) The DG provisionally supports consolidation, conditional on evidence protecting vulnerable users' service levels throughout the transition and on union consultation preceding any announcement — with the whole direction remaining provisional pending ministerial discussion.
- C) The DG has rejected the recommendation until ministers decide.
- D) The DG requires the implementation analysis before forming any view.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The letter's architecture is: hedged acceptance ("minded to accept") + two named conditions + an explicit provisionality marker pending ministers. B reproduces all three layers. A converts "minded to accept ... provisional" into approval and misorders the announcement logic — consultation must *begin before* any announcement, which is a sequencing constraint, not an announcement trigger. C mistakes provisionality for rejection: "minded to accept" is a positive lean, held open. D reverses the letter's order — the DG has formed a provisional view and is commissioning analysis to satisfy a condition of it, not deferring all judgement. At your level, mapping a senior stakeholder's position with this three-layer precision (lean, conditions, status) is the difference between briefing your teams accurately and propagating a stronger mandate than exists — the exact discipline of owning the main stakeholder relationships.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Inference**

What can most safely be inferred about the DG's priorities?

- A) The DG regards protection of vulnerable users as a constraint worth trading delivery speed for.
- B) The DG considers union agreement more important than ministerial approval.
- C) The DG doubts the quality of the discovery report.
- D) The DG intends to announce the consolidation next month.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The letter states a trade-off explicitly: "if your implementation analysis cannot evidence this, I would rather phase more slowly" — speed is the variable the DG volunteers to sacrifice, vulnerable users' service levels the constraint held firm. That is A, inferred from the DG's own stated preference ordering. B compares two things the letter never ranks against each other — consultation is sequenced before announcement, ministers before firmness of direction; no priority between them is expressed. C has no basis — commissioning further analysis after a discovery report is process, not criticism; the DG thanks you and engages with the recommendations. D fabricates a decision from a diary note: "time with you and the service owners" is a meeting, not an announcement. The reliable technique: the safest inferences restate trade-offs the author has already made, in the author's own structure. Everything else is prediction wearing inference's clothes.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Clear-writing judgement**

You must brief your analysis team on what this letter means for their work. Which briefing line is best?

- A) "The DG has approved consolidation — full speed on implementation planning."
- B) "Nothing is decided; carry on as before."
- C) "The DG provisionally backs consolidation. Our implementation analysis, due the 20th, must evidence that service levels for vulnerable users hold throughout transition — if we can't evidence that, we design a slower phasing. Nothing is public, and nothing is final until ministers are consulted; union consultation must precede any announcement."
- D) "The DG wants the analysis by the 20th; details are in the letter if anyone wants them."

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** C translates each element of the DG's position into its operational consequence for the team: the deadline, the evidential bar the analysis must meet, the design implication if it cannot (slower phasing is a design outcome, not a failure), and the confidentiality and sequencing constraints. It neither inflates the mandate (A's error — "approved" and "full speed" would have the team building announcements into plans that must stay provisional) nor deflates it (B's error — a provisional direction with a dated deliverable is very much a decision to act on). D delegates comprehension to a document most of the team will skim, abdicating exactly the translation duty that leads carry: turning senior intent into team-actionable meaning without loss or gain. The test's clear-writing questions reward the option that is specific, faithful and consequence-oriented — the same three properties that make your real briefings trustworthy.

For questions 9 to 12, use this passage.

> **Extract from a departmental standard on analysis quality assurance**
>
> All analysis products that inform investment decisions above £1 million must undergo independent quality review before submission. A review is independent if the reviewer had no role in producing the product and does not report to the product's author. Reviewers must be accredited under the departmental scheme; accreditation lapses after two years unless renewed. Where no accredited independent reviewer is available within the department, an accredited reviewer may be sought from another department, or the product may be submitted with an explicit statement that independent review was not performed, together with the reasons and the author's own quality declaration. Products informing decisions below the threshold are encouraged, but not required, to follow the same process. This standard does not apply to analysis produced under emergency response protocols.

**Question 9 (moderate) — True / False / Cannot say**

Statement: "An analysis product informing a £800,000 investment decision that skips independent review breaches the standard."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The mandatory review requirement applies to products informing decisions "above £1 million"; £800,000 sits below the threshold, where the standard's own words make the process "encouraged, but not required". A product that skips review below the threshold therefore does not breach the standard — the statement is false on the passage's terms, not unverifiable. The trap is the word "skips", which imports a flavour of wrongdoing that invites a compliance verdict the rules do not support. Threshold-scoped obligations are everywhere in governance — procurement, business cases, data protection — and applying an above-threshold duty to a below-threshold case is the mirror image of the more notorious error (waiving duties above the line). Both misread scope, and scope is always the first thing to check.

**Question 10 (moderate) — True / False / Cannot say**

Statement: "A reviewer whose accreditation lapsed last month, but who had no role in the product and does not report to its author, can provide the independent review the standard requires."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The standard imposes two separable requirement sets: independence (no production role; no reporting line to the author) and accreditation ("Reviewers must be accredited under the departmental scheme"). The described reviewer satisfies independence but fails accreditation, which lapsed and was evidently not renewed. Since accreditation is mandatory for reviewers under this standard, the review this person provides cannot satisfy it — the statement is false. Notice the distractor's construction: it front-loads the satisfied conditions in convincing detail and slips the failed condition past you inside a subordinate clause. Real assurance failures happen the same way — a review that is genuinely independent, thorough and well-documented can still be non-compliant on a formality, and whether your organisation treats that as fatal or fixable is a governance decision, not a reading decision. The reading decision is unambiguous.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Applying the standard to a scenario**

A £3 million investment decision needs an analysis product submitted next week. The department's only two accredited reviewers both worked on early drafts of the product. Under the standard, which course is compliant?

- A) Have the less-involved accredited reviewer perform the review, noting their limited role.
- B) Seek an accredited reviewer from another department; if none can be found, submit with an explicit statement that independent review was not performed, the reasons, and the author's quality declaration.
- C) Submit without review and without any statement, since no independent reviewer exists.
- D) Have the author's line manager, who is not accredited, perform the review instead.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Walk the standard's own decision tree. The product informs a decision above £1 million, so independent review is required. Both internal accredited reviewers had a role in producing the product, so neither is independent — "limited role" is still a role, and the standard's independence definition admits no degrees, which eliminates A. The standard then provides exactly two fallbacks, in order: seek an accredited reviewer from another department, or submit with an explicit no-review statement, reasons, and the author's quality declaration — which is precisely B. C takes the fallback's outcome (submission without review) while discarding its conditions (the statement, reasons and declaration), converting a transparency mechanism into silent non-compliance. D substitutes an unaccredited reviewer, failing Question 10's lesson — and a line manager of the author would likely fail independence too if the author reports to them, though the accreditation failure alone is decisive. Multi-clause standards reward exactly this sequential application, and directing the approach to requirements and assurance across a function means being the person who can walk a team through the tree without skipping a node.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Clear-writing judgement on drafting a standard**

Your function must add a clause to this standard covering conflicts of interest. Which draft is best?

- A) "Reviewers should avoid conflicts of interest where possible."
- B) "A reviewer must declare any personal, financial or professional interest in the product's outcome before the review begins. Where a declared interest could reasonably be seen to affect the reviewer's judgement, the review must be reassigned; where no alternative reviewer is available, the fallback process in paragraph 4 applies, and the declared interest must be stated in the submission."
- C) "Conflicts of interest are unacceptable and will result in disciplinary action."
- D) "Reviewers must be conflict-free."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Test each draft against what a standard must do: define the obligation, its trigger, its consequence, and its edge cases. B does all four — a declaration duty with a timing trigger ("before the review begins"), an objective-flavoured test for when reassignment is required ("could reasonably be seen to affect"), a defined consequence (reassignment), and an edge-case route that integrates with the standard's existing fallback machinery rather than inventing parallel process. A uses "should ... where possible", which creates no testable obligation — the drafting equivalent of the untestable requirement, and the first thing your requirements standards prohibit. C specifies a sanction without defining the offence, the declaration mechanism, or the process — enforceable against no one and frightening to everyone. D states an absolute ("conflict-free") that is undefined and unachievable as written — everyone has interests; the governable act is declaration and management, not non-existence. The craft here is your own daily craft at lead level: standards you write are requirements about behaviour, and they succeed or fail on the same testability, traceability and edge-case discipline you demand of system requirements.

### Preparation tips

- **Rehearse the three-verdict discipline daily.** True = guaranteed by the passage; False = contradicted by it; Cannot say = neither. The False/Cannot-say boundary decides most marks: before answering, ask "does the passage actually address this claim, including its qualifiers?"
- **Read for architecture, not just content.** Senior correspondence has structure — lean, conditions, status markers, deliberate silences. Practise diagramming one real letter or minute a week into those layers; the tests reward exactly that decomposition, and so do your briefings.
- **Watch qualifier attachment.** Timing and scope qualifiers ("before migration", "above £1 million", "in Schedule 4") bind only their own clause. Distractors — and defective requirements — work by letting a qualifier drift across the sentence.
- **Practise on binding documents.** Framework agreements, standards and terms of reference from your own organisation are ideal material: write three statements per document (one true, one false, one unaddressed) and defend each verdict aloud, as if coaching an analyst.
- **For clear-writing questions, mark each option against three tests.** Faithful (every source element, nothing extra)? Specific (actions, owners, dates, conditions)? Consequence-oriented (does the reader know what to do)? The best option usually wins all three; the trap options win tone.
- **Manage passage time deliberately.** One careful read of the passage, then rapid answers to its cluster, beats re-reading per question. For dense governance passages, a fifteen-second mental map of the clauses ("tiers, obligations, board, exit, accountability") pays for itself.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Letting expertise answer instead of the passage.** You know how frameworks and standards usually work; the passage may differ deliberately. The test — like a contract dispute — is decided on the words present.
- **Fusing unrelated details.** Six months' notice and Schedule 4 do not imply six-month extraction obligations. When two details feel connected, check whether the passage connects them or you did.
- **Reading "prioritises X" as "rejects not-X".** Priority rules, preference orderings and encouragements are not prohibitions. Convert them carefully.
- **Treating hedged positions as decisions.** "Minded to accept", "in principle", "provisional" — briefing these as approval manufactures mandates that collapse later, at your credibility's expense.
- **Taking a fallback's outcome without its conditions.** Standards often permit an exceptional route only with transparency attached. The distractor keeps the permission and drops the paperwork.
- **Drafting with untestable words.** "Should where possible", "conflict-free", "appropriate" — in the test's drafting questions and in your own standards, the failing options are the ones no reviewer could verify.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment (SJT) presents realistic workplace dilemmas and asks you to judge the effectiveness of possible responses. There is rarely a single provably correct action; instead, responses are scored against a behavioural key built with experienced practitioners — for a role at your level, behaviours such as strategic influence, integrity under pressure, developing capability in others, proportionate escalation, inclusion, and stewardship of the profession itself.

Typical format: an online assessment of 25 to 45 minutes presenting 8 to 15 scenarios. Response formats include choosing the most effective response; choosing the most *and* least effective; ranking all responses; or rating each independently. The overall assessment is timed but individual scenarios usually are not, so deliberate reading is rewarded. For UK government digital roles, the scoring key is strongly informed by the Service Standard's user-centred ethos and Civil Service behaviours — honesty, objectivity, and working across boundaries.

For a lead business analyst, the SJT is the assessment closest to the actual substance of your job. Your level is defined by judgement exercised through others and upward: you direct stakeholder strategy rather than just managing stakeholders, you enable other analysts to select methods rather than selecting for them, you are a visible leader in a community that extends across government and beyond your employer's walls, and you carry the professional authority that makes your concessions and your challenges equally consequential. The dilemmas that fill your calendar — a director shopping for a friendlier answer, a programme gaming an assurance process, a talented analyst outgrowing their role, a supplier relationship curdling — are precisely what SJTs sample. Practising them converts instinct into articulable judgement, which is also what makes your judgement teachable.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Strategic influence and negotiation** map to **Stakeholder relationship management** and **Consultancy**: scenarios test whether you influence and negotiate with senior stakeholders to resolve issues and enable progress — holding ground on evidence while leaving parties room to move.
- **Evidence integrity at scale** maps to **Context, problem and option analysis** and **Business process improvement**: scenarios probe whether the analysis your function produces stays honest when programmes, suppliers or sponsors need it to say something else.
- **Building capability, not dependency** maps to your coaching accountabilities and **Requirements definition and management**: enabling others to select and apply appropriate methods is tested through struggling-team and talent-management scenarios where doing it yourself is the seductive wrong answer.
- **Methodology leadership** maps to **Adapting to delivery methodologies** and **Business modelling**: scenarios test whether you set and defend proportionate standards — and change them when evidence says to — rather than enforcing or abandoning them under pressure.
- **User focus and inclusion at enterprise scale** map to **User experience analysis** and **Systems analysis**: scenarios include the constituencies without a voice in governance — excluded users, frontline staff, future maintainers — and reward responses that represent them.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — A director shopping for a different answer**

Your team's options analysis for a director's initiative recommends the modest Option B over the ambitious Option C, on cost and deliverability evidence. The director asks you to have "a more experienced analyst take another look, someone who understands the strategic picture". What is the most effective response?

- A) Agree to reassign the analysis to a more senior analyst with instructions to reconsider.
- B) Meet the director to understand what strategic factors they believe the analysis missed; if there are genuinely new considerations, incorporate them transparently and re-run the appraisal; if not, stand behind the team's work and explain the evidence yourself.
- C) Politely refuse and refer the director to your head of profession.
- D) Ask the team to soften the recommendation to "no clear preference" so the director can choose freely.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The request may be answer-shopping — or it may signal that the director holds context the analysis never received. B distinguishes the two cases by engaging first: genuinely new strategic factors are legitimate inputs and incorporating them transparently strengthens the analysis; absent new factors, you defend the team's work personally, which both protects the evidence and shields your analysts from pressure they should not absorb. A concedes the principle that recommendations are negotiable by reviewer selection — once analysis can be re-staffed until it agrees, your function's products are worthless. C escalates before attempting resolution and reads as defensiveness. D corrupts the analysis to avoid the conflict — the worst outcome, since a "no preference" appraisal transfers the decision to exactly the pressure that produced it. Scored behaviours: integrity, influencing senior stakeholders through evidence, and protecting your team — the daily mechanics of owning the main stakeholder relationships.

**Question 2 (easy) — Analyst's flawed work is already with stakeholders**

An analyst in your function circulated an impact assessment to programme stakeholders yesterday. Today they come to you distressed: they have found a significant error — a doubled benefits figure — that materially strengthens the case the paper makes. No decisions have been taken yet. What is the most effective response?

- A) Tell them to correct it quietly in the next scheduled version; drawing attention now would damage their credibility.
- B) Support them to issue a prompt correction to all recipients, briefly and without drama, before any decision relies on the figure; help them check the rest of the paper; and afterwards, review together how the error escaped review — treating their coming forward as exactly the behaviour you want.
- C) Take over: issue the correction yourself under your own name so it carries authority.
- D) Report the error to the programme board as a quality failure in the analyst's work.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The clock matters: an uncorrected doubled benefit becomes embedded in decisions, and correction cost rises steeply after that. B corrects fast and completely (all recipients, before reliance), extends the check to the whole paper (errors travel in company), and — the leadership half — treats the analyst's disclosure as behaviour to reinforce, closing with a systems question (how did review miss it?) rather than a culpability question. That last move is what keeps your function one where errors surface early. A gambles the programme's decision integrity against one person's comfort, and quiet corrections have a way of surfacing loudly later. C fixes the paper but teaches the analyst that errors mean confiscation — next time, disclosure will come slower. D converts a self-reported, pre-decision error into a public failure, guaranteeing your function learns to hide mistakes. Scored behaviours: integrity, ownership, developing others, and psychological safety as an operational asset.

**Question 3 (easy) — Community leadership versus employer convenience**

You co-chair a cross-government business analysis community. Your own department, under budget pressure, tells you to stop "spending time on other departments' problems" and step back from the community for six months. The community's annual event — which you convened — is in eight weeks. What is the most effective response?

- A) Comply immediately; your employer sets your priorities.
- B) Ignore the instruction; cross-government community leadership is part of your role profile.
- C) Make the case to your management that community leadership is an explicit accountability of your role and returns value to the department — recruitment pipeline, shared practice, cross-government influence — and propose a proportionate arrangement: an orderly handover of event convening to your co-chair, with your time commitment reduced and made visible, reviewed after six months.
- D) Escalate to the head of the government business analysis profession, asking them to overrule your department.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Both pressures are legitimate: the department genuinely owns your time; the role genuinely carries community leadership ("a leader in the business analysis community across government and externally"). C negotiates rather than picking a side: it makes the community's value to the department explicit (leads often forget to evidence this until it is questioned), and offers a proportionate, reviewable reduction that keeps commitments honourable — the event transitions cleanly to the co-chair rather than collapsing. A abandons made commitments unexamined and treats an accountability of the role as a hobby; B insubordinately ignores a management instruction, which no role profile licenses; D invites an external authority into an internal resourcing question before attempting resolution — a relationship-scorching first move that would be premature even as a second one. Scored behaviours: negotiation, honouring commitments, evidencing value, and the judgement to treat competing legitimate claims as a design problem rather than a loyalty test.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Assurance gaming**

You provide analysis assurance for programmes approaching investment gates. A programme director, whose case you will review next month, invites you to "pre-review the pack informally so there are no surprises at the gate" — and adds that the last programme you failed at gate "caused six months' delay, which nobody wants repeated". What is the most effective response?

- A) Decline all contact before the gate to preserve independence.
- B) Accept the informal pre-review and, if the pack is weak, quietly help fix it so the gate goes smoothly.
- C) Offer early engagement openly and on standard terms: publish the assurance criteria, run a documented readiness session available to every programme, and be explicit that early feedback helps them fix defects but the gate review itself remains independent and its outcome unconstrained — while noting the delay remark changes nothing.
- D) Report the director's remark to the SRO as attempted intimidation.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Two things are tangled: a legitimate request (early clarity on assurance expectations genuinely improves packs and prevents wasteful gate failures) and an illegitimate pressure (the delay remark, framing a failed gate as your cost rather than the programme's defect). C separates them precisely — early engagement becomes a transparent, universal, documented offer rather than a private favour, which removes both the impropriety and its appearance, while the explicit statement that gate independence is untouched answers the pressure without escalating it. A protects independence by destroying assurance's improvement value — purity at the price of usefulness, and gates exist to improve delivery, not to fail programmes. B is capture: the reviewer who privately fixed the pack cannot independently review it, and "no surprises" becomes "no findings". D treats a deniable remark as a formal offence on first occurrence; the proportionate response is to neutralise it on the record (C does) and escalate only if pressure persists. Scored behaviours: integrity with helpfulness, transparency as the solvent for capture, proportionate response to pressure, and stewardship of the assurance system itself.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Talent outgrowing the function**

Your strongest senior analyst tells you confidentially they are considering an external role: they feel ready for lead-level work and your function has no vacancy. Losing them now would seriously hurt two programmes. What is the most effective response?

- A) Persuade them to stay by emphasising loyalty and the disruption their departure would cause.
- B) Explore what "lead-level work" means to them; identify genuine stretch you can offer now — deputising for you on a programme, leading a community workstream, directing a piece of cross-cutting analysis — be honest about the vacancy position and timeline, actively sponsor them for lead opportunities inside and beyond the function, and plan resilience for the two programmes regardless of their decision.
- C) Immediately begin succession planning and reduce their programme exposure so departure hurts less.
- D) Offer to match the external role's salary through a retention payment.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The lead-level insight is that retention and development are the same conversation, and that some departures should happen — the test is whether you behave as a steward of the person's career and the profession, or as an owner of a resource. B does the whole job: real stretch now (deputising and cross-cutting leadership are lead-level work available without a vacancy), honesty about what you cannot promise (false timeline hints are the classic retention error and cost double when exposed), sponsorship that includes external options (which paradoxically retains — people stay longer where their growth is genuinely championed, and if they go, the profession and your cross-government network gain), and programme resilience planning that de-dramatises the decision. A leverages guilt, which retains bodies and loses engagement. C treats a confidential exploration as a resignation, punishing disclosure with marginalisation — they will notice, and so will everyone watching. D answers a development question with money; even when it works, it expires, and it teaches your best people that leverage beats conversation. Scored behaviours: developing others, honesty, stewardship over ownership, and resilience planning.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Standards versus a delivery emergency**

A programme in crisis asks to skip your function's requirements standard — no traceability, no acceptance criteria review — for one release, to hit a legally significant date. The programme's own senior analyst supports the request: "the standard is right normally, but this release is small and the date is statutory." What is the most effective response?

- A) Refuse: standards that flex under pressure are not standards.
- B) Grant a blanket exemption for the release; statutory dates outrank internal standards.
- C) Work with the programme's senior analyst to apply the standard proportionately rather than binarily: identify the minimum set that protects the statutory outcome itself — traceability and acceptance criteria for the legally significant requirements, lighter treatment for the rest — document the exemption, its rationale and its expiry, and schedule the deferred work as named debt.
- D) Escalate the conflict to the programme's SRO to decide.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The trap is the binary framing both A and B accept. Standards exist to protect outcomes; the statutory date is itself an outcome the standard's machinery protects — untraceable legally significant requirements are precisely how statutory releases fail legal scrutiny after shipping. C re-derives the standard's purpose under constraint: full rigour where legal risk concentrates, proportionate treatment elsewhere, with the exemption documented, time-boxed and its deferred work named as debt rather than forgotten. Note also who C works through: the programme's own senior analyst, whose judgement supported the request — enabling others to select and apply appropriate methods is your named accountability, and co-designing the proportionate application teaches it. A defends the letter of the standard against its purpose and positions the function as delivery's obstacle — the reputation that gets standards abolished. B abandons exactly the requirements whose failure would be most expensive, and unlogged exemptions become precedents. D outsources a methodology judgement that is squarely yours; the SRO should ratify the documented exemption, not design it. Scored behaviours: proportionality, purpose-driven standards leadership, coaching through co-design, and honest debt accounting.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Inclusion challenge at programme scale**

A programme board is about to approve a channel strategy built on your function's analysis: digital-first, with the assisted digital route reduced to a phone line operated 10am–2pm. Late operational data crosses your desk: 22% of the service's current users use the assisted route, concentrated among older users and users with disabilities, and 60% of their contacts occur outside 10am–2pm. The paper is on Thursday's agenda and the programme director wants no late changes. What is the most effective response?

- A) Let the paper proceed; the data arrived after the analysis was complete, and beta will reveal any problems.
- B) Tell the programme director the analysis must be withdrawn and rewritten, missing Thursday's board.
- C) Brief the programme director immediately with the specific numbers, recommend the paper proceed with an amended assisted-digital provision (or an explicit board-level decision on the risk, with the data tabled), and make clear that as the analysis's owner you cannot let the board decide on figures you now know understate excluded users' needs.
- D) Raise the data informally with a sympathetic board member so it surfaces in discussion without your fingerprints.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The analysis carries your function's authority, and you now know the board would be deciding on a materially incomplete picture affecting the users least able to route around a bad service — 22% of users, with a provision window that misses 60% of their demand. C gets the correction into the decision through the front door and at speed: the director hears it first (relationship respect and no ambush), the recommendation preserves Thursday where possible (an amended provision or an explicit, data-tabled risk decision — boards can decide against your advice, but not without it), and the integrity line is stated plainly. A delegates an equity decision to beta, where the cost lands on excluded users before it lands on the programme — and "the data came late" will not comfort anyone at the resulting public accounting. B may be where events end up, but as an opening move it burns the relationship and the date without testing whether a targeted amendment preserves both. D is influence without accountability — if the data matters enough to surface, it matters enough to own; anonymous routes also strip the numbers of the analytical authority that makes them decisive. Scored behaviours: inclusion, user focus, courage with respect, and ownership of the analysis your name underwrites.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Supplier relationship deteriorating**

A strategic supplier's requirements interpretations keep drifting from agreed intent — each drift small, each plausibly deniable, each in the direction of reduced supplier effort. Your programme-level analysts are frustrated; the supplier's account director is charming and unfailingly cooperative in meetings. The commercial team says the contract is "not being breached as such". What is the most effective response?

- A) Instruct your analysts to accept the interpretations; without breach, there is no case.
- B) Establish a joint requirements interpretation log with the supplier: every ambiguity resolved in writing, agreed by both sides, with drift made visible as a documented pattern — and share the accumulating record with commercial colleagues so the pattern is available at the next contractual moment, while raising the trend directly and professionally with the account director.
- C) Confront the account director in the next programme meeting with accusations of bad faith.
- D) Route all future requirements through lawyers before the supplier sees them.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The problem is structural, not moral: ambiguity is being harvested, one deniable interpretation at a time, and no single instance will ever justify confrontation — which is what makes the pattern effective. B changes the structure. A joint interpretation log converts each ambiguity into a written bilateral decision (ending deniability), aggregates the drift into a visible pattern (creating the evidence that "not a breach as such" currently lacks), arms the commercial team for the next negotiation moment, and — by raising the trend directly with the account director — gives the relationship a professional route back before the record hardens. This is your Systems analysis skill's communication clause in action: forming a common understanding between users, teams and external suppliers, in writing. A surrenders the requirements baseline permanently; the absence of breach is an argument for building the record, not for waiving intent. C makes an unevidenced accusation that the charming account director will win in the room. D poisons working-level collaboration and slows delivery to litigation pace over a problem that documentation solves. Scored behaviours: constructive challenge, evidence-building patience, cross-functional teamwork with commercial colleagues, and managing the relationship while managing the record.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Most and least effective: two departments, one truth**

You lead analysis for a shared service adopted by two departments. Department A's leadership wants your utilisation analysis to show the service is under-provisioned (supporting their funding bid); Department B's leadership wants the same analysis to show over-provision (supporting their cost-reduction case). Your findings genuinely show adequate overall provision with severe peak-time bottlenecks. Both departments' directors will see the report. Identify the MOST and LEAST effective responses.

- A) Publish one report to both departments: adequate aggregate provision, severe peak bottlenecks, with the peak-time evidence broken out — and brief both directors together on what the finding means for each of their cases.
- B) Produce a single report but let each department write its own executive summary.
- C) Delay publication until the two departments align their positions.
- D) Emphasise the bottleneck findings to Department A and the aggregate adequacy to Department B, since both are true.

**Correct answer: Most effective A; least effective D**

**Explanation:** Your finding is genuinely two-sided — adequate in aggregate, inadequate at peak — and the integrity test is whether both sides reach both audiences with the same weighting. A publishes one truth, once, to everyone, and the joint briefing is the masterstroke: it prevents each department's machinery from metabolising the report into its preferred half, and it converts your analysis into the shared factual ground on which their funding argument must now be fought — which is precisely what enterprise-level analysis is for. D is least effective, and worse than B: selective emphasis of true statements to opposed audiences is the sophisticated form of dishonesty — each briefing survives fact-checking while the combination is designed to mislead, and when the directors compare notes (they will; they share a service), your function's independence is spent. B keeps the analysis clean but licenses spin at the summary layer, where busy readers live — a known laundering route, though at least the full report stands. C makes truth wait for politics, inverting the dependency: your report exists to enable their alignment. Scored behaviours: independence, transparency, influencing through shared evidence, and the courage to disappoint two masters identically.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Methodology change you disagree with**

Government-level guidance is updated: discovery phases across the profession must adopt a new standardised outcomes framework. You believe — from real experience across your portfolio — that the framework fits transactional services well but distorts discovery for complex casework services, and two of your programmes are about to start casework discoveries. As a visible community leader, your stance will be noticed. What is the most effective response?

- A) Adopt the framework fully and without comment; guidance is guidance.
- B) Publicly criticise the framework at the next cross-government community event and advise your programmes to ignore it.
- C) Adopt the framework in good faith across your portfolio, instrument the two casework discoveries to gather structured evidence on where it helps and distorts, feed that evidence through the profession's formal channels with proposed amendments — and share your experience honestly, framed as improving the framework, when the community asks.
- D) Quietly exempt your casework programmes from the framework while publicly endorsing it.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The question tests how a professional leader dissents. C works within the system at full strength: genuine adoption (not malicious compliance) gives the framework a fair test; instrumenting the casework discoveries converts your opinion into structured evidence — the only currency that changes central guidance; formal channels plus proposed amendments make you the framework's most useful critic rather than its opponent; and honest, improvement-framed communication when the community asks discharges your leadership visibility without weaponising it. This is "set and iterate standards" and "research new and evolving methodologies" behaviour — the framework is someone else's standard, and you iterate it the way you would want yours iterated. A wastes exactly the evidence your position lets you gather — silent compliance by the person best placed to see the distortion is a stewardship failure, politer than B's but real. B spends community leadership on defiance, teaches every analyst watching that guidance is optional when you disagree, and forfeits the influence that would have fixed the framework. D is the worst: public endorsement with private exemption is hypocrisy that, once discovered, ends your authority on standards permanently. Scored behaviours: professional integrity in dissent, evidence over opinion, working through institutions, and modelling the behaviour you require of others.

**Question 11 (challenging) — The benefits your predecessor promised**

Newly arrived as lead, you inherit a flagship transformation whose approved business case — signed off on your predecessor's analysis — claims benefits you quickly conclude were overstated by roughly a third: optimistic uptake assumptions, no ramp-up, double-counted savings between two workstreams. The programme is politically prominent, mid-delivery, and its quarterly benefits report is due in three weeks. What is the most effective response?

- A) Report against the approved baseline as your predecessor defined it; reopening a signed-off case is not your call.
- B) Quietly re-baseline the benefits downward in the quarterly report without drawing attention to the change.
- C) Validate your conclusion rigorously first (you are three weeks in — test the double-counting and uptake analysis with the workstream leads), then brief the SRO and programme director with the corrected model, the specific defects, and options: re-baseline formally with governance approval, present the quarterly report against both baselines with the discrepancy explained, and strengthen benefits assurance so the next case starts honest.
- D) Commission an independent external review of the business case and pause benefits reporting until it concludes.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Sequence is everything. First, verification: three weeks of context is enough to form the hypothesis and not enough to bet the programme's credibility on it — testing the double-counting with the workstream leads either confirms the defect or teaches you the case's logic; both outcomes you need. Second, the front door: the SRO owns the baseline, so the corrected model, the named defects and the governance options go to them — re-baselining is a governance act, not an analytical edit. Third, the dual-baseline quarterly report is the transparency mechanism: it keeps reporting honest during the transition without pretending the past didn't happen. A hides behind process to keep reporting numbers you believe false — each signed report now carries your name and your knowledge; "not my call" does not survive the first audit interview. B corrects the arithmetic while concealing the correction — governance discovers re-baselines eventually, and silent ones read as cover-ups even when they were confessions. D outsources and delays: an external review may eventually be right for so prominent a programme, but pausing benefits reporting on an unvalidated three-week-old hypothesis maximises alarm while minimising your own accountability for the finding. Scored behaviours: verification before escalation, integrity with due process, respect for governance ownership, and fixing the system (benefits assurance) alongside the instance.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Most and least effective: function under existential pressure**

A new chief operating officer questions whether the business analysis function should exist: "Product managers write user stories, architects do the modelling — what do your fourteen analysts add?" A restructure consultation opens in six weeks. Your analysts are anxious; two have already asked for references. Identify the MOST and LEAST effective responses.

- A) Prepare an evidence-based case for the function: quantified contribution (defect prevention rates, benefits validated, discovery outcomes), testimony from the service owners and directors your analysts serve, honest acknowledgement of where the challenge has merit, and proposed reshaping where the evidence supports it — while being straight with your team about the situation and the timetable.
- B) Lobby the directors you know best to protect the function before the consultation formally opens.
- C) Have each analyst document everything they do daily, and submit the fourteen activity logs as the function's defence.
- D) Tell your team there is nothing to worry about while you handle the politics quietly.

**Correct answer: Most effective A; least effective D**

**Explanation:** The COO has asked an analytical question about analysis — and the most persuasive possible answer is to treat it as one. A builds the case the way your function builds any case: quantified outcomes rather than activity, independent testimony from the internal customers whose word carries weight with a COO, honest acknowledgement of the challenge's valid core (overlap with product and architecture roles is real at the boundaries, and a lead who concedes and redesigns the boundary is more credible than one who defends every hectare), and proposed reshaping — which converts you from the restructure's obstacle into its most informed contributor. The straight conversation with your team completes it: they are analysts; they can read a consultation notice; and candour plus a visible plan is what actually slows the flow of reference requests. D is least effective on both fronts: "nothing to worry about" is a promise you cannot keep, discovered as false the day the consultation paper lands, converting anxiety into betrayal and accelerating exactly the departures you feared — while "handling it quietly" wastes the six weeks in which fourteen people could have helped build the evidence. B substitutes relationship capital for evidence — with a new COO watching, back-channel lobbying against their stated challenge reads as confirmation that the function cannot answer it. C documents activity, not value — fourteen diaries of busy-ness answer "what do you do?" when the question was "what do you add?", and volume is not evidence. Scored behaviours: evidence-based advocacy, candour with your people, intellectual honesty about the challenge, and leading a function the way you would advise any service to face scrutiny.

### Preparation tips

- **Answer at your altitude.** The key is calibrated to a leader who works through others, upward, and across organisational boundaries. Responses where you personally rewrite the artefact, absorb the work, or bypass the owning role score as one level too junior — even when they would "work".
- **Look for the response that changes the structure, not just the instance.** Lead-level best answers fix the case *and* the mechanism: the correction plus the assurance gap, the exemption plus its documentation, the interpretation plus the log. Train yourself to spot the option carrying both halves.
- **Separate the legitimate request from the illegitimate pressure.** Many scenarios braid the two together — early engagement with a veiled threat, a strategic steer with answer-shopping. The strongest options honour the legitimate strand explicitly while neutralising the other on the record.
- **Rehearse the front-door principle.** Corrections, dissent and bad news travel through the accountable owner first — SRO, director, author — at speed and with options attached. Anonymous routes, sympathetic intermediaries and quiet fixes are recurring least-effective candidates.
- **Mine your own history.** List five judgement calls from your leadership so far — a supplier drift, a standards exemption, a talent conversation, a pressured finding. For each, write what you did and what the best-practice response would have been. The gap is your preparation syllabus, and the exercise doubles for interviews.
- **Read the whole option before rating it.** Lead-level SJT options are long because the good ones are multi-part. A response that starts perfectly can end with a disqualifying move ("...without telling the director"), and vice versa.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Sophisticated dishonesty.** Selective emphasis of true statements, tailored summaries, silent re-baselines — options that avoid outright lying while engineering a false impression are the designed worst answers at this level. If two audiences would feel deceived on comparing notes, it fails.
- **Purity that abandons usefulness.** Refusing all pre-gate contact, enforcing standards without proportionality, halting everything pending perfect information — rigidity dressed as integrity typically ranks second-worst, behind only concealment.
- **Retention by guilt or gold.** In people scenarios, leveraging loyalty, marginalising those who explore options, and counter-offers without development all score poorly against honest stretch, sponsorship and resilience planning.
- **Escalating before attempting, or concealing instead of escalating.** The gradient is: resolve at level with evidence, escalate with options through the accountable owner, and reserve immediate escalation for bright lines — legal breach, safety, falsified evidence. Misplacing a scenario on this gradient is the most common single error.
- **Defending every hectare.** When your function, standard or analysis is challenged, options that concede nothing read as insecurity, not strength. The key rewards acknowledging the valid core of a challenge and redesigning around it.
- **Answering as your organisation behaves rather than as good practice requires.** If your environment tolerates quiet fixes and tailored briefings, answer to the framework anyway. The test measures your judgement — and so, eventually, does your career.
