## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive ability assessment is a targeted psychometric test designed to measure the exact mental processes your role demands. Unlike a generic IQ test, it uses practical workplace scenarios, artefacts and data drawn from the day-to-day reality of an executive digital leader, and it is designed to predict performance in the job rather than abstract intelligence. Questions are role-targeted, mimicking the everyday tasks and information formats of the actual post; the test is usually timed, with fifteen to thirty minutes to complete an online sitting; scoring is objective, comparing your results against a benchmark or norm group of comparable senior leaders; and the questions themselves are validated so that they predict success in the role without bias.

For a chief digital and information officer, this means the assessment will not ask you to rotate shapes or complete number sequences in the abstract. Instead, it will ask you to find the logical flaw in an investment case, spot the inconsistency between a board paper and its supporting annex, deduce the root cause of a service failure from a chain of incident reports, prioritise a portfolio of competing programmes against strategic criteria, and recognise patterns across supplier performance, cyber risk and capability data. The artefacts are the ones you genuinely handle: strategy papers, architecture decision records, risk registers, spend approvals, delivery dashboards and board minutes.

Expect a mix of question styles: multiple choice with four or five options, "select the flawed assumption" items, ordering and prioritisation tasks, and error-checking exercises where two versions of a document must be reconciled. Modern platforms may adapt difficulty to your answers, and employers typically receive a breakdown of speed versus accuracy rather than a single score, so working steadily and accurately matters as much as working fast.

### What it measures for your role

The role summary names eight professional skills for this post — capability building for digital, data and technology; ensuring resilience of digital, data and technology; financial management for digital, data and technology; innovation in digital, data and technology; leading digital products and services; making strategic performance-led change; optimising technology and data architecture; and shaping the direction of organisational strategy through digital, data and technology. Although the published framework marks the level detail for these skills as not in use, they describe exactly the cognitive territory this assessment probes, alongside your listed duties. In practice, the assessment maps to your work like this:

- **Logical deduction** reflects shaping the direction of organisational strategy: drawing sound conclusions from board papers, horizon-scanning reports and evaluation evidence, and spotting when a recommendation does not follow from its premises.
- **Pattern recognition** reflects identifying and acting on the changing landscape of digital, data and technology: seeing trends across supplier performance, incident data, adoption metrics and emerging technology signals before they become obvious.
- **Error checking** reflects being a trusted advisor to the board: the board relies on you to catch the inconsistency between a business case's benefits table and its financial annex before a decision is made on faulty figures.
- **Prioritisation** reflects advising on investment priorities across competing demands: ranking programmes, risks and capability investments against strategic criteria to maximise benefits and outcomes.
- **Problem solving** reflects ensuring resilience and overseeing sustainable digital solutions: diagnosing the true root cause of a failure or delivery problem from incomplete, sometimes contradictory operational evidence, and choosing interventions that fix causes rather than symptoms.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Logical deduction from a board paper**

A board paper you are reviewing states: "All programmes in the digital portfolio that passed their latest service standard assessment are eligible for continued funding. The Casework Modernisation Programme is eligible for continued funding."

Which one of the following conclusions can be logically drawn?

- A) The Casework Modernisation Programme passed its latest service standard assessment.
- B) The Casework Modernisation Programme did not fail its latest service standard assessment.
- C) Programmes that did not pass their latest assessment are not eligible for continued funding.
- D) None of the above conclusions can be logically drawn.

**Correct answer: D.**

**Explanation:** The paper says passing the assessment is sufficient for eligibility, not that it is the only route to eligibility. A programme might be eligible for other reasons — for example, a ministerial commitment or a statutory obligation — so option A does not follow, and neither does B (we cannot rule out that it failed but was made eligible another way). Option C reverses the statement: "all who pass are eligible" does not imply "all who do not pass are ineligible". This is the classic error of affirming the consequent, and it appears constantly in real governance papers, where sufficient conditions are quietly treated as necessary ones. As the board's trusted advisor on digital matters, you are precisely the person expected to catch it.

**Question 2 (easy) — Error checking an investment summary**

You receive two versions of an investment summary for the same programme. Version 1 (from the programme team) states: "Total whole-life cost £48.2m; expected annual benefit £13.5m from year 3; delivery confidence rating Amber; 62% of user research sessions complete." Version 2 (prepared for the board pack) states: "Total whole-life cost £48.2m; expected annual benefit £15.3m from year 3; delivery confidence rating Amber; 62% of user research sessions complete."

Which field is inconsistent between the two versions?

- A) Total whole-life cost
- B) Expected annual benefit
- C) Delivery confidence rating
- D) User research completion percentage

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** The expected annual benefit is £13.5m in the programme version but £15.3m in the board version — a transposition of digits that inflates the stated benefit by £1.8m per year. Everything else matches. Digit transposition is one of the most common real-world document errors, and it is dangerous precisely because the numbers look plausible at a glance. In a timed test, scan field by field rather than reading each document whole: comparing like with like is faster and far more reliable. At board level, an uncaught error like this could distort an investment decision worth tens of millions of pounds of public money.

**Question 3 (moderate) — Prioritising a portfolio under a spending constraint**

Your organisation faces an in-year budget reduction and you must recommend which one of four initiatives to pause. The strategic criteria agreed with the board are, in strict priority order: (1) protect legal and statutory obligations, (2) protect cyber security and resilience, (3) protect services with the largest number of active end users, (4) protect initiatives with the shortest time to benefits realisation.

- Initiative W: replaces a legacy system whose supplier support contract expires in 14 months; 40,000 users; benefits from year 2; no statutory driver.
- Initiative X: delivers a statutory reporting capability required by new legislation taking effect next year; 300 users; benefits from year 3.
- Initiative Y: implements security monitoring across the estate following a critical audit finding; no direct end users; benefits immediate.
- Initiative Z: improves an internal HR self-service tool; 12,000 users; benefits from month 6; no statutory driver.

Applying the criteria in order, which initiative should you recommend pausing?

- A) Initiative W
- B) Initiative X
- C) Initiative Y
- D) Initiative Z

**Correct answer: A.**

**Explanation:** Apply the criteria strictly in sequence. Criterion 1 protects Initiative X, the only one with a statutory driver. Criterion 2 protects Initiative Y, which addresses cyber security and resilience following a critical audit finding. That leaves W and Z competing under criterion 3, which protects *services* with the largest number of active end users. Read that wording carefully: W is a replacement programme, not a live service — its 40,000 users are today served by the existing legacy system, which keeps running whether or not the replacement is paused, and the supplier contract gives 14 months of headroom. Z's 12,000 users, by contrast, are actively using the very tool the initiative is improving, and Z also wins on criterion 4 with benefits from month 6. Pausing W delays a future service; pausing Z degrades work on a current one. So W is the sound recommendation to pause. The discriminating skill here is precise reading — asking what "active end users" actually attaches to — combined with disciplined application of agreed criteria rather than gut feel. This mirrors real investment advice at board level, where the defensibility of your recommendation depends on showing exactly how the agreed decision framework was applied.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Pattern recognition in supplier performance**

Reviewing quarterly scorecards for a strategic supplier over two years, you note the following pattern in missed service levels: Q1: 1 missed, Q2: 0, Q3: 2, Q4: 5, then the next year Q1: 1, Q2: 0, Q3: 3, Q4: 6.

A colleague argues the supplier is simply deteriorating. Which interpretation best fits the data?

- A) Steady linear deterioration across all quarters.
- B) A seasonal pattern of Q4 pressure, with slight year-on-year worsening in the second half.
- C) Random variation with no discernible pattern.
- D) Improvement in the first half of each year offsetting the second half.

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** Lay the two years side by side: Q1 (1 → 1) and Q2 (0 → 0) are stable; Q3 (2 → 3) and Q4 (5 → 6) each worsen by one. The failures cluster in the fourth quarter of both years — pointing to a seasonal driver such as year-end change freezes colliding with peak demand, or annual leave patterns thinning support cover — with a mild year-on-year worsening confined to the second half. Option A is wrong because the first half is flat, not deteriorating; C is wrong because the Q4 spike repeats too precisely to be noise; D misreads stability as improvement. The executive skill here is refusing both lazy narratives ("supplier is failing" and "nothing to see") in favour of the specific, actionable pattern: the contract conversation should target second-half resilience, not a blanket performance escalation.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Root cause deduction from incident reports**

Three incident reports land on your desk after a major outage of a citizen-facing service:

- Report 1 (service desk): "Users began reporting login failures at 09:12. Failures affected only users authenticating through the shared government identity platform."
- Report 2 (infrastructure): "A certificate on the identity integration gateway expired at 09:00. Automated renewal had been disabled during a change freeze in December and never re-enabled."
- Report 3 (security operations): "No indicators of compromise detected. Traffic patterns normal apart from authentication error spikes from 09:12."

Which statement best identifies the root cause?

- A) A cyber attack on the identity platform.
- B) A failure of the shared government identity platform itself.
- C) A lapsed operational control: certificate auto-renewal was disabled and not re-enabled, allowing an expiry to take the integration down.
- D) Users entering incorrect credentials in unusually high volumes.

**Correct answer: C.**

**Explanation:** Chain the evidence. Report 3 rules out attack (option A) — no indicators of compromise. Report 1 shows failures only for users routed through the identity integration, and Report 2 supplies the mechanism: a certificate on your own gateway expired at 09:00, minutes before failures began at 09:12, because auto-renewal was switched off during a change freeze and never restored. Option B blames the shared platform, but the failing component is your organisation's gateway, not the platform itself — an important distinction before you escalate cross-government. Option D is incompatible with the sudden, synchronised onset. Note the deeper lesson the question encodes: the root cause is not "a certificate expired" but "a temporary risk decision was never reversed" — a process failure. Executives who stop at the technical trigger fix one certificate; those who reach the process cause fix the class of failure, which is what ensuring resilience of digital, data and technology really means.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Spotting the flawed assumption in an innovation proposal**

A directorate submits a proposal: "Departments that adopted the AI-assisted correspondence triage tool reduced their response backlogs by 30% within six months. If we adopt the tool, we will therefore reduce our backlog by 30% within six months. We should proceed without a pilot to capture benefits sooner."

Which is the most serious flaw in this reasoning?

- A) It assumes results transfer between organisations with potentially different correspondence volumes, workflows and data quality, and removes the pilot that would test this.
- B) It cites a percentage rather than an absolute number.
- C) It considers only a six-month horizon rather than whole-life benefits.
- D) It fails to name the departments that adopted the tool.

**Correct answer: A.**

**Explanation:** The proposal's engine is an analogy: what happened elsewhere will happen here. That inference depends on unexamined similarity — correspondence type, volume, staff workflows, data quality, integration constraints — and the proposal simultaneously removes the very mechanism (a pilot) that would test whether the analogy holds. Options B, C and D describe presentational weaknesses that better drafting could fix without changing the decision; option A is a structural flaw that could lead to a failed deployment. This pattern matters for your duty to enable innovative approaches and emerging technologies whilst ensuring inclusivity, security and ethical practices: the executive skill is not blocking innovation, but insisting that enthusiasm is converted into testable claims. A strong answer in a follow-up interview would propose a time-boxed pilot with explicit success measures rather than a binary adopt/reject.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Interoperability logic across government**

Your architecture board records these decisions: (1) "All new citizen-facing services must expose their core data through the cross-government API standard." (2) "Services handling special category data are exempt from decision 1 unless the data is anonymised." (3) "The new benefits eligibility checker handles special category data and does not anonymise it."

A programme director asserts: "The benefits eligibility checker must therefore expose its core data through the cross-government API standard." Is this assertion:

- A) Correct — decision 1 applies to all new citizen-facing services.
- B) Incorrect — decisions 2 and 3 together exempt the checker from decision 1.
- C) Correct — exemptions require separate approval, which is not mentioned.
- D) Cannot be determined from the decisions recorded.

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** Work the rules like a lawyer. Decision 1 creates a general obligation. Decision 2 creates an exemption for services handling special category data, cancelled only where the data is anonymised. Decision 3 establishes that the checker handles special category data and does not anonymise it, so the exemption applies and the obligation from decision 1 is lifted. Option A ignores the exemption; option C invents an approval requirement that appears nowhere in the recorded decisions (a classic trap: importing plausible outside knowledge into a closed logical world); option D is wrong because the three decisions do determine the outcome. Governing architecture decisions that support scalability, reusability and interoperability across government means holding exactly this kind of rule hierarchy in your head — and noticing when colleagues apply the headline rule while forgetting the recorded exceptions.

**Question 8 (challenging) — Prioritisation with interdependencies**

Five activities compete for your enterprise architecture team's next quarter. Dependencies: the data-sharing agreement (activity P) must complete before the interoperability pilot (Q) can start; the cloud landing zone (R) must complete before both the legacy migration (S) and the security monitoring upgrade (T) can start. The team can run at most two activities at once, and each activity takes the full quarter. The board's stated top outcome for the year is completing the legacy migration; the second outcome is the interoperability pilot.

Which pair of activities should run this quarter?

- A) P and Q
- B) P and R
- C) R and S
- D) Q and T

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** Reason backwards from the outcomes. The legacy migration (S) cannot start until the cloud landing zone (R) completes, so R must run as early as possible — this quarter. The interoperability pilot (Q) cannot start until the data-sharing agreement (P) completes, so P must also run this quarter if Q is to start next quarter. Running P and R now unblocks both priority outcomes for the following quarter (S and Q can then run together). Option A wastes a slot: Q cannot legally start because P has not completed. Option C fails for the same reason: S cannot start before R completes. Option D is doubly blocked: Q needs P first, and T needs R first. The question mirrors a real executive skill — sequencing enabling work so that strategic outcomes are never waiting on a dependency you could have cleared a quarter earlier. When you see dependency questions under time pressure, sketch the chains (P→Q; R→S, R→T) before evaluating any option; the diagram answers the question almost by itself.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Reconciling contradictory management information**

Your delivery dashboard shows the flagship service's monthly active users rising 8% month on month, while the same pack reports digital take-up (the proportion of all transactions completed digitally) falling from 71% to 64% over the same period. A board member says the two figures cannot both be right. Which explanation would reconcile them?

- A) The figures are from different months and one must be a typo.
- B) Total transaction volume across all channels grew faster than digital transactions, so digital's share fell even as absolute digital usage rose.
- C) Monthly active users and digital take-up measure the same thing, so one is wrong.
- D) The dashboard supplier changed and the historic data was lost.

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** A rising absolute number and a falling share are perfectly compatible when the denominator grows faster than the numerator. If total demand surged — say, a policy change drove a spike in claims arriving by phone and post as well as online — digital transactions can rise 8% while digital's *share* of all transactions falls seven percentage points. Options A and D speculate about data problems without evidence, and option C is simply false: active users and channel share are different measures with different denominators. The executive insight is that the reconciled story is more alarming than either figure alone — demand is surging and the assisted channels are absorbing the growth, which carries cost and user-experience consequences. Leaders of digital products and services must be fluent in numerator/denominator reasoning, because most dashboard "contradictions" dissolve, and most genuine crises emerge, exactly there.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Error checking a benefits table against its narrative**

A full business case narrative states: "The programme delivers cashable savings of £6.4m per year from year 2, rising to £9.6m per year from year 4, against annual running costs of £3.2m. The benefit-cost ratio over the five-year appraisal period is 4.1." The benefits table in the annex shows: Year 1: £0; Year 2: £6.4m; Year 3: £6.4m; Year 4: £9.6m; Year 5: £9.6m; total benefits £32.0m; total running costs (years 1–5): £16.0m; stated ratio 4.1.

Which single element is inconsistent with the others?

- A) The total benefits figure of £32.0m
- B) The total running costs of £16.0m
- C) The stated benefit-cost ratio of 4.1
- D) The year 3 benefit of £6.4m

**Correct answer: C.**

**Explanation:** Verify each element. Benefits: £0 + £6.4m + £6.4m + £9.6m + £9.6m = £32.0m — consistent. Running costs: £3.2m per year for five years = £16.0m — consistent. Year 3 at £6.4m matches the narrative ("£6.4m per year from year 2, rising from year 4"). But the ratio: £32.0m ÷ £16.0m = 2.0, not 4.1. The stated benefit-cost ratio is more than double what the annex supports. In a real approval, a 2.0 ratio might still justify investment, but a case approved at a claimed 4.1 that is really 2.0 exposes the accounting officer — and you, as the board's digital advisor — to serious challenge from audit and from HM Treasury spend controls. The test skill is refusing to accept derived figures on trust: whenever a document offers you a total, a percentage or a ratio alongside its inputs, spend the fifteen seconds to recompute it.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Capability pattern recognition**

Your workforce dashboard shows, for four critical digital, data and technology job families, the current vacancy rate and the twelve-month leaver rate: software engineering (vacancies 8%, leavers 9%); cyber security (vacancies 22%, leavers 19%); data engineering (vacancies 21%, leavers 8%); product management (vacancies 7%, leavers 21%).

Which diagnosis best fits the data?

- A) All four families share the same underlying problem and need the same intervention.
- B) Cyber security has a retention-and-supply crisis; data engineering struggles to recruit but retains well; product management retains poorly but backfills quickly; software engineering is broadly stable.
- C) The organisation's pay is uncompetitive across the board.
- D) Product management is the healthiest family because its vacancy rate is lowest.

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** Read the two measures jointly rather than separately. Cyber security is losing people fast (19%) and cannot fill the gaps (22% vacancies): both taps are failing, which signals a market-wide supply problem plus internal retention issues — the most urgent case. Data engineering keeps its people (8% leavers) but carries 21% vacancies: a recruitment or pipeline problem, pointing to interventions like apprenticeships, reskilling pathways and employer-brand work rather than retention spend. Product management churns heavily (21% leavers) yet shows only 7% vacancies: it backfills quickly, but constant churn destroys continuity on product teams even when seats are warm — the low vacancy rate masks the damage, which is why option D is a trap. Software engineering is stable on both measures. Option A and option C flatten four distinct patterns into one story the data does not support. This joint-reading skill underpins your duty to ensure the organisation anticipates and adapts to changes in capability needs: single-metric narratives produce single-tool responses, and they are usually wrong.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Strategic problem solving under constraint**

Two years into a three-year strategy, your flagship transformation is consuming 60% of your change budget and has delivered 30% of its planned benefits, with the remaining benefits dependent on a data platform that is itself twelve months late. An external review offers four options. The board asks which option best protects overall strategic outcomes, given that the organisation's statutory workload is forecast to rise 15% next year and cannot be served on current systems beyond eighteen months.

- A) Stop the transformation entirely and redirect all funds to keeping current systems running.
- B) Continue the transformation unchanged, since sunk costs mean stopping would waste the investment already made.
- C) Rescope the transformation around the capacity-critical components needed within eighteen months, gate the remainder on the data platform's recovery, and fund minimal current-system remediation as a bridge.
- D) Pause the data platform and reallocate its budget to accelerate the transformation's user-facing features.

**Correct answer: C.**

**Explanation:** Anchor on the binding constraint: current systems cannot carry the statutory workload beyond eighteen months, so any acceptable option must deliver capacity-critical capability inside that window. Option A abandons the only route to that capability and merely extends the life of systems already known to be insufficient. Option B commits the sunk-cost fallacy explicitly — money already spent is not a reason to continue — and ignores the benefit shortfall. Option D is perverse: the remaining benefits *depend* on the data platform, so defunding it accelerates features that cannot deliver value. Option C does what strong executives do under constraint: it sequences by criticality, converts an open-ended programme into gated commitments, and buys a bridge for the transition period. Note that C is also the most uncomfortable option to present, because it admits the original plan has failed in part. Cognitive assessments at this level deliberately reward the answer that is analytically right rather than presentationally comfortable — as does the accounting officer, eventually.

### Preparation tips

- **Practise with your own artefacts.** Take a recent board pack, business case or delivery dashboard and interrogate it the way these questions do: recompute every derived figure, trace every recommendation back to its evidence, and hunt for the inconsistency between narrative and annex. Ten minutes a day on real documents is worth more than hours of abstract puzzles.
- **Name the reasoning patterns.** Most items at this level turn on a small set of structures: sufficient versus necessary conditions, numerator versus denominator, correlation versus mechanism, sunk cost, dependency chains, and rule-plus-exception hierarchies. When you can name the pattern, you can spot it in seconds.
- **Sketch before you select.** For dependency and prioritisation questions, draw the chain or jot the criteria order before reading the options. The options are written to sound plausible; your sketch is written to be true.
- **Manage the clock deliberately.** With fifteen to thirty minutes for the whole test, budget your time per question, take the marks that come quickly, flag the grinders and return to them. A senior leader's calm triage of their own attention is itself part of what is being measured.
- **Trust your experience, but check it at the door.** Questions define a closed world; answer from the text and data given, not from what usually happens in your organisation. The habit of asking "what does this document actually establish?" serves you in the test and in the boardroom alike.

### Common pitfalls

- **Treating sufficient conditions as necessary ones.** Governance language is full of "all X are Y" statements; the test will repeatedly invite you to reverse them illegitimately. Slow down whenever a conclusion feels obvious.
- **Accepting derived figures on trust.** Totals, percentages and ratios presented alongside their inputs are invitations to recompute. The single most common planted error is a ratio that does not match its own table.
- **Importing outside knowledge.** Your depth of experience is an asset everywhere except inside a closed logical question, where it becomes a source of unwarranted assumptions. Answer from what is stated.
- **Time-sink questions.** Multi-stage prioritisation items can consume five minutes if you let them. Skip, bank the easier marks, return. Speed-versus-accuracy reports mean an unfinished test with high accuracy can still read poorly.
- **Choosing the comfortable answer over the correct one.** At executive level, distractor options are often the politically smooth choices — continue as planned, defer the decision, split the difference. The keyed answer is the one the evidence supports, however awkward its consequences.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment evaluates your ability to analyse, interpret and make sound decisions using numerical data of the kind your role genuinely produces. It mimics the data-handling demands of the post, focusing on applied business mathematics rather than abstract formulae. The inputs are real-world artefacts — budget tables, investment appraisals, capacity forecasts, performance dashboards, workforce figures — and the mathematics itself is deliberately accessible: percentages, ratios, proportions, growth rates and cost-benefit estimation. Most assessments allow or provide an on-screen calculator, because the skill being measured is interpretation and decision-making, not mental arithmetic. What makes the test demanding is the time constraint: you will typically face strict limits that require quick, accurate extraction of the right numbers from dense tables before any calculation begins.

For a chief digital and information officer, the numbers in the test are the numbers in your working week: a technology budget running to tens or hundreds of millions of pounds; cloud consumption trending against forecast; the whole-life cost comparison between remediating a legacy estate and replacing it; the benefit-cost ratio in an outline business case heading for HM Treasury; availability percentages and incident volumes on your resilience dashboard; headcount, vacancy and salary figures underpinning your capability plan. You will rarely be asked for a single arithmetic step. Instead, questions chain two or three steps — extract, compute, compare — and the wrong options are built from the mistakes people actually make: reading the wrong row, ignoring a "figures in £000s" qualifier, or dividing by the wrong base.

Expect roughly 60 to 90 seconds per question, data presented once and reused across several questions, and multiple-choice options spaced closely enough that estimation alone will not always save you. Accuracy under time pressure — knowing when a rough check suffices and when a precise calculation is essential — is precisely the executive skill the format rewards.

### What it measures for your role

Mapped against the professional skills named in your role summary — financial management for digital, data and technology; optimising technology and data architecture; ensuring resilience of digital, data and technology; making strategic performance-led change; leading digital products and services; capability building; innovation; and shaping organisational strategy — the numeric assessment probes:

- **Budget and investment fluency** (financial management): reading multi-year budget tables, computing variances, comparing whole-life costs, and sanity-checking benefit-cost ratios before they reach the board — directly supporting your duties to negotiate and secure investment and to advise on investment priorities across competing demands.
- **Capacity and consumption reasoning** (optimising technology and data architecture): cloud spend per service, utilisation percentages, unit-cost trends, and the arithmetic of scalability — does doubling demand double cost, or better?
- **Resilience metrics** (ensuring resilience): availability percentages, incident frequency and recovery times, and the cost exposure of downtime, supporting your duty to keep the organisation safe, secure and resilient to cyber threats.
- **Performance and take-up analysis** (making strategic performance-led change; leading digital products and services): digital take-up rates, cost per transaction across channels, and benefits realisation tracking against plan.
- **Workforce arithmetic** (capability building): vacancy rates, contractor-to-permanent cost ratios and training investment per head, supporting your duty to ensure the organisation anticipates and adapts to changes in capability needs.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Budget variance**

Your directorate's technology budget for the year is £84.0m. At month 9, forecast full-year spend is £89.9m. What is the forecast overspend as a percentage of budget, to one decimal place?

- A) 6.6%
- B) 7.0%
- C) 5.9%
- D) 7.6%

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** Overspend = £89.9m − £84.0m = £5.9m. As a percentage of budget: £5.9m ÷ £84.0m = 0.0702…, so 7.0%. Option C is the classic trap: £5.9m is the absolute overspend in millions, and the option simply relabels it as a percentage. Option A comes from dividing by the forecast (£5.9m ÷ £89.9m = 6.6%) — wrong base. Always ask: percentage *of what*? Variance against budget uses budget as the denominator. This tiny discipline matters at scale: reporting a 6.6% overspend to a board when the true figure is 7.0% misstates the pressure on your budget by £0.3m.

**Question 2 (easy) — Cost per transaction across channels**

Last quarter your organisation handled 2.4 million transactions: 1.8 million digital at a total cost of £2.16m, and 0.6 million by phone and post at a total cost of £4.5m. How many times more expensive is the average assisted transaction than the average digital transaction?

- A) 5.00 times
- B) 6.25 times
- C) 2.08 times
- D) 7.50 times

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** Digital unit cost: £2.16m ÷ 1.8m = £1.20 per transaction. Assisted unit cost: £4.5m ÷ 0.6m = £7.50 per transaction. Ratio: £7.50 ÷ £1.20 = 6.25. Option D is the assisted unit cost mistaken for the ratio; option C compares total costs the wrong way (£4.5m ÷ £2.16m = 2.08) without adjusting for volume; option A is a plausible round number to tempt estimators. The two-step structure — unit costs first, then the ratio — is the single most common numeric pattern in channel-shift business cases, and it is the arithmetic behind every "each 1% of take-up saves £X" claim your teams put in front of you.

**Question 3 (easy) — Availability percentage**

A citizen-facing service has an availability target of 99.5% measured over a 30-day month (720 hours). This month it suffered two outages: one of 2 hours 15 minutes and one of 1 hour 33 minutes. Did the service meet its target?

- A) Yes — availability was approximately 99.53%
- B) No — availability was approximately 99.47%
- C) Yes — availability was approximately 99.72%
- D) No — availability was approximately 99.21%

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** Total downtime = 2 h 15 m + 1 h 33 m = 3 h 48 m = 3.8 hours. Availability = (720 − 3.8) ÷ 720 = 716.2 ÷ 720 = 0.99472, or 99.47% — just below the 99.5% target, which permits at most 0.5% × 720 = 3.6 hours of downtime. The service exceeded its downtime allowance by 12 minutes. Option A rounds carelessly in the service's favour; option C counts only the first outage. The executive point: at high availability levels the margins are minutes, not hours, so "we were only a few minutes over" and "we breached our service level" are the same statement. Knowing the downtime allowance implied by a target (99.5% of 720 hours leaves 3.6 hours; 99.9% leaves 43 minutes) lets you challenge resilience reporting at a glance.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Cloud consumption forecast**

Your monthly cloud bill is £420,000 and has grown at a steady 5% per month for the last quarter. The finance committee asks for the projected monthly bill in six months if the growth rate continues. Which is closest?

- A) £546,000
- B) £563,000
- C) £588,000
- D) £630,000

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** Compound growth: £420,000 × 1.05⁶. Compute 1.05⁶: 1.05² = 1.1025; 1.1025² = 1.2155 (that is 1.05⁴); 1.2155 × 1.1025 = 1.3401 (1.05⁶). Projection: £420,000 × 1.3401 ≈ £562,800, so £563,000. Option D uses simple growth (6 × 5% = 30%; £420,000 × 1.30 = £546,000 — actually that gives option A); option A is the simple-interest error; option C overshoots by compounding seven months. The difference between simple and compound treatment is £17,000 a month here and grows every month thereafter — exactly the gap that turns a "manageable" cloud trend into an unbudgeted pressure by year end. When you challenge a consumption forecast, your first question should always be whether growth was compounded.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Whole-life cost comparison**

You are comparing two options for a legacy case-management system. Option 1 (remediate): £6.5m upfront, then £4.8m per year to run. Option 2 (replace): £18.0m upfront over the first two years, then £2.2m per year to run from year 1 onwards alongside the build. Over a five-year appraisal period, which option is cheaper, and by how much? (Treat Option 2's running cost as £2.2m in each of the five years.)

- A) Option 1, by £1.5m
- B) Option 2, by £1.5m
- C) Option 1, by £4.1m
- D) Option 2, by £4.1m

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** Option 1: £6.5m + (5 × £4.8m) = £6.5m + £24.0m = £30.5m. Option 2: £18.0m + (5 × £2.2m) = £18.0m + £11.0m = £29.0m. Option 2 is cheaper by £30.5m − £29.0m = £1.5m. The trap answers come from arithmetic slips: option C from using four years of running costs for Option 1; option D from forgetting one year of Option 2's running costs. Notice how finely balanced the result is: a one-year slip in the replacement schedule (one more year of double-running) would add £4.8m to Option 2 and reverse the conclusion. Strong candidates state that sensitivity, and strong chief digital and information officers demand it in every remediate-versus-replace paper they sponsor — a five-year total that flips on a twelve-month delay is not a robust basis for an £18m commitment without contingency planning.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Benefits realisation tracking**

A transformation programme planned cumulative benefits of £24.0m by the end of year 3. Actual cumulative benefits are £15.6m. The programme director notes that benefits in year 3 alone were £8.4m against a year 3 plan of £9.0m. What percentage of planned cumulative benefits has been realised, and what does the year 3 comparison add?

- A) 65% realised; year 3 delivery has collapsed
- B) 65% realised; year 3 ran at 93% of plan, so most of the shortfall arose in earlier years
- C) 79% realised; the programme is broadly on track
- D) 54% realised; year 3 performance is irrelevant to the cumulative picture

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** Cumulative realisation: £15.6m ÷ £24.0m = 0.65 = 65%. Year 3 alone: £8.4m ÷ £9.0m = 0.933 = 93% of plan. So the recent run-rate is close to plan, meaning the £8.4m cumulative shortfall (£24.0m − £15.6m) was mostly incurred in years 1 and 2 — a late start rather than a failing engine. Option A misreads the year 3 evidence; option C uses the wrong division (£15.6m ÷ £19.8m of some other base); option D discards diagnostic information. The distinction matters when you advise the board on investment priorities: a programme realising 93% of plan in-year may deserve continued backing with a rebased profile, whereas one whose current-year delivery is also collapsing may not. Cumulative and in-year measures answer different questions — how far behind, and whether we are still falling behind — and executive-level numeric tests reward candidates who read both.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Cyber investment and risk exposure**

Your security team estimates the organisation faces a 20% annual likelihood of a major cyber incident with an expected cost of £12.0m per occurrence. A proposed monitoring and response investment costing £1.5m per year is assessed to cut the likelihood to 8%. Using expected annual values, what is the net annual benefit of the investment?

- A) A net benefit of £0.96m
- B) A net benefit of £1.44m
- C) A net benefit of £2.40m
- D) A net cost of £0.06m — the investment does not pay for itself on expected value alone

**Correct answer: D.**

**Explanation:** Expected annual loss now: 0.20 × £12.0m = £2.40m. Expected annual loss with the investment: 0.08 × £12.0m = £0.96m. Gross risk reduction: £2.40m − £0.96m = £1.44m. Net of the £1.5m annual cost: £1.44m − £1.50m = −£0.06m. On pure expected value the investment falls £60,000 a year short of paying for itself. Option B is the gross reduction with the cost forgotten; option A is the residual expected loss mislabelled as a benefit; option C is the current exposure. The question is deliberately uncomfortable, because the arithmetic and the right decision can point different ways: expected value ignores variance, and a single £12m loss event may be intolerable to your organisation even when its annualised cost looks manageable — to say nothing of service disruption to citizens and loss of public trust, which the £12m figure may not capture. Your duty to keep the organisation safe, secure and resilient means presenting both the expected-value arithmetic and the risk-appetite argument, and being explicit with the board about which one is doing the work in your recommendation. What the test measures is that you can produce the −£0.06m figure cleanly; what the role demands is that you know what it does and does not settle.

**Question 8 (challenging) — Workforce cost modelling**

Your digital, data and technology workforce is 400 people: 300 permanent staff at an average full cost of £68,000 and 100 contractors at an average full cost of £148,000. A capability plan proposes converting 40 contractor positions to permanent posts at the permanent average cost, with a one-off conversion cost (recruitment, onboarding, knowledge transfer) of £8,000 per post. What is the net saving in the first year, and the recurring annual saving thereafter?

- A) First year £2.88m; thereafter £3.20m
- B) First year £3.20m; thereafter £3.20m
- C) First year £2.88m; thereafter £2.88m
- D) First year £3.52m; thereafter £3.20m

**Correct answer: A.**

**Explanation:** Saving per converted post: £148,000 − £68,000 = £80,000. Across 40 posts: 40 × £80,000 = £3.20m per year recurring. First-year one-off costs: 40 × £8,000 = £0.32m. First-year net: £3.20m − £0.32m = £2.88m; thereafter the full £3.20m recurs. Option B forgets the conversion cost; option C wrongly applies it every year; option D adds it instead of subtracting. Beyond the arithmetic, note what the numbers say about capability building: a £0.32m one-off investment yields £3.20m a year, a payback measured in weeks — which is why contractor conversion is usually the first lever in any digital, data and technology capability plan you will sponsor. The realistic caveats (can the market supply 40 permanent hires? what is the productivity dip during knowledge transfer?) belong in your narrative, but the test measures whether you can produce the baseline figures crisply.

**Question 9 (challenging) — Reading a budget table with unit qualifiers**

A summary table in a finance pack reads (figures in £000s): Infrastructure: budget 22,400, forecast 23,850. Applications: budget 18,900, forecast 17,640. Cyber security: budget 9,600, forecast 10,080. Data platform: budget 12,200, forecast 12,200. A colleague summarises: "We are £1.03m over in total, driven by infrastructure and cyber." Is the total correct?

- A) Yes — the net position is £1.03m over
- B) No — the net position is £2.71m over
- C) No — the net position is £0.67m over
- D) No — the net position is £1.26m under

**Correct answer: C.**

**Explanation:** Compute each variance (forecast minus budget, in £000s): infrastructure +1,450; applications −1,260; cyber +480; data platform 0. Net: 1,450 − 1,260 + 480 = +670, i.e. £670,000 or £0.67m over. The colleague's £1.03m appears to double-count part of the infrastructure pressure or ignore the applications underspend incompletely; option B (£2.71m) sums only the overspends while discarding the underspend entirely — arguably a useful *gross pressure* figure, but not the net position claimed. The test skills here are careful signed arithmetic and unit discipline (the table is in £000s, so 670 means £0.67m, not £670m or £670). The management skill is recognising that net and gross tell different stories: a modest net overspend can conceal large offsetting movements that each deserve scrutiny — an applications underspend of £1.26m may mean prudent management, or it may mean stalled delivery quietly flattering your bottom line.

**Question 10 (challenging) — Investment appraisal across competing demands**

Three bids compete for a remaining £10.0m capital allocation. Bid 1: cost £4.0m, five-year benefits £14.0m. Bid 2: cost £6.0m, five-year benefits £19.2m. Bid 3: cost £10.0m, five-year benefits £29.0m. Bids can be funded in any combination that fits the £10.0m envelope. Which funding decision maximises total five-year benefits?

- A) Fund Bid 3 alone
- B) Fund Bids 1 and 2
- C) Fund Bid 2 alone and hold £4.0m in reserve
- D) Fund Bid 1 alone and hold £6.0m in reserve

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** Check each affordable combination. Bid 3 alone: £10.0m cost, £29.0m benefits. Bids 1 + 2: £4.0m + £6.0m = £10.0m cost, £14.0m + £19.2m = £33.2m benefits. Bid 2 alone: £19.2m. Bid 1 alone: £14.0m. The combination of the two smaller bids beats the single large bid by £33.2m − £29.0m = £4.2m for identical outlay. The trap is anchoring on benefit-cost ratios of individual bids (Bid 1: 3.5; Bid 2: 3.2; Bid 3: 2.9) and assuming the biggest bid must dominate because its absolute benefit is largest — or, conversely, funding only the single highest-ratio bid and leaving £6.0m idle, as option D does. With a fixed envelope, the question is always which *portfolio* maximises return, not which project looks best alone. This is the exact arithmetic of your duty to advise on investment priorities across competing demands to maximise benefits and outcomes, compressed into one line of addition — and it is why portfolio-level advice sometimes disappoints the sponsor of the single most impressive business case.

**Question 11 (challenging) — Digital take-up and unit-cost interaction**

A service handles 1.2 million transactions a year. Digital take-up is currently 70%; digital transactions cost £1.50 each and assisted transactions cost £9.00 each. A proposed assisted-digital and accessibility programme costing £1.2m (one-off) is expected to raise take-up to 78% from next year without excluding users who need assisted channels. What is the annual saving once take-up reaches 78%, and the simple payback period?

- A) £720,000 per year; payback 20 months
- B) £864,000 per year; payback about 17 months
- C) £720,000 per year; payback 12 months
- D) £1,080,000 per year; payback about 13 months

**Correct answer: A.**

**Explanation:** The shift moves 8 percentage points of 1.2m transactions from assisted to digital: 0.08 × 1,200,000 = 96,000 transactions. Saving per shifted transaction: £9.00 − £1.50 = £7.50. Annual saving: 96,000 × £7.50 = £720,000. Payback: £1,200,000 ÷ £720,000 = 1.67 years = 20 months. Option B inflates the shift to 9 points; option D uses a £9.00 saving per transaction, forgetting the digital channel still costs £1.50; option C pairs the right saving with the wrong division. Two executive observations belong alongside the arithmetic. First, the sensitivity: each percentage point of take-up is worth £90,000 a year here, so the difference between achieving 78% and 75% is £270,000 annually — evaluation of the programme should track realised take-up, not activity. Second, the framing in the question — raising take-up *without excluding users who need assisted channels* — is your inclusivity duty made concrete: channel-shift savings that come from abandoning users who cannot go digital are not savings a government organisation should book.

**Question 12 (challenging) — Interpreting a resilience dashboard ratio**

Your resilience dashboard shows: mean time between failures (MTBF) improved from 21 days to 30 days over the year; mean time to recover (MTTR) worsened from 2.0 hours to 3.5 hours. Assuming a 720-hour month and treating one month as 30 days, how did expected monthly downtime change?

- A) Improved from about 2.9 hours to about 2.4 hours
- B) Worsened from about 2.9 hours to about 3.5 hours
- C) Improved from about 3.4 hours to about 2.4 hours
- D) Worsened from about 2.0 hours to about 3.5 hours

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** Expected failures per month: before, 30 days ÷ 21 days per failure ≈ 1.43 failures; after, 30 ÷ 30 = 1.0 failure. Expected monthly downtime: before, 1.43 × 2.0 hours ≈ 2.9 hours; after, 1.0 × 3.5 = 3.5 hours. Despite failing less often, the service is down for longer overall because each failure now takes 75% longer to recover. Options A and C reward the intuition that fewer failures must mean less downtime; option D compares an MTTR with a downtime figure — mismatched units. The lesson generalises across your resilience duty: reliability (how often) and recoverability (how fast) are separate levers, and investment that improves one while neglecting the other can worsen the outcome users experience. When your teams celebrate an MTBF improvement, the disciplined executive question is what happened to recovery — and this piece of arithmetic is how you check the answer in your head during the meeting.

**Question 13 (moderate) — Sustainability and unit economics**

Your hosting estate consumed 4,800 megawatt-hours of electricity last year while serving 96 million transactions. After a consolidation and cloud-optimisation programme, consumption falls to 3,600 megawatt-hours while transactions grow to 120 million. By what percentage did energy per transaction improve?

- A) 25%
- B) 40%
- C) 37.5%
- D) 20%

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** Compute the unit figures. Before: 4,800 MWh ÷ 96m transactions = 0.05 kWh per transaction (4,800,000 kWh ÷ 96,000,000). After: 3,600,000 kWh ÷ 120,000,000 = 0.03 kWh per transaction. Improvement: (0.05 − 0.03) ÷ 0.05 = 0.40 = 40%. Option A is the raw consumption drop (1,200 ÷ 4,800 = 25%) ignoring the volume growth; option D is the transaction growth rate dressed up as the answer; option C mixes the two incorrectly. The pattern — an efficiency ratio improving faster than either input suggests, because the numerator fell while the denominator rose — recurs throughout sustainability reporting, and it cuts both ways: a rising total energy bill can hide improving unit efficiency during growth, and a falling bill can hide worsening efficiency during decline. Your duty to oversee sustainable digital solutions makes this ratio one you should insist on seeing alongside the headline totals in every estates and hosting paper.

### Preparation tips

- **Rebuild the classic calculations until they are reflexes.** Percentage of budget, unit cost, compound growth, expected value, simple payback, portfolio-within-an-envelope: a dozen patterns cover almost every question you will meet. Practise each until the structure — not the numbers — is what you see first.
- **Read the qualifiers before the numbers.** "Figures in £000s", "per quarter", "cumulative", "from year 2": most planted errors live in the labels, not the arithmetic. Make reading the table header and footnotes your first act, every time.
- **Name the denominator out loud.** Nearly every percentage trap is a denominator trap. Before computing, say to yourself: percentage *of what*? Ratio *against what*? It costs three seconds and saves whole questions.
- **Use estimation as a check, not a substitute.** Round the inputs, approximate the answer, then compute precisely if options are close. At this level options are often spaced to defeat pure estimation, but estimation still catches order-of-magnitude slips instantly.
- **Practise on your own management information.** Take last month's finance pack and resilience dashboard and recompute five derived figures. This doubles as preparation and as due diligence — you may be surprised what you find.

### Common pitfalls

- **Misreading scale qualifiers.** Confusing £000s with millions, monthly with annual figures, or in-year with cumulative benefits. The test plants these deliberately because real board packs do too.
- **Wrong-base percentages.** Dividing a variance by the forecast instead of the budget, or a saving by the new cost instead of the old, silently changes the answer — and the options will include the wrong-base result.
- **Simple-versus-compound confusion.** Applying growth additively when it compounds understates every forecast; the gap widens with the horizon, which is exactly where cloud and demand forecasts live.
- **Over-calculating.** Performing full-precision arithmetic when the options are far apart wastes the time you need for the genuinely close calls. Match your precision to the spacing of the options.
- **Time-sink questions.** A multi-stage portfolio question can absorb five minutes. Bank the quick marks first; an unanswered hard question costs one mark, three rushed easy ones cost three.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment evaluates how well you understand, analyse and draw correct conclusions from written business information of the kind your role actually generates. The passages use the terminology, formats and register of your working documents — strategy papers, audit reports, policy standards, supplier correspondence, board minutes, spend-control conditions — and the questions demand fact-based logic: you must answer based strictly on the text provided, setting aside everything you know from outside it. The dominant format asks you to judge statements as True (the passage supports it), False (the passage contradicts it) or Cannot Say (the passage provides insufficient information either way), supplemented by multiple-choice comprehension items that test whether you can identify a document's core argument, obligations and exceptions. Timing is tight — typically less than a minute per question — deliberately simulating the pace at which senior leaders must digest papers before meetings.

For a chief digital and information officer, verbal precision is not an academic virtue; it is a governance control. The difference between "the department must" and "the department should", between "including" and "limited to", between a finding and a recommendation in an audit report, or between what a supplier's letter states and what it merely implies, can be worth millions of pounds or a public commitment. You are the person the board trusts to have read the paper properly. This assessment measures exactly that: whether, at speed, you extract what a text establishes, notice what it does not establish, and resist the pull of plausible assumption.

The distinctive discipline of the format is the Cannot Say judgement. Most senior leaders find it the hardest, because years of experience train you to fill gaps with informed inference. In this test, an inference not licensed by the text — however professionally reasonable — makes a statement Cannot Say, not True. Practising that restraint is the core of your preparation.

### What it measures for your role

Mapped against the professional skills named in your role summary and your listed duties, the verbal assessment probes:

- **Reading strategy and policy with precision** (shaping the direction of organisational strategy through digital, data and technology): extracting commitments, conditions and dependencies from strategy documents, and distinguishing declared intent from established fact — essential for leading innovative strategies that deliver the organisation's objectives.
- **Interpreting standards and controls** (optimising technology and data architecture; financial management): parsing the obligations, exemptions and approval conditions in technology standards and spend controls, where a misread modal verb becomes a compliance failure.
- **Handling audit and assurance text** (ensuring resilience): understanding exactly what an audit or review has found, what it has recommended, and what it has explicitly not examined — the foundation of your duty to keep the organisation safe, secure and resilient.
- **Weighing supplier and legal correspondence** (leading digital products and services): identifying what a contractual letter concedes, claims and leaves open, before your organisation replies.
- **Advising the board faithfully** (trusted advisor to the board): representing documents accurately under time pressure, including being candid about what the evidence does not say — the verbal equivalent of not overstating a benefit-cost ratio.

### Practice questions

**Passage A — extract from a draft organisational digital strategy**

"Over the next three years the organisation will move to a cloud-first hosting model for all new services. Existing services will be assessed for migration in order of contract expiry, except where a service processes data classified above OFFICIAL, in which case migration decisions will be taken case by case with the National Technical Authority consulted. The strategy commits the organisation to reducing legacy-system risk, and anticipates, but does not guarantee, a reduction in overall hosting costs by the end of the period. Investment released by decommissioning will be reinvested in priority services, subject to annual budget approval."

**Question 1 (easy).** True, False or Cannot Say: "Under the draft strategy, all new services will be hosted in the cloud."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** Careful — the strategy commits to a cloud-*first* model for new services, not a cloud-*only* one. Cloud-first means cloud is the default that must be considered first, which leaves room for justified exceptions; "all new services will be hosted in the cloud" states a universal outcome the text does not support. Is that False or Cannot Say? The statement claims the strategy requires universal cloud hosting; the passage describes a different, weaker commitment, so as a description of what the strategy says, the statement is false. This item rewards knowing that in these tests the statement is judged against the passage's actual wording — and in your role, the distinction between cloud-first and cloud-only is exactly the kind of nuance you would correct in a board paper before it hardens into a commitment nobody made.

**Question 2 (easy).** True, False or Cannot Say: "A service processing SECRET data will be migrated to the cloud only after the National Technical Authority approves the migration."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C.**

**Explanation:** The passage says migration decisions for services processing data above OFFICIAL are taken "case by case with the National Technical Authority consulted". Consultation is not approval: the text does not state that the authority must approve, only that it will be consulted, and it does not state the sequencing the statement asserts ("only after"). The statement could be true in practice — but the passage does not establish it, so Cannot Say. This is the signature trap for experienced leaders: your professional knowledge of how such decisions really work invites you to upgrade "consulted" to "approves". The test — and good governance — require you to keep the two words apart.

**Question 3 (moderate).** True, False or Cannot Say: "The strategy guarantees that overall hosting costs will fall by the end of the three-year period."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** The passage is explicit: the strategy "anticipates, but does not guarantee, a reduction in overall hosting costs". The statement claims a guarantee; the passage directly contradicts that claim, so the answer is False — not Cannot Say, because the text speaks squarely to the point. Note the drafting craft here: "anticipates, but does not guarantee" is precisely the formula a careful strategy uses to avoid creating an unfunded commitment, and precisely the phrase a careless reader flattens into "costs will fall". When your organisation's strategy is quoted back to you by the finance committee, the difference between anticipation and guarantee is the difference between a forecast and a promise you must now keep.

**Question 4 (moderate).** Which of the following best describes the condition attached to reinvesting decommissioning savings?

- A) Savings will be reinvested automatically in priority services.
- B) Reinvestment is subject to annual budget approval.
- C) Savings will be returned to HM Treasury.
- D) Reinvestment requires National Technical Authority consultation.

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** The final sentence states that investment released by decommissioning "will be reinvested in priority services, subject to annual budget approval". Option A drops the condition — the single most consequential omission a reader could make, since it converts a conditional intention into a commitment; option C introduces a destination the passage never mentions; option D attaches the consultation requirement from the security sentence to the wrong subject. Multiple-choice comprehension items at this level almost always turn on which qualifier attaches to which clause. As the executive who will be held to this strategy, you would read it the same way: every "will" in a strategy document deserves a hunt for its "subject to".

**Passage B — extract from an internal audit report on cyber resilience**

"The audit examined incident response arrangements for the organisation's twelve critical services. Response plans existed for eleven of the twelve services; ten had been tested within the last year. The audit found that recovery procedures for the two highest-volume services rely on named individuals rather than documented steps, creating a single-point-of-failure risk rated High. The audit did not assess the technical effectiveness of backup arrangements, which will be covered in a follow-up review. Management has accepted all three recommendations and committed to remediation within six months. Nothing in this report should be read as assurance regarding services outside the critical twelve."

**Question 5 (moderate).** True, False or Cannot Say: "At the time of the audit, at least one critical service had no incident response plan."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A.**

**Explanation:** The passage states that response plans existed for eleven of the twelve critical services. Twelve minus eleven leaves one service without a plan, so the statement is directly supported: True. The item looks trivial, but under time pressure many candidates hesitate because the passage never writes the sentence "one service had no plan" — they must perform the small subtraction themselves. Simple arithmetic performed *on* the text is allowed and expected; what is forbidden is importing facts *from outside* the text. Keeping that boundary crisp — compute from what is given, never assume what is not — is the whole discipline of the format, and of representing audit findings accurately to a board.

**Question 6 (moderate).** True, False or Cannot Say: "The organisation's backup arrangements are technically inadequate."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C.**

**Explanation:** The audit explicitly did not assess the technical effectiveness of backup arrangements — that question is deferred to a follow-up review. The passage therefore provides no evidence either way: the statement is Cannot Say. It is not False, because the passage does not affirm the backups are adequate either; it is silent by design. This mirrors one of the most consequential misreadings in real assurance work: treating "not examined" as "no problem found". An executive who tells the board "the audit raised no concerns about backups" would be technically accurate and completely misleading. Scope limitations — "the audit did not assess", "nothing in this report should be read as assurance regarding" — are load-bearing sentences, and this assessment rewards readers who treat them that way.

**Question 7 (challenging).** True, False or Cannot Say: "Two critical services had response plans that had not been tested within the last year."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C.**

**Explanation:** Eleven services had plans; ten services had been tested within the last year. It is tempting to subtract and conclude that exactly one *planned* service was untested — but read again: the passage says ten "had been tested", without stating that the ten tested services are a subset of the eleven with plans (a service could conceivably have been tested against an ad hoc procedure). More decisively, the statement claims *two* services had untested plans. If the ten tested are all among the eleven with plans, exactly one planned service was untested, making the statement false; under other readings the number could differ. Because the passage does not pin the relationship down precisely enough to verify the specific count of two, the safest verdict the text supports is Cannot Say. The lesson: when a statement hangs on the exact overlap between two groups, check whether the passage actually defines that overlap. Real audit reports create these ambiguities constantly, and the executive habit of asking "tested — out of which population?" is what this item measures.

**Question 8 (challenging).** Which of the following most accurately summarises the audit's position on the two highest-volume services?

- A) Their recovery procedures failed testing during the audit.
- B) Their recovery depends on the knowledge of particular people rather than documented procedures, which the audit rates as a High risk.
- C) They have no incident response plans.
- D) Their backup arrangements are ineffective.

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** The passage states the recovery procedures for the two highest-volume services "rely on named individuals rather than documented steps, creating a single-point-of-failure risk rated High" — option B restates this faithfully. Option A invents a test failure the passage never describes; option C confuses these services with the single service lacking a plan (the passage does not connect them); option D drags in the explicitly out-of-scope backups. Notice how each wrong option grafts a real fragment of the passage onto the wrong subject — the standard construction of hard verbal items, and the standard way misunderstandings propagate through real organisations when a summary drifts one clause away from its source. When you brief the board on this audit, option B's precision — dependence on people, not proven failure — determines the proportionate response: documentation and cross-training, not panic.

**Passage C — extract from a letter from a strategic supplier**

"We acknowledge the service credits applied for the October service failures. However, we consider that the root cause lay in change requests initiated by your organisation outside the agreed release windows, and we reserve our position regarding liability for consequential costs. We remain committed to the transformation roadmap and are prepared to discuss revised support arrangements for the legacy platform, noting that extended support beyond March carries additional charges as set out in Schedule 7. Our account team will contact your office to arrange a review meeting."

**Question 9 (moderate).** True, False or Cannot Say: "The supplier accepts liability for the consequential costs of the October failures."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** The letter says the supplier "reserve[s] our position regarding liability for consequential costs" — the standard formula for explicitly declining to accept liability at this stage. The statement claims acceptance; the passage contradicts it, so False. Contrast this with the service credits, which the supplier does acknowledge: the letter concedes the smaller, contractually mechanical point while holding the larger, contested one open — a structure you should expect in almost all commercial correspondence. Reading supplier letters at this level means separating three registers: what is admitted (credits applied), what is contested (root cause attributed to your change requests), and what is reserved (consequential liability). Answering this question correctly is the same act as briefing your commercial director accurately.

**Question 10 (challenging).** True, False or Cannot Say: "If the organisation requires support for the legacy platform after March, it will have to pay more than it currently pays."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: C.**

**Explanation:** The letter states that extended support beyond March "carries additional charges as set out in Schedule 7". Additional charges strongly suggest paying more — but the statement asserts a comparison with what the organisation *currently pays*, and the letter does not state current charges or confirm that "additional" means additional to the current price rather than, say, charges itemised in a schedule that might restructure the overall fee. More importantly, the statement says the organisation "will have to" pay more, presupposing it takes extended support from this supplier at all; alternatives (migration before March, third-party support) are outside the text but the *obligation* claimed is not established by it. The passage makes extra cost likely; it does not make the specific comparative claim verifiable. Cannot Say. This is the hardest discrimination in the format — "strongly implied" versus "established" — and it is exactly the discipline that stops an executive turning a negotiating position in a supplier's letter into a fact in a board paper.

**Passage D — extract from a central spend-control decision letter**

"Approval is granted for the Discovery and Alpha phases of the Casework Digital Programme, up to a maximum of £3.2m. Approval for Beta is conditional on: (i) evidence of user research with groups who have accessibility needs; (ii) a revised benefits profile agreed with the finance function; and (iii) confirmation that the proposed architecture reuses the cross-government notification and payments platforms unless a documented exception is agreed. This approval does not constitute endorsement of the programme's full business case, which will be considered separately. Any material change to scope, cost or timescale must be notified within 20 working days."

**Question 11 (moderate).** True, False or Cannot Say: "The programme may proceed to Beta once it has spent the £3.2m."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** The letter grants approval only for Discovery and Alpha, and makes Beta approval conditional on three specific items of evidence: accessibility-inclusive user research, a revised benefits profile agreed with finance, and reuse of cross-government platforms (or a documented exception). Spending the £3.2m satisfies none of these conditions; money spent is not a gateway passed. The statement asserts an entitlement the letter explicitly withholds, so it is False. The wrong mental model here — treating budget consumption as progress through approval gates — is not just a test error; it is one of the most common real programme-governance failures, and as chief digital and information officer you are the person who ensures your organisation never confuses the two.

**Question 12 (challenging).** True, False or Cannot Say: "If the programme adopts a bespoke payments solution with a documented, agreed exception, condition (iii) can still be met."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A.**

**Explanation:** Condition (iii) requires confirmation that the architecture "reuses the cross-government notification and payments platforms unless a documented exception is agreed". The condition itself contains the escape clause: reuse *or* an agreed, documented exception. A bespoke payments solution accompanied by a documented, agreed exception therefore satisfies the condition as written — True. Candidates who answer False have read the headline obligation and skated over "unless"; candidates who answer Cannot Say have missed that the statement tracks the condition's own wording exactly, requiring no outside assumption. Rule-plus-exception structures are the grammar of spend controls, technology standards and architecture principles alike, and your duty to ensure architecture decisions support reusability across government is exercised precisely through such clauses: reuse as the default, exceptions as documented, justified decisions rather than quiet workarounds.

**Question 13 (challenging).** Which statement best captures the overall effect of the decision letter?

- A) The programme's full business case has been endorsed with conditions.
- B) Early phases are funded within a ceiling; progression and the full business case each face separate, explicit hurdles; and change-notification duties apply throughout.
- C) The programme is approved in full, subject to a 20-working-day review.
- D) Approval has been refused pending accessibility evidence.

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** Option B tracks the letter's architecture faithfully: a capped approval for Discovery and Alpha (£3.2m maximum); three named conditions gating Beta; an express carve-out that the full business case is considered separately; and a standing duty to notify material changes within 20 working days. Option A contradicts the carve-out sentence; option C inflates a phase approval into full approval and misreads the notification duty as a review clause; option D mistakes a condition on a future phase for a present refusal. Summarising a control document means preserving its load-bearing distinctions — what is approved, what is conditional, what is out of scope, what is a continuing obligation. That is the skill the item measures, and it is also, sentence for sentence, the skill of briefing a board accurately on where a programme actually stands.

**Question 14 (challenging).** True, False or Cannot Say: "A material change to the programme's timescale that is notified 25 working days after it occurs breaches the terms of the approval letter."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot Say

**Correct answer: A.**

**Explanation:** The letter's final sentence imposes an unqualified duty: "Any material change to scope, cost or timescale must be notified within 20 working days." A material timescale change notified after 25 working days has, by simple comparison, not been notified within 20 — the duty is breached on the letter's own terms, so True. Two hesitations tempt candidates towards Cannot Say. First, "breaches" sounds legal, and the passage never uses the word — but the question asks only whether the described conduct is contrary to the letter's stated terms, which it plainly is; you are not being asked about remedies or consequences, which genuinely are unstated. Second, candidates wonder whether the 20 days might run from discovery rather than occurrence — a fair drafting quibble in real life, but the statement stipulates notification 25 working days after the change, which exceeds the limit on any reasonable reading the passage allows. The skill is calibration in the other direction from Question 10: Cannot Say is for genuinely unestablished claims, not a refuge from every statement that requires you to apply a stated rule to described facts. Executives who over-use Cannot Say fail differently from those who under-use it — but they still fail, and in the boardroom the equivalent error is refusing to confirm what a control document plainly requires.

### Preparation tips

- **Train the Cannot Say reflex.** Your seniority is your biggest risk in this format: you know how government works, so your mind auto-completes gaps. Practise asking one question of every statement: "Where in the passage is this established?" If you cannot point to the words, it is Cannot Say — however confident your professional instinct.
- **Read for structure, not just content.** Senior-level passages are built from obligations ("must"), permissions ("may"), defaults with exceptions ("unless"), scope limits ("this report does not cover"), and hedges ("anticipates but does not guarantee"). Mark these connectors mentally as you read; the questions almost always hang on them.
- **Practise on your real papers.** Take an audit report, a spend-control letter or a supplier letter from your own inbox and write three True/False/Cannot Say statements about it. Composing items teaches the distinctions faster than answering them.
- **Manage reading time deliberately.** Skim the passage once for structure, answer from targeted re-reading, and resist re-reading the whole passage for every item. With under a minute per question, your gains come from knowing where to look.
- **Stay literal under pressure.** When tired, readers default to gist, and gist is where the errors live. If you feel yourself summarising, slow down for the specific clause the question turns on.

### Common pitfalls

- **Bringing outside knowledge.** The most experienced candidates make this error most: you know consultation usually precedes approval, that additional charges mean higher bills, that untested plans fail. The passage did not say so; the test scores the passage.
- **Confusing likely with stated.** "Anticipates", "expects", "should" mark possibilities; treating them as accomplished facts converts Cannot Say and False items into wrong True answers.
- **Attaching qualifiers to the wrong clause.** Conditions, exceptions and scope limits bind to specific subjects. Wrong options are manufactured by moving a real qualifier one clause over — check what each "subject to" and "unless" actually governs.
- **Treating silence as assurance.** "Not examined" is not "no problem". Scope-limitation sentences are among the most important in any passage; readers who skip them mistake absence of evidence for evidence of absence.
- **Poor time allocation.** Spending three minutes perfecting one Cannot Say judgement forfeits three easier marks. Flag, move on, return — the same triage you apply to a heavy board pack.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment evaluates your decision-making, interpersonal skill and professional conduct through realistic dilemmas drawn from the role itself. Unlike the logic and numeracy tests, it is not looking for a computed answer: each scenario offers several responses that could all be defended, and the assessment measures how well your choices align with the values of the organisation and the demands of the post. Typical formats ask you to identify the Most Effective and Least Effective actions from a set, or to rank or rate every option from best to worst. Timing is usually generous or absent, because the instrument measures judgement rather than speed.

At chief digital and information officer level, the dilemmas are the ones that reach your desk precisely because no one below you could resolve them: a board colleague pressing for a commitment your delivery evidence will not support; a security disclosure decision with reputational consequences; an innovation opportunity pulling against an inclusion duty; a funding negotiation where the honest answer is unwelcome. The scenarios in this section reflect UK public sector values throughout — honesty, objectivity, integrity and impartiality; putting user needs first; transparency; collaboration across organisational boundaries; and accessibility and inclusion as obligations rather than aspirations. Strong answers tend to share a signature: they address the root issue rather than the symptom, they are candid with decision-makers, they act at the right speed (neither impulsive nor passive), and they keep users and public value at the centre.

Be aware that scoring keys at executive level penalise two specific styles: heroics (seizing personal control of matters that belong to accountable owners, undermining the leaders you have appointed) and diplomacy-as-avoidance (choosing the smoothest interpersonal path while the substantive risk continues to run). The keyed "most effective" option is usually the one that combines candour, proportion and respect for accountability structures — even where it creates short-term discomfort.

### What it measures for your role

Mapped against your duties and the professional skills named in the role summary, the assessment probes:

- **Board-level candour and trust** (trusted advisor to the board; shaping organisational strategy): whether you give honest, evidence-based advice under political and interpersonal pressure, including advice the board would prefer not to hear.
- **Investment stewardship** (financial management; advising on investment priorities): whether you weigh competing demands on public money objectively, resisting both empire-building and the path of least resistance.
- **Resilience and security judgement** (ensuring resilience): how you balance transparency, user protection and operational needs during incidents and in disclosure decisions, keeping the organisation safe, secure and resilient.
- **Responsible innovation** (innovation in digital, data and technology): whether you enable emerging technologies whilst genuinely ensuring inclusivity, security and ethical practice, rather than treating those words as a footnote.
- **Leadership through others** (capability building; leading digital products and services; making strategic performance-led change): whether you strengthen the leaders and teams around you — growing capability, sustaining delivery, managing change — rather than substituting yourself for them.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (moderate) — The pre-announced deadline**

At a board away-day, the chief executive announces enthusiastically that the new benefits-application service will launch "by April, ahead of the minister's statement". Your delivery director's latest assessment, which you have read and probed, indicates that April is achievable only by skipping the accessibility audit and cutting the assisted-digital support model. The board is energised; a communications plan is already circulating.

Choose the MOST effective and LEAST effective responses:

- A) Say nothing at the away-day, then work privately with the delivery director to compress the schedule as far as possible and hope the gap closes.
- B) At the away-day, welcome the ambition and flag that you will bring the board a delivery assessment showing what April requires, including the accessibility and support implications, before the date is communicated externally.
- C) Publicly contradict the chief executive at the away-day, stating that April is impossible and the announcement is irresponsible.
- D) Instruct the delivery director to descope the accessibility audit to protect the date, noting that it can be completed after launch.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D.**

**Explanation:** Option B does everything the role demands: it preserves the chief executive's authority and the board's energy, while ensuring the decision that matters — communicating a date externally — is taken on evidence, with the accessibility and assisted-digital consequences visible. It is candid without being combative and fast without being impulsive. Option C is honest but needlessly destructive: contradicting the chief executive publicly, without the evidence in hand, spends trust that option B conserves — the same candour, delivered through a process the board can act on. Option A is diplomacy-as-avoidance: the risk continues running while the communications plan hardens, and the eventual correction becomes more expensive every week. Option D is least effective because it converts a schedule problem into a values breach: accessibility in government services is a legal and moral obligation, and "audit after launch" means launching a service that may exclude the very users a benefits service exists for. An option that quietly trades statutory inclusion duties for a political date is the definitive wrong answer in any UK public sector judgement test — and in the job.

**Question 2 (moderate) — Competing bids and a colleague's flagship**

You are finalising advice to the investment committee on next year's portfolio. The evidence ranks a data-platform bid and a cyber-remediation bid ahead of a high-profile "digital experience" programme sponsored by a fellow executive director, who lobbies you over coffee: "Back my programme this round and I'll support your capability funding next year."

Choose the MOST effective and LEAST effective responses:

- A) Advise according to the evidence, tell the colleague you will do so, and offer to work with their team on strengthening the experience programme's case for the next round.
- B) Adjust your advice to rank the colleague's programme higher; cross-directorate support matters more than marginal differences between bids.
- C) Advise according to the evidence but avoid telling the colleague beforehand, letting them discover it at the committee.
- D) Report the colleague's approach to the chief executive as an attempted impropriety before the committee meets.

**Most effective: A. Least effective: B.**

**Explanation:** Option A holds the line that makes you useful to the board at all: advice on competing demands for public money must be objective, and it pairs integrity with relationship repair — candour before the meeting, plus genuine help to strengthen the losing bid properly. Option B is least effective because it trades public value for private reciprocity: it is precisely the corruption of advice that objectivity and impartiality exist to prevent, and both the deal and the distortion would be indefensible if surfaced. Option C gets the advice right but the leadership wrong: ambushing a colleague at committee damages collaboration needlessly when a candid conversation was available. Option D over-escalates a clumsy lobbying attempt into a formal accusation; unless the approach is repeated or coercive, treating it as a conversation to be handled directly and honestly is more proportionate and preserves the working relationship that cross-government collaboration depends on. The pattern to internalise: keyed answers at this level almost always combine the evidence-based decision with proactive, respectful communication — integrity and collegiality are not alternatives.

**Question 3 (challenging) — The quiet security disclosure**

Your chief information security officer reports a vulnerability discovered in a shared component your organisation built and that four other departments now reuse. It has been exploited once, in your organisation, with no evidence of data loss. Your CISO has patched your instance. The other departments have not been informed; your communications director worries disclosure "creates a story where there isn't one".

Rank these responses from most to least effective:

- A) Notify the other departments and the relevant central security authorities immediately through established channels, share the patch and indicators of compromise, and commission a review of how reused components are monitored.
- B) Patch quietly everywhere you control, and inform the other departments only if evidence of exploitation elsewhere emerges.
- C) Ask the CISO to prepare a fuller assessment over the coming weeks before deciding whether cross-government notification is warranted.
- D) Notify the other departments but ask them informally to keep the matter out of any written record to avoid freedom-of-information exposure.

**Most to least effective: A, C, B, D.**

**Explanation:** Option A is the model answer: your duty to keep organisations safe, secure and resilient does not stop at your own boundary when you built the shared component — reuse across government, which your architecture choices rightly promote, carries a reciprocal duty to warn. Immediate notification through established channels, sharing both the fix and the means of detection, plus a systemic review, addresses incident, neighbours and root cause. Option C ranks second only because it is merely slow rather than wrong — but with one confirmed exploitation, weeks of further assessment leaves four departments exposed to a known, actively exploited vulnerability; the assessment can proceed after warning them. Option B inverts the burden: it waits for harm as the trigger for honesty. Option D is decisively last: it performs the disclosure while corrupting it, inviting other public bodies to evade transparency obligations — a values breach layered on a security decision, and the sort of "helpful" informality that ends careers and destroys interdepartmental trust. Note the general rule: in public sector judgement tests, any option that manages the record rather than the risk ranks below any option that merely mistimes the risk response.

**Question 4 (moderate) — The AI pilot and the excluded users**

A directorate proposes an AI-assisted triage pilot for a high-volume citizen service, promising major waiting-time reductions. The equality analysis is thin: it notes that outcomes for users with limited written English "will be monitored". The team is capable and enthusiastic, the technology is credible, and the minister's office has expressed interest.

Choose the MOST effective and LEAST effective responses:

- A) Approve the pilot as proposed; monitoring during the pilot is exactly how such risks should be assessed.
- B) Approve the pilot subject to strengthened conditions: a completed equality impact assessment, defined fairness metrics with stop criteria, human review of automated recommendations, and a parallel assisted route that keeps excluded users served.
- C) Reject the pilot until the AI market matures and other departments have proven the approach.
- D) Take personal ownership of the pilot, moving it into your office to ensure it is done properly.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D.**

**Explanation:** Option B is textbook responsible innovation — your duty is to *enable* innovative approaches and emerging technologies *whilst ensuring* inclusivity, security and ethical practices, and B operationalises both halves: the pilot proceeds, but with the safeguards (completed equality analysis, measurable fairness thresholds that can halt the pilot, human oversight, and a route that keeps vulnerable users served) that turn "will be monitored" from a hope into a control. Option A treats inclusion as a data-collection exercise on live citizens — discovering exclusion by letting it happen. Option C is the mirror-image failure: blanket delay abdicates the innovation duty and simply exports the learning risk to other departments. Option D is least effective even though it sounds conscientious: stripping a capable team of its work teaches your organisation that initiative is punished, does not scale beyond your personal attention, and confuses governance (setting conditions) with control (doing it yourself). Executives are scored on strengthening the system that delivers, not on personal heroics — a theme worth noticing across every scenario in this section.

**Question 5 (challenging) — Legacy funding versus visible innovation**

In spending-review negotiations, your finance director advises that the credible ask is £40m: you can weight it towards remediating fragile legacy systems that citizens never see, or towards new digital services that ministers can announce. Officials hint that a legacy-heavy bid "lacks a story" and may be cut, while an innovation-heavy bid risks leaving systems whose failure would halt statutory services. Rank these responses from most to least effective:

- A) Submit a bid weighted to visible innovation to maximise the chance of full funding, planning to divert money to legacy quietly once secured.
- B) Submit an evidence-led bid that frames legacy remediation as the foundation of every future service, quantifies the operational and cyber risk of deferral, ties each remediation to what it unlocks, and states plainly the consequences of partial funding.
- C) Submit a legacy-heavy bid presented mainly as a technical necessity, listing systems and their ages.
- D) Split the difference arithmetically between the two categories to appear balanced.

**Most to least effective: B, C, D, A.**

**Explanation:** Option B is the executive craft the negotiation duty describes: negotiating and securing investment is a persuasion task built on evidence, and B does the work — translating invisible risk into consequences decision-makers can weigh, connecting foundations to outcomes ("remediating the casework platform is what makes the new service possible"), and being honest about trade-offs. Option C has the right priorities but abdicates the persuasion: a list of old systems invites exactly the cut the officials warned of, because it makes the risk technical rather than human and statutory. Option D substitutes the appearance of judgement for judgement; arithmetic balance optimises nothing and signals to a sophisticated finance audience that you could not decide. Option A ranks last despite being tactically shrewd, because it is dishonest: securing funds for one purpose while planning to spend them on another misleads the very process that allocates public money, and its discovery would compromise your organisation's future bids and your own standing as an accounting officer's advisor. In ranking items, the dishonest-but-effective option always ranks below the honest-but-weak ones — the test, like the constitution, prices integrity above cleverness.

**Question 6 (moderate) — The struggling chief technology officer**

Eight months ago you appointed a chief technology officer to lead the architecture consolidation central to your strategy. Delivery is slipping: two architecture boards were cancelled, the design authority is issuing contradictory guidance, and two respected engineering leads have privately raised concerns with you. The CTO is technically excellent and was your strong personal choice.

Choose the MOST effective and LEAST effective responses:

- A) Give the CTO a direct, private, evidence-based conversation about the specific failures, agree a support plan with clear expectations and a review point, and tell the engineering leads how their concerns are being addressed in general terms.
- B) Quietly begin chairing the architecture board yourself and routing key decisions around the CTO to protect delivery.
- C) Wait until the annual performance cycle to raise the issues formally, avoiding demotivating a senior hire mid-year.
- D) Ask the engineering leads to gather further examples of the CTO's failings over the next quarter and report back to you.

**Most effective: A. Least effective: D.**

**Explanation:** Option A is direct leadership: specific evidence, delivered privately and promptly, with support and accountability paired and a defined review point — plus closing the loop with the people who raised concerns, without breaching confidence. This is how capability building works at executive level: you appointed the CTO, and your first duty is to give them a genuine chance to succeed with full knowledge of the problem. Option B undermines while appearing to help — the organisation quickly reads that the CTO no longer speaks with authority, guidance becomes more contradictory, and you have absorbed a job you appointed someone to do. Option C leaves a strategy-critical function failing for months to spare one person a hard conversation the role entitles them to have now. Option D is least effective because it converts colleagues into informants: it corrodes the engineering leads' relationship with their leader, politicises the department, and gathers evidence for a case you should instead be giving the CTO the chance to make unnecessary. Where a scenario contains an option that is honest, direct, prompt and supportive, it will beat every option that is indirect, delayed or performed through third parties.

**Question 7 (challenging) — The interoperability sacrifice**

A cross-government programme asks your organisation to adopt a common data standard for case records. Adoption benefits government as a whole — reuse, interoperability, cheaper future integration — but for your organisation it means eight months of remediation work, delaying two local improvements your users have asked for, and the benefits accrue mostly to other departments. Your board is sceptical: "Why should our users wait so others can integrate?"

Rank these responses from most to least effective:

- A) Recommend adoption, presenting the board with an honest account of local costs and cross-government benefits, a negotiated implementation schedule that protects the most user-critical local improvement, and the case that your organisation also depends on standards others adopted at cost to themselves.
- B) Decline adoption on the grounds that your accountability is to your organisation's users, not to other departments' integration costs.
- C) Formally adopt the standard but schedule the remediation work behind every local priority, effectively deferring it indefinitely.
- D) Escalate the decision to the cross-government programme board, asking them to mandate adoption so your board's scepticism becomes moot.

**Most to least effective: A, B, D, C.**

**Explanation:** Option A does the whole job: your duty to ensure architecture decisions support scalability, reusability and interoperability *across government* explicitly extends your accountability beyond your organisational boundary, and A carries the board honestly — costs stated, benefits located, mitigation negotiated, and the reciprocity argument made (your organisation already consumes standards others paid to adopt). Option B ranks second because it is at least honest and accountable — a reasoned, transparent refusal that the cross-government programme can contest — though it takes the narrow view of accountability the role exists to widen. Option D outsources your board's persuasion to a mandate: it may deliver adoption, but it spends the board's trust in you as their advisor and teaches them that difficult recommendations arrive as impositions. Option C is last because it is the least honest position available: public commitment with private non-delivery corrodes both the standard (other departments plan against an adoption that never comes) and your organisation's word. Note the ranking logic — an honest no outranks a dishonest yes, and both outrank the appearance of yes. That ordering recurs across executive judgement tests because it recurs across executive careers.

**Question 8 (moderate) — The live incident and the board meeting**

Twenty minutes before you present the annual technology strategy to the board, your deputy messages: a ransomware attempt has been detected and contained on a back-office network segment; the incident team is executing the response plan; no citizen services are affected; forensics will take 48 hours to confirm the containment fully.

Choose the MOST effective and LEAST effective responses:

- A) Skip the board meeting to personally direct the incident response from the security operations centre.
- B) Proceed with the board meeting as planned and mention nothing, since the incident is contained and unconfirmed findings would alarm the board unnecessarily.
- C) Proceed with the meeting, open with a brief factual notification of the incident, its containment status and the 48-hour confirmation timeline, confirm the response is being led by the accountable team with your deputy as escalation point, and continue with the strategy.
- D) Postpone the strategy presentation and convert the meeting into an emergency session on the incident.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: A.**

**Explanation:** Option C is calibrated exactly to the facts: the incident is real (so the board hears it from you, now, not from a leak or a later minute — that is what being a trusted advisor means), but it is contained and competently led (so it earns two minutes of factual briefing, not the meeting). C also demonstrates the resilience system working: plans executing, accountability clear, escalation defined. Option B gambles your credibility on forensics: if the 48-hour confirmation surfaces anything, the board learns you sat before them with an undisclosed live incident — the damage to trust would far exceed any alarm the disclosure could have caused. Option D over-corrects: converting a strategy meeting into an emergency session for a contained back-office incident signals that your organisation's resilience depends on panic, and it costs the strategy its decision slot for nothing. Option A is least effective: abandoning a board commitment to stand in the security operations centre displaces the incident commander you appointed, adds no capability the response lacks, and tells the organisation its executives do not trust its plans. The general principle: senior judgement is measured by proportion — matching the response's scale to the risk's scale — and by defaulting to candour whenever the facts are material.

**Question 9 (challenging) — The supplier ultimatum**

Your largest supplier, three years into a five-year contract underpinning a critical service, states in a meeting that unless the contract is renegotiated with a 20% uplift, they will invoke an exit clause in nine months, citing unsustainable costs. Market alternatives exist but transition would take at least twelve months. Your commercial director believes the threat is partly bluff; your delivery director says nine months of transition is impossible without service degradation.

Choose the MOST effective and LEAST effective responses:

- A) Refuse on principle to negotiate under threat and instruct the commercial team to begin immediate re-procurement.
- B) Agree the uplift to remove the risk, recovering the cost from the innovation budget.
- C) Open a structured response on parallel tracks: negotiate substantively while testing the supplier's cost claims through open-book review, commission contingency transition planning at once to change the balance of the negotiation, brief the board on the risk position, and notify the relevant commercial function across government of the supplier's conduct.
- D) Escalate directly to the supplier's chief executive, relationship to relationship, to make the problem go away.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: B.**

**Explanation:** Option C is executive-grade because it treats a strategic threat as a system to be worked, not a moment to be won: substantive negotiation grounded in evidence (open-book review tests the "unsustainable costs" claim), contingency planning that converts the twelve-month transition weakness into negotiating strength, board visibility of a critical-service risk, and cross-government intelligence-sharing so a supplier tactic against one department strengthens every department's hand. Option A mistakes rigidity for strength: with a twelve-month transition against a nine-month fuse, immediate re-procurement alone means accepting the service degradation your delivery director predicted — principle without a plan. Option D may have a place *within* option C, but alone it gambles a critical service on personal chemistry and signals that formal governance can be bypassed by whoever escalates highest. Option B is least effective on three counts: it rewards the threat (teaching this supplier and every observer that ultimatums price well), it funds the concession by cannibalising the innovation your strategy depends on, and it removes nothing — a supplier who has profited from one exit-clause threat retains both the clause and the incentive. Paying to make a threat disappear is how you subscribe to it.

**Question 10 (moderate) — The user research you didn't want to hear**

A major service redesign, publicly associated with your transformation strategy, receives challenging findings from its latest user research round: users with low digital confidence are abandoning the new journey at twice the rate of the old one. The service is due to complete its rollout in six weeks. The programme board minutes record the finding as "a training and communications issue".

Choose the MOST effective and LEAST effective responses:

- A) Accept the programme board's characterisation; user research routinely surfaces friction during transitions, and rollout momentum matters.
- B) Ask the service owner to pause further rollout for the affected user segments while the abandonment cause is diagnosed, keep the previous journey available as a safety net, and require the programme board to bring evidence, not characterisation, to the go/no-go decision.
- C) Order a full stop to the rollout and a return to the old service until abandonment rates equalise.
- D) Commission a second research round to check the finding before acting, continuing the rollout meanwhile.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A.**

**Explanation:** Option B embodies leading digital products and services in government: the finding concerns exactly the users public services exist to serve — those least able to route around a failing journey — so it warrants immediate, targeted protection (pause for affected segments, safety-net access to the old journey) and a governance correction (evidence, not narrative, at the decision point). It acts at the right altitude: you are not redesigning the journey; you are ensuring the decision-making system responds to evidence. Option D continues exposing the most vulnerable users to a known doubling of abandonment while re-asking a question the research has answered once — verification has value, but not with live harm running and a cost-free safety net available. Option C over-corrects, discarding the redesign's gains for all users because one segment is struggling, when a targeted pause achieves the protection without the demolition. Option A is least effective because it launders an inclusion failure into a communications problem: "users need training" is the oldest disguise worn by "the service doesn't meet user needs", and accepting it six weeks before full rollout converts research you paid for into decoration. In UK public sector judgement keys, options that reinterpret user evidence to fit the plan rank below options that make the plan answer to the evidence — every time.

### Preparation tips

- **Anchor on public sector values, then on proportion.** Before judging options, name the values in play — user needs, honesty with decision-makers, transparency, collaboration, inclusion — then ask which option addresses the root issue at a scale matching the risk. Most keyed answers sit at that intersection.
- **Watch for the two executive traps.** Options that have you personally seizing work from accountable owners, and options that preserve harmony while risk keeps running, are systematically penalised. If an option feels heroic or feels smooth, interrogate it.
- **Rank the dishonest option last, even when it is clever.** Tactical misdirection — softened papers, quiet reallocations, off-the-record requests — reliably anchors the bottom of the key, because its discovery cost always exceeds its convenience.
- **Rehearse with your real dilemmas.** Take three difficult decisions from your last year and write four response options for each, including the ones you rejected. Articulating *why* the rejected options were weaker is precisely the reasoning the test samples.
- **Answer as the role, in this sector.** The question is not what a generic bold leader would do, but what the accountable digital leader of a public organisation should do — with public money, public trust and statutory duties in the frame.

### Common pitfalls

- **Answering idealistically rather than effectively.** "Consult everyone and reach consensus" sounds virtuous but often leaves risk running; keys reward options that are principled *and* practically decisive.
- **Selecting passive options.** Waiting for more data, deferring to the next cycle, or monitoring a live harm are recurrent distractor patterns; delay is a decision, and the test scores it as one.
- **Ignoring the organisational culture stated in the scenario.** UK public sector scenarios embed values of transparency, accessibility and collaboration; options that optimise announcement value or personal position against those values rank low however energetic they sound.
- **Over-escalation and under-escalation.** Reporting a colleague's clumsy lobbying to the chief executive, or handling a cross-government vulnerability entirely in-house, both misjudge altitude. Match the response to the smallest system that can genuinely resolve the issue.
- **Forgetting you are being scored on judgement, not knowledge.** There is no formula to retrieve; read every option fully, because the difference between the best and second-best choice is usually one clause — who acts, how fast, and what is said to whom.
