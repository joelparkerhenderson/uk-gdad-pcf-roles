# Data Ethicist (Head of Data Ethics) - Psychometric Assessment Resources

## Introduction

Welcome. This document is a practice and preparation resource designed specifically for you as a Head of Data Ethics working within the UK Government Digital and Data profession. Whether you are preparing for an internal assessment, benchmarking your own capabilities, or simply investing in your continued professional development, this guide will help you approach psychometric assessments with confidence and clarity.

Psychometric assessments are structured, standardised exercises that measure the mental capabilities and behavioural tendencies that underpin effective performance in a specific role. They are widely used across the UK Civil Service and the broader public sector because they offer an objective, evidence-based complement to interviews and portfolio reviews. For a leadership role such as yours — one that sits at the intersection of data science, law, social science, policy, and organisational culture — these assessments probe exactly the capabilities you exercise daily: rapid synthesis of complex documents, sound quantitative judgement about risk and evidence, precise reading of policy and legislation, and mature situational judgement when mediating between stakeholders on high-stakes ethical questions.

This document is organised into four substantial sections, each covering one assessment type:

1. **Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment** — pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and problem solving using the artefacts you genuinely handle: ethics review registers, algorithmic impact assessments, framework documents, and escalation queues.
2. **Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment** — interpreting tables, percentages, ratios, budgets, and performance metrics of the kind that appear in bias audits, training programme evaluations, and ethics board reporting.
3. **Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment** — dense passages drawn from data ethics policies, legislation summaries, board papers, and stakeholder correspondence, followed by True / False / Cannot Say and comprehension questions.
4. **Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment** — realistic dilemmas involving ministers' offices, data science teams, civil society groups, legal advisers, and your own ethics function, testing how you weigh competing pressures.

Each section explains what the assessment measures, maps its dimensions to the specific skills in your role profile, provides a rich set of worked practice questions calibrated to a head-of-profession level, and closes with preparation tips and common pitfalls.

How to use this document: work through the practice questions under realistic conditions first — timed, without notes — then study the worked explanations carefully, even for questions you answered correctly. The explanations model the reasoning style that assessors reward. Treat wrong answers as diagnostic information, not failure: they show you precisely where to focus. You already operate at a senior level; this resource is about sharpening, not proving.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive ability assessment is a targeted psychometric test that measures the exact mental processes needed for your role, rather than abstract intelligence in general. Unlike a generic IQ test, it uses practical workplace scenarios and data drawn from the everyday tasks and artefacts of the job. For a Head of Data Ethics, that means the test materials look like ethics review registers, algorithmic impact assessment summaries, escalation queues, framework version histories, and cross-departmental governance charts — not abstract shapes and number strings.

The typical format is an online, timed assessment lasting 15 to 30 minutes, with objective scoring against a benchmark or norm group appropriate to senior professional and leadership roles. Well-constructed assessments are validated to ensure they predict job performance without introducing bias — a design constraint you will appreciate more than most candidates, given that assurance of fairness in automated and semi-automated decision tools is central to your professional practice. Many platforms offer short, ungraded practice questions before the real test begins, and some adapt question difficulty based on your previous answers. Employers usually receive a breakdown of speed versus accuracy rather than a single score, so working carefully and steadily is a legitimate strategy.

For your role, cognitive assessment matters because the intellectual core of leading a data ethics function is synthesis under pressure: you must quickly read and interpret complex documents from a range of sources, distil what is relevant, spot inconsistencies between what a team claims and what its evidence shows, and prioritise a portfolio of consequential risks with imperfect information. A cognitive test compresses those demands into short, sharp exercises.

### What it measures for your role

The assessment dimensions map directly onto the skills in your role profile:

- **Analysis and synthesis (data ethics)**: drawing together qualitative and quantitative evidence, reading complex documents quickly, and distilling them to what is relevant. Cognitive items test this through error checking, logical deduction from documentary evidence, and identifying which findings actually follow from stated data.
- **Managing decisions and risks**: working with consequential or complex risks and applying different risk methodologies in proportion to the risk. Prioritisation and problem-solving items test whether you can rank issues rationally when criteria conflict.
- **Problem management**: ensuring the right actions are taken to investigate, resolve and anticipate problems. Fault-diagnosis style items test whether you can trace a reported symptom back to its most plausible cause in a process or governance chain.
- **Data ethics and privacy**: overseeing compliance with standards and legislation. Error-checking items test the precision needed to spot where a register, framework, or assessment record contradicts the rules it claims to follow.
- **Applied social sciences** and **Analysis and synthesis** together: several items ask you to evaluate whether a stated conclusion is warranted by the study design described — the daily work of challenging assumptions made in data science projects.

### Practice questions

Work through these under timed conditions if you can — roughly two to three minutes each — then study the explanations.

**Question 1 (easy) — Error checking an ethics review register.**
Your ethics secretariat maintains a register of algorithmic impact assessments (AIAs). An extract reads:

- Project Alpha: AIA submitted 03 March; risk rating High; board review required within 20 working days; board review held 02 April.
- Project Bravo: AIA submitted 10 March; risk rating Medium; board review required within 40 working days; board review held 29 April.
- Project Charlie: AIA submitted 17 March; risk rating High; board review required within 20 working days; board review held 22 May.
- Project Delta: AIA submitted 24 March; risk rating Low; board review not required; board review held 01 April.

Which entries are inconsistent with the stated rules?

A) Alpha only
B) Charlie only
C) Charlie and Delta
D) Bravo and Charlie

**Correct answer: C.**
**Explanation:** Alpha's review on 02 April is within 20 working days of 03 March, so it complies. Bravo's review on 29 April is within 40 working days of 10 March (roughly 36 working days), so it complies. Charlie is High risk and therefore due within 20 working days of 17 March — approximately mid-April — but the review happened on 22 May, well outside the window. Delta is rated Low and marked "board review not required", yet a board review was held; that is an inconsistency between the rule and the record (it may be benign, but on an error-checking task the mismatch must be flagged). The trap in this question is option B: candidates spot the obvious lateness of Charlie and stop checking. Error-checking items reward exhaustive verification of every row, exactly as you would expect of a function that assures compliance records across an organisation.

**Question 2 (easy) — Logical deduction from framework rules.**
Your organisational data ethics framework states: "Every project that processes special category data must complete a full AIA. Every project with a full AIA must be reviewed by the ethics board. Some projects reviewed by the ethics board are escalated to the executive risk committee."

Which one of the following conclusions must be true?

A) Every project reviewed by the ethics board processes special category data.
B) Every project that processes special category data is reviewed by the ethics board.
C) Some projects that process special category data are escalated to the executive risk committee.
D) No project without a full AIA is reviewed by the ethics board.

**Correct answer: B.**
**Explanation:** Chain the first two rules: special category data leads to full AIA, and full AIA leads to ethics board review; therefore every special-category project reaches the board. Option A reverses the implication — the board may review other projects too. Option C is tempting but not guaranteed: "some projects reviewed by the board are escalated" does not tell us those particular projects are the special-category ones. Option D also reverses the second rule: the framework says a full AIA guarantees review, not that review requires a full AIA. Formal deduction items like this reward discipline about the direction of implications — a discipline you also apply when checking whether a policy actually mandates what a team believes it mandates.

**Question 3 (moderate) — Prioritising an escalation queue.**
On Monday morning you have five items in your queue. Your framework prioritises, in order: (1) live harm to the public, (2) legal or regulatory breach, (3) reputational exposure, (4) internal process failure. Items of the same priority are handled in order of scale of impact.

- Item V: A journalist has emailed asking about the fairness testing of a benefits triage model; publication expected Thursday.
- Item W: A data science team reports that a deployed fraud model is disproportionately flagging claimants from one postcode cluster, and flagged claimants face payment suspensions this week.
- Item X: An internal audit finds that six of forty project teams skipped the AIA sign-off step last quarter.
- Item Y: A legal adviser notes that a live data-sharing pilot may lack a lawful basis under data protection legislation, affecting around 2,000 records.
- Item Z: A training session scheduled for Friday has no facilitator after a resignation.

Which is the correct order to address them?

A) Y, W, V, X, Z
B) W, Y, V, X, Z
C) W, V, Y, Z, X
D) Y, V, W, Z, X

**Correct answer: B.**
**Explanation:** Item W is live harm to the public — real claimants face payment suspensions now because of a potentially biased model — so it takes first place under criterion (1). Item Y is a probable legal breach in a live pilot, criterion (2), second. Item V is reputational exposure with a Thursday deadline, criterion (3), third. Item X is an internal process failure, criterion (4), fourth; and Item Z, while also a process problem, is smaller in scale of impact than a systemic sign-off failure across six teams, so it comes last. The common error is placing Y first because "legal breach" sounds most severe; but the framework you were given explicitly ranks live public harm above legal breach, and prioritisation items test whether you apply the stated criteria rather than your instincts. In post, you might well challenge the framework's ordering — but on the test, the framework governs.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Spotting the flaw in an evaluation claim.**
A team pilots a new "ethics self-assessment tool" with ten volunteer project teams. Nine of the ten report that the tool improved their ethical awareness, and the team concludes: "The tool improves ethical awareness across the organisation and should be mandated for all 120 project teams."

Which is the strongest single objection to this conclusion?

A) Ten teams is too small a sample for any conclusion whatsoever.
B) Volunteer teams are likely to differ systematically from teams in general, so the result may not generalise to mandated use.
C) Self-reported awareness is not a meaningful measure under any circumstances.
D) The tool should have been piloted for longer before any survey was run.

**Correct answer: B.**
**Explanation:** The decisive flaw is selection bias: teams that volunteer for an ethics pilot are plausibly more engaged with ethics than average, and a tool that works for enthusiasts may fail when mandated for the indifferent. Option A overstates — small samples weaken but do not annihilate evidence, and "any conclusion whatsoever" is too absolute. Option C is similarly absolute; self-report is weak but not meaningless. Option D is a procedural preference, not a logical objection to the inference drawn. This item mirrors your real work of evaluating and challenging assumptions in data science projects: the best critique identifies the specific inferential gap, not a generic complaint.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Pattern recognition in incident data.**
Your quarterly log of data ethics incidents by category shows:

- Q1: bias-related 4, consent-related 9, transparency-related 6
- Q2: bias-related 6, consent-related 7, transparency-related 6
- Q3: bias-related 9, consent-related 5, transparency-related 6
- Q4: bias-related 13, consent-related 3, transparency-related 6

If the underlying patterns continue, what is the most reasonable expectation for next quarter?

A) Bias-related 18, consent-related 1, transparency-related 6
B) Bias-related 15, consent-related 2, transparency-related 9
C) Bias-related 16, consent-related 2, transparency-related 6
D) Bias-related 13, consent-related 3, transparency-related 3

**Correct answer: A.**
**Explanation:** Examine each series separately. Bias-related incidents increase by a growing increment: +2, +3, +4 — so the next increment is +5, giving 18. Consent-related incidents fall by 2 each quarter: 9, 7, 5, 3, so next is 1. Transparency-related incidents are flat at 6. Only option A matches all three patterns. Option C is the classic near-miss for candidates who assume the bias series grows linearly. In practice you would never extrapolate incident data this mechanically — but pattern items test whether you can detect and extend a structure quickly, a component of scanning dashboards for emerging risk signals.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Deduction about committee composition.**
Your data ethics board must include: at least one legal adviser, at least one data scientist, at least two independent external members, and no more than six members in total. The chair must be an external member. Current confirmed members: Priya (legal adviser), Tomasz (data scientist), Elena (external), and Marcus (external, chair). Two candidates remain for the final places: Ruth (data scientist) and Owen (legal adviser). The board wants maximum disciplinary diversity, defined as the greatest number of distinct categories with more than one holder being avoided.

If both Ruth and Owen are appointed, which requirement or preference is compromised?

A) The maximum size limit is breached.
B) The external membership minimum is breached.
C) The board's diversity preference is compromised, because two categories become duplicated.
D) No requirement or preference is affected.

**Correct answer: C.**
**Explanation:** With six members the size limit (no more than six) is met exactly, so A is wrong. Two externals (Elena and Marcus) satisfy the external minimum, so B is wrong. But appointing Ruth duplicates the data scientist category (Tomasz and Ruth) and appointing Owen duplicates the legal category (Priya and Owen) — two duplicated categories, which works against the stated diversity preference. The item tests careful bookkeeping across multiple simultaneous constraints, the same mental operation as checking that a proposed governance structure satisfies its terms of reference.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Error checking a framework version history.**
The version history of your organisational data ethics framework reads:

- v1.0 published January 2023; review due every 12 months.
- v1.1 published July 2023 (minor amendment; does not reset review clock).
- v2.0 published March 2024 (major revision; resets review clock).
- v2.1 published November 2024 (minor amendment).
- Register note: "Framework fully in date; next review due March 2026."

Today is August 2025. Which statement about the register note is correct?

A) The note is correct.
B) The note is wrong: the review was due January 2025 because minor amendments never affect the clock.
C) The note is wrong: the review was due March 2025, twelve months after v2.0, and the framework is currently overdue.
D) The note is wrong: the review is due November 2025, twelve months after v2.1.

**Correct answer: C.**
**Explanation:** The clock resets only on major revisions. v2.0 (March 2024) is the last major revision, so review fell due March 2025. v2.1 was minor and did not reset the clock, ruling out D. Option B miscalculates by ignoring the v2.0 reset. As of August 2025, the framework is around five months overdue for review, so the register note claiming "fully in date" with a March 2026 due date is wrong — it looks as though someone incorrectly treated v2.1 as resetting the clock and then added a further arithmetic slip. Assurance of exactly this kind of record is part of overseeing compliance with data ethics standards, and the cognitive skill is the same: apply the rule mechanically and distrust the summary line.

**Question 8 (hard) — Diagnosing the root cause of a governance failure.**
Six project teams skipped AIA sign-off last quarter. You gather facts: (i) all six teams used the new fast-track delivery pathway introduced in April; (ii) the fast-track pathway checklist was copied from the standard pathway but the AIA sign-off line was dropped in editing; (iii) two of the six teams say they knew about the AIA requirement but assumed fast-track projects were exempt; (iv) your training records show all six teams completed data ethics induction; (v) teams on the standard pathway had 100% sign-off compliance.

What is the most plausible root cause?

A) Inadequate ethics training across the organisation.
B) A defective process artefact: the fast-track checklist omits the AIA step, so compliant behaviour was never prompted.
C) Deliberate evasion by delivery teams under time pressure.
D) The AIA requirement is disproportionate for fast-track projects.

**Correct answer: B.**
**Explanation:** The evidence pattern is decisive: perfect compliance on the standard pathway (v) plus universal training completion (iv) eliminates option A. The failure correlates exactly with the pathway whose checklist lost the AIA line (i, ii), and even the teams who knew the requirement inferred an exemption — a reasonable reading of an official checklist that omits the step (iii), which undermines option C's assumption of bad faith. Option D is a policy opinion, not a cause of the observed non-compliance. This is a problem-management item in the classic sense: ensure the right actions are taken to investigate and anticipate problems by locating the defect in the system rather than in the people. The corrective action (fix the checklist; issue a clarification; re-run sign-off for the six projects) follows straightforwardly once the root cause is correctly identified.

**Question 9 (hard) — Evaluating competing explanations for a model disparity.**
A recruitment-screening model shortlists 18% of applicants from group P and 11% from group Q. Four explanations are offered:

1. The model uses postcode, which correlates with group membership.
2. Group Q applicants apply to more oversubscribed roles.
3. The training data reflects historically biased human shortlisting decisions.
4. Random noise: the sample is only 60 applicants in total.

Which explanations could, if true, each independently account for the disparity, and which is easiest to check first?

A) All four could account for it; explanation 4 is easiest to check first.
B) Only 1 and 3 could account for it; explanation 1 is easiest to check first.
C) Only 2 and 4 could account for it; explanation 2 is easiest to check first.
D) All four could account for it; explanation 3 is easiest to check first.

**Correct answer: A.**
**Explanation:** Each mechanism is independently sufficient: proxy variables (1), differential role competition (2), inherited historical bias (3), and small-sample noise (4) can each produce an observed gap of this size. So options B and C, which rule mechanisms out, are wrong. The ordering question is about cost of investigation: checking sample size and computing whether a 7-point gap on 60 applicants is statistically meaningful takes minutes with data already in hand, whereas auditing training data provenance (3) is the slowest and most expensive check. A disciplined investigator eliminates the cheap explanation first — if the gap is within noise, the costlier investigations may be unnecessary or at least differently framed. This mirrors your skill of applying risk methodologies in proportion to the risk: sequence diagnostic effort by cost and discriminating power.

**Question 10 (hard) — Multi-constraint scheduling of ethics reviews.**
Your board meets monthly and can review at most three AIAs per meeting. Pending reviews: four High-risk AIAs (due within one meeting cycle), three Medium-risk (due within two cycles), and two Low-risk (due within three cycles). Your framework forbids deferring a High-risk AIA and requires at least one Medium-risk review per meeting once any Medium is pending.

Which schedule is compliant?

A) Meeting 1: three High. Meeting 2: one High, two Medium. Meeting 3: one Medium, two Low.
B) Meeting 1: two High, one Medium. Meeting 2: two High, one Medium. Meeting 3: one Medium, two Low.
C) Meeting 1: three High. Meeting 2: three Medium. Meeting 3: one High, two Low.
D) Meeting 1: two High, one Medium. Meeting 2: one High, two Medium. Meeting 3: two Low.

**Correct answer: B.**
**Explanation:** Test each rule. High-risk AIAs are due within one cycle and cannot be deferred — but there are four Highs and only three slots per meeting, so the constraint is impossible to satisfy literally in one meeting; the best compliant reading is that all four Highs must be cleared at the earliest possible meetings without any being displaced by lower-priority work, while still meeting the "at least one Medium per meeting" rule. Option A defers one High to meeting 2 but violates the Medium rule at meeting 1 (a Medium is pending, yet none is scheduled). Option C violates the Medium rule at meeting 1 and defers a High all the way to meeting 3. Option D clears the Highs by meeting 2 and schedules Mediums each meeting — but meeting 3 has two Lows and no Medium while no Mediums remain pending, which is fine; however D reviews only two items at meeting 3, leaving all constraints met — the flaw is that D schedules only three Mediums total, which is correct, and only two Highs plus one High equals three Highs, leaving the fourth High unscheduled. Option B clears all four Highs across meetings 1 and 2 (the earliest arithmetic allows), includes a Medium at every meeting, and completes the Lows within three cycles. The lesson: when constraints appear to conflict, find the schedule that satisfies the binding rules and breaches none — and always count every item across the whole schedule, because near-miss options usually drop one item quietly.

**Question 11 (hard) — Inference from mixed qualitative and quantitative evidence.**
You are synthesising evidence about a chatbot deployed in a public-facing service. Sources: (i) analytics show 92% of sessions complete without human handover; (ii) user research interviews with twelve participants found four who abandoned the chatbot and phoned instead, all of whom had limited English proficiency; (iii) a complaints log shows chatbot complaints doubled month-on-month, from 5 to 10 to 21; (iv) the supplier's report states the model was evaluated "on a representative sample of UK English speakers".

Which synthesis is best supported?

A) The chatbot works well for most users, and the complaint growth is explained by rising usage.
B) The chatbot likely underserves users with limited English proficiency, a group plausibly excluded by the evaluation design; the completion metric may mask abandonment through channel-switching, and complaints are growing at a rate that warrants investigation.
C) The chatbot is failing and should be withdrawn immediately pending a full equality impact assessment.
D) The evidence is contradictory and no conclusion can be drawn until a larger user research study is completed.

**Correct answer: B.**
**Explanation:** Option B is the only synthesis that integrates all four sources without exceeding them. The interview evidence (ii) and the supplier's evaluation scope (iv) converge on a specific, mechanistically plausible gap: users with limited English proficiency were likely absent from evaluation and are now abandoning the tool. The 92% completion metric (i) does not contradict this — users who give up and phone instead may not register as handovers, so the metric can mask harm; and the complaints series (iii) shows roughly doubling growth that deserves investigation. Option A explains away the complaints without evidence about usage growth. Option C overshoots: the evidence justifies investigation and probably mitigation, not immediate withdrawal — proportionality matters. Option D undervalues the evidence: qualitative signals from twelve interviews, triangulated with evaluation-scope information and a complaints trend, are exactly the kind of mixed evidence a head of data ethics must be willing to act on. Turning research data into clear findings that inform decisions means neither overreaching (C) nor deferring indefinitely (D).

**Question 12 (very hard) — Detecting an inconsistency across documents.**
Three documents cross your desk about the same automated triage project. The AIA states: "No special category data is processed; ethnicity is neither collected nor inferred." The model card states: "Input features: age band, postcode sector, service usage history, and interpreter-language flag." The DPIA states: "The interpreter-language flag records the language requested for interpreter support and is used to route cases to appropriately skilled caseworkers."

What is the most important issue to raise?

A) Nothing — no document lists ethnicity, so the AIA statement stands.
B) The interpreter-language flag is a plausible proxy from which ethnicity or national origin may be inferred, so the AIA's claim that such data is "neither collected nor inferred" needs re-examination, and the model's use of the flag needs bias assessment.
C) The DPIA and model card contradict each other about the purpose of the flag.
D) Postcode sector is special category data and the AIA is therefore false.

**Correct answer: B.**
**Explanation:** The documents are individually accurate but jointly problematic. The AIA's assurance rests on the word "inferred": an interpreter-language flag correlates strongly with national origin and, in many contexts, ethnicity — so a model using it as an input feature may effectively infer protected characteristics even though none is collected by name. Option C is wrong: the DPIA describes a routing purpose and the model card lists the flag as a feature; those are consistent, and their consistency is precisely what confirms the flag feeds the model. Option D is legally incorrect — postcode is not special category data, though it can also act as a proxy. Option A reflects the surface reading that cross-document review exists to defeat. This item compresses a signature capability of your role: expertly drawing together documents from a range of sources and distilling the one issue that matters, then converting it into an actionable finding (re-open the AIA; commission a proxy-variable bias assessment).

### Preparation tips

- **Rehearse with your own artefacts.** Take a real (suitably anonymised) AIA, register extract, or framework document and set yourself questions: what follows from this rule? Where do these two documents disagree? You will find the test format familiar because it imitates your job.
- **Practise directional logic.** Many deduction errors are reversed implications ("all A are B" read as "all B are A"). Slow down on any question containing "must", "every", "some", or "only".
- **Check every option in error-checking items.** The test rewards exhaustiveness; near-miss options exploit candidates who stop at the first fault found.
- **Manage the clock like a portfolio.** Allocate time in proportion to marks, and skip-and-return rather than sinking minutes into one hard item — the same proportionality you apply to risk.
- **Trust structured reasoning over instinct under pressure.** At your level, instinct is well-trained, but timed tests reward showing the discipline of applying stated criteria exactly as given.

### Common pitfalls

- **Answering from professional knowledge instead of the material given.** You know more about data ethics than the test author; when a stated framework rule conflicts with best practice, the test wants the rule applied. Save your critique for real life.
- **Stopping at the first error found.** Register and record-checking items usually contain more than one inconsistency; scan every row.
- **Confusing plausibility with necessity.** In deduction items, "could be true" options are placed to lure candidates away from the single option that "must be true".
- **Time sink questions.** Getting anchored on one multi-constraint scheduling puzzle can cost you three easier questions; move on and return.
- **Over-reading adaptive difficulty.** If questions feel harder as you go, that may mean you are doing well. Do not let perceived difficulty rattle your accuracy.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment evaluates your ability to analyse, interpret, and make sound decisions using numerical data presented in the formats your role genuinely produces and consumes. It is emphatically not a mental arithmetic contest: most assessments allow or provide an onscreen calculator, because the skill being measured is interpretation — extracting the right figures from a table, choosing the right operation, and sense-checking the result — under a strict time limit that simulates workplace pressure.

For a Head of Data Ethics, the numbers that matter are not balance sheets but bias audit outputs, selection-rate comparisons, training programme completion figures, ethics board capacity plans, incident trend series, survey results from your capability-building work, and the budget you steward for your function. You are the person who must hold meaningful conversations with data scientists about minimising bias in data — and that conversation is quantitative. You are also the person who presents findings to boards and senior stakeholders, where a single misread percentage can undermine an otherwise strong ethical argument. Numeric reasoning assessments for senior roles therefore emphasise multi-step problems, rates and ratios, proportional comparison, and the crucial distinction between absolute and relative change.

Typical format: 15 to 25 minutes, 12 to 20 questions, each anchored to a table, chart, or short numerical scenario. Expect roughly a minute to ninety seconds per question. Scores are usually normed against a senior professional comparison group, and reports often distinguish speed from accuracy.

### What it measures for your role

- **Analysis and synthesis (data ethics)**: expertly drawing together and evaluating quantitative data, and turning it into clear findings that inform decisions for the entire organisation. Nearly every item below ends with a decision, not just a number.
- **Data ethics and privacy**: overseeing compliance across the organisation requires reading compliance dashboards, completion rates, and audit metrics correctly — several items use exactly these artefacts.
- **Managing decisions and risks**: applying risk methodologies in proportion to the risk means quantifying likelihood, impact, and cost of mitigation; the risk-scoring and cost-benefit items test this directly.
- **Communication (data ethics)**: holding your own with data science experts on topics such as false positive rates, base rates, and disparity ratios; the bias-audit items test whether you can compute and interpret the statistics behind fairness claims.
- **Product ownership (data ethics)**: expertise in feedback gathering and evaluation mechanisms means analysing survey and adoption metrics for the tools you create — covered by the training-evaluation and adoption items.
- **Problem management**: trend extrapolation and capacity planning items test whether you can anticipate problems before they arrive.

### Practice questions

Use a calculator, as you typically may in the live test, but write down intermediate figures — transcription errors under time pressure are the most common cause of lost marks.

**Question 1 (easy) — Allocating the data ethics programme budget.**
Your annual programme budget is £240,000. The allocation is: 35% training delivery, 25% research partnerships, 20% ethics tooling, and the remainder for staff engagement events. How much is allocated to staff engagement events?

A) £36,000
B) £42,000
C) £48,000
D) £60,000

**Correct answer: C.**
**Explanation:** The named allocations total 35% + 25% + 20% = 80%, leaving 100% − 80% = 20% for staff engagement. 20% of £240,000 = 0.20 × 240,000 = £48,000. As a sense check: training is 0.35 × 240,000 = £84,000, research £60,000, tooling £48,000; then 84,000 + 60,000 + 48,000 + 48,000 = £240,000. The common slip is misreading "remainder" and computing 25% (option D reflects confusing the remainder with the research line).

**Question 2 (easy) — Selection rates in a bias audit.**
A screening model processed 15,000 applications from group A and flagged 1,200 for rejection. It processed 4,500 applications from group B and flagged 540 for rejection. What are the two flag rates, and what is the ratio of group A's rate to group B's rate?

A) 8% and 12%; ratio 0.67
B) 8% and 12%; ratio 1.50
C) 12% and 8%; ratio 1.50
D) 8% and 8.3%; ratio 0.96

**Correct answer: A.**
**Explanation:** Group A: 1,200 ÷ 15,000 = 0.08 = 8%. Group B: 540 ÷ 4,500 = 0.12 = 12%. The ratio of A's rate to B's rate is 8 ÷ 12 = 0.667. Option B computes the inverse ratio (12 ÷ 8 = 1.5) — the direction of a ratio matters, and the question asked A to B. In fairness analysis this ratio is directly analogous to the "four-fifths" style disparate impact comparison you may discuss with data science teams: a ratio of 0.67 between adverse-outcome rates would typically prompt further investigation. Read ratio directions with the same care in the test as you would in a published bias audit.

**Question 3 (easy) — Training completion gap.**
Your organisation has 4,800 staff in scope for mandatory data ethics training. At the end of Q2, 62% have completed it. Your target is 85% completion by the end of Q4. How many additional staff must complete the training in the second half of the year?

A) 960
B) 1,056
C) 1,104
D) 1,150

**Correct answer: C.**
**Explanation:** Target headcount: 0.85 × 4,800 = 4,080. Current headcount: 0.62 × 4,800 = 2,976. Additional completions needed: 4,080 − 2,976 = 1,104. Equivalently, the gap is 85% − 62% = 23 percentage points, and 0.23 × 4,800 = 1,104. Option A is 20% of 4,800 (a rounding of the gap); option B is 22%. Working with the percentage-point gap directly is faster and less error-prone than computing two headcounts — a useful technique when time is short.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Absolute versus relative change in incidents.**
Reported data ethics incidents were 40 in year one, 52 in year two, and 39 in year three. Which statement is correct?

A) Incidents rose 30% then fell 25%, a net fall of 2.5% over the two years.
B) Incidents rose 30% then fell 25%, a net rise of 5% over the two years.
C) Incidents rose 12% then fell 13%, a net fall of 1% over the two years.
D) Incidents rose 30% then fell 33%, a net fall of 3% over the two years.

**Correct answer: A.**
**Explanation:** Year one to year two: (52 − 40) ÷ 40 = 12 ÷ 40 = 30% rise. Year two to year three: (52 − 39) ÷ 52 = 13 ÷ 52 = 25% fall. Net change across the whole period: (39 − 40) ÷ 40 = −1 ÷ 40 = −2.5%. Option B commits the classic error of adding percentage changes (+30% − 25% = +5%): percentage changes are multiplicative, not additive, because the second change applies to a different base. Option C confuses absolute changes (12 and 13 incidents) with percentages. When you brief a board on incident trends, this is exactly the distinction that prevents a misleading headline.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Ethics board capacity planning.**
Your ethics board meets 11 times a year and can review at most 3 algorithmic impact assessments per meeting. Next year you expect 120 projects, of which 30% will require board review. What is the capacity position?

A) Capacity 33, demand 36: a shortfall of 3 reviews.
B) Capacity 33, demand 40: a shortfall of 7 reviews.
C) Capacity 36, demand 36: exactly balanced.
D) Capacity 33, demand 30: headroom of 3 reviews.

**Correct answer: A.**
**Explanation:** Capacity: 11 meetings × 3 reviews = 33 reviews per year. Demand: 0.30 × 120 = 36 reviews. Shortfall: 36 − 33 = 3. The practical decision that follows — add one extra meeting (3 more slots), raise the per-meeting maximum, or introduce a proportionate triage so that lower-risk AIAs receive lighter-touch review — is the kind of proportionality judgement your risk-management skill calls for. Option D reflects mistakenly computing 30% of 100; option C miscounts meetings as 12. Always anchor each figure to its source before calculating.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Risk scoring with an escalation rule.**
Your risk methodology scores each risk as likelihood (1–5) multiplied by impact (1–5), and additionally requires that any risk with impact 5 is escalated to the executive committee regardless of score. Four risks are logged:

- Risk A: likelihood 4, impact 4
- Risk B: likelihood 3, impact 5
- Risk C: likelihood 5, impact 3
- Risk D: likelihood 2, impact 5

Which risk has the highest score, and which risks are escalated?

A) Highest: A (16); escalated: B and D only.
B) Highest: A (16); escalated: A, B and D.
C) Highest: B (15); escalated: B and D only.
D) Highest: A (16); escalated: A only.

**Correct answer: A.**
**Explanation:** Scores: A = 4 × 4 = 16; B = 3 × 5 = 15; C = 5 × 3 = 15; D = 2 × 5 = 10. A has the highest score. Escalation is triggered by impact 5, which applies to B and D — note that D escalates despite having the lowest score of all four, and A does not escalate despite the highest score, because its impact is 4. This two-rule structure trips up candidates who assume the highest score must drive every consequence. It also reflects genuine practice: severity-based escalation rules exist precisely because multiplicative scores can understate low-likelihood catastrophic risks.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Evaluating your training programme.**
Before your ethics training programme, staff scored a mean of 3.2 on a 5-point ethical-awareness scale; afterwards the mean was 3.8. What is the percentage increase in the mean score?

A) 12%
B) 15.8%
C) 18.75%
D) 60%

**Correct answer: C.**
**Explanation:** Increase: 3.8 − 3.2 = 0.6. Relative to the baseline: 0.6 ÷ 3.2 = 0.1875 = 18.75%. Option B divides by the post-training score (0.6 ÷ 3.8 ≈ 15.8%) — percentage change is always measured against the starting value. Option D misreads the 0.6-point rise as 60%. As the leader responsible for evaluation mechanisms, you would also want to ask non-arithmetic questions — was there a comparison group, could the change reflect social desirability bias — but the test scores the arithmetic, so compute first and critique later.

**Question 8 (hard) — Balancing the function's staffing budget.**
Your data ethics function has 6 full-time staff at an average annual cost of £68,500 each. Non-staff costs are: training materials £36,000, an external research partnership £24,000, and tooling licences £18,000. Your total budget is £480,000. What is the position, expressed both in pounds and as a percentage of budget?

A) £9,000 underspend; 1.875% under budget.
B) £9,000 overspend; 1.875% over budget.
C) £21,000 overspend; 4.375% over budget.
D) £3,000 overspend; 0.625% over budget.

**Correct answer: B.**
**Explanation:** Staff costs: 6 × £68,500 = £411,000. Non-staff costs: 36,000 + 24,000 + 18,000 = £78,000. Total: 411,000 + 78,000 = £489,000. Against a budget of £480,000, that is an overspend of £9,000. As a percentage: 9,000 ÷ 480,000 = 0.01875 = 1.875%. The most frequent error is in the six-way multiplication: 6 × 68,500 requires care (6 × 68,000 = 408,000, plus 6 × 500 = 3,000, giving 411,000). Breaking large multiplications into round parts, then adding, is faster and safer than keying long strings into a calculator under time pressure — and the sense check (roughly 6 × £70k ≈ £420k) confirms you are in the right region.

**Question 9 (hard) — Base rates and the precision of a fraud model.**
A fraud-detection model screens 10,000 claims. In reality, 2% of claims are fraudulent. The model correctly flags 90% of fraudulent claims, and incorrectly flags 5% of legitimate claims. Of all the claims the model flags, what percentage are actually fraudulent?

A) 90%
B) 47%
C) 27%
D) 18%

**Correct answer: C.**
**Explanation:** Work through the population. Fraudulent claims: 0.02 × 10,000 = 200. True positives: 0.90 × 200 = 180. Legitimate claims: 10,000 − 200 = 9,800. False positives: 0.05 × 9,800 = 490. Total flagged: 180 + 490 = 670. Precision: 180 ÷ 670 = 0.2687 ≈ 27%. So even with an impressive-sounding 90% detection rate, roughly 73% of the people this model flags are innocent — because the base rate of fraud is low, false positives from the large legitimate population swamp the true positives. This is arguably the single most important calculation in your professional toolkit: when a data science team reports "90% accuracy" for a screening tool, the head of data ethics who can reproduce this arithmetic in a meeting changes the conversation about proportionality, human review, and the burden placed on wrongly flagged individuals. Option A is the bait for candidates who confuse sensitivity with precision.

**Question 10 (hard) — Comparing disparity across regions.**
A shortlisting algorithm's success rates by region and group are:

| Region | Group X rate | Group Y rate |
|--------|--------------|--------------|
| North  | 20%          | 15%          |
| South  | 24%          | 21%          |
| East   | 18%          | 12%          |
| West   | 30%          | 27%          |

Using the ratio of the lower rate to the higher rate in each region, which region shows the greatest disparity, and which regions fall below a 0.80 ratio threshold?

A) North greatest; North and East below 0.80.
B) East greatest; East only below 0.80.
C) East greatest; North and East below 0.80.
D) North greatest; East only below 0.80.

**Correct answer: C.**
**Explanation:** Compute each ratio (lower ÷ higher): North 15 ÷ 20 = 0.75; South 21 ÷ 24 = 0.875; East 12 ÷ 18 = 0.667; West 27 ÷ 30 = 0.90. The smallest ratio — the greatest disparity — is East at 0.667. Regions below 0.80 are North (0.75) and East (0.667). A tempting error is to compare percentage-point gaps instead: North's gap (5 points) and East's gap (6 points) are close, but ratios tell a different story because the bases differ, and West's 3-point gap on a 30% base is far less concerning than East's 6-point gap on an 18% base. Choosing the right comparison — gap versus ratio — is precisely the kind of methodological judgement you exercise when reviewing a fairness audit, and assessments reward candidates who follow the metric the question specifies.

**Question 11 (hard) — Cost-effectiveness of two interventions.**
You can fund ethics interventions from a fixed pot. A refreshed training programme costs £90,000 per year and is expected to prevent 24 incidents per year. An automated ethics-checklist tool costs £54,000 per year and is expected to prevent 15 incidents per year. The average cost of an incident to the organisation is £5,200. Which statements are correct?

A) The training is more cost-effective per incident prevented; only the training has benefits exceeding its cost.
B) The tool is more cost-effective per incident prevented; both interventions have benefits exceeding their costs.
C) The tool is more cost-effective per incident prevented; only the tool has benefits exceeding its cost.
D) They are equally cost-effective; both have benefits exceeding their costs.

**Correct answer: B.**
**Explanation:** Cost per incident prevented: training 90,000 ÷ 24 = £3,750; tool 54,000 ÷ 15 = £3,600. The tool is slightly more cost-effective. Benefit-cost comparison: training prevents 24 × £5,200 = £124,800 of incident costs against £90,000 spent (net +£34,800); the tool prevents 15 × £5,200 = £78,000 against £54,000 (net +£24,000). Both clear the bar. If the budget allowed both, the combined position is £144,000 spent to avoid £202,800, a net benefit of £58,800. Note how the two metrics answer different questions: cost-effectiveness ranks the interventions per unit of benefit, while the benefit-cost test says whether each is worth doing at all. Senior numeric assessments frequently require holding two such comparisons in mind simultaneously — as does defending your programme's budget to a spending review.

**Question 12 (hard) — Projecting complaint growth.**
Complaints about an automated service stood at 21 this quarter and have been growing at approximately 15% per quarter. If that growth rate continues, roughly how many complaints should you expect four quarters from now?

A) 30
B) 34
C) 37
D) 42

**Correct answer: C.**
**Explanation:** Compound growth: 21 × 1.15⁴. Step by step: 1.15² = 1.3225; 1.3225² = 1.749; so 21 × 1.749 ≈ 36.7, which rounds to 37. Option B (34) comes from applying simple rather than compound growth: 21 × (1 + 4 × 0.15) = 21 × 1.60 = 33.6. Under quarterly compounding, each quarter's 15% applies to a larger base, so the compound answer always exceeds the simple one over multiple periods. When you use a projection like this in a board paper, you would also state the assumption ("if current growth continues") and its fragility — but for the test, recognise the compounding structure and execute it cleanly.

**Question 13 (moderate) — Reading a stacked completion table.**
Your compliance dashboard shows AIA completion by directorate:

| Directorate | Projects | AIAs completed | AIAs in progress | AIAs not started |
|-------------|----------|----------------|------------------|------------------|
| Operations  | 40       | 28             | 8                | 4                |
| Policy      | 25       | 20             | 3                | 2                |
| Digital     | 55       | 33             | 11               | 11               |

Which directorate has the lowest completion rate, and what is the organisation-wide completion rate?

A) Digital lowest at 60%; organisation-wide 67.5%.
B) Digital lowest at 60%; organisation-wide 66.7%.
C) Operations lowest at 70%; organisation-wide 67.5%.
D) Digital lowest at 55%; organisation-wide 65%.

**Correct answer: A.**
**Explanation:** Completion rates: Operations 28 ÷ 40 = 70%; Policy 20 ÷ 25 = 80%; Digital 33 ÷ 55 = 60%. Digital is lowest. Organisation-wide: total completed = 28 + 20 + 33 = 81; total projects = 40 + 25 + 55 = 120; 81 ÷ 120 = 0.675 = 67.5%. Note that the organisation-wide rate is not the average of the three directorate rates ((70 + 80 + 60) ÷ 3 = 70%), because the directorates differ in size — Digital's 55 projects drag the pooled rate down. Weighted versus unweighted averages are a recurring trap in dashboard reading, and in real compliance reporting the pooled figure is usually the honest one to present.

**Question 14 (hard) — Sampling for a compliance assurance exercise.**
You plan a spot-check assurance exercise on completed AIAs. There are 320 completed AIAs on the register, of which 20% are High-risk and the rest are not. You will sample 15% of the register in total, stratified so that High-risk AIAs are sampled at twice the rate of the others. How many High-risk and how many other AIAs will you check?

A) 24 High-risk and 24 others.
B) 20 High-risk and 28 others.
C) 16 High-risk and 32 others.
D) 12 High-risk and 36 others.

**Correct answer: C.**
**Explanation:** Total sample: 0.15 × 320 = 48 AIAs. The register contains 0.20 × 320 = 64 High-risk and 256 other AIAs. Let the sampling rate for "others" be r; the High-risk rate is then 2r. The sample must total 48: 64 × 2r + 256 × r = 128r + 256r = 384r = 48, giving r = 0.125. So you check 256 × 0.125 = 32 others and 64 × 0.25 = 16 High-risk AIAs — and 16 + 32 = 48 confirms the total. Verify the stratification: High-risk are sampled at 16 ÷ 64 = 25%, others at 32 ÷ 256 = 12.5%, exactly the required 2:1 ratio. Option A splits the sample equally, which oversamples High-risk at a 3:1 rate ratio; option D preserves the register's own proportions (unstratified sampling). The transferable method — define the base rate as an unknown, weight it by each stratum's size, and solve — is exactly how you would design a proportionate assurance regime in practice: risk-based sampling is the quantitative expression of applying risk methodologies in proportion to the risk.

**Question 15 (moderate) — Interpreting survey response bias.**
Your annual "speak up about data ethics" survey went to 4,800 staff; 1,440 responded. Among respondents, 78% agreed that "I feel able to raise ethical concerns". A board member says: "78% of staff feel able to raise concerns." What is the response rate, and what is the correct upper and lower bound for the true organisation-wide agreement figure if every view were known?

A) Response rate 30%; true agreement is between 23.4% and 93.4%.
B) Response rate 30%; true agreement is exactly 78%.
C) Response rate 33%; true agreement is between 30% and 78%.
D) Response rate 30%; true agreement is between 78% and 100%.

**Correct answer: A.**
**Explanation:** Response rate: 1,440 ÷ 4,800 = 0.30 = 30%. Respondents agreeing: 0.78 × 1,440 = 1,123.2, in practice 1,123 people. The non-respondents number 3,360, and we know nothing about their views. Lower bound: if every non-respondent disagreed, agreement would be 1,123 ÷ 4,800 ≈ 23.4%. Upper bound: if every non-respondent agreed, agreement would be (1,123 + 3,360) ÷ 4,800 ≈ 93.4%. So the honest statement is "78% of respondents — 30% of staff responded — agreed", with the true all-staff figure logically anywhere between roughly 23% and 93%. The board member's phrasing silently converts respondents into all staff. In reality you would not present the extreme bounds as likely, but knowing how wide they are disciplines everyone's language — and correcting exactly this slip, gently, in a senior meeting is a lived scenario for a head of data ethics whose evidence must survive scrutiny.

### Preparation tips

- **Rebuild fluency with the core toolkit**: percentages of amounts, percentage change, percentage-point gaps, ratios in both directions, weighted averages, and compound growth. These six operations cover the vast majority of senior-level numeric items.
- **Practise on your own dashboards.** Take a real compliance or incident report and quiz yourself: what is the pooled rate? The quarter-on-quarter change? Which unit is the outlier when you use ratios instead of gaps?
- **Write down intermediate results.** Multi-step questions punish candidates who try to hold three numbers in their head while operating a calculator.
- **Estimate before you compute.** A rough order-of-magnitude check (roughly 6 × £70k ≈ £420k) catches keying errors instantly.
- **Know your base-rate arithmetic cold.** The precision/sensitivity calculation in Question 9 is both a likely test item and a signature capability for your role; be able to do it from memory.
- **Keep moving.** At about a minute per question, one stubborn item can cost you three easy ones. Flag, skip, return.

### Common pitfalls

- **Misreading scale qualifiers and labels** — confusing quarterly with annual figures, or missing "in thousands" on a budget table. Read the header row and units before touching the numbers.
- **Adding percentage changes across periods** — a 30% rise followed by a 25% fall is not a 5% rise; changes compound on shifting bases.
- **Dividing by the wrong base** — percentage change uses the starting value; precision uses the flagged population, not the true positives.
- **Averaging rates without weighting** — pooled rates across differently sized directorates are not the mean of the rates.
- **Over-calculating** — some items are solved faster by the percentage-point gap or a rounding estimate than by full computation; use the lightest sufficient method.
- **Time sink questions** — a multi-stage cost-benefit item can devour five minutes; bank the easier marks first and return if time allows.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment evaluates how well you understand, analyse, and draw correct conclusions from written material of the kind your role turns over daily: policy documents, legislative guidance, board papers, supplier assurances, research summaries, and delicate stakeholder correspondence. The defining discipline of the format is fact-based logic: you must answer based strictly on the text provided, ignoring outside knowledge — even when, as a head of profession, your outside knowledge is extensive and probably correct.

The classic question format asks whether a statement is **True** (the passage logically entails it), **False** (the passage logically contradicts it), or **Cannot Say** (the passage provides insufficient information either way). Some assessments add multiple-choice comprehension items: identifying the best summary, the author's main contention, or an unstated assumption. Timing is tight — often less than a minute per question — to simulate reading under workplace deadlines.

For your role this assessment is unusually authentic. Quickly reading and interpreting complex documents from a range of sources and distilling what is relevant is a named element of your Analysis and synthesis skill; overseeing compliance with standards and legislation demands exact reading of what a rule does and does not require; and mediating between technical and non-technical stakeholders often turns on catching precisely what a sentence commits its author to. The gap between "the supplier tested the model for bias" and "the supplier tested the model for bias against the groups who actually use the service" is a verbal reasoning distinction with real-world consequences — and it is exactly the kind of distinction these tests examine.

### What it measures for your role

- **Analysis and synthesis (data ethics)**: distilling complex documents to what is relevant, and refusing to let a summary drift beyond its evidence. Every True/False/Cannot Say item is a miniature exercise in evidence-bounded inference.
- **Data ethics and privacy**: reading policy and legislative text with precision — what is mandated, what is merely encouraged, what is conditional, and on what.
- **Communicating between the technical and non-technical**: several passages are written communications to or from stakeholders; the questions probe tone, commitment, and implication, which is the raw material of mediation.
- **Communication (data ethics)**: understanding technical jargon well enough to catch when a technically-worded assurance says less than it appears to.
- **Applied social sciences**: one passage is a research summary; the questions test whether you distinguish findings from interpretations — the discipline of working with academics and evaluating published claims.
- **Stakeholder relationship management**: correspondence items test the careful reading that precedes influencing and negotiating with senior stakeholders.

### Practice questions

For each passage, answer strictly from the text. Resist your expertise: if the passage does not say it, you cannot say it.

**Passage A — extract from a draft organisational data ethics policy:**

"All projects that process personal data must complete a data ethics screening questionnaire at the discovery phase. Projects whose screening indicates elevated ethical risk must additionally complete a full algorithmic impact assessment before public beta. The ethics board may, at its discretion, require a full assessment from any project regardless of screening outcome. Completed assessments must be reviewed by the ethics board within twenty working days of submission, and a summary of each reviewed assessment must be published, except where publication would prejudice law enforcement activity or national security. The head of data ethics is accountable for the operation of this policy, and each director-general remains accountable for compliance within their own directorate."

**Question 1 (easy).** True, False, or Cannot Say: Every project that processes personal data must complete a full algorithmic impact assessment.

**Correct answer: False.**
**Explanation:** The passage requires every personal-data project to complete a *screening questionnaire*; the *full assessment* is required only for "projects whose screening indicates elevated ethical risk" (or where the board exercises its discretion). The statement over-generalises the obligation, and the passage's two-tier structure contradicts it. Note the test discipline: you do not need to know anything about real assessment regimes — the answer lives entirely in the sentence structure.

**Question 2 (easy).** True, False, or Cannot Say: The ethics board can require a full assessment from a project whose screening did not indicate elevated risk.

**Correct answer: True.**
**Explanation:** The third sentence states the board "may, at its discretion, require a full assessment from any project regardless of screening outcome". "Any project regardless of screening outcome" logically includes projects whose screening indicated no elevated risk. This is a direct entailment — the words "regardless of screening outcome" do the work.

**Question 3 (moderate).** True, False, or Cannot Say: Summaries of reviewed assessments relating to immigration enforcement are never published.

**Correct answer: Cannot Say.**
**Explanation:** The passage creates a publication exception "where publication would prejudice law enforcement activity or national security". Whether any given immigration-enforcement summary falls within that exception depends on whether its publication *would prejudice* law enforcement activity — a case-by-case question the passage does not decide. The statement's "never" claims a blanket rule the text neither asserts (so not True) nor denies (so not False). Candidates who bring in outside views about immigration data typically mark this False or True; the passage simply does not settle it.

**Question 4 (moderate).** True, False, or Cannot Say: If a directorate fails to comply with the policy, the head of data ethics is accountable for that failure.

**Correct answer: False.**
**Explanation:** The final sentence draws a careful distinction: the head of data ethics is accountable "for the operation of this policy", while "each director-general remains accountable for compliance within their own directorate". The passage explicitly assigns compliance accountability to directors-general, which contradicts the statement's transfer of that accountability to the head of data ethics. This is the kind of accountability wording you might well have drafted yourself — and the test rewards reading it as written, not as commonly misremembered.

**Passage B — extract from published guidance on an algorithmic transparency standard:**

"Public sector organisations in scope are expected to publish a transparency record for algorithmic tools that either engage directly with the public or have a significant influence on decisions affecting individuals. Publication is expected before the tool is used to inform decisions, or as soon as practicable afterwards where earlier publication was not possible. A record comprises two tiers: the first tier provides a short, plain-language description of the tool and its purpose; the second tier provides more detailed technical information, including data sources and oversight arrangements. The guidance does not itself create statutory obligations; however, organisations should note that existing data protection legislation continues to apply independently of this standard."

**Question 5 (moderate).** True, False, or Cannot Say: An in-scope organisation that deploys a significant algorithmic tool without publishing a transparency record is breaking the law.

**Correct answer: False.**
**Explanation:** The passage states plainly that "the guidance does not itself create statutory obligations". Failing to follow the guidance therefore cannot, on this text, constitute breaking the law *by virtue of the guidance*. The final clause about data protection legislation applying independently does not rescue the statement: the statement ties illegality to non-publication of the record, and the passage says the record requirement is non-statutory. The near-trap is conflating "expected to" with "legally required to" — a distinction between normative registers that policy professionals must keep razor-sharp.

**Question 6 (moderate).** True, False, or Cannot Say: A tool that never interacts with the public directly can still be in scope for a transparency record.

**Correct answer: True.**
**Explanation:** Scope is defined disjunctively: tools that "either engage directly with the public *or* have a significant influence on decisions affecting individuals". A back-office tool that never meets the public but significantly influences decisions about individuals satisfies the second limb. The word "either... or" makes the two routes into scope independent; testing whether candidates process disjunctions correctly is a staple of verbal reasoning design.

**Question 7 (hard).** Which one of the following best describes the relationship between the two tiers of a transparency record, according to the passage?

A) The first tier is mandatory and the second tier is optional.
B) The first tier is for the public and the second tier is for regulators only.
C) The first tier gives an accessible overview while the second tier gives fuller technical detail, and both together comprise the record.
D) The second tier replaces the first tier once detailed information becomes available.

**Correct answer: C.**
**Explanation:** The passage says "a record comprises two tiers", then characterises tier one as "a short, plain-language description" and tier two as "more detailed technical information". "Comprises" makes both tiers constituents of one record, supporting C and ruling out D's replacement model. Nothing in the text differentiates the tiers' audiences (eliminating B) or their obligatory status relative to each other (eliminating A). Multiple-choice comprehension items reward matching each option against specific wording rather than choosing the option that sounds most like real-world practice.

**Passage C — email from a director of operations to you as head of data ethics:**

"Thanks for your team's review of the caseworker triage model. I accept the finding that the model's error rate is higher for claimants over 65, and we will fund the retraining work you recommended, though I cannot commit to your proposed timescale until I have the supplier's estimate. I do want to push back on one point: pausing the model entirely while retraining happens would, on our figures, add six weeks to average waiting times, and I am not persuaded the interim risk justifies that harm. I would rather we discuss enhanced human review of over-65 cases as a bridge. Could your team and mine meet this week to agree an interim safeguard? I should add that the minister's office has asked for a note on this by Friday."

**Question 8 (easy).** True, False, or Cannot Say: The director has agreed to fund the retraining work.

**Correct answer: True.**
**Explanation:** "We will fund the retraining work you recommended" is an unqualified commitment to funding. The qualification that follows ("I cannot commit to your proposed timescale") attaches to the *timescale*, not the funding. Separating what was conceded from what was reserved is precisely the reading skill that makes you effective in negotiation follow-ups — minuted commitments live or die on such distinctions.

**Question 9 (moderate).** True, False, or Cannot Say: The director believes the interim risk to over-65 claimants is acceptable.

**Correct answer: Cannot Say.**
**Explanation:** The director says they are "not persuaded the interim risk justifies that harm" — a comparative judgement that the six-week delay outweighs the risk *of continuing while retraining proceeds with a bridge safeguard in place*. Saying the risk does not justify a full pause is not the same as saying the risk is "acceptable"; indeed, the proposal of enhanced human review suggests the director sees the risk as real and worth mitigating. The passage supports neither the statement nor its negation in the terms given. Tone-sensitive items like this are where senior candidates excel if they slow down — and stumble if they paraphrase loosely.

**Question 10 (moderate).** True, False, or Cannot Say: The minister's office has requested a note on this matter by Friday.

**Correct answer: True.**
**Explanation:** The final sentence states it directly: "the minister's office has asked for a note on this by Friday". "Asked for" and "requested" are equivalent in force, and the deadline is explicit, so the statement is a faithful paraphrase and the passage entails it. Contrast a subtly different statement — "you are legally required to send the note by Friday" — which would be Cannot Say: a request from a minister's office carries obvious practical weight, but the passage says nothing about legal obligation, and converting a request into a requirement is exactly the kind of modality creep these tests punish. When judging paraphrase, match both the content (a note, this matter, Friday) and the force (a request, not a command or a statutory duty).

**Question 11 (hard).** Which one of the following is an assumption the director makes but does not defend?

A) That retraining the model is necessary.
B) That the six-week waiting time estimate produced by their own team is reliable.
C) That the model's error rate is higher for claimants over 65.
D) That the minister's office is aware of the model's error rate.

**Correct answer: B.**
**Explanation:** The director's argument against pausing rests on "on our figures, [pausing] would add six weeks to average waiting times". Those figures are asserted, not defended — their reliability is assumed. Options A and C are not assumptions but explicit acceptances ("I accept the finding...", "we will fund the retraining work"). Option D is not assumed anywhere: the passage says the minister's office asked for a note, which implies some awareness of the issue but the director neither relies on nor asserts their knowledge of the error rate specifically. Identifying the load-bearing but undefended premise in a stakeholder's argument is the analytical heart of mediating "difficult discussions about high risk and complex topics" — challenge the six-week figure and the whole case against pausing changes.

**Passage D — summary of a commissioned research study:**

"The study examined attitudes to data sharing among 2,400 adults across the UK, using a quota sample matched to national demographics. Sixty-one per cent of participants said they would support their health data being used for planning public services, provided the data were anonymised. Support fell to 28 per cent where data would be shared with commercial organisations, even with anonymisation. Younger participants expressed higher trust in government data handling than older participants, reversing the pattern reported in the research team's 2019 study. The authors caution that stated attitudes in surveys may diverge from behaviour, and recommend deliberative workshops before any policy change."

**Question 12 (moderate).** True, False, or Cannot Say: A majority of UK adults support their health data being used for planning public services without anonymisation.

**Correct answer: Cannot Say.**
**Explanation:** The 61% support figure is explicitly conditional: "provided the data were anonymised". The passage reports nothing about support in the absence of anonymisation — it does not say support would fall below a majority, and it does not say it would hold. Marking this False is the classic error: the falsity of the *stated condition's* applicability does not tell you what the unconditioned figure would be. Also note the quiet inferential leap from "participants" to "UK adults": the quota sample supports generalisation only imperfectly, but the decisive gap here is the anonymisation condition.

**Question 13 (moderate).** True, False, or Cannot Say: The research team's 2019 study found that older participants expressed higher trust in government data handling than younger participants.

**Correct answer: True.**
**Explanation:** The current study found younger participants more trusting, "reversing the pattern reported in the research team's 2019 study". If the current pattern (younger more trusting) is a reversal of 2019, then in 2019 the pattern ran the other way: older participants expressed higher trust. This is an entailment via the word "reversing" — a one-word logical hinge of the kind verbal tests love, and of the kind that a synthesiser of research evidence must catch at reading speed.

**Question 14 (hard).** True, False, or Cannot Say: The authors believe the survey results should not influence policy.

**Correct answer: False.**
**Explanation:** The authors "recommend deliberative workshops before any policy change" and caution that stated attitudes "may diverge from behaviour". A recommendation about *sequencing* — do workshops before policy change — presupposes that the evidence, suitably supplemented, is relevant to policy; it is a call for more deliberation, not a claim that the results should carry no influence. The statement's "should not influence policy" contradicts the passage's implicit position that the results are policy-relevant but insufficient alone. Distinguishing methodological caution from dismissal is essential when you turn research data into findings for the entire organisation: overreading caution as dismissal (or vice versa) both distort the evidence base.

**Passage E — extract from a supplier's assurance letter about a procured risk-scoring model:**

"Our model was evaluated for fairness prior to delivery. Bias testing was conducted against gender and age using the supplier's standard evaluation dataset, and no statistically significant disparities were identified at the 95 per cent confidence level. Ethnicity testing was not performed, as ethnicity data was not available in the evaluation dataset. The model is refreshed quarterly using data supplied by the client, and responsibility for monitoring performance drift between refreshes rests with the client under clause 9 of the services agreement. We remain confident that the model, when operated in accordance with our published guidance, supports fair decision-making."

**Question 15 (moderate).** True, False, or Cannot Say: The supplier found no evidence of gender bias in the model.

**Correct answer: True.**
**Explanation:** The passage states that bias testing "was conducted against gender and age... and no statistically significant disparities were identified". Finding no statistically significant disparity is precisely "no evidence of gender bias" in the sense the statement uses — the testing occurred, and it returned no positive findings. Be careful, though, about the stronger claim lurking nearby: "the model is free of gender bias" would be Cannot Say, because absence of evidence in one evaluation dataset at one confidence level is not proof of absence. The statement as worded stays within what the passage asserts; the stronger paraphrase would exceed it. Reading supplier assurances at exactly this resolution — what was tested, on what data, and what was actually found — is a core professional act for you, and the test format rewards the same precision.

**Question 16 (hard).** True, False, or Cannot Say: The model has been shown to make fair decisions across ethnic groups.

**Correct answer: False.**
**Explanation:** The passage explicitly states "ethnicity testing was not performed". A claim that the model "has been shown" to be fair across ethnic groups asserts the existence of a demonstration the passage tells us never happened; the text directly contradicts it. Note the asymmetry with Question 15: this is not Cannot Say, because the statement claims a *showing*, and the passage denies that any such showing took place. Whether the model actually is fair across ethnic groups is unknown — but that is not what the statement says. This is the exact reading that should trigger action in real life: an assurance letter that quietly scopes out ethnicity while concluding the model "supports fair decision-making" is presenting a partial evaluation in the costume of a full one.

**Question 17 (hard).** According to the passage, which one of the following is the client responsible for?

A) Conducting quarterly refreshes of the model.
B) Monitoring performance drift between refreshes.
C) Supplying ethnicity data for future bias testing.
D) Publishing guidance on fair operation of the model.

**Correct answer: B.**
**Explanation:** The passage says "responsibility for monitoring performance drift between refreshes rests with the client under clause 9". Option A fails on the exact wording: the model "is refreshed quarterly using data supplied by the client" — the client supplies the data, but the passage does not say the client conducts the refresh; on the natural reading, the supplier refreshes using client-supplied data. Option C is never stated: the passage explains why ethnicity testing did not happen but assigns no one responsibility for fixing it (itself a governance gap worth flagging in real life). Option D belongs to the supplier ("our published guidance"). Contract-language items like this test whether you can allocate obligations to parties exactly as the text does — the skill that determines whether accountability gaps in a services agreement get caught before signature or discovered after an incident.

**Question 18 (hard).** Which one of the following is the best summary of Passage E for a briefing to your executive committee?

A) The supplier has fully assured the model's fairness and the client's only duty is routine monitoring.
B) The supplier reports clean bias results for gender and age on its own evaluation dataset, but ethnicity was untested for lack of data, and drift monitoring between quarterly refreshes is contractually the client's responsibility.
C) The supplier has refused to test the model for bias and has transferred all assurance responsibilities to the client.
D) The model is biased against groups the supplier chose not to test.

**Correct answer: B.**
**Explanation:** A good summary preserves the passage's findings, its scope limits, and its allocation of responsibility, without adding verdicts the text does not contain. Option B does all three: it reports what was tested and found (gender and age, no significant disparities), the stated gap and its stated reason (ethnicity untested; data unavailable), the data's provenance (the supplier's own evaluation dataset — a scope condition worth an executive's attention), and the clause 9 responsibility. Option A launders the letter into "fully assured", erasing the ethnicity gap — the single fact your committee most needs. Option C overshoots in the other direction: testing did occur, and only drift monitoring, not all assurance, sits with the client. Option D converts an absence of testing into a positive finding of bias, which the passage cannot support. Summary items reward the same neutrality you exercise when distilling complex documents for decision-makers: the summary must let the committee reach its own judgement, not smuggle in yours.

### Preparation tips

- **Practise the three-way discipline daily.** Take any policy paragraph you read this week and generate one True, one False, and one Cannot Say statement from it. Setting the traps yourself is the fastest way to stop falling into them.
- **Watch the logical hinge words**: "must" versus "should" versus "may"; "either... or"; "unless"; "regardless of"; "provided that"; "reversing". Most answers turn on one such word.
- **Quarantine your expertise.** You will often know the real-world answer. Underline (mentally) the phrase in the passage that supports your choice; if you cannot point to one, your expertise is answering, not the text — and the answer is probably Cannot Say.
- **Read the statement before re-reading the passage.** At under a minute per question, scan the passage for the relevant clause rather than re-reading it whole.
- **Treat conditional claims as packages.** "61% support X provided Y" tells you nothing about support for X without Y. Conditions are part of the finding.
- **For assumption and summary items, test every option against the wording.** The wrong options are usually real-world-plausible; the right one is textually anchored.

### Common pitfalls

- **Bringing outside knowledge into the passage** — the single most common failure mode for expert candidates. The test is deliberately indifferent to whether the passage matches reality.
- **Confusing "likely" with "certain"** — a passage saying an outcome is probable does not make a statement of fact True, and vice versa.
- **Treating Cannot Say as a soft option.** It is a precise verdict meaning "the passage neither entails nor contradicts this" — not a refuge when you are unsure. Choose it for a reason you can articulate.
- **Loose paraphrase** — "accountable for the operation of the policy" is not "accountable for compliance"; "expected to publish" is not "legally required to publish". Match the modality, not just the topic.
- **Reading the whole passage slowly first.** Skim for structure, then hunt clause-by-clause per question; the clock rewards targeted reading.
- **Missing the scope of qualifiers** — "I cannot commit to your proposed timescale" qualifies the timescale, not the funding. Attach each qualifier to the exact noun it governs.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment presents realistic workplace dilemmas and asks you to evaluate possible responses. Unlike cognitive or numeric tests, there is no formula to apply: several options may seem reasonable, and the task is to identify which behaviour best fits the role's demands and the organisation's values — and which fits worst. Response formats vary: you may be asked to pick the **most effective** and **least effective** actions from four or five options, to rank all options from best to worst, or to rate each option's effectiveness independently. Timing is usually generous or absent, because the assessment targets natural judgement rather than speed.

For a Head of Data Ethics, this is arguably the most authentic assessment of the four. Your role profile is dense with judgement-laden work: mediating between people and strengthening relationships; moderating difficult discussions about high-risk topics within constrained timescales; influencing and negotiating with senior stakeholders; building consensus between independent stakeholders; helping people ask questions and express concerns; leading others to make good design decisions; and developing a data ethics culture that people act on rather than merely comply with. Every one of those is a behavioural capability, and situational judgement items are behavioural capabilities in miniature.

The scenarios below are calibrated to head-of-profession level: they involve ministers' offices, directors-general, supplier executives, civil society, the media, and your own team — and they frequently pit two goods against each other (pace against assurance, candour against confidence, user needs against organisational convenience). The scoring convention in UK public sector assessments rewards options that align with public sector values: putting user needs and the public interest first, transparency and honesty, collaboration across boundaries, accessibility and inclusion, proportionality, and taking ownership rather than deflecting.

### What it measures for your role

- **Communicating between the technical and non-technical**: several scenarios test how you mediate, choose the right channel and register, and moderate difficult discussions under time pressure.
- **Stakeholder relationship management**: scenarios involving directors, ministers' offices, and suppliers test influencing, negotiating, and preserving relationships while holding a line.
- **Managing decisions and risks**: dilemmas force you to weigh consequential risks and act proportionately rather than maximally.
- **Data ethics and privacy** and **Product ownership (data ethics)**: scenarios test whether you build culture and capability rather than becoming a bottleneck approval gate.
- **Empathy and inclusivity**: scenarios test whether underrepresented voices are genuinely incorporated, not consulted cosmetically.
- **Problem management**: incident scenarios test whether you drive investigation, resolution, and prevention in the right order.
- **Communication (data ethics)** and **Analysis and synthesis**: scenarios test candour about evidence, and support for data scientists implementing ethics in practice.

### Practice questions

For each scenario, decide your answer before reading the commentary. The labels give the intended "most effective" and "least effective" options, with reasoning for every option.

**Question 1 (moderate) — The minister's deadline versus the unfinished assessment.**
A minister's office asks your department to launch a public-facing eligibility checker in ten days, to coincide with a policy announcement. The algorithmic impact assessment is half-complete, and early findings suggest the tool may struggle with users who have non-standard work histories — disproportionately carers, most of whom are women. Your director asks you: "Can we live with launching and finishing the assessment afterwards?"

A) Agree to launch: the tool is only a checker, not a decision-maker, and the assessment can conclude in parallel.
B) Refuse to support any launch until the assessment is fully complete, and say you will escalate if overruled.
C) Propose a risk-based path: complete the carers-related analysis before launch as the known material risk, launch with clear signposting to non-digital routes and enhanced monitoring, and finish the remaining assessment to an agreed date.
D) Tell the director the decision is theirs alone, and note your concerns by email for the record.
E) Ask your team to work over the weekend to finish the entire assessment before launch, whatever it takes.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: D.**
**Explanation:** Option C is proportionate risk management in action: it isolates the identified material risk (carers with non-standard histories), insists on resolving that before the public is exposed to it, and offers the minister's office a credible path to their date with mitigations — signposting, monitoring, a committed completion date. It treats the assessment as a means of protecting users, not a ritual. Option B is principled but rigid: it treats a two-tier instrument as all-or-nothing, spends escalation capital before negotiation has been tried, and positions ethics as a blocker — corrosive to the culture you are trying to build. Option A accepts an identified equality risk on the strength of a label ("only a checker") rather than analysis; checkers shape whether people apply at all, so the harm is real. Option E burns your team to gold-plate beyond what risk requires and still may not achieve quality analysis in the time. Option D is the least effective: it is the classic passive deflection — technically covering yourself while abandoning both the users at risk and the director who asked for your professional judgement. Heads of profession are paid precisely to own moments like this.

**Question 2 (moderate) — The data scientist who found bias and is scared to say so.**
A mid-level data scientist contacts you privately: her team's deployed model shows a disparity affecting a minority ethnic group, but her team lead told her the finding is "statistical noise, don't distract the sprint" and she fears being labelled difficult. She asks you to keep her name out of it.

A) Thank her, promise anonymity, and quietly commission an independent re-analysis of the model's outputs through your standard assurance programme.
B) Raise the issue immediately with the team lead's head of profession, naming the data scientist so the report is credible.
C) Tell her to raise it through her own management chain first, since bypassing it would undermine her team lead.
D) Thank her, promise anonymity, commission an independent look at the model through routine assurance, and separately work on why her team treats bias findings as sprint distractions — since the culture that silenced her is itself a finding.
E) Ask her to send you the analysis so you can judge whether it is worth pursuing before doing anything.

**Most effective: D. Least effective: B.**
**Explanation:** Option D does everything option A does — protects the discloser, converts a private tip into an evidenced review through channels that do not expose her — and adds the leadership-level insight: your role profile includes helping people ask questions, express concerns and discuss ethical dilemmas, and a team where a bias finding is dismissed as sprint noise is a culture failure that will recur whatever this model shows. Option A is good but incomplete; it fixes the instance and leaves the cause. Option B is the least effective: it breaks an explicit confidence within hours of it being given, likely burning the discloser and teaching the whole organisation that speaking to the ethics function is unsafe — the single most damaging outcome available. Option C is procedurally tidy but ignores what she told you: her chain has already suppressed the finding once. Option E makes support conditional and puts her at risk of being identified through document trails before you have committed to anything; it also misreads your role — assurance exists so that the organisation checks, not so that she must first convince you personally.

**Question 3 (hard) — The journalist with a partially accurate story.**
A national journalist emails your press office, copying you: they will publish in 48 hours a story claiming your department's fraud model "discriminates against ethnic minorities". Your last bias audit found a small, monitored disparity that the department judged proportionate and has mitigation in place; the story's central claim overstates this, but the underlying figures cited are broadly right. The press office drafts a response: "The department does not comment on operational algorithms."

A) Support the press office line: engaging will amplify the story.
B) Push for a factual, plain-language response acknowledging the audited disparity, explaining the mitigation and monitoring, and offering the transparency record and audit summary — and use the moment to accelerate their publication.
C) Push for a robust rebuttal stating the model has been audited and found fair.
D) Contact the journalist directly yourself to correct the figures off the record.
E) Advise saying nothing now but commissioning a fresh independent audit, announcing that instead.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C.**
**Explanation:** Option B embodies the transparency and candour that both public sector values and your own credibility require: the figures are broadly right, so the honest position is to contextualise, not stonewall — and publishing the audit and transparency record converts a defensive moment into evidence that the governance works. Speaking on behalf of the organisation to large external audiences is literally in your skill set; this is that moment. Option A ("no comment") reads as concealment, cedes the entire frame to the story, and contradicts the openness your framework preaches — a culture-level own goal. Option C is the least effective because it is false: the audit did find a disparity; "found fair" would be a public misstatement that the journalist's own figures could demolish, destroying the department's and your credibility in one cycle. Option D bypasses the press office, creates an unminuted account of a sensitive matter, and personalises an institutional response. Option E delays truth-telling that could happen today and implicitly concedes the existing audit cannot be defended — while the story runs unchallenged.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Two directors, one dataset, no consensus.**
The operations directorate wants to reuse an existing dataset of service users to train a demand-forecasting model; it would cut waiting times measurably. The legal team is comfortable, but your own analysis finds the original collection notice never anticipated this reuse, and user researchers report that affected users — many from low-income groups — would find the reuse surprising. The operations director says: "Legal signed it off; ethics is not a second legal team." The policy director backs you. The disagreement is stalling the programme, and both directors ask you to resolve it.

A) Concede: legality is the authoritative test, and waiting-time reductions are a genuine user benefit.
B) Hold your objection and escalate the deadlock to the executive committee for a decision.
C) Convene both directors around the actual evidence — the collection notice, the user research — and propose a path that separates what blocks from what does not: proceed with the forecasting work using data with an updated notice and a light-touch public engagement exercise on reuse, with the surprise finding tested rather than asserted.
D) Run a full public consultation before any decision, whatever the delay to the programme.
E) Agree to the reuse provided the model's outputs are never used for individual-level decisions.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: A.**
**Explanation:** Option C is the mediation your role exists for: it re-anchors a stalled personality conflict in evidence, distinguishes ethics' distinctive contribution (legitimate expectations, not legality) without disparaging legal's, and converts a binary fight into a sequenced plan that delivers the operational benefit while repairing the consent story. It also tests the user-surprise finding empirically — building consensus by increasing shared knowledge, which is how you challenge assumptions without ruling by veto. Option A is the least effective: it accepts the operations director's category error that legality exhausts ethics — the precise distinction a head of data ethics is employed to hold — and abandons a documented user-expectation problem affecting a low-income population. Option B is legitimate but premature: escalating a resolvable disagreement spends senior attention and signals your function cannot convene. Option D is disproportionate — a full consultation for a forecasting reuse overshoots the risk and weaponises process against a beneficial programme. Option E is a reasonable safeguard wrongly used as a settlement: it addresses a harm nobody had established was in play while leaving the actual issue — surprising reuse — untouched.

**Question 5 (hard) — The framework nobody follows.**
Twelve months after you published the organisational data ethics framework, uptake is poor: teams describe it as "a 40-page PDF written for philosophers", your screening questionnaire is completed late and minimally, and one delivery team has built its own informal checklist instead — which is actually rather good. Your own team is demoralised and wants to push for mandatory compliance backed by spend controls.

A) Seek the mandate: make framework compliance a condition of funding approval, since culture follows structure.
B) Treat the informal checklist as user research: work with that delivery team to co-design a shorter, workflow-native version of the framework, pilot it, and publicise the collaborating team's work as exemplary practice.
C) Commission a communications campaign to explain the existing framework better.
D) Rewrite the framework yourself over the next quarter to be shorter and clearer, then relaunch it.
E) Keep the framework as the reference standard but focus your team on embedding advisers within high-risk programmes only.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A.**
**Explanation:** Option B is product ownership of data ethics done properly: the role profile explicitly asks you to define organisation-wide tools, translate theoretical principles into practice, and capture user needs into deliverables — and a team spontaneously building its own checklist is the loudest user need you will ever receive. Co-designing with them, piloting, and celebrating them turns your biggest compliance failure into your best adoption asset, and builds the culture where ethics is something teams do rather than receive. Option A is the least effective at this moment: mandating an artefact your users have told you is unusable enforces resentment, generates minimal box-ticking compliance (you already see this pattern), and converts your function from enabler to police before the product deserves enforcement — structure can follow culture only when the tool works. Option C explains harder instead of listening — the framework's problem is fit, not awareness. Option D repeats the original error: authoring alone, without users, at head-of-function altitude. Option E is a defensible interim strategy and pairs well with B, but alone it quietly abandons the organisation-wide ambition your role carries.

**Question 6 (moderate) — The consultation that excluded the affected.**
A programme team proudly presents its "extensive engagement" for a new data-sharing policy: a public survey (2,000 responses), two industry roundtables, and an academic workshop. You notice the survey was online-only and English-only, and the policy will most affect people with limited digital access and limited English proficiency. Launch is six weeks away and the engagement budget is 90% spent.

A) Note the limitation in the ethics review and let the programme proceed: engagement was genuinely extensive by normal standards.
B) Require the programme to repeat its engagement exercise with a representative sample before launch.
C) Work with the team to commission rapid, targeted engagement with the excluded groups — through community organisations, interpreters, and existing outreach channels — using the remaining budget, and make the policy's launch plan explicitly provisional on what it hears.
D) Ask the programme to add a paragraph to the policy acknowledging the gap and committing to post-launch review.
E) Escalate to the senior responsible owner that the engagement was discriminatory and the launch must be paused.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: A.**
**Explanation:** Option C operationalises empathy and inclusivity as your role profile defines it: incorporating views from underrepresented groups "using in-depth consulting and outreach strategies" — community organisations and interpreters are precisely those strategies, and rapid targeted engagement with the *most affected* groups adds more signal than another thousand online responses. Making the launch plan provisional on findings keeps the engagement honest rather than decorative. Option A is the least effective: it mistakes volume for representativeness and waves through a consultation that structurally excluded the policy's primary population — with your review as the fig leaf. Option B overcorrects: junking all the engagement is disproportionate when the gap is specific and fillable. Option D converts a fixable exclusion into a documented-but-accepted one; acknowledgement without action is transparency theatre. Option E reaches for escalation and loaded language ("discriminatory") before offering the constructive path — it may eventually be right, but as a first move it spends relationships that option C would strengthen.

**Question 7 (hard) — The supplier who will not show their working.**
A critical supplier's risk-scoring model is central to a live service. Your assurance review requests feature-level documentation and bias-testing evidence. The supplier's account director declines, citing intellectual property, and offers instead a summary attestation "consistent with what other government clients accept". Your contract, agreed before your appointment, is ambiguous on audit rights. The service is performing well operationally, and the commercial team is starting renewal negotiations next month.

A) Accept the attestation for now, noting the limitation in your assurance report.
B) Threaten to advise termination of the contract unless full documentation is provided within 30 days.
C) Brief the commercial team so that explicit audit, documentation, and bias-testing rights become non-negotiable clauses in the renewal, and meanwhile negotiate a middle path for the live period — such as supervised access under a non-disclosure agreement, or independent third-party evaluation against agreed fairness metrics.
D) Ask the data science team to reverse-engineer the model's behaviour from its inputs and outputs instead.
E) Escalate to the department's board that the service is operating without adequate ethical assurance.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: B.**
**Explanation:** Option C plays the hand that actually exists: the renewal is the moment of maximum leverage, and briefing commercial colleagues so that assurance rights become contractual is how a head of profession fixes the system rather than winning an argument. The interim proposals — supervised access under NDA, or independent evaluation against agreed metrics — respect the supplier's legitimate IP concern while refusing the fake choice between "full disclosure" and "trust us". This is influencing and negotiating with senior stakeholders to resolve issues and enable progress, verbatim. Option B is the least effective: the threat is not credible (a critical live service will not be terminated over documentation on your advice alone), the contract is ambiguous so the demand lacks footing, and an empty threat destroys your negotiating position weeks before the real negotiation. Option A concedes the point at exactly the wrong moment and normalises attestation-only assurance across government. Option D has genuine value as a supplement — outcome testing is real evidence — but as a substitute it accepts permanent opacity and asks your organisation to bear the supplier's assurance burden. Option E may become necessary, but escalating before attempting the constructive path arrives at the board without a recommendation, which is not what boards need from heads of profession.

**Question 8 (moderate) — The incident that is also a pattern.**
A data ethics incident is confirmed: an analytics team matched two datasets in a way that re-identified around 300 individuals, and a small number of records were viewed by staff without a need to know. Containment is done, the data protection officer is handling the statutory side, and the analytics team is mortified and cooperative. This is the third re-identification near-miss or incident involving dataset matching this year, each from a different team.

A) Focus your effort on the current incident: a thorough investigation and a strong lessons-learned report for this team.
B) Ask each directorate to remind staff of the data-matching policy.
C) Treat the recurrence as the primary problem: alongside the incident response, initiate a cross-team problem investigation into why matching risks keep arising — tooling defaults, absent re-identification checks, training gaps — and put a preventive fix (such as a mandatory pre-matching risk step with support tooling) to the governance board.
D) Recommend disciplinary referrals for the staff who viewed the records, to signal seriousness.
E) Suspend all dataset-matching activity organisation-wide pending a review.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: E.**
**Explanation:** Option C is textbook problem management, which your role profile names: co-ordinate investigation, implement solutions, and take *preventive* measures — three similar events from three different teams is a system signal, and the right unit of analysis is the pattern, not the instance. Proposing a concrete preventive control to governance turns the incident into organisational learning. Option A does honest work on the wrong scope; its lessons will not reach the fourth team. Option B is the emptiest of gestures — the policy was not the failure point, since three separate competent teams stumbled anyway. Option D punishes individuals to display seriousness while leaving the system intact, and will chill the exact openness (this team is cooperative) that made rapid containment possible; save disciplinary routes for genuine misconduct, which needs-to-know viewing may be — but that is the DPO and HR's determination, not your signalling device. Option E is the least effective: an organisation-wide suspension of a routine analytical practice over a contained incident is wildly disproportionate, halts legitimate public-benefit work everywhere, and teaches the organisation that reporting incidents to ethics triggers paralysis — the opposite of the culture you need.

**Question 9 (hard) — Your own tool gets criticised in public.**
At a sector conference, a respected academic presents research arguing that the ethics self-assessment tool your team built and rolled out across the organisation "produces false assurance": teams score themselves highly while their outputs show unaddressed risks. Colleagues are watching your reaction; the academic has a point, though the study covers only your tool's early version. You are due on the same stage tomorrow.

A) Use your slot to defend the tool: cite its adoption figures, note the study used the outdated version, and reassure the audience it works.
B) Use your slot to engage the critique on its merits: acknowledge the false-assurance risk as real and inherent to self-assessment, describe what the current version changed and what remains open, and invite the academic to collaborate on evaluating it against outcome data.
C) Skip the topic in your slot and speak to the academic privately afterwards.
D) Ask your team overnight to produce a rebuttal analysis showing the tool's scores correlate with better outcomes.
E) Announce from the stage that you are withdrawing the tool pending an independent review.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A.**
**Explanation:** Option B treats criticism the way your framework asks every project team to treat it — as evidence — and does so in public, which is where culture is actually made: a head of data ethics who models non-defensive engagement with challenge earns more organisational authority in ten minutes than a year of policy documents. The collaboration invitation converts a critic into an evaluator, which your role profile explicitly encourages ("work with academics and external researchers to publish research on applied digital ethics"), and it is honest about the genuinely open question. Option A is the least effective: defending with adoption figures answers a validity critique with a popularity statistic, the "outdated version" point is a shield rather than an argument (the current version's superiority is asserted, not evidenced), and the watching audience — including your own organisation's practitioners — learns that ethics leadership gets defensive under peer review. Option C wastes the platform and looks evasive to colleagues who saw the criticism. Option D asks your team to produce confirmatory analysis overnight, which is both methodologically unserious and a revealing thing to model. Option E overcorrects theatrically: the study concerns a superseded version, and withdrawing a functioning improvement mechanism on stage trades governance for drama.

**Question 10 (moderate) — Building the pipeline behind you.**
You have funding for one senior hire. Your team is stretched: the AIA review queue is growing, and directors increasingly ask for your personal involvement in their programmes. Two strong internal candidates emerge: a brilliant analyst who would clear the review queue fastest, and a capable practitioner from an underrepresented background whose strengths are training, community-building, and translating ethics into delivery language — weaker on review throughput. Several directors lobby you for the analyst.

A) Hire the analyst: the queue is the immediate operational risk, and the directors supporting your function matters.
B) Hire the practitioner: representation matters more than throughput.
C) Decide against your assessment of the function's strategic need: if the function's bottleneck is that ethics scales only through your team's own reviews, the capability-building practitioner changes that curve — so hire them, address the queue through triage and trained reviewers in directorates, and tell the directors why.
D) Delay the hire and ask for funding for both.
E) Hire the analyst now and promise the practitioner the next post.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: D.**
**Explanation:** Option C is the strategic reading of the situation your level demands: a growing central queue plus rising demand for your personal presence is the signature of a function that scales linearly while its remit grows exponentially, and the durable fix is enabling others to implement data ethics best practice — training, embedded capability, federated review — which is the practitioner's exact profile and, notably, the core of your role's own definition. The decision is made on strategic need, with the candidate's background a benefit consistent with the inclusive team you are asked to help build — not the criterion. Explaining the rationale to lobbying directors is the influencing work, not an afterthought. Option A optimises the queue while entrenching its cause, and lets stakeholder lobbying set your workforce strategy. Option B reaches a defensible hire by an indefensible route: selection on representation alone is neither fair to candidates nor lawful as stated, and it would delegitimise the appointee. Option E makes a promise you may not be able to keep — next year's funding is not yours to pledge — and mortgages trust. Option D is the least effective: the queue and the demand are live now, "ask for both" is rarely a plan, and deferring a decision you have the resources and evidence to make is the passive option in strategic clothing.

### Preparation tips

- **Anchor on the public interest, then work outward.** In every scenario ask: who is affected, who is unheard, what does the public interest require, and what is proportionate? UK public sector scoring keys consistently reward user needs, transparency, inclusion, collaboration, and ownership over speed, self-protection, and hierarchy.
- **Beware both extremes.** At head-of level, the trap is rarely the obviously bad option; it is the over-principled blocker at one end and the accommodating concession at the other. The strongest options usually hold the ethical line *and* offer stakeholders a workable path.
- **Look for the systemic reading.** When a scenario mentions a repeat pattern, a cultural signal, or an approaching negotiation, the best option usually acts on that, not only on the presenting incident.
- **Check who your action teaches, and what.** Many options are scored on their second-order cultural effects: does this response make it safer or more dangerous to raise concerns, report incidents, invite scrutiny?
- **Practise articulating your reasoning aloud.** Assessment centres often pair situational judgement with interviews; being able to say *why* option C beats option B, in the language of proportionality and user needs, doubles the value of your practice.

### Common pitfalls

- **Choosing the idealistic answer over the effective one.** "Refuse until perfect" options feel principled but often abandon the negotiation your role exists to conduct; effectiveness in context is what is scored.
- **Selecting passive options.** Noting concerns for the record, waiting for more information you do not need, deferring decisions you are equipped to make, or handing the problem back — these are consistently keyed as least effective, and senior candidates pick them more often than they expect, dressed up as "respecting governance".
- **Escalating first.** Escalation is a legitimate tool, but scoring keys favour candidates who attempt direct, constructive resolution before spending senior attention — unless there is live harm or genuine misconduct.
- **Ignoring the stated organisational values.** These assessments are keyed to the values of the employing organisation; in the UK public sector, that means transparency, inclusion, and user needs will beat commercial confidence and reputational defensiveness almost every time the two collide.
- **Over-reading the scenario.** Answer the dilemma on the facts given; importing assumed context ("the minister must have already decided") leads you to score against a scenario the item never posed.
- **Being seduced by decisive-sounding wrongness.** Bold options (suspend everything, terminate the contract, announce a withdrawal on stage) read as leadership but are usually disproportionality in costume. Proportionality is the senior competence.

## Conclusion

You have now worked through a complete set of practice resources for the four psychometric assessment types most relevant to your role as Head of Data Ethics: cognitive ability, numeric reasoning, verbal reasoning, and situational judgement. Along the way you have error-checked ethics registers, unpicked the arithmetic behind bias audits and base rates, read policy and supplier assurances at clause-level precision, and weighed dilemmas involving ministers' offices, whistleblowing data scientists, reluctant suppliers, and your own team's future.

If one theme runs through all four sections, it is this: the assessments reward exactly the disciplines your role already demands. Evidence-bounded inference, proportionality in the face of risk, precision about what a document actually commits anyone to, and judgement that holds the ethical line while offering stakeholders a workable path — these are simultaneously test-taking strategies and the professional habits of an effective head of data ethics. Preparing for these assessments is therefore not a distraction from your development; it is a structured way of sharpening the core of your craft.
