## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the exact mental processes your role demands, using practical workplace scenarios rather than abstract items. Instead of shape sequences and letter codes, you will reason about the everyday artefacts of portfolio management: RAG-rated project lists, dependency maps, governance schedules, prioritisation matrices, and delivery data. The question types cover pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving.

The typical format is an online, timed test lasting 15 to 30 minutes, with 20 to 30 questions. Many platforms are adaptive — difficulty adjusts to your performance — and scoring is objective, comparing your responses against a norm group of professionals at a similar level. Employers usually receive a profile of speed and accuracy rather than a single number, so disciplined pace matters: fast enough to finish, careful enough to be right. Most platforms offer ungraded warm-up questions before the scored section begins; always take them.

For a digital portfolio manager, cognitive assessment is particularly relevant because your professional value lies in structured thinking across many moving parts at once. On any given day you might reconcile conflicting status reports, trace the knock-on effects of a slipped milestone through a dependency chain, spot that a governance submission contradicts the tracker, and decide which of five competing demands genuinely needs attention first. A well-designed cognitive assessment reproduces those demands in miniature. It also rewards a quality your role especially needs: the discipline to conclude only what the evidence supports — because a portfolio manager who over-interprets a data point, or under-checks a report, feeds errors directly into decisions made above them.

### How this assessment maps to your role

Each dimension of the cognitive assessment corresponds to named skills in your role summary:

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **Communicating analysis and insight**: giving tactical recommendations based on your analysis starts with seeing the patterns — which projects repeatedly slip, which risks recur across teams, which figures break the trend. The portfolio-level view exists precisely so that someone notices what individual teams cannot.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **Governance and assurance**: you contribute to deciding what governance and assurance is required for a product or service, and you help teams follow the processes that result. Governance is a system of conditional rules, and applying them correctly — including knowing what they do not require — is deduction in action.
- **Error checking** maps to **Communicating analysis and insight** and **Governance and assurance**: reports, papers and trackers must agree with each other. Finding the inconsistency before a board does is one of the most valuable services a portfolio manager provides.
- **Prioritisation** maps to **Planning** and your duty to support the prioritisation of a group of projects and programmes: ranking competing demands by impact and urgency, at both task level and portfolio level, is the beating heart of the role.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Agile working** and your duty to help create environments that enable teams to deliver: when a team hits a blocker, an open-minded, iterative approach to unpicking the problem — and a willingness to test a pragmatic next step rather than wait for a perfect answer — is what gets delivery moving again.
- **Strategic pattern-connection** maps to **Strategic thinking**: explaining the strategic context of your work requires connecting individual delivery events to portfolio-level significance — recognising when three small local issues add up to one strategic one.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Pattern recognition in a RAG history**

Your dashboard shows six months of RAG ratings for four projects:

- Project Avon: G, G, A, G, G, G
- Project Brent: G, A, A, R, A, A
- Project Calder: G, G, G, G, G, G
- Project Derwent: A, G, A, G, A, G

Which project's history most suggests an unstable reporting or delivery pattern worth investigating, rather than a one-off issue or steady state?

- A) Avon
- B) Brent
- C) Calder
- D) Derwent

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Read each history as a pattern, not a set of isolated ratings. Avon shows a single amber blip in an otherwise green record — a one-off, likely already resolved. Brent shows a genuine deterioration and partial recovery — important, but it is a known issue trajectory that governance will already be tracking. Calder is steady green. Derwent alternates amber-green-amber-green-amber-green: a perfectly regular oscillation. That regularity is the interesting signal — real delivery rarely oscillates so neatly, so the pattern suggests something systematic: perhaps the team downgrades whenever a reporting deadline coincides with sprint end, or optimistic recovery claims alternate with reality checks, or the rating criteria are being applied inconsistently. A portfolio manager's added value is noticing that the shape of the data is itself evidence. Investigating Derwent's oscillation may reveal a reporting-process problem affecting confidence in the whole dashboard — which is exactly the kind of improvement you support senior portfolio managers in making.

**Question 2 (easy) — Logical deduction from an escalation rule**

Your portfolio's escalation rule states: "A risk must be escalated to the portfolio board if its impact is rated 'severe' and its mitigation is not yet funded." Risk 17 has severe impact and funded mitigation. Risk 22 has moderate impact and unfunded mitigation. Which one of the following must be true?

- A) Neither risk meets the rule's threshold for mandatory escalation.
- B) Risk 17 must be escalated.
- C) Risk 22 must be escalated.
- D) Neither risk may be discussed at the board.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The rule requires both conditions — severe impact and unfunded mitigation — before escalation becomes mandatory. Risk 17 meets the first condition but fails the second (its mitigation is funded). Risk 22 fails the first (moderate, not severe). Neither risk satisfies both conditions, so neither triggers mandatory escalation: option A. Option D overreaches in the other direction — the rule mandates escalation in certain cases; it nowhere forbids discussing other risks, and a sensible portfolio manager might still raise Risk 22 by choice. The distinction between "the rule does not require it" and "the rule forbids it" is fundamental to governance work: rules set floors, not ceilings. When you help teams follow governance processes, teams often ask "do we have to?" — and answering accurately, without inflating the rule into more than it says, keeps governance proportionate and keeps teams' trust.

**Question 3 (easy) — Error checking a portfolio summary**

A draft summary for the delivery group reads: "The portfolio comprises 15 projects. Eight are in beta, four are in alpha, and two are in discovery. Total forecast spend this year is £4.2m against a budget of £4.0m, an underspend of £0.2m." How many distinct errors does this passage contain?

- A) None
- B) One
- C) Two
- D) Three

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Check each claim systematically. First, the phase counts: 8 + 4 + 2 = 14, but the passage claims 15 projects — either a project is missing from the breakdown or the total is wrong. That is error one. Second, the spend statement: forecast spend of £4.2m against a £4.0m budget means spending £0.2m more than budget — an overspend, not an underspend. The arithmetic difference (£0.2m) is right but the direction is wrong, and direction is what a decision maker acts on. That is error two. No third error exists in the passage. Answer: two errors, option C. Both are classic portfolio-report defects: breakdowns that silently disagree with totals, and correctly calculated figures with inverted interpretations. The totals check takes seconds; the direction check is a habit of asking "is this good news or bad news, and does my sentence say which?" A summary that calls an overspend an underspend could sail through a busy meeting — until someone reconciles it later and questions everything else the portfolio office publishes.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Prioritisation across competing demands**

It is Monday, 9:30. Four items compete for your day:

1. The portfolio board meets Wednesday; the highlight report is due to the board secretariat by 17:00 today, and two projects' sections still contain unverified figures.
2. A delivery manager wants an hour today to talk through a brewing resourcing problem that "isn't urgent yet but will be".
3. A senior portfolio manager has asked for your comments on a draft governance improvement paper by Thursday.
4. Your quarterly dependency-map refresh is due Friday.

Which sequencing is most defensible?

- A) 1 first with the figures verified, then 2 today as well; 3 Tuesday or Wednesday; 4 later in the week.
- B) 2 first — people problems always outrank paperwork; then 1, 3, 4.
- C) 1, 3, 4, and ask the delivery manager to come back next week when things are calmer.
- D) Delegate 1 to the two project teams and spend the day on 2 and 4.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Item 1 is both urgent (a hard 17:00 deadline) and high impact (unverified figures in a board paper are exactly what a portfolio office must not submit), so it leads. The subtle judgement is item 2: "isn't urgent yet but will be" is a delivery manager doing you a favour — early warning is precisely what enables you to create environments where teams can deliver, and a one-hour conversation today, after the report is submitted, catches the problem while it is still cheap to solve. Deferring it a full week (option C) squanders the early warning and teaches delivery managers not to bother flagging early next time. Option B gets the human instinct right but the sequencing wrong — the board deadline is today; the resourcing problem, by the delivery manager's own account, is not. Option D miscasts delegation: verifying figures for the portfolio's own report is your quality gate, not the projects' — they supplied the figures; your role is to check them. The strongest answer sequences by impact-weighted urgency and protects the early-warning relationship: option A.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Pattern recognition across delivery metrics**

Four teams report their last four sprints' completed story points:

- Team 1: 30, 31, 29, 30
- Team 2: 24, 30, 18, 32
- Team 3: 40, 36, 33, 29
- Team 4: 22, 24, 27, 29

A senior portfolio manager asks which team's data most warrants a supportive conversation about delivery predictability. Which is the best answer?

- A) Team 1, because its numbers never improve
- B) Team 2, because its velocity swings widely from sprint to sprint
- C) Team 3, because it has the highest velocity
- D) Team 4, because it started lowest

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The question asks about predictability, so look at variability and trend, not level. Team 1 is highly stable around 30 — predictable, healthy. Team 4 is climbing steadily (22, 24, 27, 29) — improving, healthy. Team 3 is declining steadily (40 down to 29) — a genuine trend worth understanding, but it is at least predictable, and note that comparing Team 3's absolute numbers with other teams' is meaningless anyway, since story points are team-local currency. Team 2 swings from 24 up to 30, down to 18, up to 32 — a range of 14 points around a mean of 26. That volatility makes forecasting impossible: no one can plan dependent work against a team whose next sprint could deliver anything from 18 to 32. Common causes — unplanned work injections, unstable team composition, chronic over-commitment, inconsistent estimation — are all things a portfolio manager can help address. Option B. The framing matters too: "supportive conversation about predictability", not "performance management about velocity" — story points measure flow, not worth, and treating them as a league table (as options C and D implicitly do) corrodes the trust that makes teams report honestly.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Deduction through a dependency chain**

Your dependency map records: (i) Project P cannot enter public beta until Project Q's API is live; (ii) Q's API cannot go live until the security assessment is complete; (iii) the security assessment requires the updated data-sharing agreement to be signed. You learn today that the data-sharing agreement will be signed six weeks later than planned. Everything else proceeds as planned. What can you validly conclude?

- A) P's public beta will be exactly six weeks late.
- B) P's public beta cannot happen any earlier than six weeks later than currently planned, unless some activity in the chain is compressed or re-sequenced.
- C) Q's API will fail the security assessment.
- D) The delay will have no effect on P, because P and Q are different projects.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Trace the chain: the agreement gates the security assessment, which gates the API go-live, which gates P's beta. A six-week delay at the start of the chain pushes every downstream gate back by six weeks — if nothing else changes. But conclude carefully: the delay establishes that beta cannot be earlier than six weeks late via the current plan; it does not establish that beta will be exactly six weeks late, because the chain might be compressed (parallel working, expedited assessment) — or further delays might add more. Option A states false precision; option B states exactly what the logic supports, including the escape clause. Option D ignores the very dependency the map records — and cross-project blindness is what dependency maps exist to cure. Option C invents an outcome. This is the portfolio manager's daily deduction: translating an upstream slip into its true downstream meaning, neither catastrophising nor waving it away, and then asking the constructive question option B implies — what in this chain could we compress or re-sequence? That question is how you help teams deliver rather than merely recording that they cannot.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Error checking between two governance artefacts**

The portfolio risk register says Risk R-041 ("supplier exit") is owned by the Head of Commercial, rated Amber, with mitigation due 30 September. The board highlight report you are assembling says R-041 is owned by the Programme Director, rated Amber, with mitigation complete. Today is 4 September. Which statement best describes the situation?

- A) The artefacts agree in substance; minor wording differences are acceptable.
- B) The artefacts disagree on two material facts — owner and mitigation status — and both discrepancies must be resolved with the risk owner before the report is submitted.
- C) The report is more recent, so its version should be assumed correct.
- D) The register is the formal record, so the report should be silently changed to match it.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Compare field by field. Rating: Amber in both — agreement. Owner: Head of Commercial versus Programme Director — a material discrepancy, because ownership determines who the board holds to account. Mitigation: "due 30 September" versus "complete" — material again, and note the date logic: on 4 September, a mitigation due on 30 September could legitimately have completed early, so the report's claim is possible but unverified. That subtlety rules out option D's silent overwrite: the register could be the stale artefact. Option C's recency assumption is equally unsafe — highlight reports are often drafted from memory precisely because they are written last. The only sound process is option B: treat the discrepancies as questions, resolve them with whoever actually owns the risk, then align both artefacts. The general rule for portfolio quality: when two governance sources disagree, neither is authoritative until verified — and a report submitted with known unresolved discrepancies converts your uncertainty into the board's misinformation.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Applied problem solving: a blocked team**

A delivery team tells you they have been waiting eleven days for a firewall change from the central infrastructure team, blocking their private beta. They have chased twice by email. The infrastructure team's queue is famously long, and other portfolio projects will need similar changes soon. What is the most effective first move for you as portfolio manager?

- A) Tell the team to keep chasing weekly and log the delay in the risk register.
- B) Escalate immediately to the infrastructure team's director demanding priority treatment.
- C) Contact the infrastructure team to understand the queue and their prioritisation criteria, get the change appropriately prioritised, and assess whether a portfolio-level arrangement is needed for the similar changes coming behind it.
- D) Advise the team to find a technical workaround that avoids the firewall change.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** This question tests whether you convert a local blocker into both a resolution and a systemic improvement — the double move that distinguishes portfolio management from ticket-chasing. Option C does three things in the right order: understand before escalating (the queue may have criteria the request simply failed to invoke — a missing priority flag, an incomplete form); resolve the instant case; then generalise, because you already know more such changes are coming, and a standing arrangement (a service-level expectation, a batching agreement, a named liaison) prevents the next three blockages rather than merely clearing this one. That is helping to create environments that enable teams to deliver, in its most literal form. Option A documents the problem instead of solving it — risk registers record blockers; they do not remove them. Option B escalates before understanding, spending relationship capital you may need later on a case that might have a routine fix, and winning at most one queue-jump. Option D pushes a team towards bypassing a security control, which is the kind of "help" that creates audit findings. Understand, unblock, then fix the system: C.

**Question 9 (hard) — Prioritisation using a scoring framework**

Your portfolio uses a prioritisation framework scoring each initiative 1–5 on strategic alignment, 1–5 on user benefit, and 1–5 on deliverability, with total score guiding priority. A new mandatory compliance initiative arrives, scoring 2 on strategic alignment, 1 on user benefit, and 4 on deliverability — total 7, the lowest in the portfolio. Legal advice confirms the compliance deadline is statutory. What should you recommend?

- A) Rank it last, as the framework dictates; frameworks only work if applied consistently.
- B) Quietly adjust its scores upward until it ranks first.
- C) Recommend it be treated as a mandatory item outside the scored ranking, note this transparently, and flag to senior portfolio managers that the framework needs an explicit mechanism for statutory obligations.
- D) Rescore the whole portfolio with compliance as a fourth criterion before deciding anything.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The question tests whether you understand what prioritisation frameworks are for. A scoring model is a decision aid, not a decision maker — and this case exposes a structural gap: statutory obligations are not "low priority"; they are not discretionary at all, so ranking them against discretionary initiatives is a category error. Option A follows the framework off a cliff: consistency in applying a flawed model produces a portfolio that deprioritises a legal obligation — a risk no board would knowingly accept. Option B is worse: falsifying scores to force the right outcome corrupts the framework's integrity and hides its flaw, so the same failure recurs next time unwitnessed. Option D over-engineers — a full portfolio rescore is a heavy intervention for a problem with a clean immediate answer, though evolving the framework may be right eventually. Option C does what strong portfolio practice does: handle the exception transparently (mandatory items sit outside the ranked list, visibly labelled), meet the obligation, and feed the learning upward so the framework improves — which is precisely your duty to support senior portfolio managers and leadership in making improvements. Tools serve judgement, never the reverse.

**Question 10 (hard) — Multi-constraint scheduling**

You must schedule three governance reviews — Alpha assessment (A), Spend approval (S), and Risk deep-dive (D) — in the next five working days, Monday to Friday, at most one review per day. Constraints: (i) S requires the finance pack, which will not be ready until Wednesday morning; (ii) the lead reviewer for A is available on Tuesday only; (iii) D must happen after A, because the deep-dive uses the assessment's findings; (iv) the deep-dive's findings must be written up for S, requiring one clear working day between D and S. Which schedule is the only one that satisfies all the constraints?

- A) A Tuesday, D Wednesday, S Thursday
- B) A Tuesday, D Wednesday, S Friday
- C) A Monday, D Tuesday, S Thursday
- D) A Tuesday, D Thursday, S Friday

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Fix the anchored review first: constraint (ii) pins A to Tuesday, which immediately eliminates option C (A on Monday breaches the reviewer's availability). D must follow A (iii), so D is Wednesday at the earliest. Now test how late D can be: constraint (iv) demands one clear working day between D and S — a full working day with neither review in it. If D were Thursday (option D), the clear day would be Friday and S would fall beyond the week; infeasible. So D must be Wednesday. With D on Wednesday, the clear working day is Thursday, making Friday the earliest — and only — day for S within the week. Check option A against this: S on Thursday leaves no clear day between Wednesday's deep-dive and Thursday's approval, breaching (iv). Finally verify the survivor, option B, against every constraint: A Tuesday (ii satisfied), D Wednesday after A (iii satisfied), Thursday clear (iv satisfied), S Friday — after the pack's Wednesday-morning arrival (i satisfied). The method matters more than the answer: anchor the most constrained item first, propagate the dependencies forward, and test the boundary cases. It is exactly how you would slot assessments, approvals and deep-dives around reviewer availability in a real governance calendar — and it shows why one immovable constraint (a reviewer free only one day) can determine an entire week's schedule, leaving zero slack. A portfolio manager who can see that the schedule has no slack knows to protect it fiercely: any slip in the pack or the write-up pushes spend approval into next week.

**Question 11 (hard) — Interpreting an anomaly before the board**

Preparing the board pack, you notice the portfolio's total benefits forecast has risen 18% since last quarter, driven almost entirely by one project doubling its forecast. The project's status narrative does not mention any change to benefits. The pack is due tomorrow morning. What is the strongest course of action?

- A) Include the new figure — the project supplied it, so it is their accountability, not yours.
- B) Revert to last quarter's figure for that project, since unexplained changes should not reach the board.
- C) Query the project today: is the doubling real (and if so, get the explanation into the narrative) or an error (and if so, correct it)? If unresolved by the deadline, include the figure with an explicit caveat that it is under verification.
- D) Exclude the project from the benefits total this quarter and mention nothing.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** An 18% portfolio-level movement driven by one project doubling, with a silent narrative, is an anomaly demanding investigation — the combination of large change and missing explanation is the tell. Both possibilities are live: a genuine scope change the team forgot to narrate, or a spreadsheet error (a doubled row, a units slip). Option C handles both with the right sequence: verify at source today; if confirmed, the explanation belongs in the pack (a board seeing +18% will ask why, and the pack should answer before they ask); if erroneous, correct it; if the deadline arrives first, transparency about verification status keeps the board informed without asserting what you have not confirmed. Option A misunderstands the portfolio office's role — you are the quality gate between project data and board decisions; "they supplied it" does not discharge your duty to challenge anomalies. Option B silently substitutes your number for theirs, which may bury a genuine improvement and is itself an unexplained data change. Option D fabricates by omission and distorts the total. The habit being tested: large unexplained movements are queries first, figures second — with honesty about verification status when time runs out.

**Question 12 (hard) — Problem solving a capacity clash across the portfolio**

Five projects have submitted next quarter's plans. Your analysis of a shared specialist team (capacity: 3 concurrent engagements per month) shows demand of 2 engagements in month 1, then 5 in month 2, then 2 in month 3. Two of the month-2 engagements support a statutory deadline at the end of month 2; the other three are discretionary. What should your recommendation to the prioritisation forum be?

- A) Approve all plans — total quarterly demand (9) is within total quarterly capacity (9).
- B) In month 2, protect the two statutory engagements, and work with the three discretionary projects to move at least two engagements into months 1 or 3, using the spare capacity (1 slot each) in those months.
- C) Ask the specialist team to increase capacity to 5 for month 2.
- D) Tell the three discretionary projects to defer to the next quarter.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** First expose the fallacy in option A: quarterly totals (9 demand, 9 capacity) balance, but capacity is monthly, not poolable across time — month 2 demands 5 against a ceiling of 3, and month 1's unused slot cannot be "saved". Aggregate-level arithmetic hiding period-level infeasibility is one of the commonest planning errors portfolios meet. Now the allocation logic: the two statutory engagements are non-negotiable in month 2 (the deadline fixes them), consuming 2 of 3 slots. One discretionary engagement can keep the third slot; the other two must move — and months 1 and 3 each have exactly one spare slot (3 − 2 = 1), which accommodates them precisely. Option B does all this and does it collaboratively ("work with the projects" on which engagement moves where — they know their internal sequencing best). Option C asks the constraint to move before exhausting scheduling options — sometimes right, but expensive and slow (specialists are scarce by definition), so it is a fallback, not a first move. Option D over-corrects: deferral of all three discretionary projects sacrifices two slots that genuinely exist. This is your planning skill and your prioritisation duty in one exercise: model the constraint honestly, protect the mandatory, re-sequence the discretionary, and bring solutions — not just clashes — to the forum.

### Preparation tips

- **Practise on your own portfolio's artefacts.** Recalculate a dashboard figure, trace a dependency chain end to end, reconcile the risk register against the last highlight report. Job-specific tests reward exactly the checking habits this builds — and you may catch a live discrepancy, which is its own reward.
- **Rehearse the reconciliation mindset.** For any two documents describing the same thing, compare them field by field, and treat disagreements as questions to resolve rather than choices to make silently. Several test questions — and many real board-pack cycles — turn on this.
- **Drill conditional logic.** Governance rules are conditionals: "must if", "only if", "unless". Practise asking of each rule: what does it require, what does it permit, and what does it simply not address? Most deduction errors come from inflating rules beyond their words.
- **Check period-level as well as total-level arithmetic.** Totals that balance can hide months that do not. When a plan sums neatly, look at its worst single period before believing it.
- **Time-box your checking.** In tests as in report cycles, perfection is not available. Practise a two-pass discipline: answer or draft quickly, then one deliberate verification pass on the highest-stakes items.
- **Simulate mild pressure.** Do a practice set with a timer and no pauses. Cognitive tests measure your reasoning at pace, and pace is trainable — accuracy first, then speed.
- **Review your wrong answers actively.** For each miss, write one sentence naming the error type (base confusion, inflated rule, missed constraint). Patterns in your errors tell you exactly what to drill next.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Concluding more than the evidence supports.** "Cannot be earlier than six weeks late" is not "will be six weeks late". Precision about what follows — and what merely might — is the difference between analysis and speculation.
- **Treating rule-floors as ceilings.** "Escalation is not required" never means "escalation is forbidden". Rules mandate minimums; judgement still operates above them.
- **Assuming the newer document is correct.** Recency is not accuracy. When artefacts disagree, verify at source; either could be stale.
- **League-tabling incomparable metrics.** Story points, team velocities and locally defined measures do not compare across teams. Patterns within a series are meaningful; rankings across series usually are not.
- **Aggregation fallacies.** Quarterly capacity that covers quarterly demand can still fail every single month. Always test the binding period, not just the total.
- **Following frameworks off cliffs.** Scoring models and processes are aids. When a mandatory item ranks last or a rule produces absurdity, the answer is transparent exception-handling plus feeding the flaw back — not blind consistency and not quiet score-fixing.
- **Speed-reading the question stem.** "Which must be true", "how many errors", "most warrants investigation" — each stem sets a different task. Ten seconds re-reading the stem saves whole questions.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment measures how accurately and confidently you handle the quantitative material your role genuinely produces and consumes: spend against budget, RAG distributions, milestone completion rates, benefits forecasts, velocity trends, and capacity arithmetic. Questions typically present a small table, chart or short data narrative, then ask for calculations and interpretations — percentages, percentage change, ratios, weighted averages, simple forecasts, and reading values correctly from structured data.

The typical format is an online, timed test of 20 to 35 minutes with 15 to 20 questions, usually with an on-screen calculator permitted and rough working encouraged. Multiple-choice options are engineered so that common errors — dividing by the wrong base, averaging percentages, reading the wrong column — each produce one of the wrong answers. This design rewards method over instinct: if you follow a reliable procedure and sense-check magnitude, the distractors lose their power.

Employers use numeric reasoning tests for portfolio manager roles because the role is a quantitative communication hub. Numbers flow up from projects, and you validate, aggregate and interpret them before they inform prioritisation, funding and escalation decisions. An error at your desk multiplies: one wrong percentage in a highlight report can misdirect a board, misprice a decision, or send assurance effort to the wrong project. Conversely, a portfolio manager who reliably catches the doubled row, the inverted trend, and the misleading average becomes the person whose reports leadership trusts without re-checking — a professional asset worth deliberately building. The test does not require advanced mathematics; it requires the disciplined application of a small toolkit — the same toolkit this section drills, with every calculation worked step by step.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Percentages, rates and percentage change** map to **Communicating analysis and insight**: dashboards and highlight reports speak almost entirely in these forms — completion rates, spend percentages, quarter-on-quarter change. Giving tactical recommendations based on analysis requires calculating them correctly and choosing the right base every time.
- **Weighted averages and combined rates** map to **Governance and assurance**: portfolio-level figures aggregate unequal projects. Knowing when a simple average misleads — and computing totals-over-totals instead — keeps the assurance picture honest.
- **Budget and forecast arithmetic** maps to **Planning** and **Strategic thinking**: run-rate forecasts, remaining-budget calculations and burn analysis are how you provide early warning of overspend, and how you support strategic planning with credible numbers rather than hopes.
- **Capacity and demand arithmetic** maps to **Planning** and your duty to support the prioritisation of a group of projects and programmes: turning team capacities and project demands into feasible schedules is ratio and constraint arithmetic applied to real decisions.
- **Reading tables and charts precisely** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management** and your duty to help stakeholders understand the performance of work and related risks: you are often the person explaining a table to a mixed audience, and you can only explain accurately what you have extracted accurately.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Portfolio RAG percentage**

Your portfolio contains 24 projects: 15 green, 6 amber, 3 red. What percentage of projects are not green?

- A) 25.0%
- B) 37.5%
- C) 62.5%
- D) 9.0%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** "Not green" means amber plus red: 6 + 3 = 9 projects. Percentage = (part ÷ whole) × 100 = 9 ÷ 24 × 100. Step by step: 9 ÷ 24 = 0.375; 0.375 × 100 = 37.5%. Option B. Distractor C (62.5%) is the green percentage (15 ÷ 24) — the complement of what was asked, and the most common slip when a question is phrased in the negative. Distractor A is the red-only figure with a rounding nudge (3 ÷ 24 = 12.5%... actually 25% would be 6/24, the amber share); either way, partial categories are traps for anyone who skims "not green". Distractor D quotes the raw count. Two habits defeat all of them: restate the question in your own words before calculating ("amber plus red, out of 24"), and sense-check against fractions — 9 of 24 is a bit more than a third, and 37.5% fits.

**Question 2 (easy) — Remaining budget with commitments**

Project Mersey has an annual budget of £480,000. Spend to date is £310,000, and a further £95,000 is contractually committed but not yet paid. How much budget remains uncommitted?

- A) £170,000
- B) £75,000
- C) £265,000
- D) £85,000

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Uncommitted budget = budget − spend to date − commitments. Step 1: £480,000 − £310,000 = £170,000 (subtract £300,000 to get £180,000, then £10,000 more). Step 2: £170,000 − £95,000 = £75,000 (subtract £100,000 to get £70,000, then add back £5,000). Answer: £75,000, option B. Distractor A (£170,000) is the answer if you forget commitments — and that is the substantive lesson: in portfolio finance, "remaining budget" and "remaining uncommitted budget" are different numbers, and boards deciding whether a project can absorb a new cost need the uncommitted figure. A project showing £170,000 "headroom" that is actually £75,000 after commitments is a project one invoice away from an unpleasant surprise. Distractors C and D are single-step subtraction errors. When a stem mentions committed-but-unpaid amounts, the test — like a good finance business partner — expects you to treat them as spoken for.

**Question 3 (easy) — Reading a delivery table**

Quarterly milestone data for the portfolio:

| Quarter | Planned | Completed |
|---|---|---|
| Q1 | 40 | 32 |
| Q2 | 45 | 36 |
| Q3 | 38 | 34 |
| Q4 | 50 | 38 |

In which quarter was the completion rate highest?

- A) Q1
- B) Q2
- C) Q3
- D) Q4

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Completion rate = completed ÷ planned, per quarter. Q1: 32 ÷ 40 = 0.80 = 80%. Q2: 36 ÷ 45 = 0.80 = 80%. Q3: 34 ÷ 38 ≈ 0.895 = 89.5%. Q4: 38 ÷ 50 = 0.76 = 76%. Highest is Q3, option C. The trap is judging by raw completions: Q4 completed the most milestones (38) yet had the worst rate, because it also planned the most. Rates and volumes answer different questions — "how much did we deliver?" versus "how reliably did we deliver against plan?" — and Q3, the quarter with the smallest plan, was the most reliable. In portfolio reporting you will constantly choose which of these framings a stakeholder needs, and sometimes present both: "Q4 delivered our highest volume, but predictability dipped — teams over-planned." That sentence, with correct arithmetic behind it, is communicating analysis and insight doing its job.

**Question 4 (easy) — Percentage change in spend**

Monthly portfolio spend rose from £220,000 in May to £253,000 in June. What was the percentage increase?

- A) 13%
- B) 15%
- C) 33%
- D) 11.5%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Percentage change = (change ÷ original) × 100. Step 1, change: £253,000 − £220,000 = £33,000. Step 2, divide by the original (May): 33,000 ÷ 220,000 = 0.15. Step 3: 0.15 × 100 = 15%. Option B. Distractor A (13%) comes from dividing by the new value (33 ÷ 253 ≈ 0.130) — the perennial wrong-base error. Distractor C mistakes the raw change (£33k) for a percentage. Distractor D halves the correct rate via a decimal slip. A quick estimation guard: 10% of £220,000 is £22,000 and 20% is £44,000; the £33,000 change sits exactly midway, pointing at 15% before you touch a calculator. Estimation first, calculation second is the professional rhythm — it catches both your own slips and the occasional wrong figure a project submits, which is precisely the checking function the portfolio office performs.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Weighted average delivery confidence**

Three programmes report delivery confidence scores, weighted by their budget share:

| Programme | Budget (£m) | Confidence score (out of 100) |
|---|---|---|
| Alpha | 6 | 80 |
| Beta | 3 | 60 |
| Gamma | 1 | 40 |

What is the budget-weighted average confidence score for the portfolio?

- A) 60
- B) 70
- C) 72
- D) 66

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Weighted average = sum of (weight × value) ÷ sum of weights. Step 1, weight each score by its budget: Alpha 6 × 80 = 480; Beta 3 × 60 = 180; Gamma 1 × 40 = 40. Step 2, sum the weighted values: 480 + 180 = 660; 660 + 40 = 700. Step 3, sum the weights: 6 + 3 + 1 = 10. Step 4, divide: 700 ÷ 10 = 70. The budget-weighted average confidence is 70 — option B. The unweighted average, by contrast, is (80 + 60 + 40) ÷ 3 = 180 ÷ 3 = 60 — distractor A. The ten-point gap between the two is the whole point of weighting: the largest budget (Alpha, 60% of the money) carries the highest confidence, so the money-weighted picture is genuinely healthier than the one-programme-one-vote picture. Sense-check: a weighted average must sit within the range of the values (40 to 80) and should lean towards the heavily weighted ones — 70 leans correctly towards Alpha's 80. When you report portfolio confidence to leadership, choosing weighted versus unweighted is an analytical decision with real consequences: weighted answers "how confident should we be about the money?", unweighted answers "how are programmes doing on average?" State which you used, every time.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Run-rate forecast against budget**

Seven months into the financial year, the portfolio has spent £2.38m of its £4.2m annual budget. If spend continues at the same average monthly run rate, what is the forecast full-year spend, and what is the variance against budget?

- A) £4.08m — £0.12m under budget
- B) £4.2m — on budget
- C) £4.42m — £0.22m over budget
- D) £3.9m — £0.3m under budget

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Step 1, monthly run rate: £2.38m ÷ 7 = £0.34m per month (2.38 ÷ 7: 7 × 0.34 = 2.38, confirmed). Step 2, full-year forecast: £0.34m × 12 = £4.08m. Step 3, variance: £4.2m − £4.08m = £0.12m under budget. Option A. Sense-check by proportion: 7 months is 58.3% of the year, and £2.38m is 56.7% of budget (2.38 ÷ 4.2 ≈ 0.567) — spending slightly behind the even-burn line, consistent with a modest underspend forecast. Distractor C reverses the variance direction; B assumes even burn without checking; D miscalculates the rate. Two professional caveats belong with any run-rate forecast, and strong candidates carry them into real reports: it assumes spend is roughly linear (procurement spikes and year-end invoicing break that), and small monthly rounding compounds twelve-fold — better to compute the rate unrounded (£2.38m ÷ 7 × 12 = £4.08m directly). Forecasting like this is the arithmetic behind providing early warning to leadership — a £0.12m underspend flagged in month 7 is reallocatable; discovered in month 12, it is lost.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Ratio-based cost allocation**

A shared platform costing £270,000 per year is funded by three projects in proportion to their user volumes, which are in the ratio 5 : 3 : 1 (Projects Tay, Esk and Nairn respectively). A fourth project, Spey, now joins, adding user volume equal to Nairn's, and the cost is to be re-split across all four in volume proportion. How much does Tay pay after Spey joins?

- A) £150,000
- B) £135,000
- C) £120,000
- D) £96,000

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Before Spey: ratio parts 5 + 3 + 1 = 9. After Spey joins with volume equal to Nairn's (1 part), the ratio becomes 5 : 3 : 1 : 1, so parts total 5 + 3 + 1 + 1 = 10. Step 1, value of one part: £270,000 ÷ 10 = £27,000. Step 2, Tay's share: 5 × £27,000 = £135,000. Option B. (Check: Esk 3 × 27,000 = £81,000; Nairn and Spey £27,000 each; total 135 + 81 + 27 + 27 = £270,000 — reconciles.) Distractor A (£150,000) is Tay's share under the old 9-part split (£270,000 ÷ 9 = £30,000; × 5 = £150,000) — the error of forgetting that a new joiner changes everyone's denominator. That is also the substantive insight: Spey's arrival saves Tay £15,000 a year, which is why existing projects usually welcome new tenants onto shared platforms, and why portfolio managers model these splits when making the case for reuse. Cost-sharing arithmetic like this underpins many a consensus you will need to broker between projects.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Interpreting percentage-point versus percentage change**

The portfolio's milestone completion rate rose from 72% in Q2 to 81% in Q3. Which statement is numerically correct?

- A) The completion rate rose by 9%.
- B) The completion rate rose by 9 percentage points, a relative increase of 12.5%.
- C) The completion rate rose by 12.5 percentage points.
- D) The completion rate rose by 81%.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Two different measures describe this improvement, and confusing them is one of the most common errors in management reporting. The absolute change is 81 − 72 = 9 percentage points. The relative change is 9 ÷ 72 = 0.125 = 12.5% — the completion rate is 12.5% higher than it was. Option B states both correctly and labels them correctly. Option A commits the classic conflation: "rose by 9%" strictly means multiplying 72% by 1.09 (giving 78.5%), which is not what happened. Options C and D scramble the numbers. Why does this precision matter beyond pedantry? Because the two framings tell different stories: "up 9 points" sounds modest; "a 12.5% improvement" sounds substantial; both are true, and a portfolio manager who understands the distinction can choose the honest framing for the audience — and challenge a supplier or bidder whose glossy "40% improvement" turns out to be two percentage points on a small base. Communicating negative and positive information credibly depends on exactly this fluency.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Combined completion rate across unequal projects**

Two projects report testing progress. Project Frome has passed 180 of 200 test cases (90%). Project Kennet has passed 45 of 100 test cases (45%). A stakeholder asks: "So overall we're at about 67.5%?" What is the correct combined pass rate, and what error has the stakeholder made?

- A) 67.5% — no error; averaging the two rates is correct.
- B) 75% — the stakeholder averaged the rates instead of combining the totals.
- C) 72% — the stakeholder should have weighted by project budget.
- D) 62.5% — the stakeholder used the wrong denominators.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Combined rate = total passed ÷ total cases. Step 1, total passed: 180 + 45 = 225. Step 2, total cases: 200 + 100 = 300. Step 3: 225 ÷ 300 = 0.75 = 75%. Option B — and it also correctly diagnoses the stakeholder's error: (90% + 45%) ÷ 2 = 67.5% is the simple average of the rates, which treats a 200-case project and a 100-case project as equal. Because the larger project is the stronger one, averaging understates the true position by 7.5 points. Note the direction is not always flattering: had the weak project been the big one, averaging would have overstated progress — the error biases towards whichever project is smaller. Option C invents an irrelevant weighting (budget has nothing to do with test-case arithmetic). The workplace moment this rehearses is real and delicate: correcting a stakeholder's arithmetic gracefully — "close, but the projects are different sizes; combining the totals gives 75%" — is stakeholder relationship management and numerical accuracy in one sentence.

**Question 10 (hard) — Benefits realisation trajectory**

A programme's business case promises £1.2m of annual benefits once fully rolled out. Roll-out is 40% complete, and measured benefits so far are running at £360,000 per year. Assuming benefits scale in proportion to roll-out, which statement best describes the trajectory?

- A) On track — £360,000 is 30% of £1.2m, matching roll-out progress.
- B) Behind — benefits should be £480,000 at 40% roll-out; the programme is realising 75% of expected benefits at this stage, projecting to £0.9m rather than £1.2m if the shortfall persists.
- C) Ahead — £360,000 per year exceeds the £1.2m target's monthly equivalent.
- D) It is impossible to compare an annual figure with a percentage.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Build the expectation first: if benefits scale with roll-out, then at 40% roll-out the programme should be realising 40% × £1.2m = £480,000 per year. Measured benefits are £360,000 — a shortfall of £120,000. Express the realisation ratio: 360,000 ÷ 480,000 = 0.75, so the programme is achieving 75% of expected benefits at this stage of roll-out. Project that ratio forward: if the same 75% persists to full roll-out, annual benefits land at 0.75 × £1.2m = £900,000, not £1.2m. Option B captures the whole picture. Option A miscalculates (360,000 ÷ 1,200,000 = 30%, but the comparison point is 40%, so "matching" is false — 30% realised versus 40% expected is precisely the shortfall). Option C confuses annual and monthly units. Option D gives up on a comparison the assumption in the stem explicitly enables. This is the benefits arithmetic behind honest portfolio reporting: early realisation data, compared against a proportional expectation, converts "we'll see at the end" into a mid-course warning while intervention — fixing adoption, revisiting the benefits model — is still possible.

**Question 11 (hard) — Capacity arithmetic with a constraint**

A shared design team has 4 designers, each with 15 productive days per month. Next month's committed demand is: Project A, 22 designer-days; Project B, 18 designer-days; Project C, 12 designer-days. A new discovery, Project D, requests 15 designer-days in the same month. What is the maximum number of Project D's requested days the team can absorb without cutting existing commitments?

- A) 15 — all of them
- B) 8
- C) 0 — the team is already over capacity
- D) 10

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Step 1, total capacity: 4 designers × 15 days = 60 designer-days. Step 2, committed demand: 22 + 18 + 12 = 52 designer-days. Step 3, headroom: 60 − 52 = 8 designer-days. Project D requests 15; only 8 are available without cutting existing commitments. Option B. Distractor A ignores the arithmetic; C mis-adds the commitments; D is a plausible-looking guess with no derivation — a reminder that every option must earn belief through calculation. The portfolio-level decision this sets up is the interesting part: D's discovery either proceeds at reduced intensity (8 days), waits a month, or displaces committed work — and that displacement decision belongs to the prioritisation forum, armed with exactly this arithmetic. Note also the professional caveat a strong candidate would add in real life: 60 designer-days of theoretical capacity assumes zero leave, training and support load; planning teams to 100% is itself a risk, and many portfolio offices plan shared teams to 80–85% for that reason — which would make the honest headroom closer to 3 days than 8.

**Question 12 (hard) — Untangling a two-baseline dashboard claim**

A programme dashboard states: "Spend to date: £1.44m, which is 96% of profiled spend. Profiled spend to date represents 50% of the full-year budget." A board member concludes: "So they've spent 96% of the annual budget — they're about to run out." What is the actual position?

- A) The board member is right: 96% of the annual budget is spent.
- B) Spend to date is 48% of the annual budget of £3.0m; the programme is marginally behind its spend profile at the halfway point, not close to running out.
- C) Spend to date is 50% of the annual budget; the programme is exactly on profile.
- D) The annual budget is £1.5m and the programme has £60,000 left.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Unpack the baselines one at a time. Baseline 1 — the profile: £1.44m is 96% of profiled spend, so profiled spend = 1.44 ÷ 0.96 = £1.5m. Baseline 2 — the budget: the £1.5m profile represents 50% of the full-year budget, so the annual budget = 1.5 ÷ 0.5 = £3.0m. Now the real position: spend to date as a share of annual budget = 1.44 ÷ 3.0 = 0.48 = 48%. The programme has spent 48% of its budget at (roughly) the halfway point, running 4% behind profile (96% of expected) — a mild underspend, the opposite of "about to run out". Option B. The board member chained "96%" to the wrong base — profile, not budget — and options C and D make related base errors (C ignores the 96%; D mistakes the profile for the budget). The rehearsed skill is one of the most valuable in portfolio communication: percentages are only meaningful with their base attached, and dashboards that stack two baselines in one sentence will mislead busy readers unless you, the portfolio manager, either restate them plainly ("£1.44m spent of a £3.0m budget — 48%, slightly behind plan") or redesign the dashboard so the confusion cannot arise. Helping stakeholders understand performance sometimes means fixing the sentence, not the stakeholder.

### Preparation tips

- **Master the four core procedures.** Part-over-whole for shares; change-over-original for growth; totals-over-totals for combined rates; sum-of-weighted-values-over-sum-of-weights for weighted averages. Between them they answer most portfolio questions — drill each until the steps are automatic.
- **Always attach the base.** Before writing or reading any percentage, name what it is a percentage of. Profile, budget, forecast and baseline are different bases, and most dashboard confusion — and most test distractors — live in the gaps between them.
- **Estimate before you calculate.** Ten percent anchors, halves and quarters give you a target zone in seconds. If the precise answer lands outside your estimate, one of them is wrong — find out which before answering.
- **Distinguish points from percent.** A rise from 72% to 81% is 9 points and 12.5% relative. Practise saying both aloud until the distinction is reflexive; it will improve your reports as much as your test score.
- **Reconcile every allocation.** After splitting any total by ratio or weight, add the shares back up. If they do not rebuild the total, the split is wrong. This five-second check is free marks in tests and free credibility at work.
- **Recreate your own dashboard's numbers.** Take last month's portfolio pack and independently recompute five figures. The exercise reveals both your calculation fitness and, occasionally, live errors — either outcome is worth the half hour.
- **Practise under a visible clock.** Numeric accuracy degrades under time pressure in predictable ways — skipped checks, wrong columns. Timed practice teaches you your personal failure modes while the stakes are zero.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Wrong-base division.** Dividing change by the new value, spend by the profile instead of the budget, or a share by the wrong total. The single largest source of numeric-test errors — and of real dashboard mistakes.
- **Averaging rates across unequal groups.** Simple-averaging two projects' percentages is only valid when their denominators are equal, which in portfolios is almost never. Totals over totals.
- **Forgetting commitments and caveats.** "Remaining budget" that ignores contractual commitments, forecasts that assume linear burn, capacity that assumes zero leave — the test may not always penalise the missing caveat, but your board will.
- **Conflating volume with rate.** The quarter that delivered the most milestones can be the least reliable against plan. Decide which measure the question — or the stakeholder — is actually asking for.
- **Points-versus-percent conflation.** "Up 9%" and "up 9 points" are different claims. Precision here is cheap; the credibility lost to a challenged report is not.
- **Trusting a matching option.** Distractors are built from predicted errors, so finding your answer among the options proves nothing. Trust your written method and your reconciliation check instead.
- **Abandoning method when numbers look big.** £1.44m ÷ £3.0m behaves exactly like 1.44 ÷ 3. Strip the units and zeros, compute, restore them — the arithmetic never gets harder, only the notation.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment measures how precisely you read, interpret and evaluate written material of the kind that crosses a portfolio manager's desk daily: governance guidance, spend control policy, delivery status narratives, board minutes, stakeholder correspondence, and extracts from standards such as the Government Service Standard or the Technology Code of Practice. The dominant format presents a passage followed by statements to classify as **True** (the passage supports it), **False** (the passage contradicts it), or **Cannot say** (the passage provides insufficient information). Supplementary formats test inference (what follows from the passage), assumption-spotting (what an argument silently relies on), and clear-writing judgement (which version communicates best).

The typical format is an online, timed test of 15 to 25 minutes covering four to six passages with three to five statements each. The cardinal rule: judge statements against the passage alone, setting aside your professional knowledge. This discipline feels artificial but has a serious workplace analogue — the difference between "the paper states X" and "I believe X from experience" is exactly the distinction a portfolio manager must maintain when reporting to boards, because conflating them converts your assumptions into the organisation's facts.

Employers use verbal reasoning tests for portfolio manager roles because the role is textual as much as numerical. You digest project narratives and reproduce them faithfully at portfolio level; you interpret governance rules for delivery teams who will act on your interpretation; you draft papers whose wording determines what boards understand and decide. A misread exception clause, a dropped conditional, a summary that quietly sheds the caveats — each becomes a portfolio-level error with your name on it. Equally, the role rewards the positive skill the test samples: writing that turns complexity into plain, actionable English for busy senior readers. The practice questions below exercise both directions — precise reading and clear writing — on material drawn from the situations your role genuinely faces.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Comprehension and detail extraction** map to **Governance and assurance**: helping teams follow governance and delivery processes means reading process documents with exactness — what is mandated, what is permitted, what is silent — and relaying it accurately when teams ask "do we have to?"
- **True/false/cannot-say discipline** maps to **Communicating analysis and insight**: portfolio reports must distinguish what source updates actually say from what you infer. The test's "cannot say" category is the professional's "the update doesn't state that — I'll verify" made into a scoring dimension.
- **Inference and assumption-spotting** map to **Strategic thinking** and **Governance and assurance**: contributing to decisions about what governance is required means evaluating arguments — a team's case for a lighter process, a paper's case for investment — and finding the unstated assumptions that make or break them.
- **Clear-writing judgement** maps to **Communicating analysis and insight** and **Stakeholder relationship management**: shaping communications relevant to the audience, and presenting analysis clearly to specialists and non-specialists alike, is tested every time you choose between a jargon-heavy draft and a plain one. Tailoring communication to stakeholders' needs starts at the sentence level.
- **Reading under time pressure** maps to **Agile working**: iterating quickly on papers, minutes and updates requires extracting the essence of a text fast without sacrificing accuracy — the same speed-with-precision trade the timed test measures.

### Practice questions

**Passage for Questions 1–3:**

"Portfolio governance framework, section 4: All projects must obtain spend approval before awarding any contract exceeding £100,000. Approval requests must be submitted at least ten working days before the planned award date. The portfolio office may fast-track requests where a delay would cause a statutory deadline to be missed; fast-tracking reduces the notice period to three working days but requires the finance director's personal sign-off. Contracts below the £100,000 threshold follow standard procurement rules and do not require portfolio-level approval. Splitting a requirement into multiple contracts to remain below the threshold is prohibited."

**Question 1 (easy) — True, false or cannot say**

Statement: "A project awarding a £95,000 contract does not require portfolio-level spend approval."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, but only with the finance director's sign-off

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The passage states that contracts below the £100,000 threshold "do not require portfolio-level approval" and instead follow standard procurement rules. £95,000 is below the threshold, so the statement is directly supported: True. One caution the passage itself plants: the exemption assumes the £95,000 contract is a genuine requirement, not a slice of a larger one — the final sentence prohibits splitting to stay under the threshold. But the statement as written asks only about a £95,000 contract, and nothing in it indicates splitting, so A stands. Option D imports the finance director's sign-off from the fast-track clause, where it belongs to a different situation entirely — a classic distractor technique of transplanting a real detail into the wrong context. Reading each clause with its own scope, and not letting details migrate between clauses, is precisely the skill you use when teams ask you to interpret governance rules.

**Question 2 (moderate) — True, false or cannot say**

Statement: "A fast-tracked approval request can be submitted three working days before the planned award date, provided the finance director personally signs it off and the delay would otherwise cause a statutory deadline to be missed."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True for contracts under £100,000 only

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Assemble the fast-track clause's three components from the passage: fast-tracking is available "where a delay would cause a statutory deadline to be missed"; it "reduces the notice period to three working days"; and it "requires the finance director's personal sign-off". The statement reproduces all three conditions faithfully and in the right relationship — the reduced notice period applies provided the other two conditions hold. True: option A. This question rewards patient reconstruction rather than pattern-matching: each element of the statement must be checked against the passage individually, because a single altered condition (say, "five working days" or "finance team sign-off") would flip the answer to False. Option D inverts the framework's logic — fast-tracking concerns above-threshold contracts, since below-threshold ones need no portfolio approval at all. In real governance work, teams will quote rules to you partially and hopefully; your value lies in re-assembling the full conditions exactly as written before saying yes.

**Question 3 (moderate) — True, false or cannot say**

Statement: "The portfolio office must fast-track a request whenever a statutory deadline is at risk."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, if the request also has finance director sign-off

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Compare modal verbs — the smallest words with the largest consequences. The passage says the portfolio office "may fast-track requests" where a statutory deadline would be missed: "may" grants a discretion. The statement claims the office "must fast-track... whenever" the condition holds: "must... whenever" asserts an obligation. A discretion is not an obligation, so the statement contradicts the passage's actual wording: False. It is not "cannot say", because the passage does address the situation — it just grants permission rather than imposing duty. Option D cannot rescue the statement; sign-off is a requirement of fast-tracking, not a trigger that converts discretion into obligation. This may/must distinction is arguably the single most consequential reading skill in governance: a team told "they must fast-track us" will plan on a three-day approval they may not get, and the portfolio manager who conflated the modals will own the resulting slip. When you help teams follow governance processes, quote the modal verbs exactly.

**Passage for Questions 4–6:**

"Extract from a delivery status narrative: 'The payments migration remains amber. We completed the supplier integration two weeks ahead of schedule, and performance testing has begun early as a result. However, the third-party identity service released a breaking API change with thirty days' notice, and our remediation work will consume the buffer we had built for the release window. If a further breaking change lands before go-live, the December release date will not be achievable. We have asked the supplier for their change roadmap but have not yet received it. On current information we remain committed to December.'"

**Question 4 (easy) — Identifying the main point**

Which sentence best captures the update's overall message?

- A) "The project is ahead of schedule following early completion of supplier integration."
- B) "The December release has been cancelled due to third-party API changes."
- C) "The project remains committed to December, but its schedule buffer is now consumed, leaving no resilience against further third-party changes."
- D) "The project is waiting for a supplier roadmap before making any commitments."

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** A faithful summary must carry the update's overall risk posture, which has three load-bearing parts: the December commitment stands (final sentence), the buffer is gone (remediation consumes it), and the plan is now fragile — one more breaking change breaks the date. Option C holds all three. Option A cherry-picks the good news; it is true but radically incomplete, and a portfolio report built from it would leave the board unprepared for a December slip. Option B overshoots into a claim the update explicitly denies. Option D inflates a detail (the awaited roadmap) into the headline and misstates the commitment position. Notice the craft in the original narrative worth emulating: it pairs good news with its consequence ("testing has begun early as a result") and bad news with its conditional impact ("if a further change lands..."). Your summary should preserve that conditional structure — "committed but fragile" — because the condition is precisely what the board can act on, for instance by pressing the supplier for the roadmap at a senior level.

**Question 5 (moderate) — True, false or cannot say**

Statement: "The project's schedule buffer was consumed by the delay to supplier integration."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, according to the supplier's roadmap

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Check both halves of the causal claim against the passage. Was the buffer consumed? Yes — but by what? The passage attributes it to "our remediation work" responding to the identity service's breaking API change. And supplier integration was not delayed at all — it "completed two weeks ahead of schedule". The statement therefore gets the cause wrong twice over: it names an event that did not happen (an integration delay) and misattributes the buffer's consumption. Contradicted on both counts: False. This is a causal-attribution question, a common verbal-reasoning form that punishes plausible-sounding recombination of real elements — the passage genuinely mentions a buffer, a supplier, and integration, and the statement stitches them together wrongly. The workplace parallel is sharper than it looks: status narratives are routinely mis-summarised in exactly this way as they travel upward, with causes reattached to the wrong effects, and the portfolio manager who transcribes causality carefully — who keeps "why" attached to the right "what" — protects the board from acting on a false diagnosis.

**Question 6 (hard) — Evaluating the commitment's basis**

The update says: "On current information we remain committed to December." Which unstated assumption does this commitment most critically depend on?

- A) That performance testing began early.
- B) That no further breaking change from the third-party service lands before go-live.
- C) That the portfolio office approves the remediation work.
- D) That the December release was in the original business case.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** An argument's critical assumption is the one whose failure destroys the conclusion. The update itself hands you the fragility: "If a further breaking change lands before go-live, the December release date will not be achievable." The December commitment therefore stands only on the assumption that no such change arrives — and the team has flagged, with the unanswered roadmap request, that it cannot yet verify this. Option B. Option A is stated fact, not assumption, and supports rather than underpins the date. Options C and D introduce dependencies the passage never mentions. Notice the phrase doing quiet work in the original: "on current information" — a professional hedge that makes the commitment conditional on the very assumption B names. Reading for load-bearing assumptions transforms how you consume status reports: the sharp portfolio question here is not "are you confident about December?" but "what would have to stay true for December to hold, and how do we get sight of the supplier's roadmap?" That question, surfaced at the right board, is the difference between assurance and reassurance.

**Passage for Questions 7–9:**

"Extract from portfolio prioritisation guidance: 'Prioritisation decisions should balance strategic alignment, user benefit and deliverability. No single criterion is decisive: a highly aligned initiative with weak deliverability evidence should normally be deferred for further discovery rather than approved or rejected outright. Initiatives addressing legal or safety obligations are exempt from comparative prioritisation and must be scheduled to meet their obligations. Where two initiatives compete for the same scarce capability, the portfolio office should present the trade-off explicitly to the prioritisation board rather than resolve it administratively. The board's decisions, and their rationale, must be recorded and published to all affected teams.'"

**Question 7 (moderate) — True, false or cannot say**

Statement: "An initiative with strong strategic alignment but weak deliverability evidence should normally be rejected."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, unless it addresses a legal obligation

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage prescribes a specific disposition for exactly this case: such an initiative "should normally be deferred for further discovery rather than approved or rejected outright". The statement claims the normal outcome is rejection — one of the two outcomes the passage explicitly rules out as the default. Contradicted: False. The distractor logic in option D is worth dissecting: it borrows the legal-obligation exemption to create a plausible-sounding qualification, but the exemption works in the opposite direction (mandatory scheduling, not rejection), so it cannot convert a false default into a true one. The three-way distinction the passage draws — approve, reject, or defer for discovery — reflects genuinely good portfolio practice: weak deliverability evidence is an information gap, and the proportionate response to an information gap is to buy information (a discovery), not to make a permanent decision on temporary ignorance. When you support prioritisation processes, holding this "defer is a real option" line against stakeholders who want a binary yes/no is a small daily act of strategic thinking.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Applying the guidance**

Two initiatives both require the portfolio's only machine-learning engineer next quarter. The portfolio office's analyst proposes quietly scheduling one initiative first "to avoid bothering the board with a minor clash". Based only on the passage, what should you do?

- A) Accept the analyst's proposal — administrative efficiency is good practice.
- B) Present the trade-off explicitly to the prioritisation board, since the initiatives compete for a scarce capability, and ensure the decision and rationale are recorded and published.
- C) Reject both initiatives until a second engineer is hired.
- D) Ask the initiatives to negotiate directly with each other and report the outcome.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The passage speaks directly to this configuration: "Where two initiatives compete for the same scarce capability, the portfolio office should present the trade-off explicitly to the prioritisation board rather than resolve it administratively." The analyst's proposal — however well-intentioned its instinct to protect board time — is precisely the administrative resolution the guidance prohibits, and option B applies the rule plus the final sentence's recording and publication duty. Options C and D invent dispositions the passage nowhere supports. Why does the guidance insist on board visibility for what looks like a scheduling detail? Because sequencing two initiatives against one scarce engineer is a prioritisation decision wearing operational clothes: whichever goes second may miss its window, and that trade-off belongs to the body accountable for portfolio value, made in daylight with recorded rationale. This question models a recurring moment in your role — recognising when an apparently minor operational choice is really a governance decision in disguise, and routing it accordingly. Efficiency instincts are valuable servants and dangerous masters.

**Question 9 (hard) — True, false or cannot say**

Statement: "The prioritisation board's decisions are published on the department's public website."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, because government decisions must be transparent

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage requires decisions and rationale to be "recorded and published to all affected teams". The statement claims publication on the public website — a different, wider audience. Does the passage support it? No: internal publication to affected teams is what is specified. Does the passage contradict it? Also no: publishing to teams does not preclude additional publication publicly, and the passage is simply silent about any wider channel. Silence on a point the statement asserts means Cannot say: option C. Option B is the tempting over-reach — "the passage says teams, so not the website" — but that inference converts an inclusion into an exclusion, which the text does not license. Option D argues from general principle ("government transparency") rather than from the passage, the cardinal sin of verbal reasoning. The workplace analogue is precise: when someone asks "does the guidance allow X?", the honest trichotomy is allows / prohibits / doesn't address, and the third answer — with "let me check with the policy owner" attached — is frequently the correct and most professional one. Portfolio managers who can say "the framework is silent on that" resist both false permission and false prohibition.

**Question 10 (moderate) — Clear-writing judgement**

You are drafting the portfolio highlight report's summary of the payments migration update (from Questions 4–6). Which version serves a senior, mixed-specialism board best?

- A) "Payments migration: amber. Leveraging the accelerated supplier integration, the workstream has operationalised early performance testing; however, third-party API volatility has necessitated remediation activity absorbing schedule contingency, rendering the December milestone contingent on the non-recurrence of breaking changes."
- B) "Payments migration: amber. Integration finished two weeks early and testing has started. A third-party API change has used up the schedule buffer. December remains the plan, but another such change would make it unachievable. We are pressing the supplier for its change roadmap; board support in obtaining it would help."
- C) "Payments migration: amber. Some third-party issues have arisen but the team is confident and December is still on."
- D) "Payments migration: amber. The identity service provider issued a breaking change to their OAuth endpoints with a 30-day deprecation window, requiring re-work of the token-handling middleware and consuming the release contingency."

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Test each version against what a board needs: the position, the risk, and what the board can do. Option B delivers all three in plain English — the facts (early integration, consumed buffer), the conditional risk (another change breaks December), and a specific ask (support in obtaining the roadmap), which converts reporting into governance action. Option A contains the same facts entombed in officialese — "operationalised", "necessitated remediation activity", "non-recurrence" — prose that takes twice the reading effort for the same content and signals drafting habit rather than audience care. Option C is the dangerous one: "confident and still on" launders a fragile position into a comfortable one by deleting the buffer and the conditional — a board reading C would be genuinely misinformed, and the December slip, if it came, would arrive as a surprise the report had made possible. Option D is accurate but written for engineers — OAuth endpoints and middleware belong in the technical annex, not the board summary. The craft rule embedded here: a highlight report line should let a non-specialist reader repeat the position accurately to someone else. Option B passes that test; the others fail it in three different directions — obscurity, false comfort, and misplaced depth.

**Passage for Questions 11–12:**

"Extract from a spend control policy note: 'Digital spend above delegated limits requires central approval. Departments have complained that approval timelines are unpredictable, and a review found median approval time of 18 working days, with 10% of cases exceeding 40 working days. From October, a service-level commitment of 15 working days will apply to complete submissions. A submission is complete when it includes the business case, the spend breakdown, and evidence of compliance with the Technology Code of Practice. Incomplete submissions will be returned, and the clock starts only when a complete submission is received. Departments should note that the commitment applies to approval decisions, which include approval, conditional approval, and rejection.'"

**Question 11 (hard) — True, false or cannot say**

Statement: "From October, departments will receive approval of their digital spend submissions within 15 working days."

- A) True
- B) False
- C) Cannot say
- D) True, for complete submissions only

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The statement smuggles in two distortions, either of which is fatal. First, scope: the commitment applies only to complete submissions (business case, spend breakdown, TCoP evidence), and the clock starts on receipt of completeness — so "submissions" without qualification overstates the promise. Second, and decisively: the commitment is to an approval decision within 15 days, and the passage's final sentence spells out that decisions include "approval, conditional approval, and rejection". The statement promises approval — a favourable outcome — where the policy promises only an answer. A department could receive a rejection on day 15 and the commitment would be fully honoured. That mismatch contradicts the passage: False. Even option D, which patches the completeness distortion, cannot repair the outcome distortion, which is why it fails. This is the highest-value reading skill in the section: service-level commitments, contracts and policies promise processes, timescales and decisions — rarely outcomes — and the portfolio manager who briefs teams that "approval takes 15 days now" has converted a timeliness promise into a false success promise. Brief the distinction: "you'll get a decision in 15 days — make sure the submission is complete, and be ready for conditions."

**Question 12 (hard) — Inference from the statistics**

Which one of the following can be properly inferred from the passage?

- A) Before the review, half of approval cases took longer than 18 working days.
- B) The 15-day commitment will reduce median approval time by 3 working days.
- C) Most departments submit incomplete submissions.
- D) After October, no case will take longer than 15 working days.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Test each candidate against what the text licenses. Option A: the review found a median of 18 working days, and the median is by definition the point half of cases exceed — so "half of cases took longer than 18 days" restates the statistic's meaning. Properly inferred. Option B assumes the commitment will be met and that it maps onto the median — the passage announces a commitment, not a result; future performance is hoped for, not established. Option C: the passage defines completeness and warns about incomplete submissions, but gives no frequency data whatsoever — "departments should note" is advice, not statistics. Option D converts a service-level commitment into a guarantee of universal compliance, ignoring both the possibility of breach and the completeness gate (an incomplete submission could take months of elapsed time while its clock never starts). The general lesson has two halves. Statistically: know what medians actually assert — a median of 18 says nothing about how bad the tail is beyond it, which is why the passage separately reports the 10%-over-40-days figure. Rhetorically: policies announce intentions; only data reports outcomes. Portfolio managers live at the junction of the two, and inference discipline keeps you from reporting an intention as if it were already a fact.

### Preparation tips

- **Drill the modal verbs.** May, must, should, will, normally, only — governance documents turn on them, and so do test statements. When reading any rule, mentally tag each clause as obligation, permission, default or prohibition; most False statements are modal swaps.
- **Keep causes attached to effects.** When summarising, check that every "because" in your version matches a "because" in the source. Causal reattachment is both a common distractor design and a common real-world summarising error.
- **Practise the completeness check on summaries.** For any status update, list its load-bearing elements (position, risk, condition, ask), then test candidate summaries against the list. The best option carries all of them; the dangerous option is the comfortable one that sheds the risk.
- **Separate the passage's world from yours.** Your knowledge of how spend controls or prioritisation actually work in your department is a liability in the test. Answer from the text alone — and notice that this is the same discipline as reporting "what the update says" rather than "what I reckon".
- **Hunt the hedges.** Phrases like "on current information", "should normally", "we expect" are load-bearing qualifiers. Statements that strip them out change the claim — usually from conditional to absolute, and usually from True to False.
- **Read commitments as processes, not outcomes.** Service levels promise decisions and timescales, not favourable answers. Practise restating any commitment in its narrowest honest form.
- **Time-box per passage, not per statement.** Budget roughly four minutes per passage-with-statements and move on when spent. Verbal tests reward steady coverage far more than perfection on one dense text.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Conflating "False" with "not mentioned".** False requires contradiction. If the passage is merely silent, the answer is Cannot say — and in real governance work, "the framework doesn't address that" is a finding, not a failure.
- **Converting inclusions into exclusions.** "Published to all affected teams" does not mean "not published anywhere else". Specification of one thing is not prohibition of others.
- **Letting details migrate between clauses.** The finance director's sign-off belongs to fast-tracking, not to below-threshold contracts. Distractors transplant true details into wrong contexts; check each detail against its home clause.
- **Rewarding confident-sounding wrong answers.** "True, because government decisions must be transparent" argues from principle, not passage. Any option whose justification lies outside the text is wrong by construction.
- **Summarising for comfort.** The option that makes the news pleasant by omitting the fragility is not a summary; it is a distortion. In tests it costs a mark; in highlight reports it costs the board its foresight.
- **Missing the outcome/process switch.** "Approval within 15 days" versus "an approval decision within 15 days" — one word apart, a world apart. Read commitments at word level.
- **Racing the clock into skim-reading.** Verbal distractors are engineered to reward skimmers. A single careful read of the passage, then statement-by-statement verification against it, is faster overall than three anxious skims.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement test (SJT) presents realistic workplace scenarios and asks you to evaluate possible responses — selecting the most effective, the least effective, ranking all options, or rating each independently. It measures professional judgement rather than knowledge: how you balance honesty, delivery pressure, relationships, process and escalation when they pull in different directions. For a digital portfolio manager, the scenarios reflect your role's characteristic tensions: teams versus governance, transparency versus stakeholder comfort, helping versus overriding, and the constant question of what to handle yourself and what to route upward to senior portfolio managers or leadership.

The typical format is untimed or generously timed — 20 to 40 minutes for 15 to 25 scenarios. There is rarely a flawless option; the test measures whether you can distinguish good from slightly-flawed from harmful, and whether your judgements are consistent with the behaviours the organisation values. For UK government digital roles, the relevant behavioural anchors include honesty and transparency, user focus, collaboration, ownership, appropriate escalation, inclusion, and proportionate use of process. Scoring compares your responses against benchmarks set by experienced practitioners and the organisation's competency framework.

Employers use SJTs for portfolio manager roles because the role is judgement-intensive in a specific way: you hold information that others act on, you operate between teams and authority rather than over either, and your daily choices — what to flag, what to soften, whom to involve, when to push — shape whether the portfolio's governance is genuinely trustworthy or merely performed. Technical skills can be trained quickly; the instinct to keep bad news accurate, escalate early without alarmism, and challenge colleagues without damaging relationships is what assessment centres most want evidence of. The best preparation is understanding why certain responses serve the organisation better, which is what every explanation below works through.

### How this assessment maps to your role

- **Transparency and reporting scenarios** map to **Communicating analysis and insight** and your duty to help stakeholders understand the performance of work and related risks: dilemmas about softening reports, presenting unverified figures, or sitting on emerging bad news test whether your communication stays accurate under social pressure.
- **Escalation scenarios** map to **Governance and assurance** and your duty to support more senior portfolio managers and leadership: knowing what to resolve yourself, what to escalate, and how to escalate without drama is a scored dimension of nearly every portfolio SJT.
- **Team-facing scenarios** map to **Agile working** and your duty to help create environments that enable teams to deliver: how you respond to a struggling team, a defensive delivery manager, or a governance-versus-delivery clash tests whether you act as enabler or enforcer.
- **Stakeholder dilemmas** map to **Stakeholder relationship management**: influencing without authority, handling impatient senior figures, building consensus between parties with conflicting interests, and using evidence to explain decisions all appear as SJT staples.
- **Prioritisation dilemmas** map to **Planning** and your duty to support the prioritisation of a group of projects and programmes: scenarios that force trade-offs between competing demands test the same impact-and-urgency reasoning as the cognitive section, with human factors added.
- **Improvement scenarios** map to **Strategic thinking**: the strongest SJT answers often fix the system as well as the instance — spotting that today's dilemma reveals a process gap worth raising is exactly the improvement contribution your role level calls for.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Unverified good news before the board**

The day before the portfolio board, a delivery manager tells you his project's critical performance issue "is basically fixed — the overnight test run should confirm it". The draft highlight report currently rates the project red because of this issue. He asks you to change it to amber now so the board "sees the real picture". What is the most effective response?

- A) Change the rating to amber — he knows his project best, and boards dislike stale bad news.
- B) Keep red, but add a note that a fix is in final testing with results expected before the board; update the rating only if the overnight results confirm the fix.
- C) Keep red and say nothing about the fix, since unconfirmed progress is not reportable.
- D) Change the rating to amber but keep a private note to revert it if the test fails.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The behavioural principle at stake is that governance ratings reflect verified positions, not hopes — and the counter-principle is that reports should be current and fair to teams making real progress. Option B honours both: the rating stays evidence-based (the issue is not yet confirmed fixed), while the narrative gives the board the genuinely useful new information — a fix exists, confirmation is imminent — and creates a clean trigger for updating the rating on evidence. Option A rates on optimism; if the overnight run fails, the board has been misled and the delivery manager's "basically fixed" becomes your credibility problem. Option C is accurate but unfair and unhelpful — omitting known, material, favourable context distorts just as omitting risk does, and it needlessly antagonises a delivery manager who is engaging openly with you. Option D is quiet double-bookkeeping: the board sees amber while your private note concedes red might be true. The transferable pattern: ratings move on evidence; narratives carry momentum and expectation. Boards handle "red, fix in final verification" very well — it shows a portfolio office that neither panics nor spins.

**Question 2 (easy) — A team struggling with governance paperwork**

A small delivery team repeatedly submits late, incomplete governance returns. Their delivery manager says: "We're four people shipping every fortnight — your templates assume a programme office we don't have." The returns genuinely are heavyweight for a team that size. What is the most effective response?

- A) Insist on full compliance — consistency across the portfolio is non-negotiable.
- B) Exempt the team from returns until they are bigger.
- C) Work with the team to agree a proportionate lightweight return that still provides the essential assurance data, and propose the tiered format to senior portfolio managers as a portfolio-wide improvement for small teams.
- D) Complete the returns for them each month yourself.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** This scenario tests whether you treat governance as a service to decision making or a compliance ritual. The team's complaint is substantive: proportionality is a core principle of good assurance, and templates designed for programmes genuinely can smother small agile teams — the cost of the reporting must be justified by the decisions it informs. Option C fixes the instance (a lightweight return that still carries the essential data: status, risks, spend, blockers) and then fixes the system (proposing tiering to senior portfolio managers — which is precisely your duty to support leadership in making improvements). It also converts a compliance conflict into a collaborative design conversation, which is how you help create environments that enable delivery. Option A wins compliance and loses the information: teams forced into disproportionate process produce late, grudging, low-quality data — the returns were already failing. Option B swings to the opposite failure, creating an assurance blind spot precisely where oversight is thinnest. Option D is generous and unsustainable — it hides the process defect by absorbing its cost personally, guaranteeing the template never gets fixed and your capacity quietly drains. Fix processes, not symptoms.

**Question 3 (easy) — Credit and visibility**

At a senior meeting, a director praises "the portfolio office's excellent new dependency dashboard" — which was in fact designed and built almost entirely by one of your portfolio analysts, who is not in the room. What is the most effective response?

- A) Accept the praise on the office's behalf and move on — attribution details do not matter at senior level.
- B) Thank the director and name the analyst as the dashboard's designer, then let the analyst know their work was praised at the meeting.
- C) Say nothing in the meeting but tell the analyst privately that the dashboard went down well.
- D) Suggest the director email the analyst directly.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This is a low-stakes-looking scenario that SJTs use to sample collaboration, integrity and people development instincts — and the differences between options are sharper than they first appear. Option B costs one sentence and delivers three benefits: the record is accurate (senior audiences form views about who can do what — visibility is a career resource, and letting credit silently pool with you or a vague "office" misallocates it); the analyst hears both the praise and the fact that you passed it on, which builds exactly the trust that makes people bring you their best work; and the director learns the office develops capable people. Option A is the quiet integrity leak — nothing dishonest was said, but an inaccurate impression was left standing because correcting it felt unnecessary. Option C delivers the private benefit but wastes the public one, and the analyst may later learn their absence from the story. Option D outsources what is yours to do in the moment. The general behaviour: pass credit down and pass problems up — the portfolio manager who reliably does both becomes the one talented people want to work for, which is a delivery asset as real as any budget.

**Question 4 (moderate) — Pressure to pre-announce a decision**

A programme director, frustrated by prioritisation delays, tells you: "Just confirm to my supplier that the expansion is approved — the board will obviously wave it through next week, and we lose two weeks of mobilisation if we wait." You agree the approval is very likely. What is the most effective response?

- A) Confirm to the supplier — the probability is high and the delivery benefit is real.
- B) Refuse and report the director's request to the board as attempted interference.
- C) Decline to pre-announce a decision the board has not made, explain why, and offer what you can legitimately do: check whether any no-regrets mobilisation steps can proceed at the programme's own risk, and see whether the decision can be expedited — by an earlier paper, chair's action, or out-of-committee approval.
- D) Tell the director to contact the board chair himself and stay out of it.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The core principle: governance decisions exist only when made — "obviously will approve" is a forecast, not an approval, and a portfolio office that pre-announces likely decisions has replaced governance with prediction. If the board attaches conditions, defers, or (rarely but really) declines, a supplier already mobilised on your confirmation creates commercial exposure and an accountability mess with your name in it. But option C's strength is that it pairs the refusal with genuine help, which is what separates a portfolio manager from a gatekeeper: no-regrets preparatory steps at the programme's own risk are often legitimate; expediting mechanisms (chair's action, out-of-committee decisions) exist precisely for cases where decision latency destroys value; and the two-week mobilisation cost is a fact worth putting to the board secretariat. The director's frustration is partially legitimate — treat it as a signal about decision latency, possibly worth raising as a process improvement. Option A trades the integrity of the entire approval process for two weeks. Option B converts a pressured colleague into an adversary and a routine boundary moment into an incident — escalation is for patterns and refusals-to-accept-no, not first asks. Option D abdicates: routing and brokering exactly such requests is your job. Hold the line, then help within it.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Discovering an error with money attached**

Reconciling quarterly figures, you discover the portfolio office — you, specifically, two months ago — misallocated £150,000 of spend between two projects, making Project Trent look overspent and Project Wharfe underspent. Trent's team was asked awkward questions at the last board because of it. Nobody else has noticed. What is the most effective response?

- A) Correct the allocation in this quarter's figures without comment; the totals were always right.
- B) Correct it, tell your senior portfolio manager what happened, ensure the correction and its cause are noted to the board and Trent's team, and check whether the reconciliation gap that let it through needs fixing.
- C) Correct it and apologise privately to Trent's delivery manager, keeping it otherwise informal.
- D) Leave it — the totals are right, the error will wash out over the year, and reopening it invites blame.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Test each option against three duties: accuracy, fairness to those affected, and system integrity. Option B is the only one meeting all three. Accuracy: the correction is made at once. Fairness: Trent's team took public criticism caused by your error — the correction must be as visible as the harm, which means the board and the team hear it, not just the ledger; this is what restores their standing and models the honesty you ask of them in their own reporting. System: a £150,000 misallocation surviving two months means the reconciliation control failed — finding and fixing that gap converts your mistake into a portfolio improvement, which is the strongest possible ending to an error story. Option A fixes the numbers and abandons the people; "totals were always right" is exactly the rationalisation that lets allocation errors persist — boards make project-level decisions on project-level figures. Option C is warmer but leaves the formal record wrong at the level where the damage happened. Option D compounds error with concealment and gambles your credibility on non-discovery. The meta-lesson SJTs reward consistently: own errors fast, correct them at the visibility level of their impact, and mine them for systemic fixes. Managers who model this get honest portfolios; managers who bury errors get surprises.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Least effective: a slipping flagship**

The portfolio's most visible project — personally sponsored by the director general — is quietly slipping: two milestones missed, a third at risk, delivery manager still reporting green. You have raised it with her informally once; she said the team "will catch up". Which response is LEAST effective?

- A) Review the milestone evidence with her, agree what the data supports, and explain that the portfolio's report must reflect the evidenced position, with her recovery narrative alongside.
- B) Rate the project amber in the portfolio-level view yourself, clearly noting the office's assessment differs from the team's own rating, and tell her you are doing so and why.
- C) Wait one more reporting cycle to see whether the catch-up materialises, keeping notes.
- D) Alert your senior portfolio manager that the flagship's self-reporting and the milestone evidence have diverged, before the gap widens further.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Read the stem carefully — you are hunting the worst option among four, three of which are defensible. Option A is the collegial first move: evidence-based challenge with the delivery manager, preserving her voice in the narrative. Option B is legitimate portfolio practice when self-reporting and evidence diverge: portfolio-level assessments may differ from team ratings, provided the divergence is transparent and communicated — that is assurance doing its job. Option D is proper upward flagging on a high-visibility item; senior portfolio managers should never learn of a flagship's slippage after it becomes unmissable, and early warning is precisely what your role owes them. Option C is the least effective: it converts known, evidenced divergence into deliberate delay on the portfolio's most scrutinised project. "Waiting one more cycle" on a flagship means one more month of a green rating you already know the evidence contradicts, reaching a director general who is personally invested — with your notes documenting that you knew. Optimism bias plus visibility plus sponsorship pressure is exactly the cocktail that produces the delivery failures every government project review warns about, and the portfolio office exists to interrupt that cycle, not to join it politely. Watchful waiting has its place — verified early-stage wobbles, agreed recovery windows — but not evidence-versus-reporting gaps on flagships.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Two stakeholders, one truth**

The finance business partner tells you project spend forecasts must be "prudent — plan on the high side". The delivery director tells delivery managers forecasts must be "ambitious — stretch targets drive pace". Delivery managers, caught between them, ask you which instruction to follow in next month's portfolio return. What is the most effective response?

- A) Tell them to follow finance's instruction — money outranks pace.
- B) Tell them to follow the delivery director's — delivery outcomes are what matter.
- C) Tell them to submit their honest central estimate this month, and get finance and the delivery director together to agree a single forecasting convention — likely a central estimate with explicit ranges or scenarios serving both needs — then have it documented in the return guidance.
- D) Tell them to submit prudent numbers to finance and ambitious ones to the delivery director.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Diagnose before adjudicating: this is not actually a seniority contest but a requirements conflict — finance needs downside protection, the director wants motivational stretch, and both are legitimate purposes badly served by a single unqualified number. Option C does three right things in order. Immediately, it protects the data: an honest central estimate is the one figure that is defensibly true while the convention is settled — teams should never be left guessing, because guessing produces inconsistent, unauditable returns. Then it convenes the principals rather than choosing between them: forecasting conventions are exactly the kind of standard a portfolio office brokers, and the standard solution (central estimate plus explicit ranges, confidence levels or scenarios) genuinely serves both masters — prudence lives in the range's upper bound, ambition in the stretch scenario, honesty in the centre. Finally it documents the outcome in guidance, so the conflict dies rather than recurring each cycle. Options A and B resolve by rank, leaving the losing stakeholder's real need unmet and re-fighting the battle monthly. Option D is the catastrophic one: two sets of books, institutionalised — when the figures are eventually compared (they always are), every forecast the portfolio has ever issued becomes suspect. Building consensus between stakeholders with conflicting needs, using a mechanism both can live with, is stakeholder relationship management at portfolio level.

**Question 8 (moderate) — An informal heads-up with strings attached**

A delivery manager takes you aside: "Off the record — we've found a data quality problem that might mean re-running three months of migrated records. I'm telling you as a friend, not reporting it. Give us two weeks to size it before anything goes in a report." The project is mid-migration; other projects consume its data downstream. What is the most effective response?

- A) Honour the confidence fully — informal trust channels are precious, and two weeks is reasonable.
- B) Thank him for the early warning, but explain this is material — especially with downstream consumers — so it cannot stay off the record: agree what gets said now (an emerging data quality issue, being sized, report in two weeks), ensure downstream projects are alerted to a possible impact, and support his team in the sizing.
- C) Refuse the conversation — "there is no off the record with the portfolio office" — and log it immediately as a red risk.
- D) Keep it informal but secretly warn the downstream projects yourself.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The tension is real and the test is whether you can hold both goods at once: informal early-warning channels are genuinely precious (delivery managers who trust you tell you things months before reports do), and material information affecting other projects cannot be suppressed by a friendship framing. Option B threads it: it honours the spirit of the confidence (credit the early warning, support the sizing, no ambush) while being honest that materiality — not friendship — determines reportability, and a possible three-month re-run with downstream consumers is material now, in its properly caveated emerging form: "issue identified, scale being assessed". Downstream teams get warned while their mitigation options are still cheap. Crucially, done this way, the channel survives: the delivery manager learns you handle early news fairly — early disclosure is rewarded with support and accurate framing, not punishment — which is precisely what keeps such channels open. Option A makes you complicit in a two-week information gap the downstream projects will rightly resent — "when did the portfolio office know?" is a question with a bad answer. Option C protects the record and torches the relationship; delivery managers simply stop telling that portfolio office anything early, which is a worse assurance outcome than any single late report. Option D is the worst construction: secret disclosure of a confidence you claimed to keep — dishonest in both directions at once. Transparency about what you must do with information, before people hand it to you, is what makes informal trust and formal duty compatible.

**Question 9 (hard) — Governance shortcut under deadline**

A statutory deadline means Project Ribble must award its implementation contract within three weeks. The spend approval process normally takes four. The programme director proposes splitting the contract into two smaller awards, each below the approval threshold, "purely as a timing fix — we'll consolidate later". What is the most effective response?

- A) Agree — the statutory deadline creates genuine urgency, and the rules allow below-threshold awards.
- B) Refuse, and report the proposal to the finance director as attempted circumvention.
- C) Explain that threshold-splitting is explicitly prohibited regardless of motive, and immediately pursue the legitimate fast route: an expedited approval on statutory-deadline grounds — most spend control frameworks provide one — escalating today to your senior portfolio manager and the approvals team to compress the timeline.
- D) Suggest the project proceed at risk and regularise the approval retrospectively.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Two tests are embedded here: do you recognise the proposal as prohibited, and do you know the legitimate alternative? Threshold-splitting is expressly banned in procurement and spend control regimes precisely because every splitter has a sympathetic reason — the prohibition is motive-blind, and "consolidate later" is an admission that the requirement is really one contract. So option A fails on rules, and worse, exposes the project to procurement challenge that could cost far more than the four weeks. But bare refusal is only half a portfolio manager's job: the statutory deadline is a genuine, legitimate pressure, and frameworks anticipate exactly this collision — expedited and fast-track approval routes for statutory grounds exist (the verbal section's spend control passage described one). Option C refuses the prohibited path and mobilises the permitted one the same day, which is what "helping teams follow governance processes" means when the process seems to be the obstacle: the process usually has a door for this; find it fast. Option B escalates punitively over what is, so far, a proposal openly floated to you — answer it, redirect it, and reserve reporting for actual circumvention or insistence. Option D replaces one control failure with another; retrospective approval of an awarded contract is approval theatre. The strongest candidates in governance SJTs consistently show this pattern: hold the rule absolutely, treat the underlying need as legitimate, and know the compliant fast path well enough to offer it in the same breath as the refusal.

**Question 10 (hard) — Inclusion under delivery pressure**

You facilitate the portfolio's fortnightly prioritisation forum. You notice a pattern: the two most senior delivery managers dominate discussion, decisions consistently favour their projects, and two quieter delivery managers — both newer, one of whom raised a concern last month that was talked over and later proved right — have stopped contributing. The forum's decisions are formally sound and nobody has complained. What is the most effective response?

- A) Do nothing — decisions are sound, attendance is full, and adults manage their own airtime.
- B) Redesign the forum's format to structurally rebalance contribution — pre-circulated written cases from every project, timed slots, round-robin views before open discussion, and your active chairing to bring in quieter members — and speak to the two quieter managers beforehand to rebuild their confidence that contributions land.
- C) Publicly call out the two dominant managers at the next forum for excluding colleagues.
- D) Privately tell the quieter managers to be more assertive, as senior forums reward those who speak up.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** First, reject the premise in option A that no complaint means no problem: the forum has already produced a concrete failure — a correct concern was talked over, meaning the decision process suppressed information it needed — and systematically skewed allocation plus silenced members is a governance defect wearing polite clothes. The scored behaviours here are inclusion and forum stewardship, and option B addresses both at the structural level, which is where facilitators actually have power. Process changes — written cases before discussion (which advantages evidence over airtime), round-robin input before the confident voices frame the debate, timed slots, active chairing — rebalance participation without accusing anyone, and they improve decision quality for exactly the reason the talked-over concern proves: the forum's job is to hear all the evidence, and dominance patterns filter evidence by confidence rather than correctness. The pre-conversations with the quieter managers matter because withdrawal is self-reinforcing; a facilitator's explicit signal that their input is wanted, plus a format that protects it, is what reverses the spiral. Option C treats a structural problem as individual misconduct, humiliates two people whose behaviour the old format invited, and poisons the forum you have to keep chairing. Option D locates the fault in the excluded — "be more like the dominators" — which both misdiagnoses and quietly endorses the dynamic. Facilitators own their forum's dynamics: when participation skews, redesign the structure, don't referee personalities.

**Question 11 (hard) — Rank the responses: conflicting crises**

Thursday 15:00. Three things hit at once: (1) a delivery manager calls — a supplier has just given notice of insolvency proceedings, affecting two projects' contracts; (2) the board secretariat emails — the pack for Monday's board is due at 17:00 and your portfolio summary still needs its final figures; (3) a director's office asks you to join a 15:30 call about a media query on a project's costs. Rank these responses from most to least effective:

1. Take the 15:30 call briefly to triage the media query's factual needs, delegate the summary figures to your portfolio analyst with your final check at 16:30, and between and after those, start the insolvency response: alert your senior portfolio manager and commercial colleagues today, and get the two projects assessing exposure.
2. Decline the call, finish the board pack yourself first, then turn to the insolvency; brief your senior portfolio manager on the supplier issue tomorrow morning.
3. Spend the afternoon entirely on the insolvency as the biggest risk, letting the pack slip to Friday with an apology and asking the director's office to send questions by email.
4. Join the call, then spend the rest of the day on the media query in case it escalates, fitting the other two in tomorrow.

- A) 1, 3, 2, 4
- B) 1, 2, 3, 4
- C) 3, 1, 2, 4
- D) 1, 3, 4, 2

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Evaluate each response's handling of all three demands. Response 1 is clearly best: it parallelises through delegation (the analyst drafts figures, you retain the quality gate — delegation with verification, not abdication), gives the media call exactly the triage it needs (directors' media queries usually need facts fast, not your afternoon), and starts the insolvency response today — the one item where hours genuinely matter, since commercial options (payment holds, contract protections, contingency triggers) are time-sensitive and your senior portfolio manager must not learn of a supplier insolvency late. Now the harder discrimination, second place: response 3 versus response 2. Response 3 gets the prioritisation right — insolvency is indeed the biggest risk — and communicates its trade-offs honestly (apology to secretariat, email route for the query); its flaw is surrendering the pack deadline that response 1 proves was achievable by delegation. Response 2 gets a deliverable out but inverts the risk ordering: hand-finishing routine figures personally while a supplier insolvency sits unattended overnight, and briefing your senior manager "tomorrow" on a same-day-material event, is the professional error of comfort-zone prioritisation — doing the familiar urgent thing over the alarming important one. So 3 ranks above 2. Response 4 is worst: it allocates the whole afternoon to the possibility of escalation while two actual crises wait. Order: 1, 3, 2, 4 — option A. The ranking logic to internalise: parallelise where you have people, triage where a fraction of your time suffices, and give your irreplaceable hours to the item where delay is irreversible.

**Question 12 (hard) — Most and least effective: your improvement, their resistance**

You have designed an improved portfolio dashboard that corrects a long-standing distortion: the old view flattered projects that re-baselined frequently, because slippage was measured only against the latest baseline. The new view shows slippage against original baselines too. Two delivery managers whose projects look markedly worse under the new view lobby your senior portfolio manager to block it, calling it "unfair to teams that re-planned responsibly". Which is the MOST effective response, and which the LEAST?

Responses:

1. Withdraw the original-baseline view — stakeholder acceptance matters more than analytical purity.
2. Brief your senior portfolio manager proactively with the rationale and evidence; engage the two delivery managers directly on the legitimate kernel of their objection — some re-baselines are responsible — and refine the view to distinguish approved re-baselines from drift, presenting both baselines with context rather than dropping either.
3. Implement unchanged and let the data speak for itself; lobbying against transparency is self-indicting.
4. Delay the dashboard indefinitely while a working group studies measurement methodology.

- A) Most effective: 2; least effective: 1
- B) Most effective: 3; least effective: 4
- C) Most effective: 2; least effective: 4
- D) Most effective: 3; least effective: 1

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Start with the most effective. Response 2 wins because it does four things the situation actually requires: it equips your senior portfolio manager before the lobbying lands (never let your manager be ambushed about your own work); it takes the objection seriously enough to find its legitimate kernel — and there genuinely is one, since a governance-approved re-baseline after a scope change is categorically different from quiet serial slippage, and a measure that conflates them really is somewhat unfair; it improves the artefact by distinguishing the two cases, making the dashboard analytically stronger, not just more acceptable; and it keeps both baselines visible, preserving the transparency that was the whole point. That is influencing through evidence and consensus-building — resistance treated as free design review. Response 3 is the interesting wrong answer: its transparency instinct is sound, but "let the data speak" ignores that dashboards need stakeholder legitimacy to drive decisions, dismisses a partly valid objection unexamined, and leaves your senior manager undefended against lobbying you knew was coming — righteous, and less effective than engagement. Now least effective, and the discrimination the question really tests: response 4 (indefinite delay via working group) is classic improvement-killing, but it at least preserves the possibility of a better measure eventually. Response 1 is worse: it permanently restores a known distortion — a measure that rewards re-baselining and hides slippage — because the measured objected, which teaches every team that lobbying beats evidence and makes the portfolio's core delivery picture durably misleading. Capitulation that entrenches a known distortion outranks delay as the failure mode. Most 2, least 1: option A.

### Preparation tips

- **Anchor on the behaviours, not on cleverness.** SJTs score against frameworks — honesty, ownership, collaboration, proportionate escalation, inclusion. Before the test, reread the Civil Service Behaviours for your grade and your organisation's values; the highest-scoring options express them under pressure.
- **Learn the recurring portfolio patterns.** Across these twelve questions, a few templates repeat: verify before reporting; refuse the prohibited path but open the legitimate one; fix the instance and the system; escalate early on materiality, not on drama; challenge colleagues directly before going over their heads; keep both the relationship and the record. Most portfolio SJT items are one of these in costume.
- **Practise the "both goods" reframe.** The hardest scenarios pit two legitimate values against each other — trust versus transparency, pace versus process, acceptance versus accuracy. Train yourself to ask "what response serves both?" before accepting the trade-off as forced; the best option usually dissolves the dilemma rather than picking a side.
- **Rehearse least-effective and ranking formats.** They punish careless reading and reward comparative judgement. In practice, articulate why each option beats or loses to its neighbours — the discipline of ordering is different from the discipline of choosing.
- **Mine your own history.** List five genuinely awkward moments from your portfolio work — the softened report you were asked for, the shortcut proposed under deadline — and write down what the best-self response would have been. Lived anchors make test judgements fast and consistent.
- **Watch for the "comfortable option" trap.** In nearly every scenario, one option lets everyone stay comfortable now at the price of accuracy or fairness later. It is nearly always wrong, and recognising its signature — quiet fixes, waiting cycles, off-the-record accommodations — is half the test.
- **Answer as your consistent professional self.** Some instruments measure consistency across scenarios. Answer as the portfolio manager you are on your best day — honest, enabling, proportionate — not as an imagined perfect operator or a defensive minimalist.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Trading accuracy for comfort.** Softening ratings, sitting on divergence "one more cycle", pre-announcing likely approvals — every version of making things pleasant now by mortgaging the record scores badly, because it is the exact failure portfolio governance exists to prevent.
- **Escalating as a first resort — or a last one.** Reporting a colleague's first pressured request as misconduct destroys channels; briefing your manager "tomorrow" about a supplier insolvency destroys trust. Calibrate: materiality and time-sensitivity drive escalation speed, not annoyance or fear.
- **Refusing without helping.** "No, that's against the process" is half an answer. The portfolio manager's full answer names the legitimate route and mobilises it. Gatekeeping without wayfinding reads as obstruction — and scores like it.
- **Resolving principals' conflicts by rank.** When two senior stakeholders issue contradictory instructions, picking the senior one leaves the requirements conflict alive. Convene, design a mechanism that serves both needs, document it.
- **Treating structural problems as personal ones.** Dominated forums, chronic late returns, gamed metrics — the strong answers redesign formats, templates and measures; the weak ones lecture individuals about behaviour the structure invites.
- **Absorbing problems personally.** Doing teams' returns for them, hand-finishing everything yourself, honouring confidences you cannot keep — self-sacrifice that hides systemic issues is a scored failure mode, however generous it feels.
- **Misreading the question format.** Most-effective, least-effective, rank-all, rate-each: confirm which you are answering before judging. More SJT marks die to format misreads than to bad judgement.

## Conclusion

Congratulations — you have worked through a complete, role-specific set of psychometric practice materials built around the realities of digital portfolio management in government. You have reasoned through RAG histories and dependency chains, reconciled registers against reports, calculated the weighted averages and run-rate forecasts that underpin credible dashboards, read governance and policy extracts with the precision that spend controls demand, and navigated the situational dilemmas that define the role: unverified good news before a board, informal confidences with strings attached, governance shortcuts under statutory deadlines, and improvements that met resistance.

If one theme connects all four sections, it is this: your role's value is trustworthy information plus sound judgement, delivered at pace. The cognitive section trained the checking and deduction that keep portfolio data internally consistent. The numeric section trained the small toolkit — right base, right weighting, totals over totals — that keeps your figures unimpeachable. The verbal section trained the word-level precision that keeps your readings of rules and updates faithful, and your own writing plain. The situational judgement section trained the behaviours that keep all of it honest under pressure. These are not four separate test skills; they are the four faces of the same professional craft, and every hour you spent practising them was an hour spent becoming a better portfolio manager — helping teams deliver, helping stakeholders understand performance and risk, and supporting your senior colleagues with analysis they never need to re-check.

If an assessment is imminent, keep your final preparation simple. Revisit the questions you got wrong and rehearse the corrected method until you can narrate it. Practise one timed set per section in the final week, then stop — rest beats cramming, reliably. On the day: quiet room, rough paper, the ungraded warm-ups taken seriously, the clock respected but not feared, and the knowledge that adaptive difficulty rising is a sign of progress, not trouble. Afterwards, request whatever feedback report the platform offers; it is a free, objective snapshot of your reasoning profile, and worth ten self-assessments.

If no test is scheduled, use this guide as a working development tool. The habits it drills — reconcile before publishing, name the base of every percentage, quote the modal verbs, refuse the shortcut but open the legitimate route — are the daily disciplines of portfolio offices that leadership trusts. Revisit a section each month; write your own questions from your own portfolio's artefacts, which is the deepest form of practice there is; and share the techniques with your analysts and delivery managers, because a portfolio whose whole community checks totals and reads conditionals carefully is a portfolio that delivers fewer surprises.

You have invested real effort in skills that compound for an entire career. Trust that investment, keep iterating on it, and walk into your assessment — and your next board meeting — with the confidence of someone who has done the work. Good luck. You are readier than you think.
