# Associate Content Designer - Psychometric Assessment Resources

## Introduction

Welcome, and congratulations on beginning your career as an associate content designer in the UK Government Digital and Data profession. You might be here because an assessment is coming up, because you are thinking about your next step, or simply because you are curious how psychometric tests relate to the work you are learning to do. Whatever brought you, this guide was written with someone at the very start of a content design career firmly in mind.

Let us take the fear out of these assessments first, because they make almost everyone nervous. Psychometric assessments are not intelligence tests. They are not designed to catch you out, and they are not a verdict on your talent. They are structured, standardised exercises that measure the everyday thinking a particular job actually uses. For you that means checking a draft against a style guide and spotting where it diverges, noticing that a page tells users what happened but not what to do next, reading research findings and working out what they mean for a piece of content, interpreting a set of numbers about a page, and making sensible decisions when a stakeholder asks for something you are not sure about. None of that is exotic. It is the ordinary, careful thinking that good content design rests on.

Your role level shapes this whole guide. As an associate content designer you are a trainee in an entry-level position, working under supervision, expected to show aptitude, potential and an understanding of the role, and to perform basic standard checks and copy edits. Nobody assessing you at this level expects you to have redesigned an end-to-end journey or negotiated with a policy team over a legal phrase. What they are looking for is exactly what your role description names: aptitude, potential, understanding, and careful checking. Every rule, definition and figure you need appears inside the question — you never have to have memorised anything.

There is one thing worth saying about content design specifically. Your discipline is often mistaken for writing, and it is not — or not only. It is about making sure the right content appears in the right place in the right format, which means the decision to remove a paragraph, to move a sentence earlier, to turn prose into a list, or to answer a question the user has not yet asked is as much your work as the wording itself. A great deal of what these assessments measure is that judgement about what belongs where, applied to a table of numbers or a paragraph of policy prose rather than to a page.

The document is organised into four main sections:

1. A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment — pattern spotting, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation and problem solving, using style guides, drafts, content inventories and research findings.
2. A numeric reasoning assessment — percentages, proportions, averages and simple tables, using the numbers content teams actually handle, such as page views, search terms, contact volumes and completion rates.
3. A verbal reasoning assessment — reading passages like style standards, accessibility guidance and stakeholder emails, then answering true/false/cannot-say and comprehension questions.
4. A situational judgement assessment — realistic workplace dilemmas pitched at a trainee level, with guidance on the most and least effective responses and on why.

Each section has the same shape: what the assessment measures, how it maps to the named skills in your role, twelve practice questions with full explanations, preparation tips, and common pitfalls.

Here is how to get the most from it. Work slowly — there is no timer here. Decide your answer and write it down before reading the explanation, because an answer you have committed to teaches you far more than one you merely considered. Read the explanations even when you were right. And treat the questions you get wrong as the most useful part of the exercise.

A last thought. Being new is genuinely useful in content design. You have not yet absorbed the internal vocabulary that makes a page unreadable to the people it was written for, and you still notice when a sentence assumes knowledge nobody has. Hold on to that as you build the rigour this guide is about.

Grab a drink, find a quiet half hour, and let's begin.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the thinking your role genuinely demands, using practical workplace materials rather than abstract shapes and sequences. Instead of rotating cubes, you will work with what an associate content designer actually handles — a style guide, a draft page, a content inventory, a publishing checklist, a set of user research observations, a search log.

The typical format is an online test lasting fifteen to thirty minutes. Questions cluster into five families: pattern recognition, logical deduction, error checking, prioritisation, and applied problem solving. Scoring is objective, comparing your answers against a norm group at a similar level. Two features of modern platforms are worth knowing. Many tests are adaptive, so questions get harder after correct answers — if the test suddenly feels difficult, that usually means you are doing well. And most employers receive an acuity report showing speed against accuracy rather than a single score, which means careful work is rewarded and rushing is not.

For an associate content designer this section matters because so much of your early value comes from noticing. You notice that a heading is a statement where the style guide requires a question. You notice that two pages in the same service use different words for the same thing. You notice that a page tells users their application has been received but not what happens next or when. You notice that a sentence uses a term no member of the public would search for. Each of those is a small act of comparison or deduction, and each protects the quality of what users eventually read.

One reassurance before the questions. At your level, employers are looking for aptitude and potential, not encyclopaedic knowledge. You will never be asked to recall a real style guide rule from memory. Every rule you need is stated in the question. Your job is to apply stated rules carefully — which is exactly what you do at work when a senior content designer gives you direction and you carry it out.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The cognitive dimensions map onto the skills named in your role summary:

- **Pattern recognition** maps to **User-centred content design**, where showing awareness of style standards means recognising when content follows an established pattern and, more importantly, when it quietly departs from one.
- **Error checking** maps to **User-centred content design** again, and to the basic standard checks and copy edits your role level names explicitly. Before content is published, somebody must spot the inconsistency, the missed step, the term that will not be understood.
- **Logical deduction** maps to **User focus (content design)**, where seeing the purpose of user stories and focusing on user needs means reasoning correctly from evidence rather than jumping to conclusions the evidence does not support.
- **Prioritisation** maps to **Agile working (content design)**, where working in an Agile team means constantly deciding what to do first, adapting to feedback, and being transparent about why.
- **Applied problem solving** maps to **Content concepts and prototyping**, where you explore ideas about how content might work before committing to it.
- **Understanding what a request is really for** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management (content design)**, where knowing what your stakeholders find important is the foundation of a useful working relationship.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Error checking against a style guide**

Your style guide says page titles must: start with a verb, use sentence case with only proper nouns capitalised, contain no full stop, and be no longer than eight words. You review four titles:

1. Apply for a fishing licence
2. Renew Your Passport
3. Check if you need to pay tax on money you receive from abroad
4. How to report a lost driving licence.

How many titles break at least one rule?

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Check each title against all four rules. Title 1 starts with a verb, uses sentence case, has no full stop and is five words — compliant. Title 2 starts with a verb but capitalises "Your Passport", breaking sentence case. Title 3 starts with a verb and uses sentence case, but is thirteen words, breaking the length rule. Title 4 starts with "How" rather than a verb and ends with a full stop, breaking two rules. So three titles break at least one rule, giving C. Note the wording: the question asks how many *titles* break a rule, not how many *rules* are broken — there are four breaches across three titles, and confusing the two would lead you to D. In a timed test, resist stopping at the first fault you find in a title; you need to check every rule against every item to answer "how many" questions correctly.

**Question 2 (easy) — Logical deduction from a publishing rule**

Your team's rule states: "Content may only be published if it has been second-checked by another content designer, unless it is a factual correction approved by the content lead." A colleague says: "This page was published this morning and it was not second-checked." Which one must be true?

- A) The team broke the rule.
- B) The page was a factual correction approved by the content lead.
- C) Either the page was an approved factual correction, or the rule was broken.
- D) The page contains an error.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The rule is a conditional with one permitted exception, and the facts do not tell you whether the exception applies. Option A assumes it does not; option B assumes it does; you have evidence for neither. Option D introduces a claim nothing supports — an unchecked page is not automatically a wrong page, though it is more likely to be one. Only C is guaranteed in every version of events. Assessments reward this discipline of concluding only what the information guarantees, and so does the workplace: before suggesting a colleague skipped a step, the accurate and collegial move is to ask which route was used, because if the answer is "the lead approved it" you will be glad you asked rather than told.

**Question 3 (easy) — Pattern recognition across feedback**

Your team collects one-line feedback on five pages of the same service. The percentage of users answering "No" to "Did you find what you needed?" was: Page A 7%, Page B 8%, Page C 34%, Page D 9%, Page E 6%. All five pages have similar traffic. What is the most reasonable first interpretation of Page C?

- A) Page C is badly written and should be rewritten immediately.
- B) Page C is an outlier worth investigating — the cause could be the content, its position in the journey, a broken link, or something else entirely.
- C) The other four pages must be unusually good, so Page C is probably fine.
- D) The survey is unreliable and should be ignored.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Four pages cluster between 6% and 9%, and one sits at 34% — around four times the cluster. Recognising the outlier is pattern recognition; deciding what it means requires restraint. An outlier is a prompt for enquiry, not a conclusion. Page C might be confusing, but it might also be that users arrive expecting something the page does not offer, that a policy change made it out of date, or that a link on it is broken so people cannot get to what they need. Option A jumps to a solution before understanding the problem, and rewriting a page whose real fault is a broken link achieves nothing. Options C and D dodge the signal. At your level, the ideal move is to note the pattern, form a hypothesis, and raise it with your senior content designer.

**Question 4 (easy) — Prioritisation of a morning**

It is 9:30 am. Four things need attention:

1. A live page states the wrong deadline for a benefit application — users may act on it today.
2. Your stand-up is at 10:00 and you have not updated your ticket.
3. A colleague has asked you to review a draft this afternoon and wants you to read it first.
4. The weekly content community newsletter has arrived.

What should you do first?

- A) Update your ticket so the board is accurate.
- B) Raise or fix the wrong deadline on the live page.
- C) Read the colleague's draft while it is fresh.
- D) Read the newsletter before the day gets busy.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Prioritisation questions ask you to weigh impact and urgency together. Item 1 is both: a wrong deadline on a live page can cause real users to miss a benefit application today, which is a serious and irreversible harm to individual people. That outranks everything else. As an associate you may not fix it alone — the right first action might be to alert your senior content designer immediately — but either way it comes first. Item 2 is useful and takes two minutes, so it fits before stand-up but after you have raised the error. Item 3 is for the afternoon. Item 4 supports your engagement with the cross-government content community, which matters and is the least urgent thing here. The pattern to remember: harm to users outranks internal tidiness, and internal tidiness outranks nice-to-haves.

**Question 5 (easy) — Error checking against instructions**

A senior content designer asks you to make exactly three changes to a draft: (i) change "commence" to "start", (ii) change "in order to" to "to", and (iii) add a bullet list of the three required documents. You receive the updated draft from a peer and find: "start" replaces "commence", "to" replaces "in order to", the bullet list is present, and the page title has been changed from "Apply for a permit" to "Permit applications". How many of the changes made were requested?

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Count carefully. Four changes were made, but the question asks how many were *requested*. Changes (i), (ii) and (iii) were all requested and all done — three. The title change was not on the list, so it is an extra and, as it happens, one worth querying, because the new title no longer starts with a verb and would break the style rule from Question 1. The answer is 3. This mirrors an important habit: when you work after being given direction, you deliver exactly what was agreed and flag anything else rather than slipping it in. Unrequested changes are how inconsistencies creep into published content, and a title change in particular may break a link, a search result or a navigation label somewhere you cannot see.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Deduction about what content must say**

Your team's rule for confirmation pages says each must state: what has happened, what happens next, when it will happen, and how to get help. You review a page reading: "We have received your application. We will contact you." Which of the following is the most accurate assessment?

- A) The page meets all four requirements.
- B) The page states what has happened and what happens next, but not when or how to get help.
- C) The page states only what has happened.
- D) The page fails all four requirements.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Work through each requirement. "We have received your application" states what has happened, so that is met. "We will contact you" states what happens next, if minimally, so that is met too. There is no indication of when — "we will contact you" could mean tomorrow or in three months, and users have no way to know whether to wait or chase. There is no route to help. So two requirements are met and two are not, giving B. Option C is the tempting near-miss for anyone who judges "we will contact you" too vague to count, which is a defensible content judgement and the wrong answer to this question: the rule asks whether the page states what happens next, and it does. Worth noticing what the missing timescale costs — a confirmation page without one is the single most reliable generator of "have you received my application?" phone calls.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Pattern recognition in search data**

Search terms used on a service's own site search, over one month:

| Term | Searches |
|---|---|
| "how long does it take" | 1,850 |
| "contact" | 1,420 |
| "check my application" | 1,190 |
| "cancel" | 240 |
| "fee" | 180 |

What does this pattern most strongly suggest?

- A) The site search is working well.
- B) Users are looking for information about progress, timescales and how to reach someone — which suggests the service is not telling them these things where they expect to find them.
- C) The service should add a cancellation page.
- D) Users do not care about fees.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The top three terms — timescales, contact, and checking an application — account for around 4,460 of the 4,880 searches shown, and they are all versions of the same underlying need: "what is happening with my thing, and how do I find out?" Site search is where users go when they cannot find something on the page, so a concentration like this is evidence that the service is not answering these questions where people expect. Option B says this. Option A misreads search volume as success, when heavy search on a small service usually indicates the opposite. Option C over-reads a small figure. Option D is an unsupported inference: few searches for "fee" might mean the fee is clearly stated, which would be a success rather than a sign of indifference. This kind of reading is one of the most useful things a content designer can do with data that costs nothing to obtain.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Error checking against plain language rules**

Your team's standard says content must: use words users would use, explain any term the first time it appears, use the active voice, and address the user as "you". You review this sentence: "Applicants whose circumstances have altered must ensure that notification is submitted to the department within the prescribed period." How many parts of the standard does it break?

- A) 1
- B) 2
- C) 3
- D) 4

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Check each. "Applicants", "altered", "notification", "prescribed period" are not words users would use, so the first requirement fails. "Prescribed period" is a term used without explanation — and it is the most important information in the sentence, since the user has no idea how long they have — so the second fails. "Notification is submitted" is passive, so the third fails. The sentence says "applicants" rather than "you", so the fourth fails. All four are broken, giving D. A rewritten version might be: "If your circumstances change, tell us within 28 days." That is fifteen words shorter, tells the user what to do, and — crucially — supplies the deadline that the original concealed behind "the prescribed period", which is the difference between a sentence that informs and one that merely sounds official.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Applied problem solving in a review**

You are reviewing a page. It is well written, follows the style guide, and answers the question in its heading. But you notice that the question in the heading is not one users appear to ask — the search data and the contact centre both suggest people arrive wanting to know something else. You are the most junior person in the team. What is the most sensible thing to do?

- A) Approve the page; it meets the standard.
- B) Raise the observation with your senior content designer, describing what the search and contact data suggest users are actually asking, and ask whether the page is answering the right question.
- C) Rewrite the page around the question you think users are asking.
- D) Note it for the next content review in three months.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** This is the distinction between a page being well made and being the right page, and noticing it is a genuinely valuable contribution. Option B raises it through the right channel with the evidence attached, framed as a question rather than a verdict, which makes it easy for colleagues to engage with. Option A is the trap: a page can satisfy every rule in the style guide and still fail, because style compliance is about how something is written and this is about what it says. Option C acts well beyond your remit on a diagnosis you have not confirmed. Option D delays a five-minute conversation for a quarter. Your role names seeing the purpose of user stories and focusing on user needs, and this is that skill applied to something that would otherwise pass review unremarked.

**Question 10 (moderate) — Deduction about what evidence supports**

In research with nine participants, seven did not understand the phrase "relevant supporting evidence". Which conclusion is best supported by this evidence alone?

- A) 78% of users do not understand the phrase.
- B) The phrase should be deleted.
- C) Seven of nine participants did not understand the phrase, which is a strong signal that it needs replacing with wording that says specifically what documents are needed.
- D) All government content should avoid the word "evidence".

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Seven of nine is a strong qualitative signal, and C states it at that strength while pointing at the mechanism the evidence identified: participants did not know what the phrase referred to, which implies replacing it with something concrete rather than merely simplifying it. That distinction matters, because "supporting documents" is simpler and still vague, while "your payslips from the last three months" answers the actual question. Option A converts nine participants into a population rate, the commonest overreach in content reporting. Option B says delete without saying what replaces it, and the information may well be needed. Option D generalises from one phrase in one context to all government content, which no nine-person study could support. Framing ideas as testable propositions is part of your role, and C is what a well-framed one looks like.

**Question 11 (harder) — Prioritisation with competing pressures**

Your senior content designer is away. At 10:00 the following are true:

1. A developer needs your final wording for an error message before they can finish a story today.
2. The content review is at 14:00 and your page is not ready.
3. A policy colleague needs your confirmation of a legal phrase by 12:00 for a publication.
4. A colleague has asked you to review their draft this week.

What is the most sensible order?

- A) 2, 3, 1, 4 — your own commitments first.
- B) 1, 3, 2, 4 — unblock the developer, then the policy colleague, then your page, then the review.
- C) 3, 1, 2, 4 — deadlines in time order.
- D) 1, 2, 3, 4 — as listed.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Rank by consequence and deadline together. Item 1 blocks another person's work today and probably takes ten minutes; leaving a developer to invent an error message is how inconsistent, unhelpful wording gets built into a service permanently. Item 3 has a hard 12:00 deadline and also blocks somebody — and it carries a second consideration worth noticing: if you are being asked to confirm a legal phrase and you are a trainee, the honest answer may be "I am not the right person to confirm this", which you need to say by 12:00 rather than at 11:55. Item 2 is your own commitment, genuinely important, and within your control to absorb the earlier interruptions. Item 4 has a week. Option C is defensible and puts a 12:00 task ahead of an item blocking someone right now that takes minutes. Option A ranks by ownership rather than consequence.

**Question 12 (harder) — Applied problem solving about a difficult phrase**

A policy team insists a page must contain the exact phrase "you may be liable to a financial penalty", saying it is legally required. Research shows users do not understand what this means or what would trigger it. You are the most junior person involved. What is the strongest analysis?

- A) The phrase must stay, so nothing can be done.
- B) The phrase may need to appear, but nothing prevents the page also explaining in plain words what it means and when it applies — so the useful question is whether the requirement is that this wording appears, or that this wording is all that appears.
- C) The phrase should be replaced with plain language regardless.
- D) The page should link to the legislation.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The strongest analysis separates two things that get conflated: a requirement that certain wording appears, and a prohibition on saying anything else. Legal requirements are almost always the first and almost never the second, which means the apparent deadlock usually dissolves once somebody asks. A page can carry the exact phrase and immediately explain it — "you may be liable to a financial penalty. This means we can fine you up to £500 if you do not tell us within 28 days." Option B identifies that and frames it as a question you can actually ask, which is the right move for someone at your level. Option A accepts a constraint nobody has tested. Option C proposes overriding a legal requirement you are not positioned to override, and doing so would rightly be refused. Option D is a reasonable addition and does not help a user who cannot read legislation, which is most of them.

### Preparation tips

- **Practise on real content.** Take a page from your service and check it against the style guide. This is exactly the comparison muscle the assessment measures, and your team gets a free proofread.
- **Slow down to speed up.** Trainees lose more marks to rushing than to not knowing. Read each rule twice before applying it.
- **Write the rules down.** For deduction questions, jot each condition as a short line.
- **Decide what you are counting.** Titles, rules, participants, mentions — these are different numbers, and the question always specifies one.
- **Check every option.** These items usually include one nearly-right distractor built from a specific error.
- **Ask what a page is for, not only whether it is well written.** Style compliance and answering the right question are different things.
- **Do a timed drill.** Ten minutes, four questions. You are practising composure, not speed.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Stopping at the first fault.** "How many" questions need a full count.
- **Confusing a count of items with a count of breaches.**
- **Comparing raw counts across pages with different traffic.** Convert to rates first.
- **Confusing "could be true" with "must be true".** Deduction rewards only conclusions guaranteed in every case.
- **Ranking by ownership rather than consequence.** Your own deadlines feel most urgent because they are yours.
- **Approving a page because it meets the style guide.** A well-written answer to the wrong question is still wrong.
- **Accepting a constraint nobody has tested.** "It's legally required" usually means the wording must appear, not that nothing else may.
- **Panicking when questions get harder.** On adaptive tests, rising difficulty usually means you are doing well.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A numeric reasoning assessment measures how well you work with the numbers your job produces. This is the section people worry about most, so let us be clear: it is not a maths exam. There are no equations to solve and no formulae to memorise. Most platforms provide a calculator, and where they do not the arithmetic is deliberately simple. What is measured is whether you can read a table correctly, pick the right numbers out of a cluttered display, choose the right operation, and interpret the result sensibly.

The format is usually a timed online test of fifteen to thirty minutes, each item showing a small table, chart or paragraph of figures followed by a multiple-choice question. Time per item is tight, often around a minute, so the real skill is finding the relevant numbers quickly and ignoring the rest. Test designers routinely include figures you do not need.

Some content designers arrive here convinced they are "not a numbers person", and often chose the discipline partly for that reason. That belief is worth challenging, because content design runs on numbers more than it looks. You read page views to know what people actually reach. You read search terms to know what they cannot find. You read contact centre volumes to know what your page failed to explain. You compare feedback scores across pages. You check whether a change made any difference. None of that requires advanced mathematics, and all of it requires numerical care, because a misread figure sends a team to rewrite the wrong page.

At associate level the content is deliberately modest: percentages, fractions, proportions, simple averages, ratios, and reading values from tables. The techniques are equally modest. Work out what the question is actually asking. Identify the two or three numbers you need. Do the arithmetic one clear step at a time. Then sanity-check — if you have calculated that 34 out of 200 is 43%, a two-second check catches it.

A last note about the options. Wrong answers are almost never random. They are the results of specific, predictable mistakes: dividing by the wrong total, confusing a percentage with a percentage point, using the new figure instead of the old one. Finding your answer among the options is not proof that you are right.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The numerical skills tested map onto your named skills:

- **Reading tables accurately** maps to **User focus (content design)**, where gaining insights from data is part of understanding what users need. Page analytics, search logs and feedback all arrive as tables.
- **Percentages and proportions** map to **User focus** and to **User-centred content design**, because comparing how pages perform is how you decide which content to fix first.
- **Measuring change** maps to **Agile working (content design)**, where adapting and being responsive to feedback means knowing whether a change helped.
- **Understanding scale** maps to **Strategic thinking (content design)**, where knowing how many people a piece of content affects is what makes a case for spending time on it.
- **Interpreting figures cautiously** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management (content design)**, where a claim built on a misread number is hard to defend twice.
- **Spotting an implausible figure** maps to **Content concepts and prototyping**, where testing an idea starts with trusting the right data.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — Feedback rate**

Of 2,400 users who saw a feedback prompt on a page, 600 answered "No" to "Did you find what you needed?". What percentage answered No?

- A) 20%
- B) 25%
- C) 30%
- D) 40%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 600 ÷ 2,400 = 0.25, so 25%, and the answer is B. Estimate first as insurance — 600 of 2,400 is a quarter, since 1,200 would be half. Options A and C come from dividing by 3,000 and 2,000, the kind of slip a mis-keyed calculator produces. It is worth noting what this figure is and is not: it is the percentage of people who *answered the prompt*, not of everyone who saw the page, and feedback prompts are answered disproportionately by people with a strong reaction. That does not make the figure useless; it makes it a signal to compare against other pages rather than a population measure.

**Question 2 (easy) — Comparing pages fairly**

| Page | Views | "No" responses |
|---|---|---|
| A | 8,000 | 400 |
| B | 2,000 | 300 |
| C | 5,000 | 250 |

Which page has the highest rate of "No" responses?

- A) Page A
- B) Page B
- C) Page C
- D) They are equal

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Convert to rates before comparing, because the pages have very different traffic. Page A: 400 ÷ 8,000 = 5%. Page B: 300 ÷ 2,000 = 15%. Page C: 250 ÷ 5,000 = 5%. Page B is three times worse than the others, so B. The raw counts would have told you the opposite story — Page A has the most "No" responses in absolute terms — which is precisely the trap. Both readings matter and they answer different questions: Page A disappoints more people in total, and Page B disappoints a much higher proportion of the people who reach it. If you can only fix one, the answer depends on whether you are reducing total harm or fixing the page that is most clearly broken, and saying so is more useful than picking one silently.

**Question 3 (easy) — Reading a search log**

A month of site searches shows: "how long" 900, "contact us" 750, "cancel" 150, "fee" 120, "appeal" 80. What percentage of these searches are for "how long"?

- A) 35%
- B) 40%
- C) 45%
- D) 50%

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** First find the total: 900 + 750 = 1,650, plus 150 = 1,800, plus 120 = 1,920, plus 80 = 2,000. Then 900 ÷ 2,000 = 0.45, which is 45%, giving C. The step people skip is calculating the total, because the question gives you a list rather than a total and it is tempting to estimate. Note also what the finding means: nearly half of everyone who searches this site is asking how long something takes, which is a strong indication that a timescale is missing from a page where users expect it — and that is a content fix costing one sentence.

**Question 4 (easy) — Average reading time**

Five pages have average reading times of 40, 55, 45, 60 and 50 seconds. What is the mean?

- A) 48 seconds
- B) 50 seconds
- C) 52 seconds
- D) 55 seconds

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Add: 40 + 55 = 95, plus 45 = 140, plus 60 = 200, plus 50 = 250. Divide by 5: 250 ÷ 5 = 50 seconds, so B. A useful shortcut for numbers clustered around a round figure is to work from a guess: try 50, and the differences are −10, +5, −5, +10, 0, which sum to zero, confirming the mean is exactly 50. That uses much smaller numbers than long addition, which matters when a single slip changes your answer.

**Question 5 (easy) — Scale of a content fix**

A page is viewed 500,000 times a year. A missing timescale on it generates a phone call from an estimated 2% of viewers. How many calls a year would adding the timescale potentially avoid?

- A) 1,000
- B) 10,000
- C) 12,000
- D) 100,000

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** 2% of 500,000 is 0.02 × 500,000 = 10,000, giving B. Option A is 2% of 50,000; option D is 20%. The reason this calculation belongs in a content design guide is what it does to a conversation. "Two per cent of viewers ring up" sounds like a rounding error and will not get a sentence prioritised. "Ten thousand phone calls a year, which one sentence would prevent" gets it done this sprint, and it also lets somebody put a cost against it — contact centre calls have a known unit cost in most organisations, so the same arithmetic converts your sentence into a number a finance colleague recognises.

**Question 6 (moderate) — Did the change work?**

Before a rewrite, 480 of 1,200 users who saw a feedback prompt answered "No". After, 350 of 1,400 answered "No". Did the page improve, and by how much in percentage points?

- A) Yes, by 130 responses
- B) Yes, by 15 percentage points
- C) Yes, by 25 percentage points
- D) No, it got worse

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Convert to rates, because the totals differ. Before: 480 ÷ 1,200 = 40%. After: 350 ÷ 1,400 = 25%. The improvement is 15 percentage points, giving B. Option A compares raw counts across different-sized groups, which is meaningless here — fewer people answered "No" partly because... actually, more people saw the prompt after the change, so the fall in raw count is if anything an understatement of the improvement. That is worth noticing: raw counts can understate as well as overstate, depending on which direction traffic moved, which is exactly why converting to rates is not optional. This is the comparison your role means by adapting and being responsive to feedback.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Percentage change**

Views of a guidance page fell from 40,000 to 30,000 a month after a service change. What is the percentage decrease?

- A) 10%
- B) 25%
- C) 33%
- D) 75%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Percentage change divides the difference by the original value. The difference is 40,000 − 30,000 = 10,000, and 10,000 ÷ 40,000 = 0.25, a 25% decrease, giving B. Option C is 10,000 ÷ 30,000, dividing by the new figure — the most common error in this question type. Option D is the new figure as a proportion of the old, a true statement answering a different question. The rule: for percentage change, the denominator is always where you started from. Note that a fall in views is not automatically bad news for content — if the service change removed a need for the guidance, fewer views is a success — which is a reminder that a number alone rarely tells you whether something improved.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Working back to a total**

A report says 12% of users abandon a form at the evidence question, and that this is 3,600 people a month. How many reach the evidence question each month?

- A) 4,032
- B) 15,000
- C) 30,000
- D) 43,200

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** If 12% equals 3,600, the total is 3,600 ÷ 0.12 = 30,000, giving C. Stepwise if preferred: 12% is 3,600, so 1% is 300, and 100% is 30,000. Verify by reversing — 12% of 30,000 is 3,600, which matches. Option A adds 12%; option D is 3,600 × 12. This matters because colleagues quote percentages far more readily than base numbers, and 12% of 30,000 is a very different problem from 12% of 300. Establishing the base in ten seconds lets you judge whether a figure deserves the weight it is being given, and at your level it is a quick way to be useful in a meeting without having to know anything nobody else knows.

**Question 9 (moderate) — Proportion of a whole journey**

A journey has five pages. Page-level "No" feedback rates are: Page 1 4%, Page 2 6%, Page 3 28%, Page 4 5%, Page 5 4%. Which statement is best supported?

- A) The journey has a general content problem.
- B) Page 3 stands out sharply against a consistent baseline of 4 to 6%, which points at something specific to that page rather than a journey-wide issue.
- C) The average rate of 9.4% is the useful figure.
- D) Pages 1, 4 and 5 need attention because they are below average.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Four pages cluster between 4% and 6%, and one sits at 28%. That consistency across four pages is as informative as the outlier itself: it establishes a baseline, which is what makes Page 3 interpretable. Option B says this. Option A ignores the baseline and would send a team to rewrite five pages when one is at fault. Option C computes an average across pages that behave completely differently, producing a figure of 9.4% that describes none of them and would conceal the outlier entirely — this is the classic misuse of a mean and it is worth recognising instantly. Option D inverts the logic, treating below-average as a problem when below average here means performing well. Reading a set of figures for its baseline before its exception is a habit worth building.

**Question 10 (moderate) — Choosing the right denominator**

A journey shows: 20,000 users start, 16,000 reach the evidence page, 12,000 reach the declaration, 11,400 submit. What percentage of users who reached the declaration went on to submit?

- A) 57%
- B) 60%
- C) 71%
- D) 95%

**Correct answer: D**

**Explanation:** Identify the group the question names before touching any figures. It asks about users who reached the declaration, so the whole is 12,000 and the part is 11,400: 11,400 ÷ 12,000 = 0.95, which is 95%, giving D. Option A uses 20,000, answering a whole-journey question nobody asked. This is the most common trap in funnel data and it has a real consequence: telling a team that 95% of people who reach the declaration submit is a completely different message from telling them 57% of all starters finish, and the two point at different pages. Underline the group the stem names. Note where the losses actually are: 4,000 between the start and the evidence page, and 4,000 between evidence and declaration, against 600 at submission.

**Question 11 (harder) — Percentage points versus percentages**

Before a rewrite, 30% of users answered "Yes" to a page's usefulness question. Afterwards, 42% did. Which statement is accurate?

- A) Usefulness rose by 12%.
- B) Usefulness rose by 12 percentage points, which is a 40% increase.
- C) Usefulness rose by 40 percentage points.
- D) Usefulness rose by 28.6%.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The arithmetic gap between two percentages is measured in percentage points: 42 − 30 = 12 percentage points. The relative increase is a separate calculation: 12 ÷ 30 = 0.4, a 40% increase. Option B labels both correctly. Option A uses "12%" loosely, which is ambiguous and usually read as a relative change. Option C misapplies the percentage-point label. Option D divides by the new value. Why this matters: "usefulness rose 40%" and "rose 12 percentage points" describe the identical change, and the first sounds more than three times as impressive. Content designers write the sentences these numbers appear in, which puts you in an unusually direct position to decide whether a report is honest — and being the person who writes both figures is a cheap way to be trusted.

**Question 12 (harder) — When an average hides something**

Six pages in a guide have these word counts: 120, 140, 150, 160, 180 and 2,400. A colleague reports "the average page is 525 words". What is the best comment?

- A) The calculation is wrong; the average is 155 words.
- B) The calculation is right, but the mean is misleading: five of six pages are between 120 and 180 words and one very long page is pulling the figure up. Report the median of 155 with the range, and treat the 2,400-word page as a separate question — it is probably several pages that have not been split.
- C) The figure is fine as reported.
- D) The 2,400-word page should be excluded.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Check the arithmetic first: the values sum to 3,150, and 3,150 ÷ 6 = 525, so the mean is correct and option A is wrong. But no page is near 525 words — five are between 120 and 180, and one is 2,400. The median, the average of the third and fourth values, is 155, which describes the guide well. Option B says this and adds the content-specific insight that makes it the strongest answer: a 2,400-word page sitting among 150-word pages is not an outlier to be noted but almost certainly a page doing several jobs at once, which is a candidate for splitting. Option C lets a misleading summary stand. Option D discards the most interesting item in the set. Report the distribution, and treat the outlier as a lead.

### Preparation tips

- **Drill four operations only.** Percentages, percentage change, averages and ratios cover nearly everything at this level.
- **Estimate before you calculate.** Three seconds of "about a quarter" catches most serious errors.
- **Find the denominator first.** Underline the group the question names.
- **Calculate the total when you are given a list.** Search logs and feedback tables rarely hand you one.
- **Convert percentages into people.** "2% of viewers" and "10,000 calls a year" are the same fact and only one gets a sentence prioritised.
- **Read for the baseline before the exception.** Four consistent pages are what make the fifth interpretable.
- **Learn the percentage point distinction properly.** You will be writing the sentence it appears in.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Dividing by the wrong total.** Funnel questions are built around this.
- **Comparing raw counts across pages with different traffic.** Convert to rates first.
- **Averaging across things that behave differently.** A mean across five pages with one outlier describes none of them.
- **Confusing percentages with percentage points.**
- **Assuming a fall in views is bad.** It may mean the need has gone.
- **Trusting an answer because it appears in the options.**
- **Reporting a mean without checking the spread.**
- **Assuming you are bad at this.** These are learnable techniques, not a talent.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A verbal reasoning assessment measures how accurately you read. For most disciplines that is a useful proxy skill. For yours it is close to the whole job, which means this section is both the one you should find most natural and the one where a careless habit costs most.

The classic format presents a short passage — typically three to six sentences of dense workplace prose — followed by statements you must classify as True, False or Cannot Say. True means the statement follows necessarily. False means the passage contradicts it. Cannot Say means the passage neither confirms nor contradicts it. Alongside these you will meet comprehension questions, main-point questions, and questions asking which of four summaries is most accurate.

The golden rule is that you answer only from the passage. Everything you know about the style guide, everything your team does, everything obviously true about the world — set it aside. If the passage contradicts what you believe, the passage still wins. This feels unnatural and it is the biggest single source of lost marks.

Cannot Say deserves particular attention because people avoid it. It feels like an admission of defeat, so candidates reach for True or False to seem decisive. In reality Cannot Say is very often correct, and choosing it confidently is a mark of good reading.

Watch the small words, because they carry the meaning. "All" is not "most". "May" is not "must". "Should" is not "will". "Where practicable" and "unless" flag conditions that govern everything around them.

There is a payoff here specific to content design, and it is worth naming. Your job frequently involves being handed a paragraph of policy prose and asked what it actually requires, so that you can write the plain-language version. Getting that wrong in either direction is serious: understate a requirement and users miss something that matters; overstate it and you have invented an obligation government does not impose. The main-point and summary items in this section are the closest any assessment comes to testing that exact task.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The verbal skills tested map onto your named skills:

- **Reading precisely** maps to **User-centred content design**, where awareness of standards means applying them as written.
- **Distinguishing statement from inference** maps to **User focus (content design)**, where reasoning from evidence about users must not add to it.
- **Identifying the main point** maps to **User-centred content design** and to your core task of turning dense source material into content that says what it actually means.
- **Understanding qualified language** maps to **Strategic thinking (content design)**, where knowing whether something is required or recommended tells you how much room you have.
- **Reading for what is absent** maps to **User focus**, where noticing that a source never mentions a timescale is what stops you publishing a page without one.
- **Following written direction exactly** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management (content design)**, where delivering what was actually asked for is the foundation of trust.

### Practice questions

*Passage A — for Questions 1 to 4*

"All content published on the service must be reviewed against the style guide before publication. Where content is a factual correction of published material, it may be published immediately and reviewed within five working days. Content that includes legal wording supplied by the legal team must not be altered without the legal team's agreement, although surrounding explanatory content may be edited freely. Content is reviewed annually thereafter."

**Question 1 (easy)** — Statement: "All content must be reviewed against the style guide before publication." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The passage attaches an exception in the second sentence: factual corrections may be published immediately and reviewed within five working days. The statement's "all" and "before publication" together contradict that exception, so it is False. This is the most reliable pattern in the section — an absolute term in the statement, an exception in the passage. Note that the first sentence, read alone, says exactly what the statement says, which is precisely why the item works: reading to the end of a provision before concluding what it requires is the whole discipline.

**Question 2 (easy)** — Statement: "Explanatory content around legal wording may be edited without the legal team's agreement." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The third sentence states that legal wording must not be altered without agreement, "although surrounding explanatory content may be edited freely". The statement restates the second half, so it follows and is True. This is worth internalising beyond the test, because it is the specific fact that unlocks most content design arguments about legal text: the constraint attaches to the legal wording itself, not to the page it sits on, which means you can almost always explain what the legal wording means even when you cannot change it.

**Question 3 (moderate)** — Statement: "A factual correction may be published before it has been reviewed against the style guide." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The second sentence states that where content is a factual correction of published material, it may be published immediately and reviewed within five working days. Publishing immediately and reviewing afterwards is exactly what the statement describes, so it follows and is True. The item rewards holding two sentences together: the first establishes review before publication as the general rule, and the second carves out a case where the order reverses. A reader who has absorbed the general rule and stops there will mark this False. It is worth noticing why the exception exists — a published page containing a factual error is doing active harm every hour it stands, and a rule requiring review first would keep the error live while the review was arranged, which is exactly backwards.

**Question 4 (moderate)** — Statement: "Content is never reviewed after publication." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The final sentence states that content is reviewed annually thereafter, and the second sentence requires review within five working days for factual corrections. Both establish post-publication review, so the statement is contradicted and is False. The item rewards using the whole passage rather than the sentence that seems most relevant — a reader focused on the pre-publication requirement in sentence one might treat review as an exclusively pre-publication activity and answer True.

*Passage B — for Questions 5 to 8*

"The review of the guidance found that users who read the eligibility page before starting an application were more likely to complete it. The review notes that these users also differed in other respects, including whether they had applied before. The review did not assess whether the eligibility page was accurate. It recommends that the team consider whether the eligibility page should be more prominent."

**Question 5 (easy)** — Statement: "Users who read the eligibility page were more likely to complete an application." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The first sentence states this directly, and the statement restates it without addition. True. Note the passage's careful "more likely to complete" rather than "usually completed", and the statement respects it. Answer quickly and bank the time for harder items.

**Question 6 (moderate)** — Statement: "Reading the eligibility page causes users to complete applications." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The passage reports an association and then explicitly flags that the two groups differed in other respects, including whether they had applied before. Previous applicants are more likely to complete for reasons entirely unrelated to the page — they know what is required and have their documents — and they may also be more likely to look at the eligibility page out of habit. So the causal direction is genuinely unclear, and the passage neither asserts nor rules it out. Cannot Say. The content design consequence is direct: if the page causes completion, making it prominent helps; if experienced users simply read more pages, making it prominent achieves nothing and adds a step for everyone else.

**Question 7 (moderate)** — Statement: "The eligibility page is accurate." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The passage says explicitly that the review did not assess whether the eligibility page was accurate. Declared silence establishes nothing in either direction, so Cannot Say. Candidates sometimes reason that a page associated with higher completion must be doing its job, which is exactly the inference this item catches — a page could be inaccurate and still correlate with completion, and an inaccurate eligibility page that encourages people to apply when they are not eligible would be actively harmful while looking like a success in the data.

**Question 8 (harder)** — Statement: "The review recommends making the eligibility page more prominent." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The review recommends that the team *consider whether* the page should be more prominent — a recommendation to evaluate an option, including the possibility of concluding it should not be. The statement asserts a recommendation to make it prominent, which was not made, so it is False rather than Cannot Say: the passage tells you what was recommended and it was something different. This distinction pervades real review documents and produces a familiar sequence in which a recommendation to consider becomes a commitment to deliver. Content designers are unusually likely to be the person who writes the sentence recording what a review said, which makes reading it precisely a direct professional obligation.

*Passage C — for Questions 9 to 12*

"Content must be written so that it can be understood by users with a reading age of 9 where the subject matter allows. Where technical or legal terms cannot be avoided, they must be explained at first use. Content must not rely on colour alone to convey meaning. Headings must describe the content that follows them, and must be used in order without skipping levels."

**Question 9 (easy)** — Statement: "All content must be understandable by users with a reading age of 9." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The requirement is qualified: "where the subject matter allows". The statement's "all" drops that qualification, and the passage explicitly contemplates subject matter where the standard cannot be met. So the statement is contradicted and is False. This qualifier is doing important and often-misquoted work. It is not a general excuse — most content that claims difficult subject matter could be written far more simply — but it does acknowledge that some content genuinely cannot reach that level, and a content designer who cites the rule without the qualifier will eventually be caught out by a colleague who has read it.

**Question 10 (moderate)** — Statement: "Technical terms must be avoided." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The passage says that *where technical or legal terms cannot be avoided*, they must be explained at first use. That wording presupposes there are cases where they cannot be avoided and permits their use in those cases, subject to explanation. The statement asserts an absolute prohibition the passage does not impose, so False. Note the structure: a rule addressing what to do in a situation implicitly accepts that the situation occurs. Reading it as a prohibition is a common error and produces content designers who argue that a term must be removed when the actual requirement is that it must be explained.

**Question 11 (harder)** — Statement: "A page using a red asterisk to mark required fields, with no other indication, complies with the passage." True, False or Cannot Say?

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The passage states that content must not rely on colour alone to convey meaning. A red asterisk conveys "required" through two things: the asterisk symbol and its colour. Since the asterisk is present regardless of colour, a careless reading suggests colour is not the sole carrier and the page complies. But the statement specifies "with no other indication", meaning the asterisk itself is unexplained — and an unexplained asterisk is a convention, not a meaning, that many users will not know. More decisively, if the asterisk is the carrier then the page relies on an unexplained symbol; if the redness is the carrier then it relies on colour alone. Either way the meaning is not conveyed accessibly, and the passage's requirement is not met. False. The practical answer is the word "required" next to the field, which costs eight characters and removes the whole question.

**Question 12 (harder) — Main point**

Which best captures the main point of Passage C?

- A) Content must be written for a reading age of 9.
- B) Content must be comprehensible and navigable without specialist knowledge or reliance on visual cues — through plain language where possible, explanation where it is not, meaning carried in more than colour, and headings that describe and are correctly ordered.
- C) Headings must be used in order.
- D) Technical terms must be explained at first use.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The main point must cover the whole passage without adding anything. Option A drops the qualifier and ignores three other requirements. Options C and D are each true of one sentence and silent on the rest. Option B captures all four and the logic joining them: each requirement exists so content can be understood and navigated by someone who does not share the author's knowledge and may not perceive the page as the author does. Notice that B also has to be careful — it says "plain language where possible", preserving the qualifier that option A discarded. Main-point items reward the option broad enough to cover everything and disciplined enough to invent nothing, and three of these four are true details promoted too far.

### Preparation tips

- **Answer only from the passage.** Before marking anything True, point at the words.
- **Trust Cannot Say.** If you are supplying a link, that is your signal.
- **Circle the qualifiers.** All, some, most, may, must, should, unless, where practicable, where the subject matter allows.
- **Read to the end of a provision.** A blanket-sounding first sentence is frequently qualified two lines later.
- **Check whether a deadline counts the starting day.** Where the passage does not say and the answer depends on it, Cannot Say.
- **Notice rules that presuppose a situation.** "Where X cannot be avoided" permits X.
- **Distinguish "consider whether X" from "recommend X".** You may be the one writing the record of what was said.
- **Practise on real guidance.** Take a page of the GOV.UK style guide and write three statements — one True, one False, one Cannot Say.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Answering from world knowledge.** The most frequent cause of lost marks.
- **Avoiding Cannot Say.**
- **Dropping a qualifier.** "Where the subject matter allows" is doing real work, and citing the rule without it will be corrected.
- **Reading a conditional rule as a prohibition.** "Where terms cannot be avoided, explain them" is not "avoid terms".
- **Treating association as causation.** A passage naming confounds is warning you off.
- **Inferring from what a review did not examine.**
- **Using only the sentence that seems most relevant.** Passages are built so the answer needs two.
- **Confusing a true detail with the main point.**

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A situational judgement assessment presents realistic workplace scenarios and asks what you would do. There is no passage to decode and no arithmetic. You are shown a dilemma of the kind your role produces, given four or five possible responses, and asked which is most effective and often which is least. Some versions ask you to rank the options; others ask you to rate each on a scale from counterproductive to highly effective.

This section measures judgement rather than knowledge, and specifically whether your instincts align with what the profession considers good practice at your level. That is the whole calibration. The same scenario has different best answers for a trainee and for a head of profession. An associate content designer who overrules a policy colleague is overstepping; one who never raises anything is under-performing. Answer as the person your role description says you are — a trainee working under supervision, performing basic checks and copy edits, showing aptitude and potential, expected to notice things and say so.

Four principles carry most scenarios here.

Users come first, and in content design that specifically means the users who will be misled, delayed or excluded by what a page does or does not say. When an option protects somebody from acting on wrong information or from missing a deadline they were not told about, it will almost always outrank an option protecting a schedule or somebody's preference.

Act within your remit and be transparent at its edges. At associate level the best answers usually involve doing what you can competently do and promptly telling the right person about what you cannot. Neither silent struggling nor reflexive escalation scores well. The pattern is: attempt, then tell.

Address problems directly and early, with the person involved. Options involving an honest private conversation beat options involving public criticism, silence, or complaining to someone else.

Ask what a request is for. This is the distinctively content-design signature of a strong answer. Stakeholders very often ask for a specific sentence when what they need is a specific outcome, and the two are frequently satisfiable in ways nobody has considered.

One trap. Some options are attractive because they are pleasant, others because they are decisive. Neither pleasantness nor decisiveness is the criterion.

### How this assessment maps to your role

The judgement dimensions map onto your named skills:

- **Protecting users from misleading or missing content** maps to **User focus (content design)** and to **User-centred content design**.
- **Explaining a content decision** maps to **User-centred content design** and to **Stakeholder relationship management (content design)**, where knowing what stakeholders find important shapes how you explain things.
- **Handling requests and constraints** maps to **Stakeholder relationship management (content design)**.
- **Working in cycles and responding to feedback** maps to **Agile working (content design)**, where adapting and being responsive to feedback is named explicitly.
- **Exploring how content might work** maps to **Content concepts and prototyping**.
- **Understanding where content fits** maps to **Strategic thinking (content design)**, where seeing beyond the immediate task is the beginning of that skill.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy) — You do not know how to do the task**

Your senior content designer asks you to "do a content audit of the renewals journey by Thursday". You know roughly what a content audit is but have never done one. What is the most effective response?

- A) Say yes, then work it out alone from articles online.
- B) Say yes, and add that you have not done one before and would find half an hour of guidance really useful before you start.
- C) Say you cannot do it because you have not been trained.
- D) Say yes, and quietly ask another associate to help rather than telling your senior.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** Your role description says explicitly that you work under supervision and need to develop your skills, so asking for guidance is doing your job correctly rather than falling short. Option B combines willingness with honesty and makes a specific, small, easy-to-grant request — half an hour is far cheaper for your senior than reviewing a misconceived audit on Wednesday night. Option A risks producing something unusable and hides the gap so it never gets filled. Option C is least effective because it declines a development opportunity and hands the work back without offering anything. Option D avoids the awkwardness but leaves your senior with an inaccurate picture of your skills and pulls a colleague off their own work. The general principle: at your level, "yes, and here is what I need to do it well" is almost always the strongest available answer.

**Question 2 (easy) — Something you notice in live content**

Reviewing a live page, you notice it tells users to send documents to an address that changed six months ago. You are the most junior person in the team. What should you do?

- A) Say nothing; someone would have noticed by now.
- B) Raise it immediately with your senior content designer, stating clearly that the address on a live page is wrong and users may be posting documents to the wrong place today.
- C) Correct the page yourself.
- D) Note it for the next content review.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** This is a live error causing real harm now — documents posted to a former address may be lost entirely, and the users affected will not know for weeks. Option B raises it immediately with the right person and, importantly, states the consequence rather than just the fault, which is what makes people act today rather than adding it to a list. Option A is least effective: "someone would have noticed" is how errors survive for months, and being new is precisely why you spotted it. Option C is well-intentioned and beyond your remit; you also may not know the correct new address, and publishing a second wrong one would be worse. Option D defers an active harm to a scheduled review. The instinct worth building: when you find one wrong address, ask whether the same address appears on other pages — it almost always does.

**Question 3 (easy) — Conflicting instructions**

Your senior content designer asked you to finish the content audit this morning. The delivery manager then asks you to spend the morning writing copy for tomorrow's demo, calling it urgent. What is the most effective response?

- A) Do what the delivery manager asked, because they are more senior.
- B) Do what your senior content designer asked and tell the delivery manager you are busy.
- C) Tell the delivery manager about the existing task, offer a specific workable option, and ask the two of them to confirm the priority.
- D) Try to do both by working through lunch.

**Correct answer: C. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** Two legitimate instructions have collided and you cannot serve both. Resolving it is not yours to impose, but surfacing it promptly and helpfully is. What lifts option C above bare escalation is the offer of a specific option — "I could write the demo copy this morning and finish the audit after lunch, if that works" — which turns you from a bottleneck into part of the solution. Option A ranks by seniority; the delivery manager probably does not know about the audit. Option B is abrupt and leaves a real need unmet. Option D is least effective: working through lunch hides the conflict, sets an unsustainable expectation, and means nobody learns that the team is over-committing you.

**Question 4 (moderate) — A stakeholder asks for specific wording**

A policy colleague asks you to add a paragraph explaining a regulation, written in the language of the regulation itself. Research shows users do not understand that language. What is the most effective response?

- A) Add the paragraph as requested.
- B) Ask what the paragraph needs to achieve — whether it is that users understand the rule, that the department is legally protected, or that a specific form of words appears — because those three needs have different solutions and only one of them requires that exact wording.
- C) Refuse and write a plain-language version instead.
- D) Add the paragraph and a plain-language version underneath.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** This is the characteristic content design conversation, and the productive move is to establish what the request is actually for before deciding how to satisfy it. If the need is user understanding, plain language serves it better and the colleague will usually agree once that is said aloud. If the need is legal protection, the exact wording may have to appear — and can be explained alongside. If the need is simply that the policy team's work is visible, that is a different conversation again. Option B distinguishes them, and asking rather than assuming is what makes it available to someone at your level. Option A is least effective, publishing something users demonstrably do not understand while believing you have had no choice. Option C overrides a colleague's stated requirement without understanding it. Option D may well be the answer, and proposing it before asking means you may be adding a paragraph nobody needed.

**Question 5 (moderate) — Feedback on your work**

A senior content designer returns your draft with most of it rewritten and one line of comment: "too wordy". What is the most effective response?

- A) Accept the rewrite and move on.
- B) Compare the two versions carefully, work out what specifically was cut and why, then ask a couple of targeted questions about the changes you cannot account for.
- C) Ask them to explain every change.
- D) Point out that some of the detail they removed was requested by the policy team.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A heavily rewritten draft with a one-line comment is frustrating and is also a considerable amount of free teaching, provided you do the work of extracting it. Option B does that: comparing versions line by line will explain most of the changes on its own — you will see qualifiers removed, sentences split, passive constructions turned around — and it leaves you with a short list of genuinely puzzling changes worth asking about. That is a far better use of a senior colleague's time than option C, which asks them to justify forty edits, most of which you could have worked out. Option A is least effective, because accepting a rewrite without extracting the pattern means you will make the same choices next time and receive the same rewrite. Option D raises a legitimate point and leads with a defence, which makes the conversation about who was right; the same point lands better as one of your targeted questions.

**Question 6 (moderate) — A pattern you notice across pages**

While auditing, you notice that four pages in the same service each explain the same eligibility rule, in four different ways, two of which appear to contradict each other. What is the most effective response?

- A) Fix the two that contradict each other.
- B) Document all four versions with their differences, raise it with your senior content designer, and flag that at least two are inconsistent so somebody with the authority to establish the correct rule can do so.
- C) Pick the clearest version and make all four match it.
- D) Note it in the audit and move on.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** Four different explanations of one rule, two contradicting, means at least one page is wrong and nobody currently knows which. Option B does the useful work — documenting the versions and their differences is exactly the audit output that makes the problem fixable — and correctly identifies that establishing the correct rule is not your call. Option C is least effective, and it is the tempting one: making them consistent looks like a fix and could easily propagate a wrong rule to four pages instead of one, which is a considerably worse position than the inconsistency you started with. Option A does the same thing on a smaller scale. Option D records the problem without escalating something that includes a live factual error. The general lesson: consistency is not the same as correctness, and making things consistent before establishing which is right is a recognisable trap in content work.

**Question 7 (moderate) — Pressure to publish quickly**

A delivery manager asks you to publish a page today, before the second check your team's process requires, because a campaign launches tomorrow. What is the most effective response?

- A) Publish it; the deadline is real.
- B) Explain that the check exists and ask what is possible — whether someone can second-check it in the next hour, or whether the page could go live tomorrow morning — and raise it with your senior content designer if no route works.
- C) Refuse and cite the process.
- D) Publish it and arrange the check afterwards without telling anyone.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: D**

**Explanation:** The deadline is genuine and the check exists for a reason, and option B treats both as real. Asking what is possible frequently resolves it — a second check on one page takes twenty minutes and somebody is usually available — and naming the alternative of publishing tomorrow morning tests whether "today" is a real constraint or a preference. Escalating if no route works is the right fallback at your level. Option D is least effective: publishing outside the process and concealing it means nobody knows the page is unchecked, so if there is an error it will not be found, and you have quietly made yourself responsible for a decision that was not yours. Option A skips the process without even asking. Option C cites a rule without engaging with the deadline, which is technically defensible and makes you harder to work with than you need to be.

**Question 8 (moderate) — Content that is not yours**

You notice that the letter the service sends after an application uses completely different wording from the confirmation page you helped write, and gives a different timescale. Letters are handled by another team. What is most effective?

- A) Ignore it; letters are not your responsibility.
- B) Document the discrepancy — particularly the different timescales, since one of them must be wrong — and raise it with your senior content designer, asking whether there is a route to the team that owns the letter.
- C) Contact the other team directly.
- D) Change the confirmation page to match the letter.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** Two different timescales for the same thing means at least one is wrong, and a user who reads both has no way to know which to believe — which generates a phone call at best and a missed deadline at worst. Option B documents the discrepancy, singles out the part that is definitely a factual error rather than merely a stylistic difference, and seeks a legitimate route through someone with standing to use it. Option A is least effective, because "not my responsibility" describes an org chart while the user experiences one service contradicting itself. Option C acts beyond your remit and risks cutting across conversations you cannot see. Option D changes the half you control to match a half that may itself be the wrong one, which would make things worse while looking like a fix.

**Question 9 (harder) — Content that may cause harm**

Two days before launch you realise a page describing a benefit does not mention that applying may affect a different benefit the user already receives. Adding it will require policy sign-off and may delay launch. What is the most effective response?

- A) Launch and add it in the next content update.
- B) Raise it immediately with your senior content designer and the delivery manager, stating specifically that users may take an action that reduces their income without knowing, and ask for a decision.
- C) Add a sentence yourself based on your understanding of the interaction.
- D) Launch and note the gap in the content backlog.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: C**

**Explanation:** A page that omits a financial consequence may cause real users real financial harm, and that is a decision for people with authority to weigh against a launch date — though raising it, precisely and now, is entirely yours. Option B does everything within your remit, and the specificity matters: "users may reduce their income without knowing" prompts action in a way that "the page is missing something about benefit interactions" does not. Option C is least effective and is the trap for a conscientious trainee: writing your own explanation of how two benefits interact, without policy confirmation, risks publishing something wrong about somebody's money, which is worse than the omission. Options A and D both launch with a known harm and defer it to a list, which is where such gaps stay.

**Question 10 (harder) — Being asked to soften a message**

A stakeholder asks you to change "you must tell us within 28 days or you may have to pay a penalty" to "please let us know as soon as you can", saying the original sounds threatening. What is the most effective response?

- A) Make the change; the tone matters.
- B) Explain that the original tells users two things they need — the deadline and the consequence — and that removing both to soften the tone leaves people at risk of a penalty they were never warned about; then offer to work on the tone while keeping the deadline and the consequence.
- C) Refuse and keep the original.
- D) Change it to "please tell us within 28 days".

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** The stakeholder's concern about tone is legitimate — a lot of government content does read as threatening, and that has real costs in trust and in whether people engage at all. Their proposed solution is not, because it removes the two pieces of information the sentence exists to convey. Option B separates the concern from the solution and offers to address the concern properly, which is what makes it persuasive rather than obstructive. Option A is least effective: the softened version leaves a user who tells you on day 40 facing a penalty they had no warning of, which is a considerably worse experience than a sentence that sounded stern. Option C wins the point and loses the relationship and the chance to improve the tone. Option D is a partial improvement that keeps the deadline and still drops the consequence, and the consequence is the part that makes the deadline matter.

**Question 11 (harder) — A mistake of your own**

You realise that a page you published last week states a fee of £75 when the correct fee is £57. What should you do?

- A) Correct it quietly and say nothing.
- B) Correct it immediately if you can, tell your senior content designer straight away, and flag that users may have been given the wrong figure for a week and may need to be contacted.
- C) Correct it and mention it at the next stand-up.
- D) Check with someone else first whether it is really wrong.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** A wrong fee published for a week means users have been given incorrect financial information — some may have budgeted for the wrong amount, and some may have decided not to apply because £75 seemed too much. Option B does the three things that matter: fixes the live error fast, tells the person who needs to know, and — the part people skip — raises the question of whether affected users need contacting, which is not your decision but is your responsibility to surface. Option A is least effective: correcting silently leaves the consequences unexamined and, if it emerges later, the concealment will be the thing people remember rather than the typo. Option C delays disclosure of a financial error. Option D is reasonable if you are genuinely unsure, and here the fee is a transposition of the same digits, which is a recognisable and near-certain error worth acting on immediately while you confirm.

**Question 12 (harder) — An assumption in the room**

In a planning session, someone says "we don't need to worry about people who can't read this — they can always ring up". You know the contact centre is open 9 to 5 on weekdays and the service is used heavily by people in shift work. What is the most effective response?

- A) Let it go; the team has decided.
- B) Bring the specific facts into the room — that the contact centre is 9 to 5 on weekdays and many users work shifts — and ask what should happen to someone who cannot read the page and cannot ring during those hours.
- C) Argue that all content must be accessible to everyone.
- D) Raise it privately with your senior content designer afterwards.

**Correct answer: B. Least effective: A**

**Explanation:** The comment rests on an assumption — that a phone line is a universal fallback — and assumptions collapse against specific facts rather than principles. Option B supplies two facts and then asks a question, which keeps the conversation about the design rather than about who is right, and leaves room for the team to have a good answer. It is also the strongest form of challenge available to a junior person, because you are contributing information rather than an opinion. Option A is least effective: a decision made on an assumption that is factually wrong will leave real people with no route at all, and you had the facts. Option C states a true principle in a way that invites a debate about principles, which rarely changes a room. Option D is a reasonable backup and misses the moment when the decision is being made.

### Preparation tips

- **Answer as a trainee, not as a hero or a bystander.** Competent action plus prompt, honest communication upwards.
- **Put the misled or excluded user first.** When an option protects somebody from acting on wrong information, start by assuming it is strongest.
- **Ask what a request is for.** Stakeholders ask for wording when what they need is an outcome.
- **State the consequence, not just the fault.** "Users may post documents to the wrong address" prompts action; "the address is out of date" gets added to a list.
- **Do not make things consistent before establishing which is right.**
- **Extract the pattern from feedback before asking questions about it.**
- **Read the least-effective question separately.** The worst option usually conceals something or lets a harm continue.

### Common pitfalls to avoid

- **Silence out of newness.** "Someone would have noticed" is how errors survive for months.
- **Writing your own version of something you have not confirmed.** Especially about money or entitlement.
- **Publishing outside a process and not saying so.** You have taken on a decision that was not yours.
- **Softening a message by removing the information in it.** Tone and content are separable; treat them separately.
- **Matching one page to another without knowing which is correct.**
- **Accepting "they can always ring up" as a fallback.** Check the opening hours before you accept it.
- **Delaying disclosure of your own error.** Speed is what turns a mistake into evidence of integrity.
- **Leading with a defence when receiving feedback.** It makes the conversation about who was right.

## Conclusion

You have reached the end, and that is worth a moment. You have worked through forty-eight practice questions across four assessment types and read the reasoning behind every one. That is a substantial piece of preparation and considerably more than most people do.

Look back at what you covered. In the cognitive section you checked titles against a style guide, worked through rules with exceptions, read a search log for what it revealed about missing content, prioritised a morning where a wrong deadline outranked your own commitments, and separated a page being well made from a page being the right page. In the numeric section you compared pages fairly by converting to rates, read a set of figures for its baseline before its exception, converted a 2% call rate into ten thousand calls a year, distinguished a percentage from a percentage point, and noticed a 2,400-word page hiding behind an average. In the verbal section you read to the end of provisions, kept qualifiers that others drop, recognised that a rule addressing a situation permits it, and caught "consider whether" pretending to be a recommendation. And in the situational judgement section you raised a wrong address as the most junior person in the room, asked what a policy request was actually for, declined to make four pages consistent before knowing which was right, and brought contact centre opening hours to an assumption nobody had examined.

Notice how little of that was really about tests. Every one is a working skill. A content designer who counts properly, reads the source as written, asks what a request is for, states the consequence rather than the fault, and says so within ten minutes of finding their own mistake — that is simply a good content designer. Preparing for these assessments and getting better at the job turn out to be very nearly the same activity, which is the most encouraging thing about the whole exercise.

A word about where you are. You are an associate content designer: a trainee, working under supervision, performing basic standard checks and copy edits, expected to show aptitude, potential and an understanding of the role. Please read that as the invitation it is. Nobody expects the judgement of someone with a decade behind them. What they are looking for is care, curiosity, honesty about what you do not yet know, and steady improvement. And remember the advantage you currently have: you have not yet absorbed the internal vocabulary that makes pages unreadable, and you still notice when a sentence assumes knowledge nobody has.

If you want to keep going from here, a few things will help more than rereading. Read your service's site search log for a month; it is the cheapest user research available and almost nobody looks at it. Sit with a contact centre adviser for an hour and listen to what people ring up about, then find the pages that should have answered them. Read the letters your service sends, because they are frequently the part of a journey nobody has designed. Take a page you think is fine and read it aloud to someone outside government. And practise stating findings at the right strength — "seven of nine participants", not "most users" — until the accurate version comes naturally.

Finally, for the day itself. An assessment is a snapshot taken on one particular morning. It is not a verdict on your ability, your potential or your future in this profession. What actually determines how far you go is the quality of your attention to the people who read these pages and your willingness to keep learning — and someone who has read this far clearly has plenty of both.

Good luck. Go carefully, ask what things are for, and keep reading things aloud.
