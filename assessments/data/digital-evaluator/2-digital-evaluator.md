Psychometric assessments are structured, standardised exercises that measure specific mental capabilities and behavioural tendencies. They are not tests of what you have memorised. For a digital evaluator, these capabilities map directly onto your day-to-day work: spotting inconsistencies in a dataset, calculating a percentage change in a performance metric, reading government evaluation guidance carefully, or deciding how to respond when a stakeholder asks you to soften a finding.

## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive assessment measures the mental processes you use every day as a digital evaluator: recognising patterns in monitoring data, deducing logical conclusions from evaluation evidence, checking documents and datasets for errors, prioritising competing tasks, and solving problems that arise during an evaluation. Unlike a generic intelligence test, a job-specific cognitive assessment uses practical workplace scenarios and data formats that mirror your actual role, which makes it a stronger predictor of how you will perform in the job itself.

The typical format is an online, timed test lasting between 15 and 30 minutes, with multiple-choice questions. Some platforms are adaptive, adjusting question difficulty based on your previous answers. You will usually receive short, ungraded practice questions first so you can get used to the interface. Scoring is objective: your results are compared against a benchmark or norm group, and employers often receive a breakdown showing your speed versus your accuracy rather than a single number.

For a digital evaluator, the assessment content is likely to involve artefacts you genuinely handle: theories of change, logic models, indicator frameworks, evaluation plans, survey datasets, performance dashboards, and government evaluation guidance such as the Magenta Book. The questions will ask you to reason about these artefacts quickly and accurately — exactly what you do when you are quality assuring a colleague's analysis or checking that a measurement framework hangs together logically.

### What it measures for your role

The cognitive dimensions in this assessment map directly onto the skills in your role profile:

- **Quality assurance of data and analysis**: error checking questions test whether you can decide if data and analysis are accurate and fit for purpose, spot inconsistencies between tables and summaries, and peer-review outputs the way you would review a colleague's analysis.
- **Evaluation delivery**: logical deduction questions test the reasoning you use when you analyse and interpret data, distinguish descriptive findings from inferential claims, and evaluate interventions using techniques such as process evaluation, impact evaluation, and experimentation.
- **Product and service monitoring**: pattern recognition questions reflect your work developing theories of change and turning indicators into measurement frameworks — both depend on seeing how activities, outputs, outcomes, and effects connect.
- **Monitoring and evaluation across the product life cycle**: prioritisation and planning questions mirror how you manage evaluation delivery at different service standard phases and plan data requirements for future phases.
- **Evaluation planning and strategy**: problem-solving questions test how you translate stakeholder needs into research questions and choose between evaluation approaches under constraints.
- **Data ethics and privacy**: some deduction questions involve applying rules — such as consent and data handling conditions — precisely, which reflects how you apply fundamental principles of data ethics under supervision.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy)** — Theory of change sequencing

You are drafting a theory of change for a new online benefits application service. You have four elements on sticky notes, out of order:

1. Citizens complete applications online without needing telephone support
2. Reduced administrative cost per application and improved citizen satisfaction
3. Build and launch an accessible online application form
4. More citizens receive the correct benefit decision more quickly

Which sequence correctly orders these as activity, output, outcome, impact?

- A) 3, 1, 4, 2
- B) 3, 4, 1, 2
- C) 1, 3, 4, 2
- D) 3, 1, 2, 4

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** A theory of change flows from what the team does (activity) to what is directly produced (output) to the change that results (outcome) to the broader long-term effect (impact). Building and launching the form (3) is the activity — it is the work the team performs. Citizens completing applications online (1) is the output — the direct, immediate product of that activity being used. More citizens receiving correct decisions more quickly (4) is the outcome — a change in the world that the output enables. Reduced administrative cost and improved satisfaction (2) is the impact — the broader value for the public and the organisation. Option B breaks the chain by placing an outcome before the output that enables it. Option C starts with an output before any activity has occurred. Option D treats broad impact as arriving before the intermediate outcome, which reverses the causal logic. Practising this ordering matters because you will develop theories of change through discussion with stakeholders, and you must be able to spot when a draft chain skips a step or runs backwards.

**Question 2 (easy)** — Error checking a summary against a table

A colleague drafts a performance summary for a monthly report. The underlying table shows:

| Month | Completed transactions | Support calls |
|---|---|---|
| April | 12,400 | 950 |
| May | 13,100 | 910 |
| June | 12,900 | 980 |

The draft summary reads: "Completed transactions rose in each month of the quarter, while support calls fell in each month."

How many factual errors does the summary contain?

- A) None
- B) One
- C) Two
- D) Three

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Check each claim against the table. "Completed transactions rose in each month": April to May rose (12,400 to 13,100), but May to June fell (13,100 to 12,900) — so this claim is false; that is one error. "Support calls fell in each month": April to May fell (950 to 910), but May to June rose (910 to 980) — false; that is a second error. Two errors in total, so the answer is C. This is exactly the kind of check you perform when you peer-review colleagues' outputs to ensure quality: never accept a trend statement without tracing every step of the trend in the data. A single month that moves the other way invalidates an "in each month" claim.

**Question 3 (moderate)** — Deduction from service standard rules

Your department applies these rules to service assessments:

- Every service in public beta must have a performance dashboard.
- A service may only enter public beta after its measurement framework has been approved.
- Service Falcon has a performance dashboard.

Which one of the following conclusions must be true?

- A) Service Falcon is in public beta.
- B) Service Falcon's measurement framework has been approved.
- C) If Service Falcon is in public beta, its measurement framework has been approved.
- D) Service Falcon entered public beta after building its dashboard.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The rules say public beta requires a dashboard, and public beta requires prior framework approval. Having a dashboard does not prove a service is in public beta — services in other phases might also build dashboards, and the rule only runs in one direction. So A fails: it affirms the consequent, a classic logical error. B fails for the same reason — framework approval is only guaranteed for services that entered public beta, and we have not established that Falcon did. D adds sequencing information the rules never state. C is airtight: if Falcon is in public beta, then by the second rule its framework must have been approved before entry. Conditional conclusions like C are often the only ones that genuinely follow. In your monitoring and evaluation work across the product life cycle, you must apply phase-based rules precisely — assuming a service has met a requirement just because it shows one typical characteristic is exactly the reasoning slip this question punishes.

**Question 4 (moderate)** — Pattern recognition in an indicator register

Your team codes indicators in a register using a consistent pattern:

- PRC-Q-01: process indicator, qualitative, first in series
- PRC-N-02: process indicator, numeric, second in series
- IMP-N-01: impact indicator, numeric, first in series
- IMP-Q-02: impact indicator, qualitative, second in series

A new indicator measures user satisfaction scores (a numeric scale) with the outcome of the service, and it is the third outcome indicator registered. Following the pattern, what should its code be?

- A) OUT-N-03
- B) OUT-Q-03
- C) IMP-N-03
- D) N-OUT-03

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** The pattern is a three-letter indicator type (PRC for process, IMP for impact), then a letter for data type (Q for qualitative, N for numeric), then a two-digit sequence number. An outcome indicator would logically take OUT as its type code. Satisfaction measured on a numeric scale is numeric data, so N. Third in series gives 03. That yields OUT-N-03, answer A. Option B misclassifies a numeric scale as qualitative — a common confusion; the data are numbers even though they measure an attitude. Option C misfiles an outcome indicator as impact, which would corrupt your measurement framework's structure. Option D breaks the established ordering of the code segments. Spotting and extending naming conventions matters when you manage the day-to-day development of monitoring and analytical tools: consistent coding is what lets a dashboard aggregate indicators reliably.

**Question 5 (moderate)** — Prioritisation under deadline pressure

It is Monday morning. You support a senior digital evaluator, and you have these four tasks:

1. Finish cleaning the survey dataset needed by an analyst on Wednesday.
2. Respond to a policy stakeholder who emailed Friday asking which indicators could measure a new outcome — she has a decision meeting tomorrow at 09:00.
3. Peer-review a colleague's descriptive analysis, due Friday.
4. Update the evaluation plan template with formatting fixes (no deadline).

Which order of tasks is most logical?

- A) 1, 2, 3, 4
- B) 2, 1, 3, 4
- C) 3, 2, 1, 4
- D) 2, 3, 1, 4

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Prioritise by deadline proximity and consequence of delay. The stakeholder's decision meeting is tomorrow morning — if you reply after that, your input is worthless regardless of its quality, so task 2 comes first. The dataset is needed Wednesday and cleansing can take longer than expected, so it comes second; starting it early also gives you buffer if you find quality problems. The peer review is due Friday, third. The template formatting has no deadline and no downstream dependency, so it is last. Option A treats the Wednesday deadline as more urgent than a Tuesday-morning one. Options C and D promote later-deadline work above earlier-deadline work. In evaluation work, sequencing matters doubly because your outputs feed other people's work: an analyst waiting on your cleaned data, a stakeholder waiting on your indicator options. The test is not "which task is largest?" but "which delay destroys the most value?"

**Question 6 (moderate)** — Deduction about data collection coverage

Your evaluation collects weekly usage data for a service. The plan requires at least one data extract per week for eight consecutive weeks. Your log shows extracts on: Week 1 (two extracts), Week 2, Week 3, Week 5, Week 6 (two extracts), Week 7, Week 8.

A colleague says: "We have nine extracts, and the plan needs eight, so we have met the requirement."

What is the flaw in this reasoning?

- A) Nine extracts is fewer than the plan requires.
- B) The requirement is about weekly coverage, not total count — Week 4 has no extract, so the requirement is not met.
- C) The extracts in Weeks 1 and 6 should not be counted twice.
- D) There is no flaw; the requirement is met.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The plan requires at least one extract *per week* for eight *consecutive* weeks. That is a coverage condition on each week, not a total across the period. Counting nine extracts and comparing to eight substitutes an easier question (how many?) for the real one (is every week covered?). Week 4 is missing entirely, so the requirement fails no matter how many extracts other weeks have. Option A is arithmetically wrong — nine exceeds eight — and misses the point anyway. Option C is a red herring: double extracts in a week are harmless for a "at least one per week" rule; they just cannot compensate for an empty week. This distinction between totals and coverage arises constantly when you collect quantitative data and undertake quality assurance processes: a dataset can look abundant overall while still having gaps that break time-series analysis.

**Question 7 (moderate)** — Error checking a measurement framework

You are quality assuring a draft measurement framework. One section reads:

| Indicator | Data source | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Digital take-up rate | Web analytics | Monthly |
| User satisfaction score | Post-transaction survey | Monthly |
| Cost per transaction | Post-transaction survey | Quarterly |
| Completion rate | Web analytics | Monthly |

Which row most clearly needs correction?

- A) Digital take-up rate
- B) User satisfaction score
- C) Cost per transaction
- D) Completion rate

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Match each indicator to a plausible data source. Digital take-up and completion rate are behavioural measures naturally captured by web analytics — plausible. User satisfaction is an attitudinal measure appropriately captured by a post-transaction survey — plausible. But cost per transaction is a financial measure: it requires finance or operational cost data divided by transaction volumes. A post-transaction survey asks users about their experience; users do not know, and cannot report, what their transaction cost the department. The source is therefore wrong for the indicator, and row C needs correction. This is the sort of internal-consistency check you make when you turn theory of change indicators into measurement frameworks and decide whether data is fit for purpose: every indicator must be paired with a source that can actually produce it, or the dashboard built on the framework will have an empty or misleading tile.

**Question 8 (challenging)** — Constraint-based matching

Three evaluators — Amina, Ben, and Chloe — must each lead exactly one evaluation: a process evaluation, an impact evaluation, and an economic evaluation. Constraints:

- Amina will not lead the economic evaluation.
- If Ben leads the process evaluation, then Chloe leads the impact evaluation.
- Chloe does not lead the impact evaluation.
- Ben does not lead the economic evaluation.

Who leads the impact evaluation?

- A) Amina
- B) Ben
- C) Chloe
- D) Cannot be determined

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Work through the constraints systematically, using the contrapositive where it helps. Constraint 2 says that if Ben leads process, then Chloe leads impact. Constraint 3 says Chloe does not lead impact. Put together, these force the contrapositive: Ben cannot lead the process evaluation, because if he did, Chloe would have to lead impact, which constraint 3 forbids. Constraint 4 rules Ben out of the economic evaluation directly. Since each evaluator leads exactly one of the three evaluations and Ben is excluded from process and economic, Ben must lead the impact evaluation. Now verify the full assignment is consistent: Amina and Chloe split process and economic; Amina will not lead economic (constraint 1), so Amina leads process and Chloe leads economic. Re-check every constraint against this assignment: Amina is not on economic (satisfied); Ben does not lead process, so constraint 2's condition never triggers (satisfied); Chloe is not on impact (satisfied); Ben is not on economic (satisfied). The assignment is unique and consistent, so the answer is B. The key techniques here are the contrapositive — turning "if Ben leads process, Chloe leads impact" plus "Chloe does not lead impact" into "Ben does not lead process" — and elimination across an exhaustive set of options. You use identical reasoning when allocating evaluation strands across a team under availability and skill constraints, or when working out which data source must feed an indicator once the alternatives are excluded. Always finish by verifying the complete solution against every original constraint: a deduction chain can feel airtight yet still hide a slip, and the final consistency check is your quality assurance step.

**Question 9 (challenging)** — Spotting a causal inference flaw

A delivery manager shows you a dashboard: "In the three months after we launched the redesigned homepage, completion rates rose from 61% to 68%. The redesign caused a seven-point improvement."

As the evaluator, which is the strongest reason to challenge this conclusion?

- A) Seven points is too small a change to matter.
- B) Completion rates should be measured weekly, not monthly.
- C) Other factors changed over the same period — such as seasonal demand or a concurrent policy change — so the comparison cannot isolate the redesign's effect.
- D) Dashboards are not a valid source of completion data.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The manager's claim is a causal one — the redesign *caused* the improvement — but the evidence is a simple before-and-after comparison. Before-and-after designs cannot distinguish the intervention's effect from everything else that changed in the same window: seasonality, marketing campaigns, policy changes, changes in the user base, or natural trends. That is the core logic of impact evaluation, and it is why techniques such as experimentation, comparison groups, or interrupted time series exist. Option A confuses statistical or practical significance with causal validity — a seven-point rise could be very meaningful if genuinely attributable. Option B is a measurement-frequency quibble that does not address attribution. Option D is false — dashboards are a legitimate descriptive source; the problem is the inference drawn from them, not the data themselves. Your role profile includes evaluating interventions using impact evaluation and experimentation with support, and this question captures the single most important habit of mind that entails: descriptive change is not causal evidence.

**Question 10 (moderate)** — Dependency logic in an evaluation plan

Your evaluation plan has five tasks with dependencies:

- Task A: Agree research questions with stakeholders (no dependencies)
- Task B: Design survey instrument (requires A)
- Task C: Obtain ethics review sign-off (requires B)
- Task D: Field the survey (requires C)
- Task E: Draft the analysis plan (requires A)

Which pairs of tasks can be worked on in parallel?

- A) B and C
- B) B and E
- C) C and D
- D) A and B

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Two tasks can run in parallel only if neither depends on the other, directly or through a chain. B requires A; E requires A; but B and E do not require each other — once A is complete, both can proceed simultaneously, so B is correct. Option A fails because C requires B: you cannot obtain ethics sign-off on a survey instrument that does not yet exist. Option C fails because D requires C: fielding before ethics sign-off would breach the process (and the data ethics principles you apply in your work). Option D fails because B requires A. Reading dependency structures is a real planning skill for evaluators: when you produce evaluation plans and plan data requirements for future phases, identifying which strands can run concurrently is how you compress timelines without cutting corners — and recognising that ethics sign-off is a hard gate before fieldwork is how you protect participants and the evaluation's integrity.

**Question 11 (challenging)** — Consistency across statements

Three statements appear in different sections of a draft evaluation report:

1. "The survey achieved a 40% response rate from the 2,000 users invited."
2. "Analysis is based on the 750 completed survey responses."
3. "Of the 800 users who responded, 50 submitted partially completed responses that were excluded."

Are these statements mutually consistent?

- A) Yes — all three are consistent.
- B) No — statements 1 and 3 conflict about the number of respondents.
- C) No — statements 2 and 3 conflict about the number excluded.
- D) No — statement 1 conflicts with statement 2 because 40% of 2,000 is 800, not 750.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Trace the numbers. Statement 1: 40% of 2,000 invited = 800 respondents. Statement 3: 800 respondents, of whom 50 were partial and excluded — leaving 800 − 50 = 750 usable responses. Statement 2: analysis based on 750 completed responses. All three fit together perfectly: 800 responded (consistent with the 40% response rate), 50 were excluded, 750 were analysed. Option D is the trap for readers who compare statement 1's implied 800 with statement 2's 750 without noticing statement 3 explains the difference. Options B and C invent conflicts that do not exist. This mirrors genuine quality assurance work: response rate is calculated on responses received, while the analytical base is completed usable responses, and a good report states both. When you peer-review colleagues' outputs, reconciling every figure that appears more than once — and checking whether an apparent discrepancy is explained elsewhere in the document — is one of the highest-value checks you can perform.

**Question 12 (challenging)** — Applying conditional ethics rules

Your department's data ethics guidance states:

- Personal data may be used in evaluation only if participants have consented OR the data has been fully anonymised.
- Anonymised data may be shared outside the evaluation team only if a data sharing agreement is in place.
- Consented personal data may never be shared outside the evaluation team.

A policy colleague outside the evaluation team asks for a dataset. The dataset is fully anonymised, and a data sharing agreement is in place. Participants did not consent to sharing.

May you share the dataset?

- A) No — participants did not consent to sharing.
- B) Yes — the data is anonymised and an agreement is in place, which satisfies the sharing rule.
- C) No — anonymised data may never leave the evaluation team.
- D) Cannot say — the rules do not cover anonymised data.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Apply each rule to the facts. The data is fully anonymised, so its use in evaluation is permitted regardless of consent (rule 1's second branch). For sharing, the relevant rule is the second: anonymised data may be shared outside the team if a data sharing agreement exists — and one does. The third rule prohibits sharing *consented personal data*; it does not apply here because what is being shared is anonymised data, not personal data relying on consent. Option A wrongly imports a consent requirement into the anonymised-data branch; once data is fully anonymised, the rules treat it under the anonymisation pathway. Option C states a prohibition the rules do not contain — rule 2 explicitly permits sharing under conditions. Option D is wrong because rule 2 directly covers this case. Your skill profile asks you to apply fundamental principles of data ethics and privacy under supervision and to share risks through appropriate channels: applying layered conditional rules precisely — neither more permissively nor more restrictively than written — is the cognitive core of that skill. In practice, you would still confirm with your senior evaluator before sharing, but the logical reading of the rules is clear.

**Question 13 (easy)** — Categorising evaluation methods

Which one of the following is NOT primarily a method for understanding whether an intervention *caused* an observed change?

- A) Randomised controlled trial
- B) Difference-in-differences comparison
- C) Descriptive statistics of service usage
- D) Matched comparison group design

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Options A, B, and D are all impact evaluation designs: each constructs a counterfactual — an estimate of what would have happened without the intervention — through randomisation, comparison over time between groups, or statistical matching. Descriptive statistics of service usage summarise what happened (volumes, rates, averages) but include no counterfactual, so they cannot by themselves attribute change to a cause. They are essential for monitoring and for process evaluation, but they answer "what is happening?" rather than "did the intervention cause it?". Your role profile lists using descriptive and inferential analytical approaches and evaluating interventions using impact evaluation: keeping the descriptive/causal boundary sharp in your mind is foundational to both, and this question checks that the boundary is instinctive.

### Preparation tips

- **Practise with your own artefacts.** Take a real theory of change, indicator register, or evaluation plan from your work and quiz yourself: which element is an output versus an outcome? Which indicator lacks a viable data source? This builds the exact pattern library the test draws on.
- **Rehearse under gentle time pressure.** Cognitive tests reward calibrated speed. Try answering questions like those above in 60 to 90 seconds each, then loosen or tighten the limit based on your accuracy.
- **Trace, don't skim, when checking.** For error-checking questions, verify every claim against every relevant cell of the data. A summary that is mostly right is still wrong.
- **Learn the one-directional rule.** Many deduction questions turn on the difference between "all X are Y" and "all Y are X". If a rule runs one way, never run it backwards.
- **Use your support network.** At your level you work with a senior digital evaluator and receive guidance and training — ask them to talk through how they check a framework or spot a causal overclaim. Hearing an expert's reasoning aloud accelerates your own.
- **Rest and settle before the test.** These assessments measure processing quality, which drops sharply when you are tired or flustered. Do the practice questions provided in the test interface — they are free calibration.

### Common pitfalls

- **Affirming the consequent.** Concluding that a service is in public beta because it has a dashboard, when the rule only says public beta requires a dashboard. Always check which direction a rule runs.
- **Substituting totals for coverage.** Counting nine extracts against a requirement of eight weekly extracts, and missing the empty week. Match the *structure* of the requirement, not just its magnitude.
- **Accepting causal language for descriptive evidence.** "Rates rose after launch" is not "the launch raised rates". In your role this pitfall has real consequences, so tests probe it deliberately.
- **Getting anchored on a time-sink question.** One convoluted matching puzzle can consume five questions' worth of time. Flag it, move on, and return if time allows.
- **Bringing outside knowledge into rule-application questions.** Apply only the rules as written, even when your workplace's real policy differs. The test measures precise rule-following, not policy recall.
- **Rushing the easy questions.** Simple sequencing and error-checking items are where careless slips cost most, because you *can* get them right. Bank those marks with care.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment evaluates your ability to analyse, interpret, and make sound decisions using numerical data of the kind your role genuinely produces and consumes. It is not a test of abstract mathematics: the emphasis is on business-relevant arithmetic — percentages, ratios, rates, averages, budgets, and projections — applied to realistic tables, charts, and dashboards. For a digital evaluator, that means performance metrics, survey response figures, take-up rates, cost-per-transaction calculations, sample sizes, and evaluation budgets.

The typical format is an online, timed multiple-choice test. Time limits are usually strict — often around a minute per question — which rewards quick, accurate data extraction rather than laborious computation. Most assessments allow or provide an onscreen calculator, because they prioritise interpretation over mental arithmetic: the hard part is deciding *which* calculation the question requires and reading the data correctly, not the arithmetic itself.

For your role, numeric reasoning is not an add-on: it is the substance of the job. When you report on the performance of products and services, you convert raw counts into rates and changes. When you support the development of a performance dashboard, you choose denominators and baselines. When you undertake quality assurance, you re-run other people's arithmetic. And when you communicate findings, you must translate numbers into accurate plain statements — a skill this assessment probes from the opposite direction, by asking you to translate statements back into numbers.

### What it measures for your role

The numeric dimensions of this assessment map onto your role profile skills as follows:

- **Evaluation delivery**: questions about descriptive statistics, rates, and comparisons reflect how you analyse and interpret quantitative data you have collected and data from other sources, using descriptive and inferential approaches.
- **Product and service monitoring**: reading dashboard-style tables, computing take-up and completion rates, and comparing indicators against targets mirror your work turning theory of change indicators into measurement frameworks and supporting performance dashboards.
- **Quality assurance of data and analysis**: several questions ask you to check whether a stated figure is consistent with the underlying data — exactly what you do when you decide whether analysis is accurate and fit for purpose, and when you peer-review colleagues' outputs.
- **Evaluation planning and strategy**: sample size, response rate, and fieldwork capacity calculations reflect the planning arithmetic behind translating stakeholder needs into feasible research designs.
- **Monitoring and evaluation across the product life cycle**: budget and projection questions echo how you plan data requirements and evaluation activity across phases.
- **Communicating analysis and insight**: distinguishing percentage points from percentages, and relative from absolute change, underpins presenting analysis in clear, non-misleading ways to specialist and non-specialist audiences.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (easy)** — Percentage change in completions

A digital licensing service recorded 8,000 completed applications in January and 9,200 in February. What was the percentage increase in completed applications?

- A) 12%
- B) 15%
- C) 20%
- D) 9.2%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Percentage change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100. The increase is 9,200 − 8,000 = 1,200. Divide by the January baseline: 1,200 ÷ 8,000 = 0.15, which is 15%. A common slip is dividing by the *new* figure: 1,200 ÷ 9,200 ≈ 13%, which does not appear among the options here but often does in real tests — always divide by the earlier (baseline) value unless the question explicitly asks otherwise. This calculation is the bread and butter of reporting on the performance of products and services: month-on-month change is usually the first thing a stakeholder asks about, and getting the baseline right is what makes your figure defensible.

**Question 2 (easy)** — Survey response rate

You invite 2,500 service users to complete a post-transaction survey. You receive 875 responses. What is the response rate?

- A) 28.6%
- B) 35%
- C) 40%
- D) 25%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Response rate = responses ÷ invitations × 100 = 875 ÷ 2,500 = 0.35 = 35%. A quick way without a calculator: 10% of 2,500 is 250, so 875 ÷ 250 = 3.5 lots of 10%, giving 35%. Option A (28.6%) is what you get if you mistakenly compute 2,500 ÷ 875 and misplace the operation. Response rates matter throughout your evaluation delivery work: they determine whether your quantitative data is likely to be representative, they feed sample size planning for future waves, and they are one of the first numbers a peer reviewer will recompute when quality assuring your survey analysis — so make computing them second nature.

**Question 3 (moderate)** — Cost per transaction across channels

Your monitoring data shows monthly figures for two channels of the same service:

| Channel | Monthly operating cost | Monthly transactions |
|---|---|---|
| Online | £48,000 | 32,000 |
| Telephone | £54,000 | 12,000 |

How much cheaper, per transaction, is the online channel than the telephone channel?

- A) £1.50
- B) £3.00
- C) £4.50
- D) £6,000

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Compute each channel's cost per transaction. Online: £48,000 ÷ 32,000 = £1.50 per transaction. Telephone: £54,000 ÷ 12,000 = £4.50 per transaction. The difference is £4.50 − £1.50 = £3.00, answer B. Options A and C are the individual channel figures — a classic distractor pattern where the test presents an intermediate result as an answer option, hoping you stop one step early. Option D subtracts the total costs, ignoring the "per transaction" requirement entirely. Cost per transaction is a standard economic indicator in government digital services and feeds economic evaluation, one of the techniques in your evaluation delivery skill. Always finish by re-reading exactly what quantity the question asks for: a difference of unit costs, not a difference of totals.

**Question 4 (moderate)** — Digital take-up rate

In one quarter, a service processed 45,600 transactions online, 11,400 by paper form, and 19,000 by telephone. What was the digital take-up rate?

- A) 55%
- B) 60%
- C) 65%
- D) 70%

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Digital take-up = digital transactions ÷ all transactions. Total transactions = 45,600 + 11,400 + 19,000 = 76,000. Take-up = 45,600 ÷ 76,000 = 0.60 = 60%. A useful sanity check: 45,600 is a bit more than half of 76,000 (half would be 38,000), and 60% of 76,000 is 45,600 exactly. The most common error is choosing the wrong denominator — for example, dividing digital by non-digital transactions (45,600 ÷ 30,400 = 1.5, i.e. a 3:2 ratio) or forgetting one channel when totalling. Take-up is a headline indicator on most government service dashboards, and when you identify and present options for measuring indicators to stakeholders, being crisp about the definition — which channels count, and what sits in the denominator — is as important as the arithmetic.

**Question 5 (moderate)** — Weighted average satisfaction

Your post-transaction survey reports satisfaction on a 5-point scale, split by channel:

| Channel | Mean satisfaction | Respondents |
|---|---|---|
| Web | 4.2 | 1,500 |
| Telephone | 3.6 | 500 |

What is the overall mean satisfaction across all 2,000 respondents?

- A) 3.90
- B) 4.05
- C) 4.10
- D) 3.95

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** You cannot simply average 4.2 and 3.6 (which gives 3.90, distractor A) because the groups are different sizes. Weight each mean by its respondent count: (4.2 × 1,500 + 3.6 × 500) ÷ 2,000 = (6,300 + 1,800) ÷ 2,000 = 8,100 ÷ 2,000 = 4.05. The result sits closer to the web figure because web respondents are three times as numerous — a sanity check worth doing every time: a weighted average always lies between the group means and nearer to the bigger group. Weighted averages appear constantly when you analyse and interpret survey data: combining channels, combining regions, or combining waves. Reporting the unweighted average of subgroup means is one of the most frequent errors you will catch when peer-reviewing colleagues' analysis, so learn to spot it both in tests and in real reports.

**Question 6 (moderate)** — Planning survey invitations

Your evaluation plan requires 400 fully completed survey responses. From previous waves you expect a 25% response rate, and you expect 80% of responses to be fully completed (the rest partially completed and unusable). How many users should you invite?

- A) 1,600
- B) 2,000
- C) 500
- D) 1,280

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Work backwards through the pipeline. Each invitation yields, on average, 0.25 responses, and each response has a 0.8 chance of being complete — so each invitation yields 0.25 × 0.8 = 0.2 usable responses. To get 400 usable responses you need 400 ÷ 0.2 = 2,000 invitations. Check forward: 2,000 invited × 25% = 500 responses; 500 × 80% = 400 completes. Correct. Option A (1,600) applies only the response rate and forgets the completion filter. Option C (500) stops after computing the responses needed. Option D multiplies incorrectly (1,600 × 0.8). This pipeline arithmetic is core to designing quantitative research with support: fieldwork budgets and timelines depend on invitation volumes, and under-inviting is the commonest reason evaluations end up with underpowered samples.

**Question 7 (moderate)** — Percentage points versus percentages

A service's completion rate rose from 64% to 72% after an assisted digital support line was introduced. Which statement is accurate?

- A) Completion rose by 8%, a relative increase of 12.5 percentage points.
- B) Completion rose by 8 percentage points, a relative increase of 12.5%.
- C) Completion rose by 12.5 percentage points, a relative increase of 8%.
- D) Completion rose by 8%, so nearly one in ten more users now complete.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The absolute change in a rate is measured in *percentage points*: 72 − 64 = 8 percentage points. The *relative* change compares that difference to the baseline: 8 ÷ 64 = 0.125 = 12.5%. Option B states both correctly. Option A swaps the units — a very common and consequential confusion. Option C reverses the two numbers. Option D uses "%" loosely for a percentage-point change and then draws a fuzzy conclusion. Why does this matter so much for you? Because your role is to present analysis in clear ways to both specialist and non-specialist audiences: a minister's office reading "completion rose 12.5%" versus "completion rose 8 percentage points" may take away different impressions, and only precise wording keeps you accurate and transparent. Numeric reasoning tests love this distinction, and so do the analysts who will peer-review your reports.

**Question 8 (moderate)** — Evaluation budget allocation

An evaluation budget of £120,000 is allocated as follows: 35% to fieldwork and data collection, 25% to analysis, 15% to reporting and dissemination, and the remainder to project management and contingency. How much is allocated to project management and contingency?

- A) £25,000
- B) £30,000
- C) £36,000
- D) £42,000

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** First find the remainder percentage: 100% − 35% − 25% − 15% = 25%. Then 25% of £120,000 = £30,000. You can verify by computing all lines: fieldwork 0.35 × 120,000 = £42,000; analysis 0.25 × 120,000 = £30,000; dissemination 0.15 × 120,000 = £18,000; sum = £90,000; remainder = £120,000 − £90,000 = £30,000. Both routes agree. Option D (£42,000) is the fieldwork line — again an intermediate figure planted as a distractor. Option C (£36,000) is 30% of the budget, a plausible-looking but unfounded percentage. Budget arithmetic like this supports your work producing evaluation plans and presenting monitoring and evaluation options to stakeholders: options usually differ in cost, and stakeholders will trust your comparisons only if your percentage allocations reconcile to the total. Always check the shares sum to 100% before computing.

**Question 9 (challenging)** — Compounded target versus actual

A service had 20,000 monthly active users at baseline. The performance target is 5% growth per month, compounded. After two months, actual monthly active users are 21,600. By how many users does actual performance fall short of the compounded target?

- A) 400
- B) 450
- C) 600
- D) 0 — the target has been met

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** The compounded target after two months is 20,000 × 1.05 × 1.05 = 20,000 × 1.1025 = 22,050. Actual is 21,600, so the shortfall is 22,050 − 21,600 = 450 users. The trap is option A: if you treat two months of 5% growth as a simple 10%, the target becomes 22,000 and the shortfall appears to be 400. Compounding matters because the second month's 5% applies to the already-grown figure (21,000), adding 1,050 rather than 1,000. Option D tempts anyone who compares actual (21,600) to the one-month target (21,000) by mistake. When you report on performance against targets — a routine part of reporting on the performance of products and services — always establish whether a growth target compounds or accumulates simply; the difference grows with every period, and stating a shortfall of 400 when it is 450 is exactly the kind of discrepancy a quality assurance review should catch.

**Question 10 (moderate)** — Fieldwork capacity planning

Your qualitative research design requires 24 user interviews. Two researchers are available, and each can conduct at most 3 interviews per working day. What is the minimum number of working days needed to complete all interviews?

- A) 3 days
- B) 4 days
- C) 6 days
- D) 8 days

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Combined daily capacity = 2 researchers × 3 interviews = 6 interviews per day. Days needed = 24 ÷ 6 = 4 working days, answer B. Option D (8) is what a single researcher would need (24 ÷ 3). Option C (6) mistakes the daily capacity for the day count. Option A (3) has no valid derivation and punishes guessing. In practice you would add buffer for no-shows and rescheduling — typically assuming 10–20% of interview slots are lost — but the question asks for the minimum, so read the question stem precisely and resist "improving" it with real-world adjustments the arithmetic does not request. This capacity calculation is the quantitative backbone of helping with research activities and other data collection: interview schedules, venue bookings, and participant incentives all flow from getting this simple division right, and from knowing whether the question at hand wants the ideal minimum or a buffered plan.

**Question 11 (challenging)** — Compound percentage decline

A service's transaction error rate was 12.5% at baseline. Process improvements reduced it by 20% in the first quarter, and then by a further 10% of its new level in the second quarter. What is the error rate after both reductions, and what is the total relative reduction from baseline?

- A) 9.0% error rate; 28% total reduction
- B) 8.75% error rate; 30% total reduction
- C) 9.0% error rate; 30% total reduction
- D) 10.0% error rate; 20% total reduction

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Apply the reductions successively to the *rate itself*. First reduction: 12.5% × (1 − 0.20) = 12.5% × 0.80 = 10.0%. Second reduction: 10.0% × (1 − 0.10) = 10.0% × 0.90 = 9.0%. So the final error rate is 9.0%. Total relative reduction from baseline = (12.5 − 9.0) ÷ 12.5 = 3.5 ÷ 12.5 = 0.28 = 28%. Successive reductions of 20% and 10% do not sum to 30% (option C's trap) because the second reduction acts on an already-reduced base: in multiplicative terms, 0.80 × 0.90 = 0.72, a 28% total reduction. Option D stops after the first quarter. Option B invents a blended figure. Note also the layered subtlety: the error rate is itself a percentage, and the reductions are percentages *of that percentage* — reading which kind of percentage a sentence means is precisely the comprehension skill that separates strong performance dashboards and reports from misleading ones, and it will be tested repeatedly.

**Question 12 (challenging)** — Break-even analysis for economic evaluation

An economic evaluation finds that each transaction completed through a new digital service saves £2.40 compared with the paper process it replaces. The digital service costs £180,000 per year to run. How many transactions per year must the service handle for its savings to cover its running costs?

- A) 43,200
- B) 60,000
- C) 75,000
- D) 90,000

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** Break-even volume = annual running cost ÷ saving per transaction = £180,000 ÷ £2.40 = 75,000 transactions. To do this cleanly without a calculator: £180,000 ÷ £2.40 is the same as 1,800,000 ÷ 24 (multiply both by 10 to clear the decimal), and 1,800,000 ÷ 24 = 75,000. Check forward: 75,000 × £2.40 = £180,000. Option A is £180,000 × 0.24, a decimal slip. Option B comes from dividing by £3, and option D from dividing by £2 — both punish careless rounding of the unit saving. Break-even reasoning is central to economic evaluation, one of the named techniques in your evaluation delivery skill: it converts an abstract "value for money" question into a concrete volume threshold that delivery teams and policy colleagues can compare against actual and forecast demand. When you present such figures, state the assumptions (constant unit saving, excluded set-up costs) so your tactical recommendations are transparent.

**Question 13 (easy)** — Reading scale qualifiers

A dashboard table is headed "Monthly active users (thousands)". It shows April: 3.2 and July: 4.8. How many more monthly active users were there in July than in April?

- A) 1.6
- B) 160
- C) 1,600
- D) 16,000

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The difference in table units is 4.8 − 3.2 = 1.6 — but the header says the figures are in *thousands*, so the real difference is 1.6 × 1,000 = 1,600 users. Option A is the raw table difference with the scale ignored; options B and D scale by the wrong power of ten. Scale qualifiers ("in thousands", "in millions", "£000s") are among the most common causes of wrong answers in numeric reasoning tests and of genuine errors in performance reporting. Build the habit of reading the full table header, axis labels, and any footnotes *before* touching the numbers — in your quality assurance work, checking units and scales is one of the fastest, highest-yield review steps you can perform on a colleague's dashboard or analysis output.

**Question 14 (challenging)** — Interpreting an A/B test

Your team runs an experiment on a service's start page. Over the same fortnight, 4,000 users see version A and 4,000 users see version B, allocated at random:

| Version | Users | Completed transactions |
|---|---|---|
| A (current) | 4,000 | 520 |
| B (redesigned) | 4,000 | 580 |

Which statement correctly describes the result?

- A) Version B's completion rate is 14.5%, an improvement of 1.5 percentage points and a relative uplift of about 11.5% over version A.
- B) Version B produced 60 more completions, an improvement of 60 percentage points.
- C) Version B's completion rate is 14.5%, a relative uplift of 1.5% over version A.
- D) Version B's completion rate is 5.8%, versus 5.2% for version A.

**Correct answer: A**

**Explanation:** Compute each completion rate. Version A: 520 ÷ 4,000 = 0.13 = 13%. Version B: 580 ÷ 4,000 = 0.145 = 14.5%. The absolute difference is 14.5 − 13 = 1.5 percentage points. The relative uplift compares that difference to A's baseline: 1.5 ÷ 13 ≈ 0.1154, about 11.5%. Option A states all three figures correctly. Option B confuses a count of 60 completions with percentage points — units matter. Option C repeats the percentage-point figure but mislabels it as the relative uplift. Option D misplaces a decimal (580 ÷ 4,000 is 0.145, not 0.058). Note what the arithmetic alone does *not* tell you: whether a 1.5-point difference could plausibly have arisen by chance. Because allocation was random and the samples are reasonably large, the design supports causal interpretation, but a statistical test would still be needed before declaring a definitive effect — your inferential analysis skill picks up where the descriptive arithmetic stops. Experimentation is a named technique in your evaluation delivery skill, and reading experiment tables precisely — rates, point differences, relative uplifts, and their limits — is how you turn an A/B test into a trustworthy tactical recommendation for the delivery team.

### Preparation tips

- **Rebuild the classic calculations until they are automatic**: percentage change, response rate, weighted average, cost per unit, take-up rate, break-even volume, and compounded growth. These seven patterns cover the large majority of role-relevant numeric questions.
- **Practise on real monitoring data.** Take a month of your own service's dashboard figures and set yourself questions: what changed, by how many percentage points, by what relative percentage, at what cost per transaction? Familiar data lowers the cognitive load of unfamiliar question formats.
- **Use the calculator for arithmetic, not for thinking.** Decide the method first — which numbers, which operation, which denominator — then compute. Reaching for the calculator before understanding the question wastes your scarcest resource, time.
- **Estimate before you calculate.** A rough answer ("about 60%", "roughly £3") catches keying errors and eliminates implausible options instantly.
- **Watch denominators like a hawk.** Most numeric distractors come from the wrong baseline or the wrong total. Say to yourself explicitly: "out of what?"
- **Time-box each question.** If a multi-stage question is consuming more than double the average time available, flag it, move on, and return. Banking three easy questions beats perfecting one hard one.
- **Verify forwards after solving backwards.** Whenever a question makes you work backwards — invitations from required completes, break-even volumes from costs — take five seconds to run the answer forwards through the scenario (2,000 invited at 25% and 80% completion really does give 400). The forward check catches almost every pipeline error and costs almost nothing.
- **Learn the distractor patterns.** Most wrong options in numeric tests are manufactured predictably: an intermediate result presented as final, the wrong denominator, an unweighted average, a simple sum of successive percentages, or a misplaced decimal. Once you can name the pattern behind each distractor, implausible options eliminate themselves before you calculate.

### Common pitfalls

- **Misreading chart and table labels** — confusing monthly with quarterly data, or ignoring scale qualifiers like "in thousands". As Question 13 shows, the arithmetic can be perfect while the answer is a thousand times out.
- **Dividing by the wrong baseline** — computing percentage change against the new value instead of the old, or building a take-up rate on the wrong total.
- **Treating percentage points and percentages as interchangeable** — an 8-point rise from 64% is a 12.5% relative rise; swapping them misleads your audience and fails the question.
- **Adding successive percentages** — 20% then 10% off is a 28% reduction, not 30%, because the second acts on a reduced base.
- **Averaging subgroup averages without weighting** — group sizes matter; the unweighted mean of means is almost always wrong.
- **Over-calculating** — performing precise multi-step computations when estimation or a divisibility check would identify the correct option in seconds.
- **Sinking time into one hard question** — a single multi-stage problem can cost you three or four easier marks; skip and return.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment evaluates how well you understand, analyse, and draw correct conclusions from written material of the kind your role actually demands. For a digital evaluator, that means dense, careful prose: government evaluation guidance, service standard documentation, departmental data policies, stakeholder emails, and draft evaluation reports. The assessment mirrors the exact reading and comprehension demands of your job — extracting rules, following conditions, distinguishing what a document states from what it merely suggests, and noticing what it does not say at all.

The classic format presents a passage followed by statements you must classify as **True** (the passage states it or it follows necessarily from the passage), **False** (the passage contradicts it), or **Cannot Say** (the passage provides insufficient information either way). Some questions instead use multiple-choice comprehension: identifying the best summary, the author's main point, or the correct application of a stated rule. Tests are usually time-pressured — often less than a minute per question — and the golden rule is absolute: answer based *strictly* on the text provided, ignoring your outside knowledge, however expert it is.

That last rule deserves emphasis for someone in your role. You know a great deal about evaluation, and in a verbal reasoning test that knowledge can betray you: a statement can be true in the real world yet "Cannot Say" against the passage, because the passage never addresses it. The discipline of separating "what this document establishes" from "what I happen to know" is not just test technique — it is the same discipline you apply when your findings must be grounded in the evidence you actually collected, not in what you expected to find.

### What it measures for your role

The verbal dimensions of this assessment map onto your role profile skills as follows:

- **Communicating analysis and insight**: comprehension questions test whether you can identify the main message of a passage and how claims are qualified — the mirror image of shaping and sharing communications relevant to the audience and presenting findings clearly to specialist and non-specialist readers.
- **Evaluation planning and strategy**: passages that set out stakeholder needs and constraints test how precisely you extract requirements — the reading half of translating stakeholder needs into research questions and evaluation approaches.
- **Data ethics and privacy**: policy-excerpt passages test whether you can apply written rules on consent, anonymisation, and data handling exactly as drafted, which reflects applying fundamental principles of data ethics and privacy under supervision.
- **Quality assurance of data and analysis**: True/False/Cannot Say discipline is quality assurance applied to text — deciding whether a claim is supported, contradicted, or simply not evidenced, exactly as you do when peer-reviewing the narrative sections of colleagues' reports.
- **Monitoring and evaluation across the product life cycle**: passages drawn from service standard documentation test your reading of phase-based requirements, supporting your work ensuring the team measures performance appropriately for the relevant phase.
- **Evaluation delivery**: distinguishing established findings from tentative or conditional statements in report passages reflects how you analyse and interpret evidence and avoid overclaiming.

### Practice questions

**Passage 1 — Departmental evaluation guidance (extract)**

"All evaluations of digital services costing over £500,000 must include both a process evaluation and an impact evaluation. Evaluations of services below this threshold must include a process evaluation, and should include an impact evaluation where feasible. A theory of change must be developed at the start of every evaluation, regardless of cost, and must be reviewed whenever the service moves between service standard phases. Economic evaluation is required only where a funding body requests it. Evaluation plans must be approved by the head of evaluation before fieldwork begins, except where fieldwork consists solely of analysis of existing administrative data, in which case approval may be granted retrospectively within twenty working days of the analysis commencing."

**Question 1 (easy)** — True, False, or Cannot Say: "A theory of change is required only for evaluations of services costing over £500,000."

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The passage states a theory of change "must be developed at the start of every evaluation, regardless of cost". The statement claims the requirement applies *only* above the threshold, which the phrase "regardless of cost" directly contradicts. When a statement narrows a universal rule ("every evaluation") to a subset ("only... over £500,000"), and the passage explicitly rejects that narrowing, the answer is False — not Cannot Say. Note the method: locate the exact sentence, compare its scope words ("every", "regardless") with the statement's scope words ("only"), and judge the match.

**Question 2 (moderate)** — True, False, or Cannot Say: "An evaluation of a £300,000 digital service that includes only a process evaluation complies with the guidance."

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** For services below the £500,000 threshold, the passage says evaluations "must include a process evaluation, and should include an impact evaluation where feasible". In UK government drafting, "must" marks a mandatory requirement while "should" marks a recommendation. A £300,000 service is below the threshold; including a process evaluation satisfies the mandatory element, and the impact evaluation is recommended ("should... where feasible") rather than required. The statement is therefore True. The distractor logic here is the must/should distinction — one of the most important reading skills in government, and one this assessment will test repeatedly. If the passage had said "must include an impact evaluation where feasible", the answer would have turned on feasibility, which the question does not address.

**Question 3 (moderate)** — True, False, or Cannot Say: "Retrospective approval of an evaluation plan is permitted where fieldwork includes a small number of user interviews alongside analysis of administrative data."

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The exception permits retrospective approval only "where fieldwork consists *solely* of analysis of existing administrative data". Fieldwork that *includes user interviews* does not consist solely of administrative data analysis, so the exception cannot apply, and the default rule — approval before fieldwork begins — governs. The word "solely" does all the work: a single word can restrict an exception to a narrow case, and careful evaluators (and careful test-takers) read exceptions more strictly than main rules. If you answered Cannot Say, you may have felt the passage does not mention mixed fieldwork — but it does not need to: "solely" excludes every mixed case by definition.

**Question 4 (challenging)** — True, False, or Cannot Say: "Economic evaluation is never included in evaluations unless a funding body requests it."

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The passage says economic evaluation "is required only where a funding body requests it". That governs when economic evaluation is *required* — it says nothing about whether teams may choose to *include* one voluntarily. "Never included unless requested" is a claim about practice and permission; "required only where requested" is a claim about obligation. The passage neither states that voluntary economic evaluations occur nor forbids them, so there is insufficient information: Cannot Say. This is the subtlest of the True/False/Cannot Say distinctions — confusing "not required" with "not done" — and it mirrors a real reporting discipline: when you communicate findings, "the guidance does not require X" and "X does not happen" are very different sentences, and only one of them is supported by the text.

**Passage 2 — Service standard performance measurement note**

"During private beta, teams must define their key performance indicators and begin collecting baseline data. Performance data collected during private beta must not be published, because the service is available only to an invited group of users and the data is unlikely to represent wider service performance. From the start of public beta, teams must publish their mandatory performance metrics quarterly. Teams may additionally publish service-specific metrics at their own discretion, provided the figures have passed internal quality assurance. Any published figure later found to be materially inaccurate must be corrected within ten working days of the inaccuracy being confirmed, and the correction must be noted on the publication page."

**Question 5 (easy)** — True, False, or Cannot Say: "Teams are permitted to publish performance data collected during private beta if it has passed internal quality assurance."

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The passage is categorical: performance data collected during private beta "must not be published". The quality assurance condition in the passage attaches to a different case — *service-specific* metrics published during public beta ("provided the figures have passed internal quality assurance"). The statement grafts that condition onto the private beta rule, creating a permission the passage nowhere grants and expressly denies. Watch for this pattern: a test statement that stitches together phrases from two different sentences of the passage. The stitched sentence sounds familiar precisely because each half is real — but the combination is false.

**Question 6 (moderate)** — True, False, or Cannot Say: "The reason private beta data must not be published is that publishing it would breach users' privacy."

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The passage gives its reason explicitly: the service "is available only to an invited group of users and the data is unlikely to represent wider service performance". The stated rationale is representativeness, not privacy. The statement asserts a different reason as *the* reason, which contradicts the passage's own explanation. Be careful: if the passage had given *no* reason, the answer would be Cannot Say — a privacy rationale might then be neither confirmed nor denied. Because the passage commits to a specific reason, a competing reason presented as the passage's rationale is False. In your own reports, this is why stating reasons matters: readers will attribute rationales to you if you leave a gap, so fill it accurately.

**Question 7 (moderate)** — True, False, or Cannot Say: "A team in public beta that publishes only its mandatory performance metrics each quarter complies with the note."

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** From the start of public beta, teams "must publish their mandatory performance metrics quarterly" — the team does this. Additional service-specific metrics are something teams "may" publish "at their own discretion"; "may" grants a permission, not a duty, so declining to publish extras breaches nothing. Every obligation in the passage that applies to this team is met, so the statement is True. The must/may distinction is the sibling of must/should from Passage 1: obligations ("must"), recommendations ("should"), and permissions ("may") form the three-level vocabulary of government drafting, and verbal reasoning tests for public sector roles lean on all three.

**Question 8 (challenging)** — A published figure was confirmed materially inaccurate on Monday 2 June. The correction was made and noted on the publication page on Friday 13 June. True, False, or Cannot Say: "The team met the correction deadline."

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The deadline is "within ten working days of the inaccuracy being confirmed". Confirmation was Monday 2 June. Counting working days after confirmation: Tuesday 3rd (1), Wednesday 4th (2), Thursday 5th (3), Friday 6th (4), Monday 9th (5), Tuesday 10th (6), Wednesday 11th (7), Thursday 12th (8), Friday 13th (9). The correction on Friday 13 June falls on the ninth working day — within ten — and it was "noted on the publication page" as required. Both conditions are met, so True. This question rewards showing your working: write the day count out rather than estimating "about two weeks", because ten working days spans two calendar weeks exactly and the margin here is a single day. Deadline arithmetic embedded in prose is common in both verbal tests and real compliance checking.

**Passage 3 — Email from a policy stakeholder**

"Thanks for sharing the interim findings. I understand the evaluation will not report final impact estimates until the autumn, and that the interim figures on take-up are descriptive only. However, the minister's office has asked for a one-page summary next week. Could you draft something that sets out the take-up trend so far, notes clearly that no causal conclusions can yet be drawn, and lists the outcome measures the final report will cover? Please do not include the regional breakdown — the sample sizes are too small for reliable regional figures, as your team advised us last month. If your head of evaluation is content, we would also like to reference the planned autumn publication date."

**Question 9 (easy)** — True, False, or Cannot Say: "The stakeholder is asking the evaluator to present causal conclusions from the interim data."

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The email asks for the opposite: a draft that "notes clearly that no causal conclusions can yet be drawn". The stakeholder explicitly builds the evaluator's caveat into the request. This statement tests whether you read the request itself or a stereotype of stakeholder pressure — a reminder that in verbal reasoning, as in stakeholder management, you must respond to what was actually written. The email is a model of a well-behaved request; recognising that accurately is as important as spotting a problematic one.

**Question 10 (moderate)** — True, False, or Cannot Say: "The evaluation team previously advised that regional sample sizes were too small for reliable figures."

**Correct answer: True**

**Explanation:** The email says: "the sample sizes are too small for reliable regional figures, as your team advised us last month". The phrase "as your team advised us last month" attributes the advice to the evaluation team and dates it, so the statement is directly supported. A subtle point: you are taking the *email's* word for this — the passage is the evidence, and within the world of the passage the attribution is stated as fact, which is what True/False/Cannot Say questions ask you to judge. Contrast this with Question 11, where the email stops short of establishing the claim.

**Question 11 (challenging)** — True, False, or Cannot Say: "The head of evaluation has approved referencing the autumn publication date."

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The email says: "If your head of evaluation is content, we would also like to reference the planned autumn publication date." The word "if" marks this as a condition that has not yet been resolved — the stakeholder is requesting a check, not reporting an outcome. Nothing in the passage says whether the head of evaluation has been asked, has agreed, or has refused, so the truth of the statement cannot be determined: Cannot Say. If you answered False, you overreached the other way — the passage does not deny approval either; it is simply silent on the outcome. Conditional and future-referring phrases ("if", "subject to", "we would like") are the most reliable signposts of Cannot Say territory.

**Question 12 (moderate)** — Which of the following best summarises what the stakeholder is requesting?

- A) A one-page summary of final impact estimates, including regional breakdowns, for the minister's office.
- B) A one-page summary of the descriptive take-up trend with clear causal caveats and a list of forthcoming outcome measures, excluding regional breakdowns.
- C) Early publication of the final report to meet the minister's timetable.
- D) A revised evaluation plan with larger regional sample sizes.

**Correct answer: B**

**Explanation:** Map each option to the email's wording. Option B matches point for point: "sets out the take-up trend so far" (descriptive trend), "notes clearly that no causal conclusions can yet be drawn" (causal caveats), "lists the outcome measures the final report will cover" (forthcoming measures), "do not include the regional breakdown" (exclusion). Option A contradicts the email twice — final impact estimates are not available until autumn, and the regional breakdown is expressly excluded. Option C invents an acceleration of publication the email never requests; referencing a planned date is not early publication. Option D turns a stated limitation into a request for redesign, which appears nowhere. Best-summary questions reward elimination by contradiction: strike every option containing one detail the passage rules out, and what remains is the answer.

**Passage 4 — Data ethics and privacy policy (extract)**

"Research participants must be informed of the purpose of data collection before data is collected, except where the research uses only data already published by the department in aggregate form. Consent must be recorded in writing or by an auditable digital equivalent. Participants may withdraw consent at any time before the analysis dataset is finalised; after finalisation, withdrawal requests must still be acknowledged, but data already incorporated may be retained where removal would compromise the integrity of the analysis. Interview recordings must be deleted within three months of transcription. Anonymised transcripts may be retained for up to five years for secondary analysis, subject to annual review."

**Question 13 (moderate)** — True, False, or Cannot Say: "A participant who withdraws consent after the analysis dataset is finalised is entitled to have their data removed from the dataset."

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** After finalisation, the passage says withdrawal requests "must still be acknowledged, but data already incorporated may be retained where removal would compromise the integrity of the analysis". Retention is expressly permitted in that circumstance, so an unconditional entitlement to removal contradicts the passage. Read the structure carefully: the participant's guaranteed right after finalisation is acknowledgement, not removal. Notice too what would make this Cannot Say — if the statement had said "will have their data removed", the answer would depend on whether removal would compromise integrity, which the passage leaves open; but "is entitled to" asserts an unconditional right the passage denies. Precision about what a policy guarantees, permits, and denies is the reading core of applying data ethics principles in your work.

**Question 14 (challenging)** — True, False, or Cannot Say: "Anonymised interview transcripts must be deleted after five years."

**Correct answer: Cannot Say**

**Explanation:** The passage says anonymised transcripts "may be retained for up to five years for secondary analysis, subject to annual review". This sets a *maximum retention period for the secondary-analysis purpose* — it permits retention up to five years; it does not state what must happen at the five-year point, nor whether retention for other purposes under other provisions is possible. "May be retained for up to five years" is a permission with a ceiling, not a deletion mandate. The statement converts a bounded permission into an obligation ("must be deleted"), and the passage neither asserts nor contradicts that obligation in terms — deletion at five years is consistent with the passage but not compelled by its words. When a statement is a plausible *implementation* of a rule rather than a *restatement* of it, the disciplined answer is Cannot Say.

**Passage 5 — Draft evaluation report, executive summary (extract)**

"Take-up of the online renewal service grew steadily over the twelve-month evaluation period, reaching 71% of all renewals by month twelve. Survey evidence indicates that users who received the reminder email were more likely to renew online than those who did not, although the reminder was not randomly allocated and the two groups differ in age profile. Qualitative interviews suggest that confidence with online forms, rather than awareness of the service, is the principal barrier among users who continue to renew by post. The evaluation found no evidence that the assisted digital telephone route was used by the user groups it was designed for, but call volumes were only available for seven of the twelve months. We recommend that the reminder email be tested through a randomised trial in the next phase, and that assisted digital call data be collected consistently before conclusions are drawn about that route."

**Question 15 (moderate)** — True, False, or Cannot Say: "The evaluation established that the reminder email caused users to renew online."

**Correct answer: False**

**Explanation:** The passage reports an association — users who received the reminder "were more likely to renew online" — and then immediately withholds causal endorsement: "the reminder was not randomly allocated and the two groups differ in age profile", and the report recommends the email "be tested through a randomised trial in the next phase". A document that flags non-random allocation, names a confounder, and calls for a trial is explicitly declining to claim causation, so a statement that the evaluation "established" a causal effect contradicts the passage's own framing. This is the verbal-reasoning twin of the cognitive section's causal inference question: strong association language plus explicit design caveats equals no causal claim.

**Question 16 (challenging)** — Which of the following is the most defensible conclusion from the passage about the assisted digital telephone route?

- A) The assisted digital route failed and should be decommissioned.
- B) The intended user groups did not need an assisted digital route.
- C) The available evidence found no sign of intended use, but the data is incomplete, so conclusions should wait for consistent call data.
- D) The route was used mainly by user groups it was not designed for.

**Correct answer: C**

**Explanation:** The passage makes two moves that must be held together: it "found no evidence that the assisted digital telephone route was used by the user groups it was designed for", and it notes that "call volumes were only available for seven of the twelve months", recommending consistent collection "before conclusions are drawn about that route". Option C reproduces exactly this pairing — a null finding plus an explicit data limitation and a recommendation to withhold judgement. Option A leaps from absence of evidence to a decommissioning decision the report never makes. Option B converts "no evidence of use" into "no need", a different claim entirely. Option D asserts who *did* use the route, about which the passage says nothing. Absence of evidence, evidence of absence, and incomplete data are three different things, and your role — deciding if data and analysis are accurate and fit for purpose — depends on keeping them separate under time pressure, exactly as this question requires.

### Preparation tips

- **Train the three-word vocabulary.** "Must" obliges, "should" recommends, "may" permits. Before your assessment, skim any UK government service manual or guidance page and highlight every must/should/may — then say aloud what each sentence does and does not require.
- **Practise the Cannot Say discipline.** For every statement, ask two separate questions: "Does the passage support this?" and "Does the passage contradict this?" Only if both answers are no is the answer Cannot Say. Most errors come from collapsing these into a single vague impression.
- **Anchor every answer to exact words.** Get in the habit of pointing (literally, with your cursor) at the phrase that decides the answer. If you cannot find the deciding phrase, you are probably importing outside knowledge.
- **Beware stitched statements.** Test-writers combine two genuine phrases from different sentences into one false claim. Verify that the *connection* between clauses exists in the passage, not just the clauses themselves.
- **Manage reading time deliberately.** Skim the passage once for structure (what does each sentence govern?), then read each statement and return to the relevant sentence for careful comparison. Reading the passage exhaustively before seeing the questions usually wastes time.
- **Use your day job as training.** Every time you read evaluation guidance or a data policy this month, draft two True/False/Cannot Say statements about it and test a colleague. Writing the questions teaches the patterns faster than answering them.

### Common pitfalls

- **Bringing in outside knowledge.** You may know how evaluations really work in your department; the test scores you on the passage alone. Expertise makes this pitfall more dangerous for you, not less.
- **Confusing "not required" with "not done"** — and, more generally, confusing obligation, permission, and practice. A passage about what must happen rarely tells you what actually happens.
- **Missing restrictive words.** "Solely", "only", "except", "regardless" — small words that flip answers. Slow down whenever you meet one.
- **Treating a stated likelihood as a fact.** "The data is unlikely to represent wider performance" is a qualified claim; a statement rendering it as certainty ("the data does not represent...") overstates it.
- **Answering the stereotype instead of the sentence.** Assuming stakeholder emails must contain pressure, or policies must forbid things, leads you to answer a passage that was never written.
- **Poor time allocation.** Spending three minutes perfecting one Cannot Say judgement while three easy True items go unanswered. Flag, move, return.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment evaluates your decision-making, interpersonal skills, and professional conduct through realistic workplace dilemmas. Unlike the cognitive, numeric, and verbal assessments, there is often no single objectively right answer: several responses may seem reasonable, and the assessment measures how well your choices align with the values and behaviours the organisation needs from someone in your role. For a digital evaluator in UK government, those values include putting user needs first, transparency and honesty about evidence, collaboration across disciplines, accessibility and inclusion, protecting research participants, and using public money responsibly.

The typical format presents a short scenario followed by several possible actions. You are asked either to select the **most effective** and **least effective** actions, or to rank or rate all the options from best to worst. Timing is usually generous or untimed, because the assessment aims to capture your natural judgement rather than your speed. There is no trick: read the scenario carefully, notice who is affected and what is at stake, and choose the responses that address the real problem constructively.

For your role level, scenarios will typically involve the working relationships named in your role profile: the senior digital evaluator you work with, delivery and policy teams, research participants, analysts whose work you peer-review, and stakeholders who receive your findings. Strong answers tend to share a shape: they engage with the problem rather than avoiding it, they are honest about evidence and its limits, they use the support structures around you (you are expected to receive guidance — using it is a strength, not a weakness), and they protect users, participants, and the integrity of the evaluation.

### What it measures for your role

The judgement dimensions of this assessment map onto your role profile skills as follows:

- **Communicating analysis and insight**: scenarios about presenting findings test whether you shape communications to the audience honestly — including when findings are unwelcome or when someone asks you to overstate them.
- **Data ethics and privacy**: scenarios about participant data, consent, and access test whether you apply ethical principles under supervision and share risks through appropriate channels rather than sitting on them.
- **Evaluation planning and strategy**: scenarios about stakeholder requests test how you work with stakeholders to understand their needs and influence evaluation decisions with support, balancing responsiveness with methodological integrity.
- **Quality assurance of data and analysis**: scenarios about errors — yours and colleagues' — test how you handle quality problems constructively, including peer review and owning mistakes.
- **Evaluation delivery**: scenarios about fieldwork difficulties and analytical disagreements test practical judgement in collecting and interpreting data with support.
- **Monitoring and evaluation across the product life cycle**: scenarios involving delivery deadlines and phase pressures test how you keep measurement appropriate to the phase while collaborating with the delivery team.

### Practice questions

**Question 1 (moderate)** — Pressure to soften a finding

You have drafted a performance report showing that completion rates for a service fell for the third consecutive month. The delivery manager, who is friendly and under pressure, asks you to "lead with the positives and maybe leave the completion trend for the next report, once we've fixed the form". Your senior digital evaluator is on leave until next week; the report is due in three days.

- A) Agree to move the completion trend to the next report, since the fix may resolve it anyway.
- B) Keep the completion trend in the report, presented accurately alongside the genuine positives and the delivery team's planned fix, and tell the delivery manager this is what you intend to do.
- C) Refuse outright and tell the delivery manager that asking you to change a report is inappropriate.
- D) Delay the report until your senior evaluator returns so they can decide.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A.**

**Explanation:** Option B does everything the situation requires: it preserves the integrity and transparency of the evidence (the trend stays in), it is fair to the delivery team (the positives and the planned fix are reported too, which is accurate context rather than spin), and it is open with the stakeholder about what you will do. Note that including the planned fix actually serves the delivery manager's legitimate interest better than omission would — transparency and empathy are not opposites. Option A is least effective: omitting an unwelcome trend from a performance report is exactly the compromise of honesty that evaluation exists to prevent, and "we'll fix it soon" is a prediction, not a finding. Option C protects the evidence but damages the relationship needlessly — the manager made a request under pressure, not a demand for misconduct; a collaborative explanation is available and better. Option D avoids the decision and misses the deadline; escalation is for genuine impasses, and a three-day timeline with a clear right answer is not one. If the manager insisted after your explanation, contacting your senior evaluator or their cover for advice would then be proportionate.

**Question 2 (moderate)** — A participant becomes uncomfortable

During a user research interview you are helping to run, a participant becomes visibly upset while describing their difficulty using a benefits service, and says, "I'm not sure I should be telling you all this." The interview is being recorded with their prior consent, and you still have half your questions to cover.

- A) Pause the recording, remind them participation is voluntary, that they can skip questions, take a break, or stop entirely, and let them decide how to proceed.
- B) Move quickly to the next question to take their mind off the distressing topic.
- C) Reassure them their answers are really valuable and encourage them to continue so the data is complete.
- D) End the interview immediately and delete the recording to be safe.

**Most effective: A. Least effective: C.**

**Explanation:** Option A applies the core ethics of research with people: consent is ongoing, not a signature captured at the start. Pausing the recording, restating their control (skip, break, stop), and letting them choose respects their autonomy and wellbeing while keeping the door open to continuing — many participants, once their control is affirmed, are happy to carry on. Option C is least effective because it uses the value of the data to pressure a distressed person to continue — putting the evaluation's needs above the participant's, which inverts the ethical priority and is precisely what "apply fundamental principles of data ethics" guards against. Option B ignores the participant's expressed doubt; changing the subject is avoidance, not care, and their consent question remains unaddressed. Option D is well-meant but overshoots: unilaterally ending and deleting removes the participant's choice in the other direction, discards data they may be content to give, and the deletion decision should follow your consent procedures rather than an on-the-spot instinct. Afterwards, mention the episode to the senior evaluator you work with — sharing how it was handled is part of sharing data ethics risks through appropriate channels.

**Question 3 (challenging)** — You find an error in your own published work

A month after your team's quarterly performance report was published, you discover that you made a formula error: digital take-up was published as 64% but is actually 59%. The corrected figure weakens a story the department has been telling about channel shift. Nobody else has noticed.

- A) Correct the formula in your working files so future reports are right, and say nothing about the published figure since the next report will supersede it.
- B) Tell your senior digital evaluator immediately, show them the working, and support the standard correction process for the published figure.
- C) Wait to see whether anyone queries the figure; if they do, correct it promptly.
- D) Quietly update the published figure without drawing attention to the change.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C.**

**Explanation:** Option B is the professional gold standard and should feel like the obvious move: errors happen, and the measure of an analyst is what they do next. Reporting it immediately to the senior evaluator you work with, evidence in hand, engages the correction process (published statistics are typically corrected with a note, as transparency requires), protects the department from continuing to repeat a wrong number, and builds rather than destroys trust — colleagues learn that your figures are self-correcting. Option C is least effective because it converts an honest error into concealment-by-waiting: every week the figure stands, decisions and communications compound on it, and discovery by someone else later is far more damaging than self-report now. Option A fixes the future while knowingly leaving the public record wrong — the published 64% continues to mislead. Option D corrects the number but hides the correction, breaching the transparency norm that published corrections are noted; silent edits, if discovered, look like manipulation even when motivated by embarrassment. The fact that the true figure weakens a favoured narrative changes nothing — that is exactly when the integrity of evaluation matters most.

**Question 4 (moderate)** — Stakeholder wants an evaluation to "prove" success

A policy colleague asks you to help design monitoring for a new digital service, opening with: "We need an evaluation that shows this works — the minister has committed to it publicly." Your senior evaluator has asked you to lead the first scoping conversation.

- A) Explain that evaluation must be designed to find out whether the service works, not to show that it does — then work with the colleague to identify what success would look like, what indicators would evidence it, and what the risks of an honest evaluation are for them.
- B) Decline to work with the colleague and report the conversation to your senior evaluator as an integrity risk.
- C) Design the monitoring around the indicators most likely to show positive movement, since take-up typically grows in a new service's first year anyway.
- D) Agree to the framing for now to keep the relationship warm, and plan to fix the design later once trust is established.

**Most effective: A. Least effective: C.**

**Explanation:** Option A treats the colleague's words as what they usually are — a loosely-phrased expression of political pressure, not a corruption attempt — and responds by doing your actual job: working with stakeholders to understand their needs and translating them into research questions and evaluation approaches. Explaining the purpose of evaluation while engaging seriously with their situation (what success looks like, which indicators, what honest findings would mean) turns a bad framing into a good scoping conversation. Option C is least effective: deliberately selecting indicators for their likelihood of looking positive is designing bias into the measurement framework — a corruption of evaluation that no relationship benefit can justify, and one that typically unravels at service assessment. Option D concedes the framing and hopes to renegotiate later, but designs agreed early are hard to unwind, and "fix it later" rarely survives delivery pressure. Option B escalates before attempting the conversation you were asked to lead; mentioning the exchange to your senior evaluator afterwards is sensible and proportionate, but refusing to engage treats a colleague as an adversary and forfeits your influence on the design — influencing evaluation decisions with support is precisely your role.

**Question 5 (easy)** — Unsure how to run an unfamiliar analysis

You have been asked to run a difference-in-differences analysis for the first time. You have read about the method but never applied it. The analysis is due in two weeks, and your senior digital evaluator has capacity to help.

- A) Attempt it alone using online tutorials; asking for help two years into the job would look bad.
- B) Ask the senior evaluator to do it for you, since they would be faster.
- C) Ask the senior evaluator for an early session to agree the approach, attempt the analysis yourself, and book a checkpoint for them to review your working before the deadline.
- D) Suggest switching to a simple before-and-after comparison, which you already know how to do.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: D.**

**Explanation:** Your role level explicitly includes receiving guidance and training to support your work and develop your skills — using that support intelligently is what the level is *for*. Option C is the model: you take ownership of the work (developing your own capability), you de-risk it early (agreeing the approach before investing two weeks), and you build in quality assurance (a review checkpoint before the deadline). Option D is least effective because it quietly downgrades the analysis to fit your current comfort: a before-and-after comparison answers a weaker question than difference-in-differences (it loses the comparison group that supports causal interpretation), so you would be trading the evaluation's validity for your convenience — and doing so without flagging the trade-off to anyone. Option A gambles the deadline on unsupported self-teaching and mistakes asking for help for weakness; in analytical professions the opposite is true. Option B keeps the deadline safe but wastes the development opportunity and leaves you no more capable next time. Choosing C is not just the "nice" answer — it is the one that best serves the evaluation, your team, and your growth simultaneously.

**Question 6 (challenging)** — Peer review finds a problem in a friend's analysis

You are peer-reviewing descriptive analysis by a colleague you get on well with. You find that they have excluded three weeks of data as "outliers" without documented justification, and the exclusion flatters the trend. The report using this analysis goes to the programme board on Friday.

- A) Approve the analysis — your colleague is experienced and presumably had good reasons for the exclusion.
- B) Raise the exclusion with your colleague directly: ask for the justification, explain that it must be documented and the sensitivity shown (results with and without the excluded weeks), and agree what needs to change before Friday.
- C) Report the exclusion to your senior evaluator as suspected data manipulation before speaking to your colleague.
- D) Rerun the entire analysis yourself from scratch and submit your version instead.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A.**

**Explanation:** Peer review exists to catch exactly this. Option B does what your quality assurance skill describes — peer-review colleagues' outputs to ensure quality — in the way most likely to fix the problem and preserve the relationship: you ask before you accuse (there may be a legitimate reason, such as a recording outage in those weeks), you name the standard (undocumented exclusions are unacceptable regardless of motive; sensitivity analysis shows the audience what the exclusion does), and you keep the Friday deadline in view. Option A is least effective because it substitutes trust for review: "presumably had good reasons" is precisely the assumption peer review exists to replace with evidence, and friendship makes rigour more important, not less. Option C escalates on the worst interpretation before giving your colleague the chance to explain — if the exclusion turns out to be justified but undocumented, you have damaged trust for nothing; escalation becomes right only if the colleague resists correcting a real problem. Option D fixes the numbers but bypasses the colleague entirely, duplicates work days before a deadline, and turns review into replacement — teaching nobody anything and souring the team. Directness with respect beats both silence and escalation-first.

**Question 7 (moderate)** — Competing requests, one afternoon

At 14:00, three things land at once: the delivery team asks you to check "odd-looking" dashboard figures before their 16:00 show-and-tell; a policy stakeholder asks for the sample size of last quarter's survey for a briefing tomorrow morning; and your senior evaluator asks for comments on a draft evaluation plan by end of week.

- A) Work on the evaluation plan comments first, since they come from your senior evaluator.
- B) Check the dashboard figures first (a wrong figure shown at 16:00 damages trust in the data), send the sample size afterwards (a two-minute lookup well before tomorrow), and schedule the plan comments for later in the week.
- C) Send the sample size first because it is quickest, then the dashboard, then the plan.
- D) Ask your senior evaluator which task to do first.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A.**

**Explanation:** Rank by deadline and by the cost of failure. The dashboard check has the nearest hard deadline (16:00) and the highest stakes: if wrong figures are presented, the team either misinforms its audience or loses confidence in its own monitoring — and checking possible data errors is squarely your quality assurance job. The sample size is genuinely urgent (tomorrow morning) but tiny; it fits comfortably after the dashboard work, still hours before it is needed. The plan comments have the furthest deadline (end of week). So B is the effective sequence. Option C is seductive — clearing the quick task first feels productive — and defensible if it truly takes two minutes; but as a strategy it is weaker than B stated, because it delays the one task that could miss its deadline for a task that cannot. Option A is least effective: it ranks tasks by the seniority of the requester rather than by deadline and consequence, missing the 16:00 window entirely for work due Friday — seniority is not urgency, and your senior evaluator would say so. Option D delegates upwards a prioritisation you are equipped to make; asking is reasonable when priorities genuinely conflict, but these do not — they sequence cleanly.

**Question 8 (challenging)** — Asked to share participant data

An analyst from another team emails you: "Can you send over the interview transcripts from your evaluation? We're doing related work and it would save us re-interviewing the same users." The transcripts are pseudonymised but contain detailed personal stories. Participants consented to their data being used "for this evaluation".

- A) Send the transcripts — the analyst is a civil servant working on related public business, and re-interviewing users wastes their time.
- B) Refuse and do not respond further, since the request was improper.
- C) Explain that the consent covers this evaluation only, so you cannot share the transcripts as they stand — then raise the request with your senior evaluator to explore legitimate routes, such as fresh consent, anonymised summaries, or sharing findings rather than data.
- D) Send heavily redacted excerpts, removing the most personal passages yourself.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: A.**

**Explanation:** The deciding fact is the consent wording: participants agreed to use "for this evaluation" — not for related work by other teams. Option C honours that limit (the immediate answer is no), treats the requester as a colleague with a legitimate need rather than a threat (there are proper routes: re-consent, anonymised outputs, sharing findings), and uses the channel your role profile names — sharing data ethics and privacy risks through appropriate channels by involving your senior evaluator. Option A is least effective: good intentions and shared employment do not expand consent, pseudonymised detailed stories carry real re-identification risk, and "it saves time" is never a lawful basis — this is the scenario's core test. Option D is a subtler failure: redaction performed unilaterally still shares data beyond its consent, and you are not positioned to judge alone what makes a personal narrative safe. Option B gets the refusal right and everything else wrong — the request is a normal one that deserves a helpful answer, the analyst's underlying need (avoiding duplicate burden on the same users) is a genuinely good instinct worth solving properly, and silence teaches nothing and helps nobody. The strongest professionals say no to the mechanism while saying yes to the mission.

**Question 9 (moderate)** — Findings challenged in a meeting

Presenting interim findings to the delivery team, you show that users over 65 abandon the online form at twice the average rate. A senior developer interrupts: "That contradicts our analytics — I think your survey is just biased." Several people look at you, waiting.

- A) Defend your survey firmly and point out that you are the evaluation specialist in the room.
- B) Concede the point and offer to remove the finding from the interim report.
- C) Take the challenge seriously and openly: acknowledge that survey and analytics measure differently, offer to compare the two sources with the developer after the meeting, and note what each source can and cannot show.
- D) Move on to the next slide to avoid a public disagreement.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: B.**

**Explanation:** A challenge to your data in public is an opportunity to model how evidence works. Option C neither capitulates nor entrenches: it treats the discrepancy as an empirical question (two sources disagreeing is information, not an attack — perhaps analytics measures sessions while your survey measures people, or abandonment is defined differently), commits to a concrete reconciliation with the challenger, and is honest about the limits of both sources. This is communicating analysis to a specialist audience under pressure, and collaborating across disciplines rather than defending turf. Option B is least effective: withdrawing a finding because someone frowned at it — with no evidence the finding is wrong — abandons users over 65 whose difficulties the finding represents, and signals that your reports bend to whoever pushes; the accessibility and inclusion stakes make this concession particularly costly. Option A escalates a data question into a status contest; being right about the survey will not matter if the room learns that questioning you triggers defensiveness. Option D dodges in full view — the challenge stands unanswered, which most audiences read as conceded, and the developer's reasonable point never gets its proper investigation. Curiosity in public, reconciliation in private, and the finding stands or falls on evidence.

**Question 10 (moderate)** — Deadline versus data quality

The programme board meets Monday. On Thursday afternoon you discover that the latest month of data in your performance report contains a known collection fault: roughly 8% of transactions were not logged during a system migration. Correcting the data properly will take until Tuesday. Your report is otherwise complete.

- A) Publish the report as it is — 8% is small, and the board needs numbers.
- B) Present the report with the affected month clearly flagged: state the undercount, its cause and approximate size, present that month's figures as provisional, and give the date corrected figures will be available.
- C) Withdraw the report from the board agenda until the data is corrected.
- D) Adjust the affected month's figures upwards by 8% to compensate for the undercount.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: D.**

**Explanation:** The board's real need is not "numbers" but "an accurate picture on which to make decisions" — and an accurate picture can include known imperfection, honestly described. Option B delivers everything available on time: eleven clean months, one provisional month with the fault's cause and magnitude stated, and a firm date for correction. This is transparency in exactly the form decision-makers need, and it is what your quality assurance skill — deciding whether data is fit for purpose — looks like when the answer is "partly". Option D is least effective and worth dwelling on: inflating figures by "roughly 8%" manufactures data. The undercount is approximate and probably uneven across transaction types; a silent adjustment presents invented precision as measurement, and if the adjustment ever surfaces, every figure you have published becomes suspect. (A documented, methodologically justified estimation could be legitimate — but not invented on a Thursday and not silently.) Option A publishes figures you know are wrong without saying so — an 8% undercount can easily reverse a month-on-month trend. Option C sacrifices eleven months of sound evidence and the board's decision cycle to perfect one month, when flagging achieves the same integrity at no cost. Report what you know, including what you know is broken.

**Question 11 (easy)** — Credit for shared work

Your senior digital evaluator presents your joint evaluation findings to a director. Afterwards, the director emails the team praising "an excellent piece of analysis" and naming only the senior evaluator, though you built the measurement framework and ran most of the analysis. You feel deflated.

- A) Reply-all to the director's email listing the parts of the work that were yours.
- B) Say nothing; credit flows upwards and complaining looks petty.
- C) Mention it to your senior evaluator directly and without accusation — you were glad the work landed well, and you'd appreciate your contribution being visible in future.
- D) Mention the situation to other colleagues so at least the team knows the work was yours.

**Most effective: C. Least effective: D.**

**Explanation:** Option C addresses the right person, in the right channel, with the right tone. Most credit omissions are thoughtless rather than malicious, and a direct, unaccusatory conversation gives your senior evaluator the chance to fix it — good seniors will, often immediately and publicly. It also practises a skill your career genuinely needs: raising interpersonal issues early, specifically, and without heat. Option D is least effective because it routes the grievance everywhere except where it can be resolved: corridor versions of "who really did the work" curdle team trust, invite sides-taking, and will likely reach your senior evaluator in distorted form — the worst possible opening for the honest conversation you needed anyway. Option A makes a director's praise thread into a public correction of your own senior colleague — the substance may be fair but the forum guarantees embarrassment and reads as self-promotion. Option B swallows the problem, which works once but compounds: visibility of your contributions is legitimate career capital at your level, and silently ceding it breeds the resentment that eventually leaks out badly. Direct, private, prompt, and generous — that combination wins.

**Question 12 (challenging)** — Theory of change disagreement

In a theory of change workshop you are facilitating with support, the delivery team insists that "increased site traffic" should be recorded as a key outcome of their awareness campaign. You believe traffic is an output at best, and that the outcome should be something like "more eligible users start an application". The room is getting impatient and the product manager says, "Let's just write traffic down as the outcome and move on."

- A) Write traffic down as the outcome to keep the workshop moving; the wording can be tidied later.
- B) Hold the distinction, briefly and concretely: ask "if traffic doubled but applications didn't move, would the campaign have worked?" — then propose recording traffic as an output leading to the application outcome, and move on.
- C) Insist on the methodologically correct answer and explain output/outcome definitions until the room accepts them.
- D) Defer the whole question to your senior evaluator after the workshop.

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A.**

**Explanation:** The output/outcome distinction is not pedantry — it determines what the team will measure and celebrate. If traffic is enshrined as the outcome, the measurement framework that follows will declare the campaign successful when visits rise, even if not one additional eligible user applies. Option B protects that substance at minimal social cost: the "if traffic doubled but applications didn't move" question makes the abstract distinction concrete in one sentence, the proposed chain (traffic as output leading to application starts as outcome) gives the team their metric *and* the right one, and the workshop moves on. This is developing a theory of change through discussion with stakeholders, with support — influence through a well-chosen question rather than authority. Option A is least effective because workshop artefacts harden: "tidied later" rarely happens, and the flawed chain propagates into indicators, dashboards, and eventually a service assessment where the gap gets found. Option C is right on substance, wrong on method — a definitional lecture to an impatient room loses the audience and, with it, the point. Option D outsources a call you are equipped to make in the moment and lets the workshop conclude with the wrong chain on the wall; consulting your senior evaluator afterwards about how it went is good practice, but the facilitation moment was yours.

### Preparation tips

- **Anchor yourself in the values before the test.** Re-read the Civil Service values and your framework's emphases: user needs, honesty about evidence, collaboration, accessibility and inclusion, protecting participants. Strong options serve these; weak options serve convenience, appearances, or comfort.
- **Look for the option that engages the problem directly and constructively.** Across these twelve questions, notice the pattern: the most effective options address the issue with the right person, honestly, and soon. The least effective ones conceal, delay, comply against the evidence, or route the problem away from where it can be solved.
- **Use your support structures in your answers.** At your level, involving your senior digital evaluator appropriately is a strength — but notice the difference between *consulting* them (strong) and *delegating your judgement* to them when a task is within your competence (weak).
- **Read what the scenario actually says.** Just as in verbal reasoning, do not answer a stereotype. A stakeholder asking a clumsy question is not a villain; a friendly colleague's analysis still needs full review.
- **Rate options against the scenario's real stakes.** Ask: who is affected — users, participants, the public record, the team? The best option usually protects the most vulnerable interest at the least relational cost.
- **Be honest, not idealistic.** Choose what would genuinely work in a real office, not what sounds saintly. Assessors score practical effectiveness, and "raise it directly and kindly" usually beats both "report it upwards immediately" and "let it go".

### Common pitfalls

- **Choosing passive options.** Waiting to see if anyone notices, moving to the next slide, deferring whole decisions upwards — options that delay action or pass the problem along score poorly, because they leave the situation unowned.
- **Answering idealistically rather than effectively.** "Refuse and report" sounds principled but often skips the direct conversation that would actually fix the issue. Effectiveness includes the relationship that remains afterwards.
- **Complying to please.** Softening findings, conceding to a public challenge, or writing the wrong outcome on the wall to keep a meeting happy — agreeableness that costs the evidence its integrity is the most heavily penalised pattern for evaluation roles.
- **Escalating first.** Escalation is right when direct engagement has failed or the issue exceeds your remit; as a first move against a colleague it signals poor judgement and damages trust.
- **Ignoring your role level.** Answering as though you were the head of evaluation (unilateral decisions, no consultation) or as though you were helpless (everything referred upwards) both misjudge a level defined by working with a senior evaluator and receiving guidance while owning real work.
- **Forgetting the participant and the public.** In ethics-flavoured scenarios, options are ultimately ranked by how well they protect research participants and the accuracy of the public record — not by internal convenience, however compelling.
