Psychometric assessments are structured, standardised exercises that measure the mental processes and judgement your role demands. For a senior digital evaluator, that means far more than abstract puzzles. A well-designed, job-specific assessment mirrors the real substance of your work: interrogating a theory of change for logical gaps, spotting an inconsistency in a measurement framework, interpreting service performance data under time pressure, reading dense government evaluation guidance with precision, and making sound judgement calls when stakeholders, budgets, ethics, and evidence pull in different directions.

Employers and government departments use these assessments because they are among the most reliable predictors of on-the-job performance, and because they offer a fair, consistent way to compare people against the demands of a role rather than against each other's backgrounds. As someone who manages evaluations, assures the work of other evaluators, and influences stakeholders to resource evaluation properly, you will recognise the underlying principle: decisions are better when they are grounded in structured, comparable evidence. ## Workplace job-specific cognitive assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific cognitive ability assessment measures the exact mental processes your role requires, using practical scenarios and data rather than abstract IQ-style puzzles. For a senior digital evaluator, the assessment typically draws on the artefacts you handle every working week: theories of change, logic models, evaluation plans, measurement frameworks, data quality specifications, service dashboards, and analytical outputs produced by evaluators whose work you assure.

The typical format is an online, timed test lasting 15 to 30 minutes, often with 15 to 25 questions. You will usually see short ungraded practice items first so you can get comfortable with the interface. Some platforms are adaptive, adjusting question difficulty based on your previous answers, so do not be alarmed if the questions seem to get harder: that often means you are doing well. Scoring is objective and compared against a norm group, and many employers receive a breakdown of speed versus accuracy rather than a single number. Questions used in properly governed recruitment are validated to predict job success without bias, which matters in the public sector context where fairness and legal defensibility are non-negotiable.

Why does this matter for your role specifically? Senior evaluation work is, at its core, applied reasoning under constraint. When you review a theory of change, you are testing a causal chain for logical validity. When you assure another evaluator's analysis, you are error checking. When you decide which of five competing evaluation activities to resource this quarter, you are prioritising under uncertainty. When a measurement framework produces indicators that contradict each other, you are diagnosing a fault in a system. A cognitive assessment compresses these demands into short, sharp exercises, and practising them sharpens exactly the reasoning you deploy at work.

### What it measures for your role

Each cognitive dimension maps directly to skills named in your role profile:

- **Logical deduction and causal reasoning** map to your **Evaluation delivery** skill: evaluating digital services using process evaluation, impact evaluation, theory of change, experimentation and economic evaluation all depend on rigorous if-then reasoning about activities, outputs, outcomes and unintended effects.
- **Error checking and inconsistency detection** map to your **Quality assurance of data and analysis** skill: specifying how data should be cleansed and prepared, bringing data together from different sources, and communicating the limitations of data all require you to spot the discrepancy others miss.
- **Prioritisation and problem solving** map to your **Evaluation planning and strategy** skill: ensuring monitoring and evaluation plans are appropriate and proportionate, identifying evidence needs, and considering wider policy and practical constraints are exercises in structured prioritisation.
- **Pattern recognition in indicators and frameworks** maps to your **Product and service monitoring** skill: turning theory of change indicators into measurement frameworks, and working with performance analysts on dashboards, requires you to see structure and anomaly in sets of metrics.
- **Rule application across phases** maps to your **Monitoring and evaluation across the product life cycle** skill: matching measurement activity to discovery, alpha, beta and live phases is a classification and rule-application task.
- **Interpreting mixed evidence** maps to your **Communicating analysis and insight** skill: turning complex data into clear, actionable stories starts with correctly grasping what the data does and does not show.

### Practice questions

Attempt each question before reading the answer. Aim for roughly 90 seconds per question.

**Question 1 (easy) - Theory of change sequencing**

A theory of change for a new digital licensing service contains five elements, currently jumbled:

1. Applicants receive decisions within 10 working days
2. Build an online application form with automated validation
3. Reduced administrative burden on businesses
4. Fewer incomplete applications are submitted
5. Caseworkers spend less time chasing missing information

Which ordering correctly runs from activity, to output, to intermediate outcomes, to final outcome?

A) 2, 4, 5, 1, 3
B) 2, 1, 4, 5, 3
C) 4, 2, 5, 1, 3
D) 2, 4, 1, 5, 3

**Correct answer: A.**

**Explanation:** The activity is building the form with automated validation (2). Its direct output is fewer incomplete applications (4), because validation catches errors at the point of submission. That leads to the intermediate outcome that caseworkers spend less time chasing missing information (5), which in turn enables decisions within 10 working days (1). The final outcome for businesses is reduced administrative burden (3). Option B places the 10-day decision standard before the mechanism that makes it possible; options C and D break the causal chain in similar ways. In your role, developing theories of change with stakeholders means constantly checking that each link genuinely precedes and plausibly causes the next.

**Question 2 (easy) - Error checking a measurement framework**

You are assuring a measurement framework drafted by a junior evaluator. It contains these four rows:

| Indicator | Baseline | Target | Data source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Digital take-up rate | 42% | 65% | Service analytics |
| User satisfaction | 71% | 80% | Post-transaction survey |
| Cost per transaction | £8.40 | £11.20 | Finance system |
| Completion rate | 58% | 75% | Service analytics |

Which row most likely contains an error requiring the evaluator's attention?

A) Digital take-up rate
B) User satisfaction
C) Cost per transaction
D) Completion rate

**Correct answer: C.**

**Explanation:** Three of the four indicators show targets representing improvement over baseline. Cost per transaction, however, targets £11.20 against a baseline of £8.40: a 33% cost increase. For a digital service, cost per transaction is almost always expected to fall as take-up and completion improve. The most plausible explanation is a transposition or drafting error, and as the assurer you would query it rather than silently correct it, since there is a small chance the increase is intentional (for example, a planned service enhancement). Error checking is not just spotting the anomaly; it is knowing the right professional response to it.

**Question 3 (moderate) - Logical deduction from evaluation rules**

Government guidance your team follows states: "An impact evaluation must include a counterfactual. If a randomised design is not feasible, a quasi-experimental design must be used. If neither is feasible, a theory-based approach may be used, but the evaluation must not be described as measuring net impact."

An evaluator's draft report on a benefits eligibility checker says: "Randomisation was not feasible. We therefore used contribution analysis, a theory-based approach, and we conclude the service produced a net impact of 12,000 additional successful claims."

Which statement is logically correct?

A) The report is compliant because randomisation was infeasible
B) The report breaches the guidance because a theory-based approach was used without first ruling out quasi-experimental designs, and because it claims net impact
C) The report breaches the guidance only because it claims net impact
D) The report is compliant because contribution analysis includes an implicit counterfactual

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** The guidance sets a strict hierarchy. Ruling out randomisation permits a quasi-experimental design; only ruling out both permits a theory-based approach. The draft jumps from "randomisation was not feasible" straight to contribution analysis without addressing quasi-experimental options, so the design choice is not yet justified. Separately, the guidance explicitly forbids describing a theory-based evaluation as measuring net impact, and the report does exactly that. Option C catches only the second breach; options A and D ignore the conditional structure of the rule. Assuring evaluations against government guidance demands exactly this kind of careful conditional reasoning.

**Question 4 (moderate) - Prioritisation under resource constraint**

You have capacity to commission exactly two evaluation activities this quarter. The service is entering public beta. Four candidate activities:

1. A process evaluation of the private beta, informing immediate design iterations
2. A five-year economic evaluation of the full programme
3. Setting up baseline data collection for the impact evaluation, which cannot be reconstructed retrospectively
4. A literature review of comparable international services

Which pair is the most defensible priority?

A) 1 and 2
B) 2 and 4
C) 1 and 3
D) 3 and 4

**Correct answer: C.**

**Explanation:** Two tests decide this: time-criticality and phase-appropriateness. Baseline data collection (3) is irreversibly time-critical; once beta users flow through without a baseline, the impact evaluation's counterfactual is permanently weakened. The process evaluation of private beta (1) is phase-appropriate and feeds decisions being made right now. The economic evaluation (2) matters but is not yet urgent at beta and depends on impact data that does not exist. The literature review (4) is useful but can be done any time, by others, or later. Your role explicitly requires ensuring monitoring and evaluation plans are proportionate to the service standard phase and identifying the resources evaluation activities need: this question is that judgement in miniature.

**Question 5 (moderate) - Pattern recognition in dashboard indicators**

A performance dashboard you developed with performance analysts shows six months of data for a digital service:

| Month | Visits | Started applications | Completed applications | Support calls |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Jan | 40,000 | 12,000 | 7,800 | 2,100 |
| Feb | 42,000 | 12,600 | 8,190 | 2,150 |
| Mar | 44,000 | 13,200 | 8,580 | 2,230 |
| Apr | 46,000 | 13,800 | 8,970 | 2,300 |
| May | 48,000 | 14,400 | 7,200 | 3,900 |
| Jun | 50,000 | 15,000 | 7,500 | 4,100 |

What is the most significant pattern change requiring investigation?

A) Visits stopped growing in May
B) The start rate (started applications as a share of visits) collapsed in May
C) The completion rate (completed as a share of started) dropped sharply in May while support calls spiked
D) Support calls have grown steadily in line with visits throughout

**Correct answer: C.**

**Explanation:** Check each claim against the data. Visits grew every month, so A is false. Started applications remain exactly 30% of visits in every month, so B is false. From January to April, completions are 65% of starts (7,800 of 12,000, and so on), and support calls track visits at roughly 5%. In May, completions fall to 50% of starts (7,200 of 14,400) while support calls jump to about 8% of visits. That paired break in two related indicators, at the same moment, strongly suggests something changed in the service in May, perhaps a release that introduced a blocking defect. D is false because the support-call ratio was stable and then jumped. Spotting covariation between indicators, not just movement in one, is the essence of monitoring a live service.

**Question 6 (moderate) - Data quality fault diagnosis**

You have specified that survey data and service analytics should be joined on a case reference number before analysis. The joined dataset should contain one row per completed application. An evaluator reports that the joined dataset has 9,400 rows, but service analytics shows 8,200 completed applications and the survey has 3,100 responses.

What is the most likely explanation?

A) The survey over-sampled completers
B) The join has produced duplicate rows, most likely because case reference numbers are not unique in one of the source datasets
C) Service analytics is undercounting completions
D) 9,400 is plausible because the two sources should be added together

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** A correctly specified join keyed on completed applications can never produce more rows than the number of completed applications: 8,200 is the ceiling. A result of 9,400 rows means some case references matched more than once, duplicating rows, which typically happens when a reference number appears multiple times in one source (for example, a user submitting the survey twice, or analytics logging repeated completion events). Option D reflects a misunderstanding of joins; options A and C could not by themselves push the row count above 8,200. Setting up systems to get data ready for use, and specifying how data should be cleansed, means recognising this signature instantly: row counts that exceed the logical maximum always indicate a join or deduplication fault.

**Question 7 (moderate) - Rule application across service phases**

Your department applies these internal rules, derived from the Service Standard: during discovery, teams gather qualitative evidence of user needs but do not set quantitative targets; during alpha, teams test hypotheses and define candidate indicators; during beta, teams collect baseline and live performance data against those indicators; during live, teams run full impact evaluation.

A programme board asks four teams to report. Which team's activity is misaligned with its phase?

A) A discovery team conducting user interviews about needs
B) An alpha team drafting candidate indicators for completion and take-up
C) A beta team declining to collect performance data until the impact evaluation begins at live
D) A live team commissioning an impact evaluation

**Correct answer: C.**

**Explanation:** The rules assign baseline and live performance data collection to beta. A beta team deferring all data collection until live breaches its phase obligation and, worse, destroys the baseline the impact evaluation will need: you cannot retrospectively collect data on what beta performance looked like. Teams A, B and D each match the stated rule for their phase. Ensuring the team is measuring performance as appropriate for the relevant service standard phase is a named part of your role, and this style of question tests whether you can apply a phase-based rule set quickly and accurately.

**Question 8 (hard) - Confounding logic in impact claims**

A policy team claims: "After we launched the redesigned appointment booking service in April, missed appointments fell 20% between April and July. The redesign therefore caused the reduction."

You know that: (i) a national reminder-by-text programme also launched in May; (ii) missed appointments always fall in summer months; (iii) the service's user base grew 15% over the period.

Which combination of facts most directly undermines the causal claim?

A) (i) and (ii), because both are plausible alternative explanations for the fall
B) (iii) alone, because more users means more appointments
C) (i) alone, because national programmes always dominate local changes
D) (ii) alone, because seasonality explains everything

**Correct answer: A.**

**Explanation:** A causal claim from a simple before-and-after comparison is vulnerable to any factor that changed at the same time and could plausibly produce the same effect. The text reminder programme (i) is a classic confounder: it started within the measurement window and directly targets missed appointments. Seasonality (ii) is a secular trend: missed appointments would have fallen anyway. Each independently weakens the claim, and together they make it unsafe; neither "always dominates" (C) nor "explains everything" (D), which are overclaims in the opposite direction. User base growth (iii) affects volumes but has no clear directional effect on the missed-appointment rate. This is the reasoning at the heart of impact evaluation: before accepting attribution, systematically ask what else changed.

**Question 9 (hard) - Deductive reasoning about survey logic**

A survey routing specification reads: "All respondents answer Q1. Respondents who used the digital channel answer Q2 and Q3. Respondents who used the phone channel answer Q4. No respondent may answer both Q2 and Q4. Respondents who report an accessibility need at Q1 also answer Q5, regardless of channel."

Which respondent record is impossible under this specification?

A) Answered Q1, Q2, Q3, and Q5
B) Answered Q1 and Q4 only
C) Answered Q1, Q3, and Q4
D) Answered Q1 and Q5 only

**Correct answer: C.**

**Explanation:** Work through the rules. Record A is a digital user with an accessibility need: valid. Record B is a phone user with no accessibility need: valid. Record D is odd at first glance because the respondent answered neither channel block, but the specification does not say every respondent must have used one of the two channels (they might have used a face-to-face channel and reported an accessibility need), so it is not prohibited. Record C, however, contains Q3, which is only asked of digital-channel users, alongside Q4, which is only asked of phone users; and since digital users answer Q2 and Q3 together, a Q3 answer without Q2 also breaches the routing. Either way, C cannot occur if the routing worked. Checking real response data against routing logic like this is exactly how you quality assure data collection, and impossible records are the fingerprint of a broken questionnaire script.

**Question 10 (hard) - Prioritising assurance findings**

You are assuring an evaluation report due at a ministerial briefing in 48 hours. You find four issues:

1. The report's headline finding cites a 9 percentage point improvement, but the underlying table shows 6 percentage points
2. Two charts use inconsistent colour schemes
3. The limitations section does not mention that the comparison group was self-selected
4. Three typographical errors in the annex

Which pair must be fixed before publication, whatever else slips?

A) 1 and 2
B) 1 and 3
C) 2 and 4
D) 3 and 4

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** Rank the issues by their capacity to mislead decision-makers. Issue 1 is a factual contradiction in the headline finding: publishing it would put a wrong number in front of a minister. Issue 3 is an omitted material limitation: a self-selected comparison group threatens the validity of the entire causal claim, and failing to disclose it breaches the standards of transparency your assurance role exists to uphold, including your responsibility to communicate the limitations of data. Issues 2 and 4 are cosmetic; fix them if time allows. The discipline here mirrors real assurance: separate what makes the evidence wrong or misleading from what merely makes it untidy.

**Question 11 (hard) - Unintended effects reasoning**

A theory of change for an online-only benefit application service predicts faster processing and higher satisfaction. As the senior evaluator, you are asked which unintended effect the evaluation should be designed to detect. Which candidate is both plausible and a genuine unintended effect rather than a restatement of intended outcomes?

A) Processing times fall faster than predicted
B) Digitally excluded applicants stop applying, so caseload falls while unmet need rises
C) Satisfaction rises among digital users
D) Staff need training on the new system

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** An unintended effect is a consequence outside the intended causal chain, often affecting people the design overlooked. Options A and C are intended outcomes varying only in magnitude. Option D is an implementation input, foreseeable and planned for. Option B describes a perverse effect: an online-only channel may exclude people without digital access or skills, and the resulting caseload drop could even be misread as success unless the evaluation deliberately measures unmet need among non-applicants. Designing evaluations that explain links between activities, outputs, intended outcomes and unintended effects is written into your role, and this question tests whether you can distinguish a true side effect from a rebadged intention.

**Question 12 (hard) - Synthesising contradictory evidence**

Three evidence sources on a passport renewal service reach different conclusions. Service analytics show completion rates rising from 70% to 85%. A user researcher's qualitative study finds users describing the service as confusing. A post-completion survey shows satisfaction at 90%.

Which interpretation best reconciles all three sources without discarding any?

A) The qualitative study is wrong because two quantitative sources outvote it
B) The survey only reaches people who completed, so it cannot capture the frustrated users who abandoned; rising completion and high completer satisfaction can coexist with genuine confusion among a struggling minority
C) The analytics are wrong because users say the service is confusing
D) All three sources are equally valid, so no conclusion is possible

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** Good synthesis asks what population each source describes. The post-completion survey suffers survivorship bias: it samples only successful users, who are naturally more satisfied. Analytics describe aggregate completion, which can rise even while 15% of users still fail, and the qualitative study may be surfacing exactly those strugglers, or points of confusion that completers overcame. Option B holds all three findings as simultaneously true of different slices of the user base. Options A and C resolve the tension by discarding evidence, and option D refuses to synthesise at all. Analysing and interpreting data you have collected alongside data from other sources, and doing so without privileging the convenient finding, is central to senior evaluation practice.

**Question 13 (moderate) - Resource logic for evaluation activities**

You are identifying resources for next year's evaluation programme. The rules you must satisfy: every impact evaluation needs a statistician; every process evaluation needs a qualitative researcher; a statistician can support at most two evaluations; a qualitative researcher can support at most three. You have two statisticians and one qualitative researcher. The proposed programme contains three impact evaluations and four process evaluations.

What is the minimum additional recruitment needed to deliver the programme as proposed?

A) One statistician only
B) One qualitative researcher only
C) One statistician and one qualitative researcher
D) No additional recruitment is needed

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** Work each constraint separately and do the capacity arithmetic. Statisticians: three impact evaluations each need statistician support; each statistician covers at most two evaluations, so your two statisticians provide up to four slots. Three required slots fit within four available, for example one statistician takes two impact evaluations and the other takes one. No statistician recruitment is needed. Qualitative researchers: four process evaluations each need qualitative researcher support; one researcher covers at most three, leaving a shortfall of one evaluation, so exactly one additional qualitative researcher is required. Option A addresses the constraint that is already satisfied; option C recruits a statistician you do not need; option D ignores the researcher shortfall. Many candidates choose C under time pressure by intuiting that "more evaluations than people" must mean shortages everywhere, rather than checking each ceiling. Identifying the resources needed for evaluation activities is a core duty at your level, and the discipline is identical in the test and the job: enumerate the stated constraints, do the arithmetic for each, and resist filling gaps with plausible but unevidenced assumptions.

### Preparation tips

- **Practise with your own artefacts.** Take a real theory of change or measurement framework from a past evaluation and deliberately hunt for the weakest causal link or the least consistent indicator. This builds the same muscles the test exercises.
- **Rehearse under time pressure.** Senior professionals often lose marks not through reasoning errors but through perfectionism. Set 90 seconds per question and practise committing to an answer.
- **Read the question stem twice, the options once.** Many cognitive items turn on a single qualifier: "most likely", "must", "cannot". As someone who assures analytical wording for a living, use that instinct.
- **Do the ungraded practice items properly.** They exist to remove interface surprises. Treat them as a pilot study, not a formality.
- **Manage adaptive-test psychology.** If questions get harder, that is usually a good sign. Do not let perceived difficulty trigger rushing.
- **Sleep and setting matter.** Take the test rested, in a quiet space, with stable internet, exactly as you would advise a fieldwork team to control their data collection conditions.

### Common pitfalls

- **Overthinking validated items.** These questions are legally vetted and deliberately self-contained. Do not import knowledge of your department's actual guidance; answer from the material given.
- **Losing time to a single hard question.** As with a difficult multi-stage problem in any timed test, flag it, move on, and return. One unanswered hard question costs less than three rushed easy ones.
- **Confusing plausibility with logic.** A conclusion can sound like something that would happen in government and still not follow from the premises given. Deduce; do not recognise.
- **Ignoring speed-versus-accuracy reporting.** Many platforms report both. Wild guessing to finish inflates speed at the cost of an accuracy profile that assessors will see.
- **Assuming seniority exempts you from practice.** Familiarity with evaluation concepts helps, but test-taking fluency is a separate, practisable skill. Even experienced evaluators benefit from a few timed rehearsal sessions.

## Workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific numeric reasoning assessment evaluates your ability to analyse, interpret, and make sound decisions using numerical data drawn from the role itself. It is not a test of abstract mathematics: the questions focus on percentages, ratios, rates, budgets, and cost-benefit estimations, presented through the tables, dashboards, and financial figures a senior digital evaluator genuinely works with. Expect inputs such as service performance tables, evaluation budgets, sample size calculations, survey response figures, and cost-per-transaction comparisons.

The typical format is online and strictly timed, commonly 20 to 25 minutes for 15 to 20 questions, which works out at roughly a minute per question once reading time is accounted for. Most assessments allow or provide an onscreen calculator, because the point is interpretation and decision-making, not mental arithmetic. The time pressure is deliberate: it simulates the reality of extracting the right number quickly from a dense table during a delivery board meeting or a ministerial briefing preparation.

For your role, numeric fluency is not optional. You collect and analyse quantitative data through research, you assure the numerical work of other evaluators, you turn theory of change indicators into measurement frameworks with quantified baselines and targets, and you identify and cost the resources evaluation activities need. When a performance analyst sends you a dashboard extract, or a policy stakeholder asks "is a 4 percentage point change meaningful?", or a finance colleague asks you to justify an evaluation budget, you are doing under natural conditions exactly what this assessment does under controlled ones. A strong numeric reasoning score signals to employers that your quantitative judgement can be trusted at the pace senior work demands.

### What it measures for your role

- **Percentages, rates, and percentage point changes** map to your **Evaluation delivery** skill: descriptive and inferential analysis of completion rates, take-up rates, and outcome measures depends on flawless handling of the difference between a percentage change and a percentage point change.
- **Interpreting tables and dashboards** maps to your **Product and service monitoring** skill: working with performance analysts on dashboards reporting service analytics means reading multi-column tables quickly and accurately.
- **Budgets, unit costs, and cost-benefit figures** map to your **Evaluation planning and strategy** skill: influencing stakeholders to secure resources, and to your role-level duty to identify the resources needed for evaluation activities.
- **Sample sizes, response rates, and weighting** map to your **Quality assurance of data and analysis** skill: specifying data preparation, bringing data together from different sources, and communicating the limitations of data all have numerical foundations.
- **Reconciling figures across sources** maps to your **Quality assurance of data and analysis** and **Evaluation delivery** skills: numbers from analytics, surveys, and administrative data rarely agree perfectly, and you must quantify and explain the gaps.
- **Presenting derived figures** maps to your **Communicating analysis and insight** skill: tactical and strategic recommendations often hinge on a correctly computed ratio or projection.

### Practice questions

An onscreen calculator is assumed. Show your own working before checking the explanation.

**Question 1 (easy) - Completion rate change**

A digital service's completion rate rose from 62% in Q1 to 71% in Q2. A junior evaluator drafts: "Completion improved by 9%." What is the accurate way to state the change?

A) It improved by 9%, the draft is correct
B) It improved by 9 percentage points, which is a relative increase of about 14.5%
C) It improved by 14.5 percentage points
D) It improved by 9 percentage points, which is a relative increase of 9%

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** The difference between 71% and 62% is 9 percentage points. The relative change is 9 ÷ 62 = 0.1452, or about 14.5%. Describing a percentage point change as a percentage change is one of the most common errors you will correct when assuring evaluation reports, and it materially changes how impressive a result sounds: "9%" understates this improvement, while in other cases conflating the two can overstate. Precision here is a communication duty as much as an arithmetic one.

**Question 2 (easy) - Survey response rate**

You send a post-transaction survey to 12,500 service users. You receive 2,875 responses, of which 375 are incomplete and excluded from analysis. What is the usable response rate?

A) 23.0%
B) 20.0%
C) 26.0%
D) 22.5%

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** Usable responses are 2,875 − 375 = 2,500. The usable response rate is 2,500 ÷ 12,500 = 0.20, or 20.0%. The gross response rate of 23.0% (2,875 ÷ 12,500) is answer A, a trap for anyone who skips the exclusion step. When you communicate the limitations of data, distinguishing gross from usable response rates matters: analysis is only as representative as the responses actually analysed.

**Question 3 (easy) - Evaluation budget shares**

Your evaluation budget is £240,000, allocated as follows: fieldwork 45%, analysis 25%, reporting and dissemination 12.5%, project management the remainder. How much is allocated to project management?

A) £30,000
B) £36,000
C) £42,000
D) £48,000

**Correct answer: C.**

**Explanation:** The stated shares sum to 45% + 25% + 12.5% = 82.5%, leaving 100% − 82.5% = 17.5% for project management. Then 0.175 × £240,000 = £42,000. Answer B (£36,000) is 15%, a likely slip if you mis-add the given shares. Budget-share arithmetic like this appears constantly when you identify the resources needed for evaluation activities and defend allocations to finance stakeholders.

**Question 4 (moderate) - Cost per completed transaction**

A service processed the following in one quarter:

| Channel | Transactions completed | Operating cost |
|---|---|---|
| Digital | 96,000 | £288,000 |
| Phone | 14,000 | £322,000 |
| Post | 5,000 | £185,000 |

A policy stakeholder claims the phone channel costs about seven times more per completed transaction than digital. Is this claim right, and what are the unit costs?

A) Yes: digital £3.00, phone £23.00
B) No, it is closer to eight times: digital £3.00, phone £23.00
C) No, it is closer to six times: digital £3.50, phone £21.00
D) Yes: digital £3.00, phone £21.50

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** Digital unit cost: £288,000 ÷ 96,000 = £3.00. Phone unit cost: £322,000 ÷ 14,000 = £23.00. The ratio is 23 ÷ 3 = 7.67, which rounds nearer to eight times than seven. So the unit costs in option A are right but its endorsement of "about seven times" is loose; option B gives both the correct figures and the more accurate multiple. In your assurance role you would flag the stakeholder's claim as directionally right but numerically understated, and this distinction matters when the figure is about to appear in a published report. For completeness, post costs £185,000 ÷ 5,000 = £37.00 per transaction, the most expensive channel of all.

**Question 5 (moderate) - Weighted satisfaction across user groups**

A survey reports satisfaction separately for two user groups. Group X: 1,800 respondents, 85% satisfied. Group Y: 600 respondents, 65% satisfied. An evaluator reports overall satisfaction as "the average of the two groups, 75%". What is the correct overall satisfaction figure?

A) 75.0%
B) 80.0%
C) 78.5%
D) 82.0%

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** A simple average of 85% and 65% ignores the very different group sizes. Compute the weighted figure: satisfied respondents are (0.85 × 1,800) + (0.65 × 600) = 1,530 + 390 = 1,920, out of 2,400 total respondents. Then 1,920 ÷ 2,400 = 0.80, so 80.0%. The unweighted average of 75% understates satisfaction by 5 percentage points because it gives the smaller group equal influence. Spotting unweighted averages masquerading as overall figures is one of the highest-value checks you perform when assuring the analysis of other evaluators.

**Question 6 (moderate) - Sample size and fieldwork cost trade-off**

Your impact evaluation needs 1,100 completed survey interviews. Two fieldwork options:

- Option 1: telephone interviews at £32 per completed interview, expected completion yield of 100%
- Option 2: online panel at £18 per invited participant, with an expected 60% completion yield

Which option is cheaper for 1,100 completed interviews, and by how much?

A) Option 2, cheaper by £2,200
B) Option 1, cheaper by £2,200
C) Option 2, cheaper by £13,200
D) The two options cost the same

**Correct answer: A.**

**Explanation:** Option 1 costs 1,100 × £32 = £35,200. Option 2 must invite enough participants that 60% completion yields 1,100: invitations needed are 1,100 ÷ 0.60 = 1,833.3, round up to 1,834, costing 1,834 × £18 = £33,012, or using the unrounded figure roughly £33,000. The difference is £35,200 − £33,000 = £2,200 in favour of Option 2. Candidates who forget to gross up the online panel for its 60% yield compute 1,100 × £18 = £19,800 and wrongly select C's reasoning. Costing data collection realistically, including non-response, is exactly what you do when identifying resources for evaluation activities, and the yield adjustment is where the real-world arithmetic lives.

**Question 7 (moderate) - Reconciling analytics and administrative data**

Service analytics record 48,600 completed applications in a month. The case management system records 45,900 cases created in the same month. Assuming the case management figure is authoritative, what percentage of the analytics count is an overcount?

A) 5.6%
B) 5.9%
C) 2.7%
D) 4.5%

**Correct answer: A.**

**Explanation:** The overcount is 48,600 − 45,900 = 2,700. As a share of the analytics figure it is 2,700 ÷ 48,600 = 0.0556, about 5.6%. Answer B (5.9%) is the overcount as a share of the case management figure (2,700 ÷ 45,900), a subtly different quantity: the question asks what percentage of the analytics count is overcounted, so the analytics figure is the denominator. Duplicated completion events, refreshes, and test traffic routinely inflate analytics, and when you bring data together from different sources, quantifying the discrepancy and choosing the right denominator are the first steps in communicating the limitations of data.

**Question 8 (moderate) - Projecting take-up against a target**

Digital take-up was 40% at the end of year 1 and has grown by 6 percentage points per year for two further years, reaching 52%. The published target is 70% by the end of year 6. If the current linear trend continues, what take-up level will be reached at the end of year 6, and is the target met?

A) 70%, target exactly met
B) 64%, target missed by 6 percentage points
C) 76%, target exceeded
D) 58%, target missed by 12 percentage points

**Correct answer: A.**

**Explanation:** From the end of year 3 (52%) to the end of year 6 is three further years. At 6 percentage points per year, take-up gains 3 × 6 = 18 points, giving 52% + 18 = 70%: exactly on target. Answer B mistakenly projects only two years; answer C projects four. Counting periods correctly, from the end of year 3 to the end of year 6, is where this question is won or lost, and the same off-by-one care applies whenever you set trajectory expectations in a measurement framework. Of course, in a real evaluation you would also challenge the assumption of continued linear growth, since take-up curves typically flatten as the remaining users become harder to shift, which is precisely the kind of caveat your recommendation would carry.

**Question 9 (hard) - Statistical significance and sample sizes**

An A/B experiment on a service's start page shows: version A, 4,000 users, 58.0% completion; version B, 4,000 users, 59.2% completion. The analysis note says the difference is not statistically significant at the 95% confidence level, with a margin of error of ±2.2 percentage points on the difference. A stakeholder wants to roll out version B immediately, citing "a clear 1.2 point win". Which numerical statement best supports your advice?

A) The observed difference of 1.2 percentage points is smaller than the ±2.2 point margin of error, so the true difference could plausibly be zero or negative; the result does not yet justify a rollout claim
B) 59.2% is greater than 58.0%, so version B is proven better
C) The margin of error means version B is actually worse
D) The experiment needs exactly twice the sample to be significant

**Correct answer: A.**

**Explanation:** The observed difference (59.2 − 58.0 = 1.2 percentage points) sits well inside the uncertainty interval of ±2.2 points, meaning the data are consistent with a true difference anywhere from −1.0 to +3.4 points. Version B may be better, no different, or slightly worse. Option B mistakes the sample difference for proof; option C overcorrects, as non-significance does not mean B is worse; option D invents a precise sample requirement the data given cannot support (the required sample depends on the effect size you want to detect, and cannot be derived from these figures alone). Experimentation is a named technique in your evaluation delivery skill, and translating significance and margins of error into rollout advice, without either overclaiming or dismissing a promising signal, is a hallmark of senior practice. A constructive recommendation would be to continue the experiment to a pre-agreed sample size or use a sequential design.

**Question 10 (hard) - Benefit-cost ratio under scenario change**

An economic evaluation estimates a digital service's annual benefits at £4.2 million (time savings £2.8m, error reduction £1.4m) against annual costs of £1.5 million, a benefit-cost ratio (BCR) of 2.8. A quality assurance review finds the time savings were valued using a wage rate 25% too high, and identifies £0.3 million of omitted annual maintenance costs. What is the corrected BCR, to one decimal place?

A) 2.0
B) 1.9
C) 2.4
D) 1.7

**Correct answer: A.**

**Explanation:** Correct the benefits first. The time savings of £2.8m were computed with a wage rate 25% too high, so the corrected figure is £2.8m ÷ 1.25 = £2.24m. Note the division: a value inflated by 25% is corrected by dividing by 1.25, never by subtracting 25%. Subtracting would give £2.8m × 0.75 = £2.1m, which is wrong because 25% of the inflated figure is larger than 25% of the true figure; that error path leads to benefits of £3.5m and a BCR of £3.5m ÷ £1.8m = 1.94, which is option B, the deliberately planted trap. The correct path: total benefits £2.24m + £1.4m = £3.64m; corrected costs £1.5m + £0.3m = £1.8m; BCR = £3.64m ÷ £1.8m = 2.0 to one decimal place. In real assurance work, a published BCR that cannot be reproduced from its inputs is a finding you would raise formally, and economic evaluation is a named technique in your evaluation delivery skill: knowing how to unwind a valuation error, rather than approximately patch it, is what separates assured figures from merely adjusted ones.

**Question 11 (hard) - Ratio reasoning for resource allocation**

Your assurance workload standard says one senior evaluator can assure the outputs of 4 evaluators working on standard evaluations, or 2 evaluators working on complex impact evaluations. Next year's programme requires assuring 10 evaluators on standard work and 5 on complex impact work. How many senior evaluator full-time equivalents (FTEs) does assurance require?

A) 3.75
B) 5.0
C) 4.5
D) 6.0

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** Convert each workload to FTE using its own ratio. Standard work: 10 evaluators ÷ 4 per senior = 2.5 FTE. Complex impact work: 5 evaluators ÷ 2 per senior = 2.5 FTE. Total: 2.5 + 2.5 = 5.0 FTE. Answer A (3.75) comes from wrongly applying the 4:1 ratio to all 15 evaluators; answer D from applying the 2:1 ratio to everyone. Mixed-ratio capacity calculations like this are the numerical backbone of identifying the resources evaluation activities need, and presenting the 5.0 FTE figure with its workings is what turns a resourcing request into one that stakeholders accept.

**Question 12 (hard) - Compound change in a performance metric**

A service's monthly error rate fell 20% in April, rose 10% in May, and fell 25% in June. The error rate at the end of March was 8.0%. What is the error rate at the end of June, and what is the net change over the quarter?

A) 5.28%, a net fall of 34%
B) 5.20%, a net fall of 35%
C) 5.28%, a net fall of 2.72 percentage points
D) Both A and C are correct

**Correct answer: D.**

**Explanation:** Apply the changes multiplicatively: 8.0% × 0.80 = 6.40% after April; 6.40% × 1.10 = 7.04% after May; 7.04% × 0.75 = 5.28% after June. The net relative change is 0.80 × 1.10 × 0.75 = 0.66, a 34% fall, and the absolute change is 8.00 − 5.28 = 2.72 percentage points. Options A and C therefore describe the same result in two valid ways, relative and absolute, and D is correct. Option B comes from adding the percentage changes (−20 + 10 − 25 = −35), the classic compounding error. When you present quarterly trends to stakeholders, offering both the relative and the percentage point framing, and never summing sequential percentage changes, keeps your analysis both accurate and honest.

**Question 13 (moderate) - Data cleansing yield**

You specify a cleansing pipeline for merged survey and analytics data. Starting from 21,000 records: 6% are removed as duplicates; of the remainder, 10% are removed for failing validation; of what is left, 500 are removed as test traffic. How many records survive for analysis?

A) 17,266
B) 17,764
C) 17,146
D) 18,270

**Correct answer: A.**

**Explanation:** Apply each stage to the survivors of the previous one. Duplicates: 21,000 × 0.94 = 19,740. Validation: 19,740 × 0.90 = 17,766. Test traffic: 17,766 − 500 = 17,266. Answer D applies both percentage removals to the original 21,000 (21,000 − 1,260 − 2,100 − 500 = 17,140, near option C), understating the interaction between stages. Sequential cleansing arithmetic is exactly what you check when you set up a system to get data ready for use: each rule operates on the output of the rule before it, and the order of operations changes the final count.

**Question 14 (moderate) - Dashboard indicator ratio check**

A performance dashboard shows a digital service handled 30,000 transactions in a month with 1,950 failed submissions and 750 support escalations. The service standard your team works to requires the failure rate to stay below 6% and escalations to stay below 3% of transactions. Which statement is correct?

A) Both indicators meet the standard
B) The failure rate breaches the standard; escalations meet it
C) The failure rate meets the standard; escalations breach it
D) Both indicators breach the standard

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** Failure rate: 1,950 ÷ 30,000 = 0.065, or 6.5%, which is above the 6% ceiling, so it breaches. Escalations: 750 ÷ 30,000 = 0.025, or 2.5%, below the 3% ceiling, so it meets the standard. A common speed error is to eyeball 1,950 against 30,000 as "about 6%, fine": the standard says below 6%, and 6.5% is not. Threshold indicators demand exact computation, not approximation, because the whole point of a dashboard you build with performance analysts is to trigger action when a line is crossed. Half a percentage point here is 150 real users a month whose submissions failed.

**Question 15 (hard) - Attrition in a longitudinal evaluation**

Your impact evaluation follows a cohort across three survey waves. Wave 1 achieves 2,400 responses. Attrition between waves is expected to be 25% from wave 1 to wave 2, and 20% from wave 2 to wave 3. Your analysis plan requires at least 1,500 respondents who completed all three waves. Assuming everyone who responds to a later wave also responded to all earlier waves, does the design meet the requirement, and what is the wave 3 count?

A) Yes, wave 3 has 1,560 respondents
B) No, wave 3 has 1,440 respondents
C) Yes, wave 3 has 1,500 respondents exactly
D) No, wave 3 has 1,320 respondents

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** Apply attrition multiplicatively: wave 2 retains 2,400 × 0.75 = 1,800; wave 3 retains 1,800 × 0.80 = 1,440. That is 60 short of the 1,500 required, so the design fails the requirement. Answer A comes from averaging the attrition rates (22.5% overall loss applied once: 2,400 × 0.65 = 1,560), understating compound loss; answer D applies 25% twice. The remedy in practice is to gross up wave 1: to land 1,500 at wave 3 you need 1,500 ÷ (0.75 × 0.80) = 1,500 ÷ 0.60 = 2,500 wave 1 responses. Planning data requirements for future phases, a named duty in your monitoring and evaluation across the product life cycle skill, is exactly this arithmetic done before fieldwork begins rather than regretted after it.

### Preparation tips

- **Rebuild your percentage instincts.** Practise percentage point versus percentage change, reversing a percentage increase (divide, do not subtract), and compound changes until they are automatic. These three patterns account for a large share of numeric reasoning errors at every level of seniority.
- **Practise reading tables backwards.** Given an answer option, ask which cells would need to be combined to produce it. This is faster than computing every option and mirrors how you check other people's figures in assurance work.
- **Use the calculator for arithmetic, your head for structure.** Decide the operations before touching the calculator. Most errors are structural (wrong denominator, wrong number of periods), not computational.
- **Estimate before you calculate.** A rough order-of-magnitude check catches misplaced decimals and wrong denominators, the same sanity-checking you apply to a dashboard figure that looks too good.
- **Bank the easy questions first.** With roughly a minute per question, secure the straightforward rate and share calculations before investing in multi-stage items.
- **Rehearse with realistic material.** Departmental performance packs, published evaluation reports, and service dashboards make excellent practice fodder: pick a table and generate your own questions.

### Common pitfalls

- **Misreading scale qualifiers.** Confusing monthly with annual figures, or missing "in thousands" on a column header, silently corrupts every downstream calculation. Read headers and footnotes before the numbers.
- **Over-calculating.** Some questions yield to estimation or elimination in seconds. Computing every option exactly is a time sink that pressures the rest of the test.
- **Getting stuck on a multi-stage question.** One difficult compound-change or weighting item can consume four questions' worth of time. Flag, skip, return.
- **Summing sequential percentage changes.** Percentage changes compound multiplicatively. Adding them is the single most common error in trend questions, and in real published reports too.
- **Choosing the trap denominator.** When a question asks for a share "of" something, that something is the denominator. Several options in any well-built test are the correct numerator over the wrong denominator.
- **Forgetting the yield adjustment.** Costs and sample sizes must be grossed up for non-response, exclusions, and incomplete records, in the test as in fieldwork planning.

## Workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific verbal reasoning assessment evaluates how well you understand, analyse, and draw correct conclusions from written material of the kind your role actually demands. The passages mirror your reading diet as a senior digital evaluator: government evaluation guidance, service standards, evaluation plans, stakeholder emails, data sharing agreements, ethics submissions, and published evaluation reports.

The defining rule is fact-based logic: you must answer strictly from the text provided, ignoring everything you know from outside it. Most questions require a judgement of True, False, or Cannot Say. "True" means the passage logically entails the statement. "False" means the passage logically contradicts it. "Cannot Say" means the passage provides insufficient information either way, however plausible or implausible the statement seems from your professional experience. Alongside these, expect some multiple-choice comprehension items asking for the best summary, the author's main argument, or the correct application of a stated rule.

Timing is tight, typically less than a minute per question once the passage is read, deliberately simulating workplace deadlines. Expect 20 to 30 questions across 4 to 8 passages in 20 to 25 minutes.

This assessment matters intensely for your role because senior evaluation practice is a reading discipline as much as an analytical one. You assure evaluation plans against government guidance, which means detecting exactly what a standard requires, permits, and forbids. You manage relationships through written communication where a misread sentence can commit your team to the wrong scope. You publish reports where every claim must be supported by the evidence cited, and where you must distinguish what your data shows from what it merely suggests. The Cannot Say judgement, in particular, is the psychometric twin of a skill you exercise daily: refusing to let a plausible inference masquerade as an evidenced finding.

### What it measures for your role

- **Precise comprehension of guidance and standards** maps to your role-level duty to ensure evaluations follow government guidance and meet standards, and to your **Evaluation planning and strategy** skill: agreeing evaluation approaches means first reading what the guidance actually requires.
- **Distinguishing evidence from inference** maps to your **Evaluation delivery** and **Quality assurance of data and analysis** skills: analysing and interpreting data, and communicating its limitations, both depend on separating what is demonstrated from what is assumed.
- **Reading stakeholder communications accurately** maps to your **Evaluation planning and strategy** skill and your role-level duty to manage relationships with stakeholders, user researchers and performance analysts: scope, commitments, and concerns often arrive buried in long emails.
- **Interpreting ethics and privacy documentation** maps to your **Data ethics and privacy** skill: assessing and constructively challenging proposed data ethics policies is close reading with consequences.
- **Judging arguments in reports** maps to your **Communicating analysis and insight** skill: to communicate negative and positive information credibly, you must first identify precisely what a text claims and how strongly.

### Practice questions

For each passage, answer every question strictly from the passage text. Then check the explanations, which point back to the exact wording.

**Passage 1 - Evaluation guidance extract**

"Departments must publish an evaluation plan for all major programmes before public beta begins. The plan must set out the evaluation questions, the methods to be used, and the arrangements for publishing findings. Where an impact evaluation is planned, the plan must state the counterfactual approach. Departments should, where practicable, publish interim findings within twelve months of the start of public beta. Final evaluation reports must be published within six months of the evaluation's completion, unless ministers agree an exemption on national security grounds."

**Question 1 (easy):** True, False, or Cannot Say: "An evaluation plan for a major programme must be published before public beta begins."

**Correct answer: True.**

**Explanation:** The first sentence states this directly: "Departments must publish an evaluation plan for all major programmes before public beta begins." The statement simply restates the obligation, so it is True. Easy verification questions like this reward answering from the exact sentence rather than from memory of similar guidance you have read at work.

**Question 2 (easy):** True, False, or Cannot Say: "Interim findings must be published within twelve months of the start of public beta."

**Correct answer: False.**

**Explanation:** The passage says departments "should, where practicable, publish interim findings within twelve months". "Should, where practicable" is a qualified expectation, not an obligation; the statement's "must" converts it into an unqualified requirement, which contradicts the passage's wording. Distinguishing "must" from "should" is precisely the skill you use when assuring evaluation plans against guidance: the two words create different compliance obligations, and the passage's drafting is deliberate.

**Question 3 (moderate):** True, False, or Cannot Say: "A department that completes an evaluation must publish the final report within six months unless a national security exemption is agreed by ministers."

**Correct answer: True.**

**Explanation:** The final sentence states: "Final evaluation reports must be published within six months of the evaluation's completion, unless ministers agree an exemption on national security grounds." The statement is a faithful paraphrase, preserving both the obligation and its single stated exception. Note what would make it False: adding exceptions the text does not contain, or dropping the ministerial agreement condition. Faithful paraphrase versus subtle distortion is the entire game in these items.

**Question 4 (moderate):** True, False, or Cannot Say: "Evaluation plans for minor programmes do not need to be published."

**Correct answer: Cannot Say.**

**Explanation:** The passage imposes obligations on "all major programmes" and says nothing at all about minor programmes. Silence is not permission: the absence of a stated requirement for minor programmes does not entail that no requirement exists elsewhere. Professionally you might know departments often exempt minor programmes, but the test forbids importing that knowledge. This is the classic Cannot Say structure: the statement concerns a category the passage never addresses.

**Question 5 (hard):** True, False, or Cannot Say: "A plan for a process evaluation that does not include an impact evaluation need not state a counterfactual approach."

**Correct answer: True.**

**Explanation:** The passage's counterfactual requirement is conditional: "Where an impact evaluation is planned, the plan must state the counterfactual approach." If no impact evaluation is planned, the condition is not met, so the stated obligation does not apply, and nothing else in the passage imposes a counterfactual requirement on other evaluation types. The statement is the valid contrapositive reading of a conditional rule. Candidates often answer Cannot Say here out of caution, but the logic is airtight within the passage: the only counterfactual obligation given is triggered by impact evaluation, so its absence releases the plan from that stated obligation. Reading conditional requirements accurately is exactly what you do when judging whether guidance applies to a particular evaluation design.

**Passage 2 - Stakeholder email**

"From: Head of Policy. Subject: Evaluation scope for the eligibility checker. Thanks for the draft plan. The director has confirmed funding for the process evaluation and the first wave of the user survey. Funding for the second survey wave will be reviewed after the spending settlement in November. We cannot commit to the economic evaluation this year. Please note the minister has asked for early findings before the select committee hearing in March. I remain concerned that the comparison sites you propose differ from the pilot sites in ways that could complicate interpretation, though I defer to your team on method."

**Question 6 (easy):** True, False, or Cannot Say: "Funding has been confirmed for the process evaluation."

**Correct answer: True.**

**Explanation:** The email states: "The director has confirmed funding for the process evaluation and the first wave of the user survey." The statement matches the first element exactly. In stakeholder correspondence, identifying what is actually confirmed, as opposed to expected or under review, protects your team from planning against funding that does not yet exist.

**Question 7 (moderate):** True, False, or Cannot Say: "The second survey wave will be funded after the spending settlement in November."

**Correct answer: Cannot Say.**

**Explanation:** The email says funding for the second wave "will be reviewed" after the settlement. A review is not a commitment: the outcome could be funding, partial funding, or refusal. The statement asserts an outcome the text leaves open, so the passage neither entails nor contradicts it. Misreading "will be reviewed" as "will be funded" is exactly the wishful-reading error that leads teams to schedule fieldwork they cannot pay for.

**Question 8 (moderate):** True, False, or Cannot Say: "The Head of Policy has instructed the evaluation team to change its comparison sites."

**Correct answer: False.**

**Explanation:** The Head of Policy voices a concern ("I remain concerned that the comparison sites you propose differ from the pilot sites...") but explicitly adds "though I defer to your team on method". Expressing concern while deferring on method is the opposite of an instruction to change; the passage contradicts the claim that an instruction was given. Reading the difference between a stakeholder's registered concern and a stakeholder's direction matters enormously when you manage those relationships: one requires a considered response, the other requires either compliance or escalation.

**Question 9 (moderate) - Multiple choice:** Which of the following best describes what the evaluation team can currently rely on, according to the email?

A) Funding for the process evaluation, the first survey wave, and early findings delivered before the March select committee hearing
B) Confirmed funding for the process evaluation and the first survey wave, a ministerial request for early findings before March, and no economic evaluation funding this year
C) Full funding for both survey waves once the spending settlement concludes
D) Funding for all planned activities except the economic evaluation

**Correct answer: B.**

**Explanation:** Option B captures each element with the correct status: confirmed funding for two items ("The director has confirmed funding for the process evaluation and the first wave of the user survey"), a request rather than a commitment regarding early findings ("the minister has asked for early findings"), and the explicit refusal for the economic evaluation ("We cannot commit to the economic evaluation this year"). Option A wrongly upgrades the minister's request into something the team can "rely on" as an agreed deliverable. Option C converts a review into a promise. Option D overstates, because the second survey wave is unresolved, not funded. Constructing an accurate status summary from a mixed message is the core skill of managing stakeholders in writing.

**Passage 3 - Data ethics submission extract**

"The evaluation will link survey responses to administrative records using pseudonymised identifiers. Respondents will be informed of the linkage at the point of consent, and may complete the survey without agreeing to linkage. Linked data will be held in a secure environment accessible only to named analysts, and will be destroyed eighteen months after publication of the final report. No individual-level data will be shared with the delivery team or the policy team. Aggregate findings will be published only where cell sizes are ten or greater. The evaluation team judges the residual risk of re-identification to be low, given the pseudonymisation and access controls, but acknowledges the risk cannot be reduced to zero."

**Question 10 (easy):** True, False, or Cannot Say: "Respondents can complete the survey even if they refuse data linkage."

**Correct answer: True.**

**Explanation:** The passage states respondents "may complete the survey without agreeing to linkage". The statement is a direct restatement. Consent structures like this, where linkage consent is separable from participation consent, are exactly what you check when working with stakeholders to identify and address ethical and privacy concerns.

**Question 11 (moderate):** True, False, or Cannot Say: "The evaluation team claims that re-identification of individuals is impossible."

**Correct answer: False.**

**Explanation:** The final sentence explicitly "acknowledges the risk cannot be reduced to zero" while judging it low. Claiming impossibility would contradict this acknowledged residual risk, so the passage contradicts the statement. Honest ethics documentation never claims zero risk, and part of assessing and constructively challenging data ethics policies is checking that risk language stays calibrated: "low" and "impossible" are different claims with different accountability consequences.

**Question 12 (moderate):** True, False, or Cannot Say: "Individual-level data will be shared with the department's statistics team."

**Correct answer: Cannot Say.**

**Explanation:** The passage rules out individual-level sharing with two named groups: "No individual-level data will be shared with the delivery team or the policy team." A statistics team is neither of these, and the passage says access is limited to "named analysts" without stating which team those analysts belong to. The text neither permits nor forbids the specific sharing described, so the answer is Cannot Say. The temptation is to reason that a prohibition on two teams implies prohibition on all teams, or conversely that "named analysts" could include statisticians; both are inferences beyond the text.

**Question 13 (hard):** True, False, or Cannot Say: "A finding based on a group of eight respondents will not be published as an aggregate figure."

**Correct answer: True.**

**Explanation:** The passage commits: "Aggregate findings will be published only where cell sizes are ten or greater." Eight is fewer than ten, so publication of that aggregate is excluded by the stated rule. The word "only" does the logical work here: it makes cell size of at least ten a necessary condition for publication. Had the passage said findings "will be published where cell sizes are ten or greater" without "only", the eight-respondent case would have been unaddressed and the answer Cannot Say. Small-cell suppression rules protect against re-identification in published statistics, and reading "only" correctly is the difference between a compliant publication and a privacy incident.

**Passage 4 - Published evaluation report extract**

"Completion rates rose from 61% to 74% over the twelve months following the redesign. Our difference-in-differences analysis, comparing the redesigned service with three similar services that were not redesigned, suggests that between 7 and 10 percentage points of this improvement is attributable to the redesign itself, with the remainder explained by seasonal effects and a department-wide identity verification upgrade. Qualitative interviews indicate that the simplified evidence-upload step was the feature users most frequently credited for an easier experience. However, users with assisted digital needs reported little improvement, and completion for this group remains 22 percentage points below the service average. We recommend that the next design iteration prioritise the assisted digital journey."

**Question 14 (moderate):** True, False, or Cannot Say: "The report attributes the entire 13 percentage point improvement to the redesign."

**Correct answer: False.**

**Explanation:** The report attributes "between 7 and 10 percentage points" to the redesign, "with the remainder explained by seasonal effects and a department-wide identity verification upgrade". Attributing the entire improvement to the redesign directly contradicts this partition. When you communicate analysis and insight, this is the discipline in action: a credible report divides an observed change between the intervention and other explanations, and a careless summary that reassembles the whole change under one cause misrepresents the analysis.

**Question 15 (hard):** True, False, or Cannot Say: "The evidence-upload step caused the improvement in completion rates."

**Correct answer: Cannot Say.**

**Explanation:** The qualitative finding is carefully worded: interviews "indicate" the upload step was "the feature users most frequently credited". Users crediting a feature is evidence of perceived helpfulness, not a causal demonstration; the passage's causal claim (the difference-in-differences estimate) operates at the level of the whole redesign, not any single feature. The passage neither asserts nor denies that the upload step caused the improvement, so Cannot Say. Notice the division of labour between methods: quantitative analysis carries the attribution claim, qualitative analysis explains mechanism and experience. Collapsing that distinction is one of the most common errors you will correct in draft reports.

**Question 16 (hard) - Multiple choice:** Which statement best captures the report's overall message?

A) The redesign succeeded for most users, a measurable part of the improvement is attributable to it, but assisted digital users have been left behind and should be the next priority
B) The redesign failed because assisted digital users saw little improvement
C) The improvement was mostly caused by seasonal effects and the identity verification upgrade
D) Completion rates improved and all user groups benefited from the simplified upload step

**Correct answer: A.**

**Explanation:** Option A holds all four elements of the passage in balance: the overall improvement, the partial attribution ("between 7 and 10 percentage points... attributable to the redesign"), the equity gap ("users with assisted digital needs reported little improvement"), and the forward recommendation. Option B inflates one negative finding into overall failure. Option C misreads the attribution: 7 to 10 points of a 13 point improvement is the majority, so "mostly caused by" other factors is wrong. Option D contradicts the assisted digital finding. Communicating negative and positive information together, without letting either eclipse the other, is a named element of your communicating analysis and insight skill, and this question rehearses exactly that balance.

**Passage 5 - Internal assurance standard**

"All evaluation outputs must pass two-stage assurance before release. Stage one is a technical review by an analyst who was not involved in producing the output. Stage two is a senior review focusing on interpretation, limitations, and presentation. An output that fails stage one must be corrected and re-reviewed at stage one before proceeding to stage two. Senior reviewers may waive stage one for minor outputs, defined as outputs that will not be published and will not inform a funding decision. The waiver must be recorded in the assurance log. Outputs informing ministerial decisions are never minor outputs, regardless of publication status."

**Question 17 (moderate):** True, False, or Cannot Say: "An unpublished briefing that informs a ministerial decision can have its stage one review waived if the waiver is recorded in the assurance log."

**Correct answer: False.**

**Explanation:** Trace the definitions. The waiver applies only to "minor outputs", defined as outputs that "will not be published and will not inform a funding decision". The final sentence then adds an override: "Outputs informing ministerial decisions are never minor outputs, regardless of publication status." A briefing informing a ministerial decision is therefore never a minor output, so it can never qualify for the waiver, however faithfully the log is completed. The recording requirement is a necessary condition for a valid waiver, not a route to eligibility. Layered definitions with an override clause are common in assurance standards, and this is precisely the kind of rule you apply when assuring the work of other evaluators.

**Question 18 (moderate):** True, False, or Cannot Say: "An output that fails stage one may proceed directly to stage two once it has been corrected."

**Correct answer: False.**

**Explanation:** The passage requires that a failed output "must be corrected and re-reviewed at stage one before proceeding to stage two". Proceeding directly after correction, skipping the repeat stage one review, contradicts the explicit re-review requirement. The trap is that the statement sounds almost compliant because correction is indeed required; the missing re-review is what makes it False. In quality assurance, "fixed" and "verified as fixed" are different states, in this test and in your assurance log alike.

**Question 19 (hard):** True, False, or Cannot Say: "The technical reviewer at stage one must be more senior than the analyst who produced the output."

**Correct answer: Cannot Say.**

**Explanation:** The passage's only requirement for the stage one reviewer is independence: "an analyst who was not involved in producing the output". It says nothing about relative seniority. Many organisations do require senior review, and stage two is indeed described as "a senior review", but that describes stage two, not stage one. The statement adds a condition the text neither imposes nor rejects, so Cannot Say. Reading requirements at the correct stage, without letting adjacent detail bleed across, is a fine-grained discipline that mirrors checking which controls apply at which point in an assurance workflow.

### Preparation tips

- **Train the Cannot Say reflex.** Senior evaluators are inference machines: your professional instinct is to reason beyond the text. In the test, that instinct is the enemy. Practise asking "does the passage state or logically entail this, yes or no?" and refusing credit to anything else.
- **Read the statement first, then hunt.** For True/False/Cannot Say items, read the statement, then scan the passage for the governing sentence. Reading the full passage in depth first usually wastes time you do not have.
- **Mark the modal verbs.** "Must", "should", "may", "will", "only", "unless": these small words carry the logical load in guidance and policy text. Most False answers are built by swapping one modal for another.
- **Practise on your natural reading material.** Magenta Book-style guidance, service standards, published evaluation reports, and ethics documentation make ideal practice passages. Write your own True/False/Cannot Say statements for a colleague and swap.
- **Watch for scope words.** "All", "some", "no", "the delivery team and the policy team": statements that widen or narrow the passage's scope are the test writer's favourite trap, and yours to spot.
- **Keep your pace honest.** At under a minute per question, two passes are affordable; four are not. Answer, flag genuine uncertainty, and move.

### Common pitfalls

- **Importing professional knowledge.** You may know what evaluation guidance really requires, or how consent usually works. The test scores you on this passage, not on the real world. Answering from experience rather than text is the single biggest source of error for experienced professionals.
- **Confusing plausible with entailed.** A statement that is very likely true in reality is still Cannot Say if the passage does not support it. Likelihood is not entailment.
- **Misreading qualified claims as absolute.** "Suggests", "indicates", "where practicable", "judged to be low": qualified language deliberately limits a claim. Treating a hedged finding as a firm fact flips True to False or Cannot Say without you noticing.
- **Overusing Cannot Say as a safe harbour.** When a conditional rule logically settles a case, as with the counterfactual question above, Cannot Say is wrong. Caution is not a substitute for reasoning.
- **Losing the negation thread.** Statements combining a negative with an exception ("will not be published unless...") reward slow, deliberate parsing. Rushing negations is how strong readers give away easy marks.
- **Spending too long on one passage.** If a passage's subject feels awkward, its questions are still answerable from the text alone. Do not let unfamiliarity with the scenario slow you; the logic, not the domain, is being tested.

## Workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment

### About this assessment

A workplace job-specific situational judgement assessment evaluates your decision-making, interpersonal effectiveness, and professional conduct through realistic workplace dilemmas. Unlike the cognitive, numeric, and verbal assessments, there is usually no single objectively right answer: several responses may seem reasonable, and the assessment measures how well your behavioural choices align with the values and demands of the role and organisation. For a senior digital evaluator in UK government, that alignment means public sector values: putting user needs first, transparency and honesty about evidence, collaboration across disciplines, accessibility and inclusion, and the courage to communicate unwelcome findings.

Scenarios are short, hypothetical, and drawn from the real texture of the job: a policy stakeholder pressuring you over a negative finding, an evaluator whose analysis you cannot assure in the time available, a delivery team resisting data collection, an ethics concern surfacing mid-fieldwork. Response formats vary: most commonly you select the Most Effective and Least Effective actions from a set, or rank or rate every option from best to worst. Timing is generous or absent, because the assessment targets your natural judgement rather than your speed.

For a senior role, expect scenarios that test leadership dimensions: managing the evaluators you assure, protecting evaluation independence under political pressure, balancing proportionality against rigour, and handling stakeholder relationships where the honest answer is not the welcome one. Assessors are typically looking for responses that address root causes rather than symptoms, act at the right level of escalation (neither passing problems upward reflexively nor sitting on risks), preserve relationships while holding professional lines, and keep users and evidence integrity at the centre.

This assessment matters for your role because judgement is the substance of senior evaluation work. Methods can be taught; the daily choices about what to say to whom, when to compromise and when to hold firm, and how to deliver difficult messages so they are heard, are what distinguish a senior evaluator whose evaluations are trusted and used from one whose reports sit unread.

### What it measures for your role

- **Stakeholder influence and relationship management** map to your **Evaluation planning and strategy** skill (influencing stakeholders to secure resources, agreeing approaches with diverse stakeholders) and your role-level duty to manage relationships with stakeholders, user researchers and performance analysts.
- **Integrity in communicating findings** maps to your **Communicating analysis and insight** skill: communicating negative and positive information to stakeholders, and giving tactical and strategic recommendations even when they are unwelcome.
- **Ethical judgement** maps to your **Data ethics and privacy** skill: working with stakeholders to identify and address ethical and privacy concerns, and constructively challenging proposed policies.
- **Leadership of evaluators** maps to your role-level duty to assure the work of other evaluators, including handling quality problems in ways that develop people rather than merely correcting outputs.
- **Proportionality and pragmatism** map to your **Evaluation planning and strategy** and **Monitoring and evaluation across the product life cycle** skills: ensuring plans are appropriate and proportionate within real policy and practical constraints.
- **Multidisciplinary collaboration** maps to your role-level duty to work in a multidisciplinary team, where evaluators, user researchers, performance analysts, designers and policy colleagues bring different priorities.

### Practice questions

For each scenario, decide your answer before reading the commentary. Where the format is Most/Least Effective, choose one of each.

**Question 1 (moderate) - Pressure to soften a negative finding**

Your impact evaluation of a flagship digital service finds no measurable effect on the primary outcome. The deputy director sponsoring the service asks you to "reframe the finding more positively" in the published report, suggesting the summary emphasise user satisfaction instead and move the null result to a technical annex. The report is due for publication in two weeks.

Options:

A) Agree to restructure the report as asked, since the deputy director is accountable for the service
B) Refuse outright and escalate immediately to the head of profession, copying the deputy director
C) Explain that the null finding must remain prominent for the report to be credible and compliant with publication standards, offer to add fair context (including the satisfaction results and limitations), and share a revised draft that is both honest and balanced
D) Publish the report unchanged without responding to the deputy director

**Most effective: C. Least effective: A.**

**Explanation:** Option C holds the professional line, the headline finding cannot be buried, while treating the stakeholder as a partner: satisfaction findings and limitations genuinely belong in a balanced report, and offering a revised draft keeps the relationship working. This is communicating negative and positive information to stakeholders done properly. Option A is least effective because it corrupts the evidence record: relocating a null result to an annex to create a positive impression misleads readers, breaches transparency, and destroys the evaluation's value; agreeing also signals that your assurance can be negotiated. Option B escalates before attempting resolution, spending relationship capital and skipping the conversation that usually solves the problem; escalation is right if C fails. Option D avoids the conflict rather than managing it, damaging trust and inviting the same pressure later with less goodwill.

**Question 2 (moderate) - Assuring a flawed analysis under deadline**

You are assuring an evaluator's analysis due with the programme board tomorrow morning. You find that the comparison group construction is flawed in a way that likely overstates the service's impact. The evaluator is capable but defensive about criticism, and reworking the analysis properly needs three or four days.

Options:

A) Approve the analysis, noting your concern verbally to the evaluator for next time
B) Tell the programme board the analysis is delayed, explain that assurance identified an issue affecting the headline estimate, agree a short extension, and work with the evaluator on the fix
C) Rework the analysis yourself overnight and submit your own version under the deadline
D) Send the analysis to the board with a caveat footnote saying the results are provisional and unassured

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A.**

**Explanation:** Option B accepts the real trade-off: a briefly late, correct analysis serves the board better than a punctual, misleading one, and saying that assurance caught an issue is the assurance system working, not failing. Working with the evaluator on the fix also develops them, which is how you assure the work of other evaluators sustainably. Option A is least effective: knowingly approving an analysis that overstates impact defeats the entire purpose of assurance and puts a wrong number into a governance decision; a verbal note for next time fixes nothing now. Option C might rescue the deadline but bypasses the evaluator entirely, teaching them nothing, straining the relationship with a defensive colleague, and leaving a single overnight analysis itself unassured. Option D transfers your quality problem to the board: decision-makers are poorly placed to weigh "provisional and unassured", and the caveat will not stop the headline number circulating.

**Question 3 (hard) - Ethics concern surfacing mid-fieldwork**

Midway through fieldwork for a process evaluation, a researcher tells you that interviewers have been collecting more personal detail than the consent materials described, because the interview guide's follow-up prompts drifted during piloting. Around 40 interviews are affected. The fieldwork schedule is tight and the policy team is eager for findings.

Options:

A) Pause the affected data collection, correct the interview guide and consent alignment, assess the affected records with your data protection colleagues, and inform the relevant governance forum with a plan to remedy
B) Quietly fix the interview guide going forward and carry on, since the extra detail is useful context and no participant has complained
C) Delete all 40 affected interviews and restart fieldwork from scratch without telling the policy team why
D) Complete fieldwork as planned and note the issue in the final report's limitations section

**Most effective: A. Least effective: B.**

**Explanation:** Option A does the four things a data ethics incident requires: stop the leak (pause and correct), assess the exposure (with data protection expertise, since the right treatment of the 40 records depends on the specifics), be transparent with governance, and plan a remedy. It treats consent drift as a compliance and trust issue, not an inconvenience. This is your data ethics and privacy skill under live conditions: identifying and addressing concerns with the right stakeholders. Option B is least effective because it knowingly retains data collected beyond consent and conceals the issue; "no one complained" is not a consent basis, and "useful context" makes the retention deliberate rather than accidental. Option C overcorrects: destroying data may itself be the wrong remedy (assessment might support retention with re-consent, or partial redaction), and concealing the reason from stakeholders trades one transparency failure for another. Option D treats an active ethical problem as a reporting footnote, letting the breach continue for the remaining fieldwork.

**Question 4 (moderate) - Delivery team resisting measurement**

A delivery team entering public beta tells you they will not implement the analytics events your measurement framework needs, saying the sprint is full and "we can add tracking later". Without those events, the baseline for two key indicators in the agreed evaluation plan will be lost permanently.

Options:

A) Accept the delay and plan to reconstruct the baseline later from whatever data exists
B) Raise it with the service owner immediately as a blocking risk, explaining that the baseline is unrecoverable, quantifying what the evaluation loses, and offering a descoped minimal event set that fits the sprint
C) Tell the delivery team the evaluation plan is mandatory and they must comply
D) Ask a performance analyst to estimate the indicators from server logs instead, without telling the delivery team or service owner about the change

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A.**

**Explanation:** Option B is effective on three axes at once: it escalates at the right level (the service owner owns the trade-off between sprint scope and evidence), it communicates the irreversibility clearly, which is the fact that changes the decision, and it offers a proportionate compromise, the minimal event set, showing you have engaged with delivery pressure rather than dismissed it. This is evaluation planning and strategy in practice: influencing stakeholders while considering practical constraints. Option A is least effective because it silently accepts the one loss that cannot be repaired; "reconstruct later" is a comforting fiction when the events were never fired, and you would be assuring an impact evaluation you know is compromised. Option C asserts authority you may formally have, but commanding a full sprint team rarely produces good instrumentation and burns the collaborative relationship your role depends on. Option D might salvage partial data but conceals a material change to the agreed measurement approach from the very stakeholders who agreed it, undermining transparency and the dashboard work you share with performance analysts.

**Question 5 (hard) - Ranking: theory of change workshop conflict**

You are facilitating a theory of change workshop for a new digital service. The policy lead insists the service will "obviously" reduce fraud and wants that outcome locked in without discussion. The user researcher present has evidence suggesting users misunderstand the eligibility rules, which could increase error rather than reduce fraud. The workshop has 45 minutes left and the theory of change underpins the evaluation plan you must publish.

Rank these responses from most to least effective:

1. Surface the user researcher's evidence now, facilitate a structured discussion of the causal assumptions behind the fraud outcome, and record it as a hypothesis to test rather than a settled outcome
2. Accept the policy lead's outcome to keep the workshop moving, and revisit it privately with the user researcher afterwards
3. Adjourn the workshop and reconvene once the policy lead and user researcher have resolved their disagreement between themselves
4. Side openly with the user researcher and tell the policy lead the fraud outcome is wrong

**Correct ranking: 1, 2, 3, 4.**

**Explanation:** Response 1 is what a theory of change workshop is for: causal assumptions are exposed to evidence and disagreement is converted into testable hypotheses. Recording fraud reduction as a hypothesis respects the policy lead's belief while protecting the evaluation from building on an untested assumption, and it uses the multidisciplinary room, exactly why the user researcher is there. Response 2 keeps momentum but defers the conflict to a smaller, less legitimate forum; the published theory of change may meanwhile harden around the untested claim, though at least the issue survives somewhere. Response 3 abdicates facilitation: the disagreement is substantive and belongs in the workshop, and delegating its resolution to the two parties privately removes both the evidence and the structure that would resolve it well. Response 4 is worst because it converts an evidential question into a personal contest, damages the policy relationship you need for resourcing and use of findings, and misstates the epistemic position: the researcher's evidence justifies testing the assumption, not declaring it wrong. Developing theories of change through discussion with stakeholders means holding the space where beliefs meet evidence, not picking a winner.

**Question 6 (moderate) - Performance analyst disagreement on a dashboard**

You and a performance analyst are building the service dashboard from your measurement framework. The analyst wants to feature a "total visits" metric prominently because it always goes up and "keeps the board happy". Your framework instead prioritises completion rate, assisted digital completion, and cost per transaction, which currently show a mixed picture.

Options:

A) Insist the dashboard contain only the indicators in your measurement framework
B) Let the analyst lead, since dashboards are their specialism
C) Agree the dashboard should lead with the framework indicators tied to the theory of change, explain why a vanity metric misleads the board, and offer to include visits as context lower down with a clear note on what it can and cannot show
D) Build two dashboards: one flattering version for the board and one accurate version for the team

**Most effective: C. Least effective: D.**

**Explanation:** Option C wins on substance and relationship together. The indicators that operationalise the theory of change are what the dashboard exists to show, that is your product and service monitoring skill, turning theory of change indicators into measurement frameworks and dashboards, and explaining the reasoning respects the analyst's expertise while correcting the incentive problem. Including visits as labelled context is honest: the metric is real, it just cannot carry the story alone. Option D is least effective because maintaining a flattering version for decision-makers and an accurate one for insiders is institutionalised misleading of the board, worse than a single imperfect dashboard, and corrosive the moment it is discovered. Option A gets the content right at the cost of the collaboration, treating a colleague's input as an obstacle. Option B abdicates your responsibility for measurement integrity; deference to specialism does not extend to letting a vanity metric headline a governance product.

**Question 7 (hard) - Proportionality under pressure for rigour**

A junior evaluator on your team proposes a full randomised controlled trial with a two-year follow-up for a small internal tool used by 300 caseworkers, arguing "the guidance says experimental designs are strongest". The tool's annual cost is modest, a decision on wider rollout is needed in six months, and the delivery team could support light-touch measurement now.

Options:

A) Approve the RCT proposal, since stronger designs produce stronger evidence
B) Coach the evaluator on proportionality: agree the evidence need with stakeholders, then design to the decision, perhaps a before-after study with caseworker feedback and operational metrics reported within the decision window, keeping stronger designs for higher-stakes questions
C) Reject the proposal and set the evaluation scope yourself to save time
D) Ask the evaluator to survey stakeholders about which design they would prefer

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A.**

**Explanation:** Option B does two jobs at once. It gets the methodological judgement right: proportionate evaluation matches design cost and timeline to the decision at stake, and a two-year RCT that reports eighteen months after the rollout decision answers a question nobody can still act on. And it develops the evaluator through coaching rather than overruling, which is how assuring the work of others builds capability. This is your evaluation planning and strategy skill: ensuring plans are appropriate and proportionate, identifying the actual evidence need. Option A is least effective because it mistakes methodological maximalism for rigour: the strongest design for the question and constraints is the right design, and spending two years and disproportionate resource on a modest tool fails the public value test the guidance itself embodies. Option C reaches a sensible scope but wastes the development moment and signals that proposals are simply overridden. Option D confuses stakeholder agreement on evidence needs, which is good practice, with delegating design choice to preference, which is not: stakeholders should shape the questions and constraints; the method is your team's professional judgement.

**Question 8 (moderate) - Unwelcome interim findings and a select committee**

Interim findings from your evaluation suggest a digital service is performing well for most users but failing a vulnerable group. The head of policy asks you to hold the interim findings entirely until after a select committee hearing next month, "to avoid giving the committee a stick to beat us with". The published evaluation plan committed to sharing interim findings with the programme board this month.

Options:

A) Hold the findings as asked, since select committee handling is a policy responsibility
B) Share the findings with the programme board as committed, tell the head of policy in advance that you will do so, and offer to help prepare an accurate, contextualised account of the findings for the committee hearing
C) Leak the findings to the committee clerk to guarantee transparency
D) Share only the positive findings with the board this month, holding the vulnerable-group finding back for later

**Most effective: B. Least effective: C.**

**Explanation:** Option B keeps the published commitment, an evaluation plan is a public promise about process, and internal governance (the programme board) is precisely where an emerging equity problem must land quickly so it can be fixed. Telling the head of policy first respects the relationship and gives them time to prepare committee handling; offering help with an accurate account addresses their legitimate concern without corrupting the evidence flow. The vulnerable-group finding also engages inclusion directly: the users failed by the service are the ones a user-needs-first culture exists to protect. Option C is least effective: bypassing your own governance to leak selectively is a serious breach of trust and process that would end your ability to operate, whatever its transparency motive. Option A subordinates a published commitment and an active user harm to presentational timing. Option D is selective reporting, arguably worse than a full delay because it actively misrepresents the balance of evidence to the board.

**Question 9 (hard) - A colleague's data sharing shortcut**

A performance analyst you work with closely offers to send you row-level service analytics, including user identifiers, over email "to save time setting up the secure transfer, since we're all on the same programme anyway". You need the data this week to meet an analysis milestone you promised the programme board.

Options:

A) Accept this once, given the deadline, and ask them to use the secure route next time
B) Decline the email transfer, use the secure route even if the milestone slips a few days, tell the analyst why the identifiers make this non-negotiable, and flag the near-miss so the transfer process gets faster for everyone
C) Accept the data but delete the identifier columns immediately after receiving it
D) Report the analyst to their line manager for proposing an insecure transfer

**Most effective: B. Least effective: A.**

**Explanation:** Option B holds the line where it must be held, identifiable user data never travels by convenience route, while treating the analyst as a partner rather than an offender: explaining the reasoning maintains the relationship, and flagging the friction in the secure process addresses the root cause, because shortcuts flourish where compliant routes are slow. A short milestone slip, honestly communicated to the board, is a small price. This is your data ethics and privacy skill in daily practice: addressing concerns with stakeholders and improving processes, not just refusing things. Option A is least effective because "just this once" is how data incidents happen: the breach occurs at transmission, the deadline does not mitigate it, and accepting normalises the shortcut for a colleague who will offer it again. Option C misunderstands the exposure: the identifiable data has already transited email and sits in two mailboxes; deleting columns afterwards cannot undo that. Option D leaps to escalation over a proposal you can simply decline and correct; reserve reporting for refusal to comply or actual incidents, or you spend trust you will need.

**Question 10 (moderate) - Resourcing an evaluation nobody wants to fund**

Discovery is ending on a service expected to affect two million users, and the programme has allocated no budget for monitoring and evaluation. The programme director says evaluation can be "picked up at live if the service takes off". You have one slot at next week's investment board.

Options:

A) Use the slot to present the evidence needs and risks: quantify what unfunded evaluation loses (baselines, phase-gate evidence, the ability to demonstrate benefits), propose a costed, proportionate M&E plan phased across alpha to live, and ask for a decision
B) Accept the director's position and plan to bid for evaluation funding once the service is live
C) Ask the evaluators on your team to do the monitoring work informally within their existing allocations
D) Email the investment board a copy of central government evaluation guidance and ask them to enforce it

**Most effective: A. Least effective: B.**

**Explanation:** Option A is influencing stakeholders to secure resources, named explicitly in your evaluation planning and strategy skill, done the way investment boards respond to: concrete losses, a costed proportionate ask, phased to the life cycle, and a clear decision request. Framing evaluation as the programme's ability to demonstrate its own benefits aligns your ask with the director's interests rather than against them. Option B is least effective because it accepts the one plan that cannot work: with no measurement before live, there is no baseline, no phase-gate evidence, and no possibility of impact evaluation, so the promised "pick up at live" evaluation would be permanently hobbled, for a service affecting two million users. Option C hides the cost rather than securing it: informal work is unfunded work, it burns your team, and it signals that evaluation is free. Option D outsources your influencing job to a document: guidance quoted without a costed plan and an advocate rarely moves an investment decision.

### Preparation tips

- **Anchor on public sector values before you start.** User needs first, transparency about evidence, collaboration across disciplines, accessibility and inclusion. When two options both seem defensible, the one that better serves users and evidence integrity usually scores higher.
- **Answer as the senior professional you are, at your best.** Situational judgement rewards your considered judgement, not your worst Tuesday. Choose what you would do with a clear head, but keep it practical: idealised answers that ignore constraints score poorly too.
- **Look for the option that addresses the root cause.** Options that fix the immediate symptom while leaving the underlying process, relationship, or incentive broken are usually mid-ranked. The strongest options often pair the immediate action with the systemic one.
- **Calibrate escalation.** The best answers usually attempt direct resolution first and escalate when the issue is irreversible, unresolved, or above your authority. Reflexive escalation and quiet absorption both lose marks.
- **Rehearse your real dilemmas.** Before the assessment, recall three difficult situations from your own evaluation practice: a pressured finding, a quality problem, an ethics concern. Ask what worked, what you would do differently, and why. This tunes your judgement to the scenarios far better than memorising "ideal" answers.
- **Read every option fully before choosing.** Most/least formats hide the discriminating detail in a subordinate clause: an otherwise strong option may conceal a concealment, or a weak-looking one may include the crucial conversation.

### Common pitfalls

- **Choosing the idealistic answer over the effective one.** "Refuse and escalate to the permanent secretary" sounds principled but skips every practical step a senior professional would actually take. Assessors want workable integrity, not gestures.
- **Selecting passive or deferring options.** Waiting, hoping, noting for next time, or handing the problem untreated to someone else are consistent low scorers, especially at senior level where you are expected to own the resolution.
- **Ignoring the values context.** In UK public sector scenarios, options that compromise transparency, user needs, accessibility, or data ethics for convenience or presentation are rarely the intended best answer, even when they look efficient.
- **Forgetting your leadership dimension.** At senior level, scenarios involving other evaluators test whether you develop people while fixing problems. Options where you silently redo someone's work, or correct without teaching, undervalue the assurance relationship.
- **Treating relationships as obstacles.** Options that win the argument but scorch the stakeholder usually rank below options that hold the same line while keeping the partnership working. You will need the same stakeholders next quarter.
- **Overthinking toward cynicism.** Do not select an option because you suspect the test wants a ruthlessly pragmatic answer. Well-built public sector SJTs consistently reward honest, collaborative, user-centred effectiveness.

## Conclusion

You have now worked through four complete assessment types, each grounded in the real substance of senior digital evaluation: cognitive reasoning with theories of change, measurement frameworks and assurance findings; numeric reasoning with completion rates, budgets, benefit-cost ratios and attrition arithmetic; verbal reasoning with guidance extracts, stakeholder emails, ethics submissions and published reports; and situational judgement across the pressured, human moments where evaluation integrity is really decided.

If you attempted the questions actively, you have done far more than prepare for a test. You have rehearsed the core disciplines of your profession: checking causal chains link by link, choosing the right denominator, refusing to let plausible inference pass as evidence, and holding professional lines while keeping stakeholder relationships strong. Every explanation you disagreed with and then worked through is a small act of the same quality assurance you apply to others' work, turned constructively on your own reasoning.

Be encouraged by what these assessments cannot measure, too. Your accumulated judgement from real evaluations, your relationships across the multidisciplinary team, your feel for when a finding will land and when it needs more groundwork: none of that is captured in a timed test, and all of it travels with you into any assessment centre or new role.

As next steps: revisit the questions you found hardest after a week and try them cold; generate your own practice items from a departmental performance pack or a published evaluation report; and, if you are preparing for a specific assessment, ask the recruiting team which test provider they use so you can practise the exact format. Beyond assessment preparation, the same skills reward continued investment through the Government Evaluation Community and cross-government analytical networks, through mentoring the evaluators whose work you assure, and through the steady habit of reflecting on your own decisions as rigorously as you evaluate services.

You manage evaluations that help government understand whether its digital services deliver value for the public. That work deserves an evaluator operating at full confidence. Keep practising, keep reflecting, and take the next assessment as what it is: a structured chance to show the judgement you exercise every day.
